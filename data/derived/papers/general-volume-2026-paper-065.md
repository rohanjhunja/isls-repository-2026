# Tools and Methods to Support data Visualization Literacies through Programming

**Conference:** ISLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
My research has focused on supporting the development of computational and data visualization literacies through the design of constructionist tools and the analysis of students’ programming trajectories. During my PhD, I developed PlayData, a block-based programming environment that enables learners to construct data visualizations. To investigate how they engage with the tool, I created methods for capturing and analyzing process data from the environment, including a machine-learning approach for modeling programming trajectories and the use of Epistemic Network Analysis to characterize the connections among students’ Data Visualization Programming moves over time. These approaches reveal how learners navigate open-ended programming tasks and how computational and data-related practices can mutually reinforce one another. Building on this foundation, my research agenda seeks to advance our understanding of how learners develop data and computational literacies and to inform the design of learning environments that foster meaningful engagement with computing. Research background Throughout my research trajectory, I have worked on two inter-related topics: (1) the development of computational tools rooted in constructionist learning perspectives, and (2) unveiling students’ learning trajectories in open-ended tasks focused on programming and data visualization. My research is grounded in prior work on data visualization and computational literacies, both central to understanding and participating in the world today. However, many of these solutions still rely on analyzing final products, offering only a limited perspective on the practices in which students engage. The use of learning analytics techniques to analyze large volumes of data generated by log-capturing tools has expanded our understanding of learning processes in programming tasks, enabling more detailed descriptions of their pathways (Berland et al., 2014; Blikstein et al., 2014). Despite advances in this area, process-oriented metrics could still benefit from important improvements, including collecting information about the programming process as a continuous flow, rather than as isolated records captured in save or compile states; and considering the specific characteristics of different types of tasks, recognizing that trajectories vary according to context (for example, when developing a program to analyze data, the pathways followed will differ from those involved in creating code to solve a mathematical problem). Epistemic Network Analysis (ENA) can also contribute to examining how events in a programming session connect to one another and how these connections evolve over time. ENA is a quantitative ethnography technique that identifies connections among coded elements in data and represents them through a network model (Shaffer et al., 2016; Shaffer & Ruis, 2017). Although ENA has been widely used to analyze student discourse, log data in programming environments can also be explored using these techniques (Fernandez et al., 2025). In my doctoral research, I developed an automated method using machine learning techniques to describe students’ trajectories as they created data visualizations using the PlayData programming environment (Figure 1, left). Additionally, I used ENA to characterize, through connections between events, the main actions in which students engaged during these activities (Figure 1, right), which I termed Data Visualization Programming moves (DVP moves). By analyzing these connections temporally, I generated insights into the dynamics among these a ctions and into how programming sessions evolve over time (further details can be found in Fernandez et al., 2025).

Design-based research The Design-Based Research (DBR) approach (Barab & Squire, 2004; DBRC, 2003) combines the systematic design of tools or intervention strategies implemented in real-world settings with reflective practices among those involved, considering the challenges and affordances encountered. This type of research is characterized by continuous cycles of design, enactment, analysis, and redesign, employing documentation methods that make it possible to connect theory with outcomes of interest. I have been working with DBR since my master’s research (which involved the design of physical computing toolkit) and, more recently, in the development of PlayData (Fernandez, Blikstein, et al., 2024; Fernandez, Freitas, et al., 2024). Moving forward My broader goal as a researcher is to contribute to understanding how people learn computing and data science in contexts of authorship and design. Building on the background described in the previous section, the research agenda I plan to pursue unfolds across three interconnected strands encompassing methodological, pedagogical, and cognitive aspects.

Refinement of methods for analyzing learning trajectories: To describe, categorize, and visualize students’ trajectories in programming tasks, I aim to keep developing a line of research centered on the application of machine-learning techniques combined with ENA and qualitative video analysis. The types of questions I am currently interested in understanding include: How do different techniques for segmenting data affect the results and patterns obtained from automated analyses of learning trajectories? What are the most effective ways of visualizing patterns related to learning trajectories to support feedback for teachers and learners? Development of tools, resources, and pedagogical strategies for data visualization and computational literacies: Additionally, I am interested in contributing to the development of educational tools, resources and pedagogical strategies aimed at integrating computing and data science. This includes, for example, incorporating new features into the PlayData environment and documenting its affordances and challenges in introductory programming/data-science courses. I am also particularly interested in developing functionalities that support the for this line of research include: How do different pedagogical approaches, types of tasks, and forms of student organization (e.g., individual tasks or pair programming) influence trajectories in programming environments? How do the characteristics of the tools impact the trajectories of learners? Understanding how interactions with AI tools influence programming learning: Generative-AI-based tools have radically transformed how people code and learn to code. Given the growing role that these tools play in CS learning (often without pedagogical intentionality), I have recently become interested in better understanding how interactions with them affect students’ ways of thinking about and with their code. In this sense, I aim to investigate how learning trajectories unfold in both AI-supported and non-AI-supported contexts, examining how these interactions shape learners’ strategies, conceptual understanding, and agency in the programming process. Ultimately, I expect that my work may provide researchers and educators with new insights into how learners develop computational and data literacies, and to inspire the design of constructionist tools to engage them in productive explorations with computers.
