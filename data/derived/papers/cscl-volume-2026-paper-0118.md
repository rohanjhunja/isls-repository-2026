# Enhancing K-12 Teachers’ Confidence and Competence in STEM Integration Using Blended Learning in Professional Development

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
STEM-integrated learning in K-12 education is essential for students to build career readiness. However, building confidence and competence in STEM teachers to enact new, cross-disciplinary curriculum is difficult, and the best way to design high-quality professional development (PD) programs for teaching STEM integration is unclear. This study introduces a blended learning PD model that added a new online course to a traditional onsite research experience to scaffold teachers’ comprehension of mechanobiology knowledge and practices of pedagogy. Piloting the model with seven K-12 teachers, we examined how this blended format scaffolded in-person and online collaborative learning. Findings show the online course enhanced teachers’ grasp of complex research concepts and competence in teaching STEM-integrated topics. This study highlights the potential of an online course to promote confidence and competence in teachers conducting cutting-edge, interdisciplinary STEM research.

Fostering student engagement in STEM via real-world research contexts is necessary to strengthen workforce preparation (NRC, 201 2). This requires K-12 educators to shift towards STEM integration, applying cross-disciplinary knowledge to solve complex problems  (Schweingruber et al., 2014). It further requires an emphasis on engineering design practices to enhance students' ability to apply interdisciplinary knowledge (NGSS Lead States, 2013; Moore et al., 2014). Despite the interdisciplinary nature of modern STEM, K-12 education has been slow to keep pace (Yoon et al. 2024; Caner & Ogan-Bekiroglu, 2025). Targeted PD for STEM-integrated topics must support gains in two essential constructs: confidence, or self-efficacy in delivering curricula, and competence, the Content Knowledge (CK) and Pedagogical Content Knowledge (PCK) required to enact it (Acar & Büyüksahin, 2021; Caner & Ogan-Bekiroglu, 2025). Research Experiences for Teachers (RETs) are effective mechanisms for this, offering in-person, collaborative environments with STEM researchers  (Yoon et al., 2024). However, the optimal design features of RETs vary widely and are not fully understood (Enderle et al., 2014). To address these challenges, we propose a blended learning model, leveraging technologies and online platforms to add flexibility and tailored content, amplifying learning outcomes  (Tabak & Kyza, 2018; Tayebinik & Puteh, 2013). Our theoretical mechanism posits that asynchronous online learning  builds foundational CK by utilizing multimedia assets to facilitate understanding via multiple cognitive channels. This allows  in-person time to be dedicated to high-quality PCK development and collaboration. We focused this RET on mechanobiology, a field merging physics, engineering, and biology, providing an authentic context for interdisciplinary STEM integration. We piloted the blended design in a six-week mechanobiology RET in 2025 at a northeastern U.S. research center. Participants were seven local K-12 teachers representing diverse STEM subjects and teaching experience levels (ranging from 3 to 17 years). Teachers worked with a graduate lab mentor on a research project while completing three online course modules. The online modules included multimedia assets, peer discussion boards to establish peer support, discipline-specific scaffolds, examples of real-world mechanobiology applications, and expert lectures to be paired with in-person flipped classrooms. We collected pre-and post-program surveys, online coursework (lesson plans, discussion entries), and transcripts from three focus groups. W e analyzed open-ended surveys and transcripts using interaction analysis to investigate collaboration. We employed a modified grounded theory approach to evaluate 114 minutes of focus group audio, iteratively searching for evidence of teachers connecting their learning to specific design features (Glaser, 2008). The results section is organized around two key findings: 1) the blended PD model supported teachers’ build of confidence and competence in STEM-integrated teaching, and 2) specific design features, including examples of real-world applications and opportunities for collaboration, were instrumental in this change. 674 Teachers report grasp of CK after computer-supported learning in PD Six of the seven teachers completed the post-program survey and indicated high levels of confidence (on a 1– 5 scale, with 1 being the lowest) in their understanding of “what mechanobiology is ”, “why it matters” (Mean = 4.67), and “how physical forces, such as stress and strain, affect cells and tissues” (Mean = 4.33). These results reflect consistently strong confidence in CK related to mechanobiology, which coincides with the evidence of a teacher explicitly stating in a focus group session that he gained confidence after the blended RET, mentioning “I am the expert in mechanobiology in the room now.” Five out of the six teachers agreed that they gained confidence in “implementing hands-on or project-based learning experiences in mechanobiology with [their] students” (Mean = 4.2) and in “teaching interdisciplinary STEM to [their] students” (Mean = 4.2), indicating an increase in confidence of PCK in STEM integration. Four teachers indicated in the post-survey that they understood the core principles of mechanobiology and could connect them to their teaching after completing the online course. The 2025 cohort stated that the online course included foundational concepts in mechanobiology and STEM integrated knowledge that led to elevated confidence in completing lab work. Speaking of his experience participating in the RET for the second year, a teacher  compared the in-person program in 2024 with the new blended design added in 2025: “[The online course] really helped me apply [mechanobiology content knowledge] in the lab this year, because last year I was a little lost, and this year, I have a much better grasp on things.” Completing the online course concurrently with lab research also benefited first year teachers  in building a foundation of knowledge in mechanobiology. One first time participant said that learning about mechanobiology from a blended learning environment was helpful: “I think [the online course] helped just give…background information and a foundation…as the summer went on…I felt like I was understanding things better and better.” Another first year RET participant reported that online course content prepared her to participate in lab meetings: “we’re in these…intimidating lab meetings, with everybody and the PIs…I liked the fact that I knew what [mechanosensing proteins] were prior to entering these meetings…it was good just having that constant repetition from this course.” A teacher further explained “It's pretty overwhelming when you start in the lab with all these, you know, new machines, new people…So it was really helpful to have a solid grasp on science concepts first.” This suggests new and returning teachers agreed that  the online course strengthened their understanding  of complex, mechanobiology knowledge and promoted confidence in lab research. Design features of the online course towards developing teachers’ competence Key design features for developing competence in STEM-integration included real-life applications, classroom examples, and expert access, which were implemented with multimedia assets including lab and classroom videos, podcasts, images, text, hands-on lesson plans, student worksheets, and teacher guides. Teachers showed gains in CK, shifting from general to applied understandings of mechanobiology. For instance, one teacher’s definition of mechanobiology evolved from an “interdisciplinary of [sic] biological systems” (pre-survey) to “understanding diseases, helping cure and prevent such diseases” (post-survey). Four of six teachers ranked interacting with “actual research and innovations” as the most useful feature, alongside multimedia assets, peer discussion boards, discipline-specific scaffolds, and real-world application examples. The application-based approach also supported PCK development with teachers  preparing lesson plans using specific industry skincare examples to inspire classroom activities  and captivate students uninterested in the traditional curriculum.  Additionally, combining online expert resources with in-person “flipped classroom” meetups established a social accountability structure that deepened content engagement and collaborative PCK reflection more effectively than self-directed modules alone. For instance, teachers noted that viewing an expert’s classroom enactment and subsequent discussions made them reflect  on their instruction and explained the accountability stimulated engagement: “when I would see these hour-long lectures, I was bought in, because I’ m meeting these people tomorrow.” Finally, while the experience of being a student boosted their confidence, teachers exhibited varying preferences for how CK and PCK should be sequenced. Some prefer to build deep conceptual understanding before practicing pedagogy, and others favor concurrent learning, indicating a need for flexible online professional development structures. This pilot study advances the field by providing a scalable blueprint for research-based PD. O ur findings demonstrate that utilizing online modules to manage the complexity of STEM-integrated CK allows teachers to reserve in-person interactions for deep PCK reflection and collaboration (Acar & Büyüksahin, 2021; Caner & Ogan-Bekiroglu, 2025). This addresses the core requirement of the NGSS – a focus of engineering design process

– and responds to the need to bring engineering practices to biology classes (Lead States, 2013). Future research

will focus on modularizing dense content to offer more flexible pathways between deep content acquisition and integrated pedagogical reflection.
