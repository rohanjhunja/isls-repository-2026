# How Do Novice and Experienced Teachers Differ in AI Interaction during Learner-Centered Lesson Planning?: Ordered Network Analysis

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study explores how novice and experienced teachers interact with generative AI during learner-centered lesson planning. To ensure that generative AI supports lesson planning regardless of a teacher’s experience, it is essential to understand the differences between novice and experienced teachers. Ordered Network Analysis showed that experienced teachers considered learners and context before evaluating generative AI responses, unlike novice teachers. This finding implies the need for tailored AI guidelines based on experience level.

## Introduction

Learner-centered lessons have long been emphasized, but many teachers continue to struggle due to a lack of knowledge about how to plan such lessons  (Silva et al., 2021). Generative AI can provide resources for lesson planning (vanden Berg & du Plessis, 2023), potentially addressing these challenges. However, the effectiveness of generative AI depends on the user’s  experience and knowledge  (Kehoe, 2023). For example, experienced teachers, drawing on their diverse backgrounds, can make flexible, context-appropriate decisions, whereas novice teachers often find it difficult to consider learners and contextual factors when  planning lessons (Pratiwi, 2024). As a result, t he quality of learner-centered lessons varies depending on the  teacher's experience, even when generative AI  is used. To ensure that generative AI supports high-quality learner-centered lesson planning regardless of a teacher’s experience, it is essential to understand the differences between novice and  experienced teachers and provide tailored guidelines for their use. In response to this need, this study aims to visual ize and examine the differences in AI interactions between novice and experienced teachers during lear ner-centered lesson planning with generative using Ordered Network Analysis  (ONA). ONA generates a directed weighted network (Tan et al., 2023), allowing for the identification of differing AI interaction patterns between novice and experienced teachers.

## Participants and procedures

The participants were Korean elementary school teac hers. Teachers with 1-3 years of teaching experience were classified as novice teachers, while those with mor e than 3 years of experience were categorized as ex perienced teachers (Huberman, 1993). The group was evenly split between novice teachers (50%) and experienced teachers (50%) with the total of 10 participants.

Prior to the experiment, teachers received free ChatGPT accounts and were tasked with designing a oneperiod lesson on modern communication methods. This topic was selected for its suitability in designing a learnercentered class within limited time and validated th rough a pilot test with in-service teachers.  The research participants individually designed a learner-centered lesson for 60 minutes  on a video conferencing platform. They simultaneously shared both the ChatGPT free ve rsion window and the lesson plandocument window, verbalized their thoughts using a think-aloud method, and underlined the parts of the lesson plan that incorporated generative AI’s responses. The lesson planning process was observed and recorded by two researchers.

## Data analysis

To analyze the recorded interactions between teachers and generative AI using ONA, the teachers’ discourse and behavior were coded.  The codes were developed through a deductive approa ch based on a literature review (Maaravi et al., 2021; vanden Berg & du Plessis, 2 023) and an inductive approach through open coding.  A total of 9 codes were derived: Initial Prompting, Follow-up Prompting, AI Re-prompting, Accepting AI Response, Rejecting AI Response, AI Elaborating, Considering Learners and Contexts, Search Materials, and Lesson Planning. The researchers held a pilot coding session to ensure consistent code application and analysis standards among researchers. Two coders analyzed videos together and resolved disagreements through discussion. ONA was employed to visualize the developed codes a nd compare the sequenced interaction patterns with AI between t eachers based on their experience. The Stanza Windo w method was used to form a network model of the connections between interactions, with the stanza size set to 4. Nodes representing interactions were sized proportionally to their frequency, with large r nodes indicating higher frequency. The edges conn ecting the nodes indicated the strength of sequential occurrences, with thicker edges representing more frequently occurring events. Finally, the chevron on the edge indicates that the side it points to has a higher occurrence rate. To interpret the ONA graphs, the method used by Shin et al. (2024) for graph interpretation was applied.

## Results and discussion

Figure 1 visualizes the differences in interaction patterns between experienced teachers (Red) and novice teachers (Blue) using ONA. The reason why “Lesson Planning"” is prominently observed is because the teachers’ actions were centered around writing lesson plans. To compare the distribution of projection points be tween groups in the ONA space, a Mann-Whitney U test was used  (Swiecki et al., 2020). A statistically significant difference between experienced teachers (Mdn=-0.27, N=5) and novice teachers (Mdn=0.18, N=5) was found on the X axis (MR1) of the ONA space  (U=0, p=0.01, r=1.00). The leftward skew of “Consideration Learners and Context s” on the X axis suggests it is mainly observed in experienced teachers. Figure 1 Differences in Interaction Patterns between Experienced Teachers (Red) and Novice Teachers (Blue) Experienced teachers and novice teachers showed dif ferences in whether they went through the “Considering Learners and Contexts” when evaluating  AI responses, after creating prompts but before writing lesson plans. Experienced teachers created prompts for the generative AI, then conside red learners and contexts before accepting, rejecting, or elaborating on the AI's response. They then proceeded to write lesson plans. This pattern was consistent even when experienced teache rs searched for information using the internet or t extbooks without using AI. These findings align with prior  research suggesting that experienced teachers actively utilize practical knowledge during lesson planning (Koni & Krull, 2018). On the other hand, novice teachers, compared to experienced teachers,  went through “Considering Learners and Contexts” le ss frequently both after creating prompts for AI and before writing lesson plans, as well as after searching for information and before writing lesson plans. This difference aligns with previous research findi ngs, which indicate that variations in how users assess and utilize generative AI ’s responses depend ing on their prior knowledge levels  (Lee, 2024). Since differences appear in the sequence of interaction p atterns with generative AI between experienced teac hers and novice teachers, it is necessary to take this into account when developing guidelines for teachers. Sp ecifically, guidelines are needed to prompt teachers to consider the actual classroom context when evaluating the responses of generative AI.  Despite the significance of this study, it does not  address how factors such as AI proficiency and perspectives on learner-centered lessons influenced these differences, suggesting a need for further research.
