# Emotion as a Condition and Target of Learning Design During Emergency Remote Teaching

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In this study we seek to understand the recursive relationships between feeling, sense-making, and rapid online course design at the onset of COVID-19. To elucidate how emotion was implicated in learning design decisions, we offer a qualitative analysis of nine instructor interviews from diverse academic disciplines. Originally developed for an open online course, these interviews exemplify how instructors at a large midwestern university moved their residential courses online at the onset of the pandemic.

## Introduction

The purpose of this research is to elucidate the relationships between feelings, sense-making, and the practice of learning design during the period of emergency remote teaching during the early months of COVID-19 (Hodges et al., 2020). To advance these insights, we draw o n situative theories of learning (Lave, 1991), emot ional configurations (Vea, 2020), and reification (Wenger, 1998) as analytic lenses. Through a qualitative a nalysis of nine instructor interviews in an open online course, we highlight how instructors’ and students’ emotions operated recursively as both a condition and target of learning design. In detailing the recursive dynamics of emotion, we specifically attend to the process of reification a nd the ways that emotion was both reified and serve d as a condition for reification. The research question that guides this study is: How did instructors relate feelings, sensemaking, and learning design during the period of emergency remote teaching at the onset of the pandemic?

## Theoretical Perspectives

Situative theories of learning foreground meaning as a process of negotiation between people in a specific social context. This process of (re)constructing meaning is “not just doing in and of itself” but the “doing in a historical and social context that gives structure and meaning to what we do (Wenger, 1998, p. 47). Emotional configurations can be understood in similar terms as situative theories of learning. Rather than conceptualizing emotions such as grief, anger, and anxiety as static, internalized states, emotional configurations attend to the relationships people make between feelings, sense-making, and social practice. In this way, emotion becomes meaningful when it shapes—and is shaped by—social practice and sense-making (Vea, 2020). Following Wenger (1998), we understand reification as the process by which people project meaning onto the material world. This process of reification gives material “points of focus around which the negotiation of meaning becomes organized” (p. 58). In the conte xt of our study, we are specifically interested in how emotion—as a target of learning design—becomes reified, and as a condition for learning design, how it  serves to reify other design considerations.

## Data and Method

Our data set consists of nine academically diverse instructor interviews created for use in an open online course on resilient teaching. The interview protocol consisted of five questions, which instructors could answer via video call or through a written response. We used directed content analysis (Hsieh & Shannon, 2005) and a conceptual framework developed by Vea (2020) as a starting poi nt for analyzing the data. In the first round of co ding, the researchers open-coded the data set for language that explicitly referenced emotional states (e.g., anxiety, worry). After the initial round of coding, two coders worked within a smaller subset of coded data to identify segments of discourse where emotion was implicated within instructors’ course design process. After collecting segments of data that linked emotion to learning design, the re searchers began to generate sub-themes that were co mmon across multiple segments of data. During the iterative process of generating sub-themes and merging sub-themes into larger themes, the researchers were guided by theoretical considerations from situative theories of learning and emotional configurations.

## Findings

Collectively, these findings show how emotion functioned recursively as a condition and target of course design. Emotion as a condition of practice Emotions such as isolation, concern, and anxiety were common among instructors during the period of emergency remote teaching. The loss of classroom community and collaboration in particular were often cited as catalysts for these emotions.

As a condition of learning design practice, the emo tions of concern and anxiety functioned to reify instructors’ desire for community and connection within online classes. For instance, some instructors articulated that the role of the teacher in emergency remote teaching shifted away from didactic lectures towards relationship building within the class community.

In an effort to keep the class connected, some instructors adopted a“flipped classroom” where short video lectures were pre-recorded and posted online, and s ynchronous discussions were offered as opportunitie s to discuss the recorded material. In this way, feeling s of concern and worry flowed through the practice of course design, and motivated a desire for increased commun ity and connection; these desires subsequently reif ied community building practices such as the “flipped classroom.” Emotion as a target of practice While emotions such as concern and worry flowed through the practice of course design, emotion was also reified as an explicit target of design efforts as well. For instance, instructors overwhelmingly acknowledged that student stress and anxiety had increased due to the pandemi c. To alleviate those negative emotions, instructor s shaped new practices and materials with the intent of positively impacting students’ emotions. In one illustrative example, an instructor included light-hearted hippopotamus videos at the beginning of class time explicitly to “lighten the mood.” Reifying student emotion as a target of course desi gn also came with certain tensions, particularly concerning the course goals that had been articulat ed before the pandemic. While many instructors want ed to provide additional flexibility and accommodations during this difficult time, there was a perception that doing so would be inauthentic to the professional field they were training students for. When a theater instructor explained her decision for extending deadlines, she noted that it ran contradictory to the common practices of the profession: “In theater, deadlines are very firm (opening night  is opening night) but in these stressful times, it  would have been inhumane to be that strict.” These tensions su ggest that reifying emotion as a target of learning  design implicates other considerations within the milieu of the learning environment.

## Discussion

While there is little doubt that emotion figured pr ominently in learning design during the phase of em ergency remote teaching, the question remains how such insi ghts will influence the field of Learning Sciences more broadly. One promising direction for future inquiry may be attending to the tensions that arise when e motion is reified as a target of learning design. While our s tudy showcased how reifying emotion as a target of learning design proved incongruous with pre-defined course g oals, future studies may find that reifying emotion  comes with different tensions given different contexts.
