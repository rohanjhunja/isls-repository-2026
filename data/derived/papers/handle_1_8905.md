# Silly or Substantive?: How Video Club Can Help Science Teacher Candidates See Strengths in Students’ Contributions

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Teacher learning occurs at the intersection of practice, critical discourse, and reflection. Yet, the fast-paced nature of teaching makes stepping back to see students’ ideas as resources difficult for teacher candidates. Video club (Barnhart & van Es, 2020) affords a collaborative space for teacher candidates to slow down so that they can uncover and challenge underlying assumptions about students and their contributions. This study explores video club as a tool for helping teacher candidates see strengths in students’ contributions. We analyze data from science methods courses for teacher candidates, finding that video club can help them to see value in students’ contributions that they initially perceived as “silly.”

## Objectives and significance

Equitable science teaching requires noticing students’ strengths (Louie et al., 2021) and building upon students’ multiple ways of knowing (Warren et al., 2020). Whi le research has explored how to help teacher candid ates (TCs) notice students’ disciplinary resources (e.g., Robertson et al., 2015), less is known about how to help TCs take up students’ questions and ideas when they do not align with the pathway prescribed by the curriculum (e.g, Reiser et al., 2021). This paper illustrates the po wer of video as a reflective tool for TCs to notice, value, and leverage student contributions. Specifically, we focus on how video club (Barnhart & van Es, 2020) can help TCs reframe students’ “silly” questions and ideas as generative resources for science learning, positioning students as competent inquirers (Keifert & Stevens, 2019). Ulti mately, this shift can contribute to an inclusive c lassroom community where a teacher transitions from “the-one-who-teaches, to one who is taught in dialogue with  the students” (Freire, 2018, p. 80). To this end, we as k: How can video clubs help science teacher candida tes see strengths in students’ “silly” contributions?

## Theoretical background

What teachers notice about students’ contributions (e.g., questions, ideas, actions) is shaped by the sociopolitical frames through which teachers see their students (Louie et al., 2021). Pervasive deficit ideologies shape teacher noticing (Louie, 2020), constraining opportunities for learning (Horn, 2007) as teachers position stud ents’ contributions as not being relevant or appropriate (Flores & Rosa, 2015). Moreover, science education typically draws on exogenous understandings of inquiry, valuing classroom forms of professional practices (NRC, 2012) rather than taking students’ contributions seriously (Keifert & Stevens, 2019). As a result, generativ e resources like students’ playful interactions are often dismissed or overlooked in science classrooms (Keifert et al., 2021). Thus, there is a need for teacher educators to understand how deficit perspectives of students’ contributions can be surfaced, challenged, and rearticulated (Philip, 2011) in order to help teachers attend to issues o f equity and inclusion and see students’ resources as strengths (Louie et al., 2021). We propose that video club could be used as a tool to help teacher candidates reframe student contributions (e.g., reframe contributions that they had initially dismissed as “silly” instead as substantive). In video clubs, teachers share and analyze short video c lips of their own classrooms (van Es & Sherin, 2007). Through repeated viewing and discussion, video clubs support teachers to consider classroom discourse, curricular materials, and teaching strategies in the context of rich and complex classroom interactions (Borko et al., 2008).  We propose that specific affordances of video club (watching videos repeatedly, pausing for discussion), when paired with anti-deficit framing (Louie et al., 2021), could help TCs see students’ contributions as resources integral to sc ience learning and notice resources that may have b een overlooked as they were teaching. In addition, thes e reflective discussions could enable TCs to collec tively imagine how they could cultivate students’ strength s in the context of the lesson they are watching or  in their future teaching.

## Method

This study was conducted in two methods courses at a large university in the midwestern US that took p lace virtually using Zoom in Autumn 2021. One course focused on middle childhood science (grades 4-8) and included 15 TCs; the other focused on secondary science (grades 7-12) and included 7 TCs. We use pseudonyms that were ICLS2022 Proceedings 961 © ISLS chosen by TCs to represent themselves. We focus our  analysis on video clubs, which took place 3 times during each course. We use interaction analysis (Jordan & Henderson, 1995) and inductive coding (Charmaz, 2006) to explore how this design supported teachers to see s trengths in students’ contributions, focusing on ho w they reframed students’ contributions as substantive rather than “silly.” During video clubs, TCs selected, watched, and disc ussed short video clips of their teaching (3-4 minutes) in small groups with four of their peers and a facilitator from the research team. TCs shared clips from their field placement as well as from their “microteaching” assignments (a mini-lesson planned and taught by TCs to their peers). To build toward an anti-deficit frame, before beginning video clubs, TCs read a case excerpt from Warren et al. (2020) about Jonathan, an African Ame rican boy who argued that the sun is living – an id ea that aligns with contemporary systems-based perspectives in science, but that was initially dismissed by Jo nathan’s teacher. TCs discussed the case from the perspective of the teacher and the student, then reflected on connections to their own experiences as teachers and learners. Video club was introduced as a way to help TCs practice seeing students’ strengths that might similarly be overlooked during teaching. Video club discussion was guided by three prompts: (1) How does the teacher or activity make space for students’ ideas, (2) How could the teacher or activity make space for students’ ideas, (3) What strengths do you notice in students’ contributions?

Our analytic focus on “silly” questions and ideas emerged from a tension initially raised in the secondary science class. In her video club, Fairygirl explained that only “genuine” questions or ideas (rather than “silly” questions or ideas) were welcome in her classroom: “I think t hat that's really important to talk about in your c lassroom to make it a safe and respected environment for everyb ody to bring their own opinions. Not silly opinions  or silly facts, but genuine questions, genuine ideas should always be welcomed.” Here, Fairygirl distinguished between “genuine” and “silly.” Yet, in reality, there are n o objective criteria that can help teachers disting uish between these categories. Moreover, resources like children’s play that might be constructed as silly (Keifert  & Stevens,

learning (Keifert et al., 2021; Pierson & Brady, 2020). Based on this interaction, when video clubs were introduced in the middle childhood science class, w e attended specifically to how TCs constructed stud ents’ contributions as “silly” (or inappropriate) versus “genuine” (substantive or generative). Below, we pr esent examples from the middle childhood class, focusing on two sessions: (1) the “practice” video club, whe re the whole class participated in a video club using a video of the instructor, Pierson, teaching in a 6th grade classroom, and (2) the first video club in which TCs shared videos of their own instruction. We show how video club helped TCs surface and challenge tensions related to “sill y” questions in science classrooms, reframing stude nts’ contributions as substantive rather than “silly.” W e propose that slowing down and collaboratively see king out students’ strengths in their video clubs supported TCs’ shifts from constraining to expansive perspect ives of students’ contributions.

Noticing and responding to students’ contributions The framing above helped TCs consider how they could invite and cultivate students’ contributions in their own teaching, even if their students were afraid of bei ng “wrong” or “silly.” When sharing his own teachin g video, Shoobop paused to highlight a moment when he says “ There’s no wrong answers in this classroom.” Margar et commented on this statement, saying “it’s important to open up the floor to the idea that nothing is off the table.” With this comment, Margaret affirmed that all stude nts’ contributions could be valuable for learning. To encourage Shoobop to share his perspective, the facilitator asked: “so what were you thinking?” He explained: One of my favorite things to do in the classroom is  just kind of like, if it feels like one angle isn't working, where, like, as before I was trying to go, like, there head on, like, let’s talk about the content, I was, like, “well if we try to think outside any context, like, what we think is right or wrong answer, like let's discuss, like, what could be.” I don't know. I don’t think I'd go so far as to say like, “what would this look like in a par allel universe,” but, like, “why don't we talk about that?” and then whatever we end up talking about we can then draw back to more, like, the actual meat and potatoes of the lesson.

This response echoes themes from the “practice” video club: that it is important to hear students’ ide as because they can connect to the “actual meat and potatoes of the lesson.” Yet, it also positions students’ contributions as supportive of the curricular pathway, rather than a n opportunity to open new lines of inquiry. In this  sense, Shoobop’s explanation indicates openness to student s’ ideas, but does not offer students epistemic aut hority (Haverly et al., 2020; Miller et al., 2018). Other students expressed similar stances. Ren said, “He was ready to go with it. He just kept the train rolling and encouraging that line of thought. I think that gave a great opportunity for students to share their thoughts.” Billy added “He doesn't necessarily tell them if their ideas are right or wrong, but he does tell them, ‘yeah, that's a great idea’ and he still uses the ideas to just continue on that conversation.” Once again, TCs positioned students’ ideas as eithe r “correct” or “incorrect” (even if the teacher wit hholds this information), which could lead to deficit perspectives of students’ ideas. Still, these statements illustrate a shift in the TCs’ stance away from narrowly focusing on idea s represented in their standards and curricula and toward inviting and valuing students’ ideas. Later in the discussion, TCs discussed challenges with this approach. They said it can be difficult to respond to students’ ideas in the moment. Shoobop shared a strategy that he used to slow down during instruction: repeating students’ contri butions (to allow himself time to process). He expl ained: “I think that’s something that generally makes me uncomfortable in the classroom is like I very much so want to be the expert, which I think every teacher should be, and from time to time, when I'm like ‘OK well I act ually like need to think about what that person just said,’ be cause I'm also a literal human being and need to th ink about things.” Slowing down in this way allowed Shoobop to create space for himself to notice and respond, which in turn contributes to a classroom community where students’ ideas are valued. ## Conclusions and implications

Our findings have implications for the design of science methods courses that aim to support equitable teaching. Our data demonstrate how video club can help TCs see strengths in students’ contributions. Here, we focus on the contrast between framing students’ contributions as silly versus substantive, but we propose that this  lens could be applied to other student contributions seen as “inappropriate” for science learning. Video clubs allow TCs time to pause and reflect on students’ contributions and imagine generative possibilities in their responses. Over time, this could lead to shifts toward anti-deficit approaches that value and take seriously students’ contributions. We conjecture that as these TCs continue with video cl ub, they could shift toward seeing students’ contri butions as valuable in their own right (rather than only as means to the “meat and potatoes” of the lesson).
