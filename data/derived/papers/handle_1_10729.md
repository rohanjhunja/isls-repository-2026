# Impact of Spatial Positioning on Collaborative Small Group Equity

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Using video of collaborative small group work, we focus on three low-participating students in relatively inequitable small groups to develop a better understanding of what contextual features afford and constrain their participation. Several potential features emerged. In this paper, we focus on how students’ spatial positioning impacts in/equitable group dynamics. We propose the concept of a group’s “center of attention” to better understand why certain positioning affords or constrains students’ opportunities to learn. We find these low-participating students’ participation is afforded when their spatial positioning is improved and constrained otherwise. This finding may support more equitable small groups by suggesting strategies to increase the participation of the students who suffer the most from inequity.

## Introduction and framework

Research in math and science education grounded in sociocultural views of learning suggest that collaborative small groups can serve to bolster students’ learning and create a more equitable learning environment (e.g., Kelly, 2013; Theobold et al, 2020). However, this pedagogy  can sometimes have the unintended effect of amplif ying inequities (e.g., Shah & Lewis, 2019; Reinholz et a l, 2022). In this study, we focus on low-participating students in relatively inequitable small groups. Understanding these students is essential because we believe inequities in small groups harms these students the most.

As a theoretical and analytic beginning point, we u tilize Esmonde’s (2009) definition of equity in collaborative small groups. She defines equity in t hat context as “the fair distribution of opportunit ies to learn” (p. 249). In this definition, there are two intertwining considerations when analyzing an opportunity to learn. First, we must consider students' access to disciplinary c ontent and discourse practices. For all students in  a group to have this access, there must be some degree of participation from each group member, since communication with others is central to what it means to have access to discourse practices and content in this view (Esmonde, 2009). Shaw and Lewis (2019) build on Esmonde’s definition by proposing the concept of participatory equity, which is the fair distribution of opportunities to participa te. Through these lenses, participatory equity is a  necessary condition for an equitable collaborative small group.

The second consideration of fair access is students’ access to positive identities as knowers and doers of the discipline (Esmonde, 2009). Analytically, we draw on Engle et al.’s (2014) influence framework to assess this. In this framework, influence within a small group is socially negotiated through student discourse. Our view is that students who consistently have low or no influence in a group do not have fair access to positive identities as knowers and doers of scie nce, especially when one or two students negotiate a disproportionate amount of influence. Engle et al. identify components of the negotiation of influence, one of which is students’ access to the conversational floor. Participatory equity is close ly related to access to the conversational floor (Shah & Lewis,

2019) and is a prerequisite for a student gaining fair access to positive identities.

For these reasons, low-participating students are worth examining. With th e goal of supporting more equitable collaborative small groups, it makes sens e to focus on those harmed most by inequity. We aim to find strategies to afford greater participation, influence, and ultimately fairer access to learning opportunities.

## Data source and methodology

We use video of collaborative small groups in a college pre-orientation program that aimed to improve retention among STEM-interested students from historically under-represented groups (Franklin et al., 2018). The program ran for nine days with part of each day spent  learning science cont ent in an inquiry format. The small group activities were recorded by a team of researchers. Small groups were together for multiple days at a t ime, giving us an opportunity to study each group in detail and observe variation across days. The groups in our investigation worked together from days 4 to 9. Students spent most of the instructional time in a classroom setting working on tasks related to understanding the mechanisms of climate change. Our analysis focused on three groups we perceived as relatively inequitabl e. Using Esmonde’s (2009) definition of equity, we noticed that in these groups, one or two students tended to dominate on-task conversation and group influence, shutting out the ideas of other students and thus b locking their opportunities to learn. Our focus groups had one or more students who rarely participated in on-task discussion. This dynamic prevented these students from exerting influence over the group’s work, which in turn blocked them from accessing positive identities as knowers and doers of science. We focused on the lowest participating student from each group. Our analysis addressed two relevant questions. First, what constrains the students from participating more frequently? Second, when the student did participate, what features afforded that? Our analysis aimed to generate codable features that appeared to constrain or afford low participating students’ equitable access. For each focus group, we watched all available footage, a total of 25 hours of video. The first author complied field notes from video viewing and generated tentative codes based on patterns in the data. The tentative codes were presented to the second author and another researcher familiar with the data for discussion and revision. In a second round of analysis, we looked for both more support and counterexamples for the tentative claims regarding features that afford or constrain participation. This iterative process identified four features in the data that appeared to impact the participation of the focus students across our three groups.

## Results

First, we found that instructor and peer  “callouts” tended to result in subsequent increases in participation.  We use “callout” to refer to instances in which an instructor or peer communicates that a student is expected to participate, such as when an instructor directs a q uestion at a specific student, or when a classmate indicates that the student hasn’t fulfilled their role in a group project. Second, we observed that off-task interactions sometimes provide low-participating students access to the conve rsational floor. This is consistent with work in math education (e.g., Langer-Osuna, 2018; Langer-Osuna et al, 2018). Third, we found that when these students took on routine tasks, they often used th at participation as a stepping stone to more substantial contributions. Finally, we found that students’ spatial positioning relative to one another had a significant impact on participation. In this paper, we focus on th e final finding, which we feel might often be neglected and can have straightforward instructional implications.

Much past research on equity in small groups focuses on coding discourse to assess equity (e.g. Deitrick et al., 2016). Other research suggests the importance of students’ spatial orientation on their relative influence (e.g. Leander, 2002; Engle et al., 2014). In both groups, we noticed the influence of not only students’ orientation in space, but also their positioning relative to th e other group members.  We found it useful for making sense of our data to use the concept of a group’s “center of attentio n”, which we defined as the physical middle point between the two highest influence group members. This was typically the point where group members physically and visually oriented themselves during on-task discussion, as well as where materials or equipment being used were located. We found this concept  helped explain our sense of the impact of spatial positioning. We provide three examples to illustrate this.

Case 1: Varun’s repositioning across days An interesting example of the impact of spatial pos itioning came from a student named Varun (all names are pseudonyms). His group included  Herb (white, male), Jakob (white, male), and Brett (white, male). There is evidence in the data  the other three group members were friends outside the program and Varun (Asian, male) was not part of th at group. Perhaps for this reason, Varun rarely participated in the frequent off-task discourse. When the group was on-task, Jakob and Brett dominated, with Herb and Varun contributing infrequently. From our turns of talk analysis, we found Varun participated significantly more on day 9 than on days 4 and 5. This change coincided with a shift in Varun’s spatial position at the table. On days 4 and 5 Varun  sat at one edge of the table, with Jakob and Brett on the other half (see Figure 1). Varun’s difficulty negotiating access to the conversational space  was made worse by the fact that the group ha d to use a laptop as part of their work, which was not visible to all four group members at once. Typically, the laptop was located near the center of attention and oriented towards the other three group members. Varun either had to move or reorient the screen to see it. These spatial positioning factors appeared to constrain Varun’s participation on days 4 and 5. This changed near the end of day 5, when Varun move d his chair between Jakob and Brett to view the laptop screen. Unlike similar previous instances, he stay ed there for the rest of the period, a seating change that persist ed for the remainder of the program. Unfortunately, we don’t have video from this group’s table camera on days 6 and 8, but video from other cameras shows that the seating change persist ed during these days and suggests Varun’s participation increased on those days.

Day 9 is marked by a  sharp increase in Varun’s participation  compared to days 4 and 5  in both off-task and on-task discussion, which was likely supported by his change in position at the table. On day 9, the group’s center of attention was roughly at Varun’s location. Because of this, the l aptop was always pointed towards him, so both Jakob and Brett could see it. What’s more, Varun controlled the laptop for much of this day, which led to him bonding with Jakob, who in one instance joked that Varun was hacking the laptop. Additionally, there were multiple instances when group members asked Varun what was on the laptop screen, giving him a positive identity as a knower and doer of science.  It is clear that Varun’s spatial positioning on day  9 also granted him greater access to the conversational floor, giving him a more equitable opportunity to learn. Case 2: Varun’s Group’s Lab Work Another clear example of spatial positioning constraining participation occurred when Varun’s group worked in the lab on day 7. Jakob and Brett sat down directly in front of the lab equipment, leaving Varun at the edge of the table next to Brett, with Herb on the other side next to Jakob (see Figure 2). This spatial positioning affected Herb as well as Varun. Because the table was not rounded like the one in the inquiry classroom the class met  at on the other days, both Varun and Herb often move d behind Jakob and Brett to try and get a word in. Jakob and Brett contributed the ideas that were incorporated into their final experimental design, in addition to doing most of the experiment itself. Unlike days 4 and 5, Varun did actively attempt to participate for much of the day, which may be a continuation of his recent increase in particip ation on day 6.  However, he was often ignored by Jakob and Brett and was almost never given substantive responses.  In one instance, the group struggled to burn a wooden tongue depressor broken into pieces in order to mea sure the carbon dioxide released. Varun tried to suggest a better way to hold the piece, but after his idea was rejected by Jakob, Varun did not get the chance to try burning the wood himself. A few minutes later, Brett ma de the same suggestion and Jakob tried it. This instance further situated Jakob and Brett as knowers and doers of science and denied Varun access to these identities. Getting the chance to try burning the wood himself could have given Varun this access. Overall, day 7 is a story of Varun being denied access to the conversational floor due to spatial positioning. Case 3: Jessica’s Positioning Another student we analyzed, Jessica, was the lowest participating student in her group, whose other members were Pat (white, non-binary), Justin (white, male), and Brittany (biracial [Native American/Hispanic], female). Unlike Varun, Jessica (white, female) was friends with the other members  and often participate d in off-task interactions, but she rarely contributed to the group’s on-task work. Discussion was largely dominated by Justin and to a lesser extent Brittany. Pat contributed relatively infrequently but more often than Jessica. Unlike Varun, Jessica’s participation trajectory went from more active on days 4 and 5 to less active on day 9 (Hutchison, 2022). While other factors  likely contributed to this shift, the data suggest  spatial positioning play ed a role. Jessica’s group generally sat in the same spots throughout the program  (see Figure 3). In fact, Jessica benefited from her position between Justin and Brittany, the two highe st status group members. This afforded her participation in instances on days 4 and 5. For example, on day 5 the group was tasked with building a model of the atmosphere using a tank. When the model was being built, it was usually positioned between Justin and Brittany, within arm’s reach of Jessica. In addition, the materials used to build the model were often set down in front of Jessica, which led to her taking an active role in building the tank. Jessica  not only use d the materials, but she also contributed multiple ideas related to the tank itself. Jessica’s positioning granted her access to the building of the tank, which in turn allowed her to successfully contribute to its design. On day 9, the group ha d to create an equation to model the change in  the amount of carbon dioxide in the atmosphere over one year. For this activity, Justin took the lead as usual. He went back and forth with Pat about their ideas for the equation. This resulted in Pat being the second highest status in the group, a role usually occupied by Brittany. While that is interesting by itself, it  also impacted Jessica because  the group’s center of attention shifted from her location to between Pat and Justin. This shift added yet another barrier to participation for Jessica. It is worth noting that Brittany, bein g on the opposite side of the table, also struggle d to participate. While she did make active attempts to participate as usual, she o ften struggled to be heard and was frequently overtalked by Pat or Justin. When even a relatively  high-participating student like Brittany is constrained by her position, it is not surprising that Jessica suffered even more from the shift.

## Conclusion

We expect spatial positioning affords or constrains low-participating students more than high-participating ones. This suggests several instructional implications. For one, paying attention to where students are positioned can give a better understanding of dynamics within a small group. Instructors must know how groups function inequitably to attenuate it. In addition, instructors have some control over how students are positioned. We see this as an important topic for teacher professional development. Curriculum designers may also benefit from considering spatial positioning, such as in implementing intentional rotation of seats along with roles. We close by reminding the reader this is one of four features we identified that impact low-participating students. We see potential instructional implications in those as well. Broadly, we see this work as a potentially rich area of investigation in the effort to support more equitable small group interactions.
