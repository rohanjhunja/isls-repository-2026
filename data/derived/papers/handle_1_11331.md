# Do Teachers Protect Their Students’ Data? A Qualitative Interview Study Probing Teachers’ Knowledge of Data Security

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In our digital age, teachers’ responsibility for data protection and the handling of their students’ sensitive data is steadily increasing. However, neither professional development programs nor IT security trainings are specifically designed to educate teachers on IT security or take their existing knowledge into account to create targeted interventions. Therefore, the present study aims to investigate teachers’ knowledge of internet security and, in particular, how they protect their students’ data. A typological content analysis of interview data from 14 teachers revealed that the majority have inadequate knowledge about secure email communication and do not adequately protect their students’ data, highlighting the need for interventions that address these critical gaps in teachers’ knowledge and skills.

## Introduction

With the increasing digitization of schools and lea rning, teachers are faced with important privacy and internet security challenges. However, the ability of teachers to meet these challenges has been a marginal issue in learning science research on teacher professional developmen t. For example, when teachers' technological knowle dge, technological pedagogical knowledge, and technological pedagogical content knowledge are mea sured to draw conclusions for teacher education (see Guggemos & Seufert, 2021), their knowledge of IT security threats, secure email communication, and data protection plays litt le or no role, appearing in a single item related t o malware management. This suggests that IT security is not a  major focus of the skills being assessed.  Moreover, research suggests that the quality of teachers' professional training in cybersecurity is inadequate (Pham et al., 2019). However, teachers must teach students to navigate digital spaces safely and protect the sensitive data they store about their students, such as grades, home addresses, or personal information. Because schools are such valuable hubs of personal data, they are attractive targets for hackers (Morrow, 2020). While there are laws (e.g. GDPR, 2016) that prescribe how teachers must handle sensitive data, it is questionable whether teachers are even aware of these laws or able to comply with the digital data protection req uirements they impose. In this context, it seems worth investigating whether teachers are aware of their responsibility to protect students' sensitive data. To properly protect sensitive data, teachers need to understand certain aspects of IT security and be aware of measures to prevent cyberattacks. The knowledge users have about various IT security issues, such as malware management, is often referred to as a mental model.  Mental models are defined as a user-constructed representation of an object, idea, or process that a person has in their mind and that guides their interactions with the outside world. They are integrated representati ons of a situation in working memory in order to so lve tasks (Johnson-Laird, 1983).  To understand teachers' mental models of IT securit y issues, we distinguish between complete and incomplete mental models. These are characterized either by a full understanding of a concept (i.e., complete) or by prior knowledge that contains clear gaps (i.e., incomplete) (e.g., Abdi et al., 2 019; Schaewitz et al., 2021). Mental models can be further divided in to structural and functional models. Structural mental models summarize systematic know ledge, contain information about the structure of a  system, and are independent of specific tasks. In contrast, functional mental models  represent practical task-related knowledge and contain information about how individual components are use d to perform a given task. They emphasize what a system cando rather than its underlying structure (Alaqra et al., 2023). For users such as teachers, who primarily need knowledge to use IT products in their daily lives, the focus should be on acquiring functional ment al models (Alaqra et al., 2023).

One medium teachers frequently use in their daily work is email. Regarding email security, research has shown that incomplete or incorrect mental models of  email use are common. People often feel overconfident in identifying phishing emails (Wang et al., 2013, October; Hong et al., 2013), while at the same time struggling to recognize them  (Pattinson et al., 2012). While studies have shown that teachers use email extensively in their daily work (Chun et al., 2009), research has primarily focused on investigating the nature, extent, advantages, and disadvantages of email communication in teachers' daily work (see Chun et al., 2009; Heffernan & Selwyn, 2021; Ottenbreit-Leftwich et al., 2010). In the present study, we fo cus on investigating teachers' knowledge of secure email communication.

Many teachers use more than one email account, check their email up to five times a day, and even while on vacation (Chun et al., 2009). Studies have shown that teachers use email communication every day as a gateway to their colleagues and students for quick communic ation, as well as to promote information sharing and social interaction (Ottenbreit-Leftwich et al., 2010; Chun et al., 2009). In addition, email allows teachers to stay in touch with students' parents to reduce miscommunication and keep them informed (Ottenbreit-Leftwich et al., 2010). A dangerous form of email is spam, which can affect a ny user with a valid email address. One type of spa m is phishing emails  (Parsons et al., 2016), created by cybercriminals w ith the goal of capturing victims ’ personal information by using HTML formatting and links to fake website s. By entering personal credentials such as passwords and usernames, attackers gain access to sensitive information. Malware is another internet threat that can harm users via email. It is often delivered as an attachment and can lead to encryption of files (ransomware), leaving users without access to their data, often without realizing it (Brewer, 2016). Against this background, we formulate the following two research questions: Are teachers aware of their responsibility and the importance of protecting the ir students’ data? (RQ1) What do teachers know about secure email communication? (RQ2)

## Method

To investigate our research questions, we conducted semi-structured interviews with 14 teachers (nine cisgender men, five cisgender women), with an average age of M = 47.17 (SD = 11.72). The average duration of employment as a teacher was M = 5.36 years (SD = 1.65). The teachers worked at two grammar schools with identical technical infrastructure in a city in the Ruhr area, located in the federal state of North Rhine-Westphalia (NRW), and taught at a high-school level.

The semi-structured interviews followed a protocol containing questions about general knowledge of phishing, malware, cybersecurity, and email security. Additionally, we asked about how teachers use their school email address es in daily work, how they protect their email s from potential cyber threats, and the types of individuals they communicate with via email. To assess whether cybersecurity was covered during their university studies, we also asked several questions about their past experiences and desires for future professional training. To perform a type-forming qualitative content analysis according to Kuckartz (2018), we first developed a category system that included the dimensions we aimed to analyze – namely, awareness of data protection and knowledge of secure email communication. For this purpose, we coded the entire dataset (n = 14 interviews) and used MAXQDA 2022  (VERBI Software, 2021). We created a cod ebook both deductively  – based on prior research and assumptions about IT security and data protection i n schools – and inductively, based on the actual data after analyzing the first interviews.

After coding the first 20% of the material, two rat ers resolved ambiguities in the definition and application of codes and re-coded the interviews. Following Kuckartz's typification process, we filtered through the interviews to define key characteristics on which to base our typ es. This established the feature space for the typification process. We then reduced our codebook to the six categories shown in Table 1, which were also the most prevalent in the dataset. Based on these chara cteristics, we grouped individual cases into distinct types, assigning each teacher to one type. In this phase of the analysis, we differentiated teacher types along a spectrum for each of the six characteristics (e.g., mental models vary on the spectrum from non-existent to complete). ## Results

Through our typification process we identified three types of teachers. As illustrated in Figure 1, these types range on different expressions o f awareness for data protection (x-axis, RQ1) and knowledge about secure email communication (y-axis, RQ2).

## Research Question 1

Our first type of teachers, Type 1 teachers (n = 5), stand out due to their lack of awareness of data security, which can be summarized as “I prefer to leave it all to the professionals/experts”. They are further characterized by their irresponsible handling of sensitive data and their lack of knowledge about data protection. They trust  the companies that provide digital tools to follow privacy policies. It should be noted that most of the Type 1 teachers would also send sensitive data by email. Type 1 tea chers deny any responsibility for data protection a nd do not comply with data protection regulations.

For type 2 teachers (n = 6), which can be described as "school is not a target, no one is interested in us", the results show a diffuse pattern of data protection awareness. There is agreement that schools are not seen as the primary target of attacks, as hackers tend to focus on companies or clinics for blackmail. In this respect, they show similarities to Type 1 teachers in that they also reject responsibility for data protection. But unlike Type 1 teachers, Type 2 teachers create their own data security standards. They simply choose to practice digital privacy in its most basic form, namely analog. While they are aware of some irresponsible data protection practices, they trust that the programs used in the school are compliant, so they have no obligation to protect data. This can be seen in the context of the assumption that the content communicated by teachers is of no interest to attackers. A clear difference from the previous two types of teachers can be seen in the awareness of Type 3 teachers (n = 3) when handling sensitive data. These teachers show a high level of awareness of the importance of privacy and can be described as "better safe than sorry". T ype 3 teachers know that sensitive data can only be  sent by email with permission. They also delete data in a t imely manner and store it on external media, and ar e aware of data regulation protocols.

## Research Question 2

The three types of teachers also show clear differences in their mental models of secure email communication and their knowledge of how to deal with email threats. Type 1 teachers show large gaps in knowledge or no knowledge at all, are unable to name different email threats and do not know what they entail. They are not awar e of the dangers of email threats and have little knowledge of how to protect themselves from potential attacks. For Type 1 teachers, knowing and applying measures is synony mous with knowing and dealing with email threats. T heir lack of knowledge is also reflected in their mental models, especially in their structural mental models about email threats such as phishing and malware, which are terms that they cannot even define. Type 2 teachers, on the other hand, seem to have a basic understanding of potential email threats and how to take action. However, teachers are usually u naware that these actions are protective measures a gainst an attack. The fact that there are clear gaps  in their knowledge is particularly evident in their  incomplete structural and functional mental models of phishing and malwar e, which they mostly derive from experience. Type 2 teachers struggle to find personal relevance for da ta security and make no eff ort to expand their mental models because they do not expect it to be necessary.

The most educated teachers are in Type 3. In accord ance with their expert handling of sensitive data, they are able to demonstrate secure behavior in dai ly practice because they know and actively use a wi de range of measures and strategies to protect themselves against an attack. Type 3 teachers therefore have well-developed mental models and are able to create synergies. They have almost complete functional mental models of phishing and structural mental models of malware.

## Discussion and conclusion

As schools are a valuable hub for sensitive data, teachers’ awareness of school data protection and their knowledge about secure email communication is of high importance. Given that learning in schools is becoming increasingly digital, learning science research should  begin to identify and investigate these necessary teacher co mpetencies, as well as develop techniques to increase teachers'  awareness and knowledge of IT security and cyberse curity threats, which has so far been the focus of IT security research rather than of the learning sciences community. This exploratory interview study reveals that teachers vary widely in their awarenes s and knowledge of data protection and secure email communication. The se differences highlight the need for targeted inte rventions that consider teachers’ existing experiences and understanding. Legislative support, such as mandatory training sessions led by IT exper ts, could play a key role in making secure email communication a regular part of  teachers' routines. Sasse et al. (2023) propose a model in which developing IT security skills requires a series of progressive steps. Following this "security learning curve", individuals must first be made aware of cyber threa ts, then informed about their nature, equipped with  practical knowledge, and ultimately empowered to apply secure behaviors consistently in their daily work. In light of the study's findings, tailored interven tions should aim to raise basic awareness and provi de foundational knowledge for Type 1 teachers. For Type 2 teachers, the focus should be on integrating secure email practices into their daily routines. T ype 3 teachers, who are already proficient in this area, can serve as valuable resources within their schools. By acting as role m odels and peer educators, they can support colleagu es further down the learning curve. These strategies can help establish lasting behavioral change. In the long term, teachers will not only be better equipped to recognize digital threats and understand their significance but will also be able to respond to them independently and with confidence.

Before developing and testing such targeted interventions,  further research is needed to replicate our findings with larger and more diverse samples. The present study’s sample consisted of 14 high school teachers from a German city in the Ruhr area, located in the federal state of NRW. In these sch ools, the same tools were used for digital communication. Although it was possible to identify different teacher types even within two highly comparable schools, further research is required to determine whether additional types exist in other schools or countries, and thus, whether our findings can be generalized.
