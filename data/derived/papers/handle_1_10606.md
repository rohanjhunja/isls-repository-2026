# Exploring Competency-Based Education Experiences - 
A Design-Based Mixed-Methods Study

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This research explores Competency-Based Education (CBE) grading to address shortcomings in traditional grading systems. CBE emphasizes comprehensive skill mastery and offers a student-centered approach. The study implemented a CBE grading schema in a computer science course which allowed students to choose assignments aligned with their preferences and track their competence through feedback. Quantitative analysis revealed significant differences in student perceptions of interest, competence, and effort, with notable effect sizes. Qualitative data identified key challenges and successful strategies that led to design moves for improvement. Network analysis visualized correlations between student struggles, successful approaches, goal achievement, and Self-Determination Theory (SDT) principles. The study demonstrates the potential of CBE grading to enhance student autonomy and motivation while addressing traditional grading limitations.

## Introduction

Traditional grading often allows students to earn a n "A" without mastering essential course skills, cr eates an incomplete assessment of their abilities, and poten tially encourages surface-level learning. Competency-Based Education (CBE) offers a solution by emphasizing co mprehensive mastery of crucial skills and knowledge  with point accumulation. Developed by learning scientist s, CBE evaluates students' proficiency in knowledge, attitudes, values, skills, and behaviors essential for their degree. It transforms how students engage  with knowledge and fosters responsibility for their learning. Instructors guide rather thandictate knowledge. Despite its potential, universities have been slow to adopt CBE grading schemas due to challenges in adapting tonew knowledge development and evaluation practices. Digital Learning Management Systems (LMS) can streamline CBE grading through automated assess ment, instant feedback, and organized course struct ures. The instructor implemented a CBE grading schema to prioritize student competence by leveraging the advantages of a digital LMS.

CBE grading in a computer science course In the past, this computer science course used a tr aditional grading system, considering attendance, o nline discussions, quizzes, assignments, projects, and exams for determining student grades. To improve the assessment of student knowledge, the course transitioned to a CBE grading schema. The instructor redefined the gr ading criteria with specific goals: offering assignment c hoices to empower students, increasing engagement, reducing academic dishonesty, and elevating course rigor by ensuring competency in essential areas. Insights from the initial two study iterations info rmed subsequent iterations. Challenges included students struggling with the array of assignment ch oices and some feeling that significant assessments  led to abrupt grade reductions. We used these insights to develop data-driven recommendations, which were integrated into the course's design for subsequent iterations.  This current study represents the third iteration of a comprehensive design-based research (DBR) approach implementing a CBE gr ading schema in an introductory computer science course. DBR is a methodological ap proach involving iterative design cycles to enhance educational methods. Using DBR, we aim to identify course-level and universal design improvements to enhance learning by using the CBE grading schema.

## Literature review

According to Holubnycha et al. (2022), CBE is one o f the central approaches in modern educational syst ems. Several articles provide insights into the challenges and considerations related to assuring that student knowledge is accurately represented by their grades in college courses. Traditionally, assessments have been pivotal in CBE as they gauge a student's mastery of specific outco mes (Albanese et al., 2008). Caniëls (2005) highlig hted that CBE can seamlessly merge practical tasks with evalu ations, leveraging online resources to minimize ass essment overhead. In their study, both educators and studen ts reported high satisfaction with this digital app roach. Even more so, the capacity to perform a job independentl y with relative ease and little support is a  key indicator in a CBE system (Kumar & Aithal, 2019). But real-world application of CBE needs more rigorous resear ch (Pellegrino, 2014). Overall, there exists a need fo r research on grading practices that prioritize acc urate representation of student knowledge and address the limitations and biases inherent in traditional grading schemas.

To address this shortfall of research, the current study evaluated CBE within a computer science cours e taught by a co-investigator. Analyzing this course with a DBR meth odology allowed the instructor to identify real-world design moves and provide meaningful empirical feedback regarding CBE.

## Theoretical framework

This research is grounded in constructivism and self-determination theory (SDT). According to Piaget (1952) and Vygotsky (1978), constructivism theory suggests tha t learners actively build knowledge through experie nce and reflection. It emphasizes hands-on, student-centered activities and the context of learning. SD T is a foundational framework for understanding human motivation and be havior across various domains. Developed by Deci and Ryan (1985), this theory posits that individuals ha ve innate psychological needs for autonomy, compete nce, and relatedness. When these needs are satisfied, they exhibit enhanced motivation and well-being. In our study, these theories play pivotal roles. Th e CBE grading schema reflects the principles of constructivism by allowing students to actively engage with course material and construct knowledge. At the same time, the choice and autonomy embedded in CBE align with SDT because students exercise their agency and thus fulfill their intrinsic needs for autonomy and comp etence. By leveraging these intertwined theories, t he study seeks to design an educational environment where students not only actively build their knowledge but are also deeply motivated to engage and succeed.

To address students receiving grades that do not re flect their knowledge, the instructor implemented a CBE grading schema. Our overarching research questi ons were: How did students experience learning in a CBE grading context, and how can these experiences inform future changes to improve the learning experiences?

## Method

This study employed an explanatory mixed methods design that combines qualitative and quantitative data to gain a more comprehensive understanding of a research to pic (Plano Clark & Ivankova, 2016). Th is design uses the outcomes of the qualitative strand to further explain the results of the quantitative strand. At the end of the course, all enrolled students (n=46) reflected on four open-ended questions and Likerttype questions. Demographics of the university were 22% students of color, 21% first generation, 21% Pell Grant eligible, and 52% women. The Intrinsic Motivation I nventory (IMI; 2022) was used to measure students' statebased motivation during the course. The IMI consist s of six subscales: interest/enjoyment, choice, pressure/tension, value/usefulness, perceived competence, and effort. Responses are given using a 5-point Likert scale. It is believed that the interest/enjoyment s ubscale is the self-report indicator of intrinsic motivation. Both self-report and behavioral measures of intrinsic motivat ion can be positively predicted by perceived choice  and perceived competence. An additional component that matters for some motivation questions is effort. The premise behind the value/usefulness subscale in internaliza tion studies is that people internalize and develop  self-regulation for activities they perceive as meaningful or beneficial for themselves. Pressure/tension is an opposite predictor of intrinsic motivation. We extracted anonymized data for the consenting students. We analyzed the data using descriptive and inferent ial statistics. In this iteration of the study, we integrated learning theory within the student reflections in order to better ground our recommendations. First, the quantitative data were grouped by subscale. Interva l data were then analyzed by calculating an indepen dentsample student’s t-test and Cohen’s d using SPSS Statistical Software. The results and ef fect sizes of the t-tests were used to represent differences in each IMI subsale between CBG and traditional teaching methods. The t-test compared the resulting subscales to the students (n =104) in a control course. Next, we coded the qualitative responses to the open-ended questions at the phrase and word level in the  MAXQDA Analytics Pro qualitative data analysis software. Semantic network analysis w as used to examine and visualize the relationships and patterns between struggles (STRUGGLE), what worked (WORKED), pe rceived goal achievement (ACHIEVE), and SDT principles (THRYSDT) in student reflections. We used inductive coding techniques within predetermined categories so that codes could emerge  from the data. In the software, correlational anal ysis was then used to produce correlation matrices at the p<0.05, p<0.01, and p<0.001 levels. The matrices were imported into the UCINet social network analysis software (B orgatti, Everett, & Freeman, 2002) and then NetDraw  social network visualization software (Borgatti, 2002) for semantic network analysis.

## Results

The inferential statistical analysis revealed signi ficant findings across several survey categories. N otably, in Q1 (Interest/enjoyment), Q2 (Perceived competence), and Q3 (Effort/importance), there were significant differences (p<0.001) with large effect sizes d=-1.52, -1.20, and-2.45, respectively. However, in Q4, (Pressure/tensi on) and Q5 (Choice) differences were not statistically significant. In contrast, Q6 (Value/usefulness) showed a statistically significant difference of p<0.01 and a moderate effect size of d=0.76. The qualitative data matrices were then used to ide ntify design moves to improve the CBE grading schema. Figure 1 is a network map of correlations at the p<0.001 confidence level showing positive, significa nt correlations between student struggles and what worked for them. Also included are nodes indicating that students felt the schema assisted them in achieving their goals and related to SDT. Girvan-Newman cluster analysis found seven clusters at Q=0.724 where cluster sizes repre sent betweenness. For exam ple, students in the red cluster struggled with their grades being easily demoted, the desire for multiple attempts on core assignments, and a need for more practice. What worked well for these stude nts was feedback about their learning, the satisfactory/unsatisfactory grading, and graded attendance. These stude nts’ experiences aligned with the SDT principle of intrinsic motivation development throu gh increased self-perception of competence. As one student stated, “The grading model allowed me to focus on learning the things I thought I would use in the future and step back on things that I thought weren’t as helpful.” The design moves that addressed these students’ struggles were to improve the structure of how to achieve the goal s of each assignment and po tentially incorporate practice assignments or example assignments before the graded assignment. What worked well for these students was the amount  of practice and preparation, rubrics, and general skil l development. The design move to address these stu dents’ struggles was to have the students brainstorm more examples of potential assignments with clear grade expectations. Students in the gray cluster struggle d with the need for more extra credit opportunities, the limited use of tokens, and the requirements of attendance a nd discussion posts in the LMS. The knowledge that the assignments reflected authentic activities worked w ell for these students. The design move to address these students’ struggles was to let the students use the tokens to design creative extra credit assignments. Students in the blue cluster struggled with the sat isfactory threshold on core assignments. The existi ng remediation is to complete a different assignment s uccessfully; however, students felt there was insuf ficient remediation for major assignments. The extensive choice of assignments worked well for these students, and their experiences aligned with the SDT principle of intri nsic motivation development through autonomy. As on e student noted, “It allowed me to truly understand t he content presented.” The design moves to address these students’ struggles was to emphasize how students can reach the satisfactory threshold by taking the initiative and re-doing unsatisfactory assignments. We worked on desi gn moves not reported here for the last three clust ers. Principles from theory did not show prominently on the map, leading us to believe we need to collect richer data. In the next iteration of this study, the analysis w ill be completed again to identify further improvem ents in implementing the CBE grading schema.

## Discussion

The evolution of educational methodologies has grap pled with balancing learner agency and structured l earning outcomes. CBE offers promise in bridging this gap. Our research underscores the potential of a CBE gra ding schema in enhancing student autonomy and motivation.

Traditional grading systems often encourage student s to chase grades rather than comprehend content, fostering superficial learning. In contrast, CBE gr ading schemas empower students to determine their l earning trajectories. The instructor also fostered a deeper engagement with the course material by allow ing students to choose assignments that align with their learning preferences. Additionally, our study emphasizes the critical role of feedback in CBE. It serves not only as a correct ive tool but also as a  means for students to assess their competence, aligning with SDT, which highlights intrinsic motivation through perceived competence. While CBE maintains rigorous standards, it ensures students master essential competencies. Beyond our specific findings, this research holds b roader implications across disciplines. It reinforc es the educational principle of student-centered, adaptive learning environments. As educat ors explore innovative pedagogies, our goal remains constant: to cultivate motivated, engaged, and empowered learners.

## Conclusion

Significant insights were gleaned regarding the effe ctiveness and potential areas of improvement for th is approach. The DBR methodology, spanning three itera tions, provided a comprehensive understanding of ho w CBE grading can be tailored to optimize student  learning experiences. These findings align with Can iëls (2005) who also found that CBE can effortlessly merge prac tical tasks with evaluations by leveraging online r esources. While CBE grading showcases promise in promoting student autonomy, deeper engagement, and mastery of core competencies, it also presents challenges in balanc ing flexibility with rigor. This finding aligns wit h several studies that focused on the challenges with grades accurately representing student knowledge. Though offering valuable insights into a CBE gradin g schema in a computer science context, this study has several limitations. The research's scope was r estricted to one course which may limit the general izability of our findings. Relying on student reflection introdu ces potential subjectivity and bias. Furthermore, b y focusing on design moves across three iterations, we may not yet fully grasp the long-term implications. Future research on CBE grading schemas should delve deeper into understanding its implications across diverse educational settings and demographics. Furt her iterations of this study are planned. Given the  initial findings in a computer science context, it would be  enlightening to investigate how CBE grading functi ons in other subjects. Additionally, the role of technology in facilitating CBE grading, especially in large classes, remains largely unstudied. Finally, longitudinal studies th at track students' l ong-term retention and application of competencies acquired through CBE grading would provide a comprehensive picture of its real-world impact.
