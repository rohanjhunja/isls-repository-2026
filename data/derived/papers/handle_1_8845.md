# Queer Orientations as Resources for Mathematics Learning

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper investigates how queer orientations shaped mathematical meaningmaking in an afterschool documentary filmmaking program. We focus on queerness as both a lived/experienced phenomenon and a lens for thinking about learning, following scholars who have examined mathematics learning from racialized and gendered lenses (E.g., Gholson & Martin, 2019; Rubel, 2016). To do so, this paper explores how the performance of and negotiations with queer identities are co-constructed and emerge with meaning making around mathematical concepts. Findings call for centering these stories as productive, critical aspects of learning such that youths’ queer positionalities and identities in and across contexts are seen as an essential aspect to advancing equity in mathematics teaching and learning.

Purpose It is late afternoon when four members of an after-school documentary filmmaking program we call Digital Studio (DS) – Zeena, Xane, Lucas, and Cai-huddle around a computer in a school’s library. They are working on compiling research for their documentary on “The LGBTQ (1) Youth Experience in Schools in 2017.” The Youth Producers (YPs) are creating a voiceover script for  the documentary they will write, film, edit, and s howcase within the course of one school semester. In small groups, they have spent the last few days looking for statistical information that describes the LGBTQ community. Thi s afternoon they record informational statistics fo und online about the relative sizes of LGBTQ populations in different U.S. cities. As they gather the information for the voiceover, their discussions weave in and out of mathematics, such as notation, mathematical operations, and proportional relationships, and affective responses  to the numbers. Amongst the note taking and calcul ations, ponderings emerge; how a statistic prompted one youth to reconsider a familial relationship, and another to hope for a desired future. A traditional lens on learnin g might isolate the explicitly mathematical parts o f the conversation, finding a largely uncontested discussion of concepts and notation. Such an approach might further relegate the personal to an uninterrogated backgrou nd, dismissing with it entire histories, identities, and relationships through which collective mathematical meaning emerges. In the analysis presented in this paper, we adopt a queer perspective and turn intentionally to  this forgotten background to reclaim the more rela tional and subjective dimension of learning. Turning in this way allows us to bring into sharp relief how participant stories are implicated in the construction of a statistic for their documentary film. In the past few decades, out of school settings ha ve occupied a place of some prominence in mathematics education research (Carraher, et al., 1985; Ma, 201 6; Nasir, 2002; Saxe, 1991). Collectively, these st udies have examined the social and cultural practice of mathematics in everyday, non-school life. Yet, only a small subset of this literature has decentered school mathematics i n informal settings, interrogating how mathematics learning and identity development are mutually constitutive of each other (e.g., Hull & Greeno, 2006; Nasir & C ooks, 2009; Radke, 2021). Such an approach has made it possible to understand how mathematics, as a practice, is the effect of interplay between subjects, artifact, and the environment. In particular, studies like Ma’s (2017) of wholebody mathematics, Nasir’s (2002) into domino and ba sketball activity, and Saxe’s (1991) of gameplay ha ve offered perspectives on how mathematics and mathema tical activity are produced in interaction and with in particular settings. We draw on this latter scholarship and extend its ideas by considering affect as another element of meaning-making in mathematics (Leander & Ehret, 2019). This paper investigates how participants’ queer ori entations, discerned through a queer analytic lens,  shaped mathematical meaning-making in an afterschool docum entary filmmaking program. We begin by positioning ourselves as researchers from a post-colonial feminist perspective with an interest in surfacing erase d stories of learning and practice in mathematics education. The  Black/feminist scholar Kristie Dotson (2014) argue s that stories often go untold due to our “inclination to understand oppression as we experience it and to ex tend our analysis of it beyond what we ourselves can see fro m our particular vantage point” (p. 57). She urges us to interrogate the places from which we see and conduc t research to unpack the stories that might be obsc ured by our particular “socioepistemic orientations” (p. 45). Ahmed (2006) writes similarly about orientations: ICLS2022 Proceedings 591 © ISLS Some things are relegated to the background in orde r to sustain a certain direction; in other words, in order to keep attention on what is faced.  Perception involves such acts of relegation that are forgotten in the very preoccupation with what it is that is faced. (p. 31) In other words, our orientation to a scene relegate s some aspects of it to the background while foregrounding others. The orientation of our gaze, therefore, affects what we see and the stories we t ell; the background, forgotten, holds some of the stories we may have lost along the way. The purpose of this paper is to search for these lost stories by critically attending to the forgotten background. We contend that by telling these lost stories, we are positioned to better understand learning as it takes place in the margins An emerging field within educational and learning sciences literature engages marginalization as an intersectional phenomenon, and examines the ways in which race, cl ass, gender, and other social subjectivities interw eave to mediate experiences of learning. Gholson and Martin  (2014; 2019) focus on Black girlhood as a unique s ocial location from which Black girls experience the mathematics classroom. They point out that the social n etworks of Black girls at school form at the intersection of their Blackness and their girlness, so that any examination of their experiences of learning must necessarily reco gnize both race and gender. Similarly, by simultane ously interrogating institutions (such as schooling and STEM education) and structures (such as race, U.S. imperialism, and neoliberalism) across time and space, Sengupta-Irving (2021) and Sengupta-Irving & Vossoughi (2019) sought to give voice to the subjective experiences of Brown girls in contemporary western education. T aking a similar approach to studying embodied mathematical learning, Takeuchi & Ishihara (2021) followed the mathematical sensemaking of a collective of migrant  women activists by attending to their social and p olitical histories of migration and labor, as well as the violence their bodies had been subjected to. Taken together, these studies share a commitment to seeing social processes—and therefore learning—as at once emplaced, embodied, racialized, gendered, and classed. We share this co mmitment and offer (queer) orientations to surface socially, geographically, and politically entangled processes of learning. While racialized (McGee, 2015; Leyva, 2016; Martin,  2012) and gendered (Ceci & Williams, 2011; Legewie & DiPrete, 2014) identities have been examined in mathematics education research, few scholars have centered the mathematical experiences of students w ith marginalized sexual identities with notable exc eptions (e.g., Esmonde, 2011; Rubel, 2016). Thus, we focus on queerness as both a lens for thinking about learning and lived/experienced phenomenon. This paper is guided by the following questions: What orientations are brought to the fore when interactions are viewed through a queer lens? And how did these orientations shape mathematical participation? Analysis revealed that youth’s reasoning followed personal trajectories into the past and future as part of their reasoning practices and that queernes s was entangled (Philip & Sengupta, 2020) with thei r mathematical reasoning. Findings call for centering  these trajectories as productive, critical aspects  of learning such that youths’ queer orientations in and across contexts are an essential aspect to advancing equit y in mathematics teaching and learning.

## Theoretical framework

This paper is centrally interested in meaning-makin g—and therefore learning—as culturally, spatially, and temporally mediated. We explore this interest by br inging together sociocultural and queer theories to  episodes under analysis. Guided by sociocultural theory, we take up the conceptualization of learning as movement (Gutiérrez, 2008), developed across settings (Jackson, 2011) and through shifts in participation in activities (Lave & Wenger, 1991). We look to queer perspectives (Ahm ed, 2006; Butler, 1990) to highlight the power rela tions that shape identity performances and learning. Butler reminds us that all identities are composed of self-making acts, composed through “corporeal signs and other discursive means,” (Butler, 1990, p.333), through which people become socially intelligible. From this perspective, identity is not an expression of what one is, rat her it is something that one does. Critical to Butler’s theor y of identity as performative is the understanding of performativity as contextual and structured by powe red relations. Thus, identities are not only dynami c, but contextually situated, non-neutral social construct ions, and understanding positioning thus requires a nalytic attention to both the in-the-moment efforts to perform and to the context in which these performances occur. A queer phenomenology involves facing the back (p. 29), allowing the researcher to make sense of a scene by deliberately attending to the background, or, in other words, the systems and labor that sust ain what comes to be foregrounded. Calling for a re-orientation to the back or background necessitates that something or things occupy the fore from which we deliberately t urn. Ahmed specifically calls for this move as an e xplicit resistance of white, cis-, and heteronormative narratives. Like Butler, Ahmed (2006) conceives of gender and race as “effects of the kinds of work that bodies do” (p. 60). In turn, the raced and gendered labor of bodies frames the kind of work it becomes possible for those bodies t o engage in. Historically othered bodies, having ar rived in places that refuse them, regularly resist dominant imperatives by occupying space (literally and figur atively) in ICLS2022 Proceedings 592 © ISLS new and unprecedented ways. Such resistances are a result of “different orientations, different ways of directing one’s desires” (p. 68). Occupying a space with new desires has the potential to extend it in new ways,  making possible new ways of doing, being, and thinking. We view the resistance of the othered body as a performance of queerness and seek to understand how queer orientat ions shape collective and individual sense-making.  By conceptualizing meaning-making as a process that ex tends across space-times (Nespor, 2004) the analysi s examined queer orientations as part of mathematical  and interpersonal sense-making by attending to the  social practices of both the collection and analysis of da ta to meet activity-related goals, the phenomena de scribed by the statistic itself, and the sociocultural and/or political concerns made relevant to sense-making by  the participants themselves. In the empirical examples that follow, these theories supported understanding the ways interactants’ subjective experiences of queerness were part of whole-group statistical meaning-making.

## Data and methods

Data comes from a larger study of mathematical learning pathways (Ma & Kelton, 2017) at Digital Studio (DS), an afterschool documentary filmmaking program that engaged underserved youth in documentary film production and civic engagement. In one school semester, Youth Producers (YPs) and an adult facilitator worked together to produce a documentary that addressed a student-selected social issue. Data included field notes, audio and video recordings, interviews, and material artifacts. Ana lysis occurred iteratively at two interrelated leve ls, the ethnographic (Spradley, 1980) and microethnographic (Streeck & Mehus, 2005). Analyses of episodes identified as “hot spots” (Jordan & Henderson, 1995) iterated between microanalytic methods of interaction analysis (Jordan & Henderson, 1995), and methods borrowed from the c onstruction of grounded theory (Charmaz, 2006). Ethnographic analysis of the full data corpus throu gh thematic memo writing (Schatzman & Strauss, 1973) supported microanalysis of episodes, focusing on how the desires of different bodies occupied with a shared task collaboratively shaped reasoning practices. Finally, analysis traced chronotopic laminations (Bhaktin,  1981) to attend to participants' social relations and histories of arrival (Ahmed, 2006) that were made relevant in interaction. Attending to how spacetimes were brought together over the course of talk and interaction (e.g., Leander, 2002) illuminated how participants continuously co-constr ucted stories about themselves and others across ti me and space, influencing their social positioning. Analysis traced how queer orientations, as one such cross-spacetime resource, emerged as part of ongoing individual and collective sense-making. ## Discussion

In the analysis of these episodes, we have detailed  processes of learning, illuminating how youth brin g their experiences and positionalities as resources for ma thematical reasoning, and how their knowledge of mathematical concepts interplay with their reflections on past experiences and future possibilities. By combining queer phenomenology with microanalytic methods of i nteraction analysis we have shown that students’ orientations to, of, and with queerness were woven into their math reasoning, as they continually worked to draw connections across their lifeworlds to “make sense of one’s own identity in the broad context of time,” (Gutiérrez & Jurow, 2016, p.567). These efforts shaped individual and collective learning, and the practice of statistics in a ICLS2022 Proceedings 595 © ISLS way that was relevant to the filmmaking task before them. Additionally, mathematical understandings youth cooperatively developed were used as tools for finding and interpreting statistics for their film and as part of traveling on multi-sited, person-centered trajectories into the past and future in relation to queerness. The pa st and future spacetimes Xane and Cai laminated were legitimate p arts of the group’s mathematical reasoning and yout h’s individual reflections about their queer positionalities and/or identities. Xane’s sense-making about the statistic, and Lucas’s estimation that followed, was entangled  with a past spacetime in which he had engaged in homophobic actions. Cai’s sense-making about the se cond statistic and ratio relationship was embedded in his future imagination of finding a partner. Additional ly, the personal relevance of the statistics to Cai ’s imagined future became a resource for extending the whole-gr oup mathematical investigation. In these ways the y outh queered (Mendick, 2006) their mathematical reasoning through their knowledge and use of mathematical concepts and their past and future experiences.

These findings extend empirical and theoretical understandings of the interrelationships between identity and mathematics learning beyond practice-related conceptions by suggesting that mathematics content itself can be a context for identity work (Radke, 2021); that is, embedded in relationships, memories, and desires rather than abstract skill that is enacted identically across d ifferent settings. What made these findings possibl e was our deliberate attention to the non-mathematical aspects of participant conversation, such as Xane’s comments about “feeling bad” and Cai’s stray remarks about finding  a partner. This intentional turn toward what is of ten categorized as background information, if it is att ended to at all, was critical to our emerging under standing of mathematical meaning-making as situated, historical, relational, and affective. We accomplished this turn through a queer phenomenology, by facing the back to examine how it sustains learning (Ahmed, 2006). To make visible the intimate nature of all learning—here mathematic al learning—we sought to queer our own orientation as researchers. A queer turn in research thus has the potential to paint more nuanced pictures of learning as intricately tangled up with memory, history, desire, and hope.

## Significance

Equity-focused research has long argued for supporting students in recognizing mathematics as valuable to their own lives (Kilpatrick, et al., 2001), seeing themse lves in the mathematics (Gutstein, 2007), and havin g their family/home knowledges valued in schooling spaces (González, et al. 2005). The findings of this paper move beyond explaining marginalized youths’ struggles by  illuminating how students' construction and use of mathematical knowledge was shaped by their social, personal, and political stories. Recognizing the pe rsonally relevant ways youth took up and reasoned about content as part of learning illuminated one way to move towards epistemological shifts in our understandings of mat h meaning-making, driven by change-making desires (Takeuchi, et al., 2020). Implications call for res earch and pedagogical efforts to center these past and future stories as productive, critical aspects of learning  so that youths’ queer orientations are seen as ess ential to advancing equity in mathematics teaching and learning. We must prioritize how youth make math content relevant to their lives in the moment and follow youth as they recruit experiences and knowledge across spacetimes to ask new questions, reflect on their pasts, and set future goals. The various approaches that fall under queer theory are committed in their refusal of fixed identities and are invested in apprehending and disrupting the binaries that emerge in discourse (Mendick, 2014). This refusal feels especially relevant to the task of telling humanizing stories of mathematics education, where mathematical knowledge continues to be conceived of as objective, neutral, and fixed, although these ideas have seen a challenge in recent years (Gutiérrez, 2013; Martin, Gholson, & Leonard, 2010). In the episodes analyzed above, a  queer research orientation surfaced the ways in which the  same statistic drew different affective interpreta tions from participants, thereby disturbing the very premise of a singular mathematics. A statistic is never objectively large or small, it depends (for instance) on how it is recruited into personal relationships, memories, and hopes. A queer lens on research into mathematical learning emphasi zes a relational view of the discipline so that wha t mathematics is becomes inseparable from who reached for it, how they reached for it, and with whom (Ah med, 2006). Implicit in this view is a conception of mat hematics as many things, as plural and not fixed (G utiérrez, 2013). It positions us to retrieve the stories of l earning we may have lost to the dangerous myth of a n apolitical mathematics (Martin, Gholson, & Leonard, 2010).
