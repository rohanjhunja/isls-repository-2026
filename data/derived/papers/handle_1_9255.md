# Supporting Engineering Education Through Collaborative Design

**Conference:** ISLS 2023

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Preparing future engineers for global work requires helping them learn to engage in complex collaborations across multiple boundaries. This paper examines how boundaries that students encountered in their global teams helped them to navigate conflict and make progress in their global engineering design projects. Following a thematic analysis of students’ data, four themes emerged around their interdisciplinary and intercultural boundary-crossing experiences namely unfamiliarity with 1) terminologies, concepts, and procedures, 2) conducting design in global settings, 3) the global procurement landscape, and 4) global community customs.

Engineering for the world Educators recognize that tackling the world’s complex problems would require engineering graduates to learn to work together across boundaries, which could be functional/disciplinary, organizational, cultural, or geographical (International Engineering Alliance, 2021). While boundary-crossing situations can lea d to a discontinuity, disruption, or dysfunction of activity, it can also result in productive conflicts that lead to innovative practices and transformational learning (Akkerman and Bakker, 2011). Jehn’s (1997) study of  group conflict highlight three types of conflict namely process conflicts, which focused on how tasks would be accomplished, task or cognitive conflict that relates to content, and relational conflict which is focused on inte rpersonal relationships. This study asks: how can the boundaries that students encounter in their global engineering design project teams help them to navigate conflict and make progress in problem-solving? This research is s ituated within the International Virtual Engineering Student Teams (InVEST) initiative at the University of  Toronto that creates virtual international research projects that enabled multi-institutional student teams to work together on real-world globally situated engineering projects while gaining insights into the diversity and complexity of human experience and perspectives that stem from cross-cultural work.

## Theoretical perspectives

This study is informed by the theoretical perspectives of 1) boundary crossing (Akkerman and Bakker, 2011)  that allowed for the examination of students’ interactions and learning experiences across multiple boundaries; and 2) the social constructivist perspective (Vygotsky, 1978) that guided students’ learning and development within socially co-constructed interactions.

## Method and analytic approach

Three InVEST project teams were selected for the study based on their multidisciplinary composition. The student teams consisted of nine chemical engineering students from UofT as well a s six civil and two mechanical engineering students from a Nigerian university. The global projects included ca ssava waste utilization, palm waste utilization, and cassava biogas anaerobic digester projects. Data we re collected from recorded team meetings, individual interviews, team management artifacts, and design artefac ts. Using thematic analysis, the collected data were inductively coded to capture boundary objects, disciplinary boundarie s, and emerging conflicts in the teams. Next, the coded data were systematically examined to  determine associated conflicts and emerging themes around the students’ productive exchanges and learning across th e boundaries that they encountered within their global project teams.

## Findings and Discussion

Based on the coding of students’ data, our preliminary findings revealed four themes that emerged around their interdisciplinary and intercultural boundary-crossing experiences as outlined in table1 below. Crossing interdisciplinary boundaries Students encountered and explicated unfamiliarity around disciplinary terminologies, concepts, and procedures which led to cognitive conflict. This allowed for knowledge acquisition and the combination of viable CSCL 2023 Proceedings  © ISLS 409 disciplinary solution options for optimizing energy generation from palm empty fruit bunch. In addition, students were unfamiliar with design conditions in local community settings that were different from their worldviews and prior engineering training. This knowledge gap led to cognitive conflicts during the design process that were tackled with divergent exploration of alternative solution options. Crossing intercultural boundaries Students’ unfamiliarity with the material procurement landscape in a country different from theirs resulted in process and relational conflicts such as delays in securing equipment, unavaila bility of materials, and  account management challenges, which required intercultural sensitivities for resol ution. Furthermore, students ’ recognition of a knowledge gap due to their unfamiliarity with the needs and customs of the community  led them to engage the community members to gain more knowledge and determine their  level of interest in the project. This led to process conflicts during the design process as the survey results yielded mixed results regarding the community’s use of gas for cooking over firewood. To tackle this, the students conducted an awareness campaign over a couple of visits to educate members on usage and address any safety concerns raised. ## Conclusion

This study highlights the disciplinary and cultural boundaries that engineering teams  encountered in their global design projects. It also indicates the associated conflicts and approaches ut ilized to navigate them namely: collaborative problem-solving, divergent thinking, and experiential investigation. Although the stu dy is limited by the sample size, it is significant as it demonstrates approaches that engi neering students can utilize to engage in productive exchanges within global learning settings. This research contribute s to both theory and practice of learning across boundaries in the specific setting of global engineering education and the larger body of empirical research on interdisciplinary collaboration and collaborative learning.
