# Designing Curricula for an Uncertain World: A Critical Action Learning Approach

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In this paper, we focus on engaging student educational designers in critical action learning and design. While ongoing efforts have investigated how to build capacity amongst schoolteachers, we argue for building capacity amongst student educational designers who will be engaged in designing resources for schoolteachers and students. This research builds on theories of critical pedagogy and critical action learning. We follow a design-based research approach to iteratively design a critical action learning toolbox that can be used to foster a generation of designers capable of producing educational technologies that prioritize equity and social responsibility. Findings suggest that adopting a layered approach – CALE researcher/educator (Layer 0), student educational designer (Layer 1), and schoolteacher (Layer 2) – provide the required reflective space to design curricula using critical action learning approach. Scaffolds such as designing a collective problem case and emergent mirrors are important constituents in this reflective space.

## Introduction

This study focuses on engaging student educational designers in critical action learning and design, responding to the current global context of rapid changes to the socio-political landscape. This highly volatile and uncer tain context suggests the need for a “new social contrac t for education to repair injustices while transfor ming the future” (UNESCO, 2021, p. 3). Existing efforts towards addressing this global call have investigated how people handle change while practicing effective citizenshi p to influence positive change in their communities  (e.g., Jonasson, 2016; Haste & Chopra, 2020). Haste and Ch opra (2020) emphasize that education must prepare individuals to manage “rupturing changes”—cataclysmic events that profoundly alter existing social and cultural narratives, such as climate change a nd global pandemics. Haste and Chopra's (2020) work  highlight the importance of civic education in enabling students to adapt to the complexities of a rapidly changing global landscape, where ambiguity and uncertainty are cons tants rather than exceptions. According to their an alysis, effective civic education should not merely impart knowledge about the society but foster resilience, adaptability, and the capacity to interpret and navigate novel, often unpredictable, circumstances (Haste & Chopra, 2020). Such an education involves building capacity amongst mul tiple stakeholders. Existing work highlights such c apacity building activities amongst schoolteachers (e.g., Carvalho et al., 2023). While teachers are important stakeholders, educational designers who design resources for teachers and schools need to be engaged in such capacity building measures as well (Aczel et al., 2008). By learning how to respond to  complex changes with a critical lens, future educational designers candesign meaningful resourc es that better prepare teachers and learners to fac e uncertain realities with resilience and criticality. In  this paper, we investigate: How can we meaningfully  engage student educational designers in learning how to design educational resources enabling critical action?

## Theoretical framework

Critical pedagogy, as proposed by Paulo Freire, pro vides a foundational approach to transformative education. Freire emphasizes the concept of conscientização, or critical consciousness, where individuals beco me aware of social, political, and economic contradictions and take action to change oppressive elements within th eir reality (Freire, 1970). Freire’s framework encourages learn ers to question existing soc ial structures, fostering empowerment through dialogue and critical reflectio n—crucial for nurturing active, engaged citizens who contribute positively to society. Carvalho et al. (2023) build on Freire’s theories with their Critica l Action Learning Exchange (CALE) framework which integrates critical pedagogy with the concept of Change Laboratory (Engeström et al., 1996) and provides a dynamic mod el for meaningfully engaging participants in critic al action learning. It enables participants to transcend esta blished routines and collaboratively reimagine prac tices that support individual and collective growth (Engeström, 2016). This is done via facilitating reflection a nd analysis of their individual and collective knowledge, activ ities, and practices (collectively referred to as “ mirrors”) followed by constructing a new model of activity th at represents a refined zone of proximal developmen t (Engeström, 2016). For this research, we build on t hese theoretical foundations and present an iterati ve process of designing a curricular toolbox for meaningfully engaging master’s level educational designers (stud ents) in learning how to design educational resources enabling critical action. CALE has developed a pedagogical approach called Cr itical Action Learning (CAL) that draws from Knowledge Building (KB) theory and Critical Pedagogy (Carvalho et al., 2021). KB provides a conceptualization of two distinct modes of engaging with knowledge and ideas, described as 'belief mode' and 'design mod e' (Bereiter & Scardamalia, 2003). Belief mode refers t o the stance we assume when evaluating knowledge cl aims and deciding what to believe, while design mode ref ers to the mindset we adopt when innovating, creati ng new knowledge, or advancing an idea. This characterizat ion shares similarities with the philosophy of 'pra xis’ from Critical Pedagogy, which stresses the importance of the educative process to interweave theoretical reflection and transformative action (Jemal & Bussey, 2018). The c oncept of 'praxis' manifests in the “problem-posing model” of education proposed by Freire (1970), which refra mes inescapable “realities” as actionable “problems.” Both KB and Critical Pedagogy conceptualize epistemic sh ifts that allow educators to design curricula where  students engage in critical reflection and transformative action toward complex problems. CALE's pedagogical framework aims to facilitate such design work by providing technology and conceptual tools that help teachers in corporate opportunities for students to engage in critical re flection and transformative action towards concrete  socio-environmental issues, to function as a community of learners, and to expand the reach of their initiatives to outside the classroom.

## Method

We use a design-based research (DBR) approach to iteratively refine the critical action learning curricular toolbox. DBR enables investigation of an educational interve ntion in authentic settings and refines the interve ntion in response to “emergent featur es of the setting” (Design-Based Research Collective, 2003, p. 6). It facilita tes iterative improvement of the interventions informed  by the context of implementation and feedback from  the stakeholders (Sandoval & Bell, 2004). Our context i ncludes university students enrolled in an educatio nal technology focused course as part of their one-year master’s programs in Educational Psychology and Education Science and Technology in the Netherlands. This pap er reports on three iterations of the curricular de sign and implementation of one topic (titled: Critical Actio n Learning, 3-4 weeks duration) within this larger course context. The number of students per iteration were 15 (1st iteration), 12 (2nd iteration), and 35 (3rd iteration). The first author served as the primary instructor for this topic in all these iterations. The instructor’s curricular design and student’s anonymous feedback on the topic serve d as the data sources for this research. In this wa y, this research also adopts the practitioner research appr oach (Campbell et al., 2003). Each iteration and as sociated reflection served as opportunities for the first au thor to improve his practice as the teacher of the topic. The coauthors who were not directly linked to everyday teaching of the topic served as ‘critical friends’, cross-checking the perceptions and interpretations presented in this paper (Campbell et al., 2003). Further triangulation was done using students’ anonymous feedback. In addition, during the third iteration, students wrote individual reflections every week, documenting their personal and group ta keaways from that week’s articles and class discuss ions. These reflected what the students valued in that it eration and helped in validating the interpretations. Howev er, due to ethical restrictions, these student reflections cannot be presented in this paper.

## Findings and discussion

Implementation 1: Origins of the CAL-toolbox 1.0 for educational designers-A constrained approach The goal here was to introduce students to Making a s a phenomenon in general and Critical Making as a pedagogical approach in particular. We wanted to introduce students to CAL and connect it to their career interests related to becoming educational techno logy researchers, designers, and consultants. Hence, having a strong technological flavor was assumed to be meaningful f or the students. We explored makerspaces as learnin g environments focusing on types of makerspaces (e.g., Fablabs), in-school and out-of-school makerspaces including online spaces, theories of learning and p edagogy underlying such environments (e.g., constru ctionism, creativity), and tools and technologies used to design such spaces. We then zoomed in on Critical Action Learning Exchange (CALE), focusing on fostering critical mak ing for effecting various societal changes. Student s participated in in-class discussions (three 90 minutes sessions in-class) and out-of-class group project work. For their project, students were asked to brainstorm on  issues (locally or globally) that concerned them t he most. In teams of 3-4, they had to design a Scratch resource (game, ani mation, story, etc.) that addressed a social issue selected by their group. They designed this deliver able along with the readings and discussion across the three weeks. Evaluation of this deliverable included vari ous aspects like the presence of detailed design, a lignment of the design, target audience, and learning goals, and quality of the artifact for effecting critical action. We used the CALE lesson design template to anchor and guide stu dents’ journey during this design process. CALE exp ert researchers joined the in-class sessions remotely to share their experiences and mentored the groups by providing feedback on their CALE lesson designs. Some of the key social issues addressed by the student-generated artifacts include housing, racism, populism politics, climate change, and bullying. Reflection and takeaways from implementation 1 The constraint of using Scratch as an authoring too l was in response to the need to provide an educati onal technology orientation and align with the objective of Making/critical making. We used Scratch since we wanted the technology component to be easy for students to  learn and use. In addition, Scratch is versatile a nd enables users to create a wide variety of content easily. However, this constraint translated into less autonomy for student educational designers. In addition, some students f ound Scratch to be too simplistic to express their curricular design ideas. This possibly resulted in the selection of topics/social issues which were possible to (re)present using Scratch. Thus, technology took the center stage and drove students’ CAL design process instead of the other way around. Anchoring to the CALE framework and design template was helpful and meaningful for the student s. This was also reflected in the topic evaluation survey (8 responses, response rate = 27.6%). On a scale of  1 (terrible) - 10 (outstanding), responses indicated that students  were satisfied with the topic (mean  = 6.4; SD = 0.7). They indicated – on a scale of 1 (great)  to 5 (terrible) – ‘meaningfulness of the interactions’ as moderately positive (mean = 2.5; SD = 0.5), and ‘level of chal lenge in the assignments’ as moderately positive (m ean = 2.6; SD = 0.9). In the open-ended question, students indicated that they “felt restricted by having to implement our idea Scratch (sic).”  But “the CALE template was helpful, as well as the gues t lecture”  highlighting the meaningfulness of these resources and expert intera ctions. Students also perceived a disconnect betwee n the lectures and the assignment - “I liked the lectures, but I feel like the assignme nt doesn’t align with this topic.” These factors could be the underlying reasons for t he moderately positive scores in the topic evaluation. There was also ambiguity amongst the students regarding the target audience for the CALE lesson designs i.e., were the educational designers designing the lessons for the mselves (imagining themselves as schoolteachers) or for real teachers (aligning with the career interests in des ign and consulting). Based on these inputs and afte r discussing potential improvements with the ‘critical friends’,  we decided to reduce the constraints and provide m ore autonomy for students to engage in critical action learning. We also deci ded to emphasize the theoretical aspects of critical action learning more and make this central to the class discussions. Implementation 2: CAL-toolbox 1.1-More autonomy for students In this implementation, we foregrounded critical making as an anchor for introducing students to CAL. To enable this, we replaced the general content on makerspace s and creativity with specific research articles on  critical making. Students participated in in-class discussions (two 90 minutes sessions in-class) and out-of-class group project work. In teams of 2-3, they had to design a lesson that empowered school students regarding critical issues around them and helped them make progress with their curricular requirements. This lesson design was facilitated with the CALE lesson design template. The student e ducational designers engaged in critical making whi le designing the deliverables for the school students.  The student educational designers had the freedom to choose the age range of their target school students and issues of interest. In addition, we removed the constraint of using Scratch as the means of realizing their designs. Students could use any means (physical or virtual) to  facilitate critical action amongst the school students. The third session was reserved to give feedback on the lesson designs. Students submitted their lesson designs and PowerPoint with voiceover explanations explicitly connecting t heir lesson plan and designed solution with the articles  read. The articles included for this iteration inc luded Matt Ratto’s (2011) article on critical making, Carvalho et al.’s (2023) article on culturally responsive CALE approach, and various online resources on CAL/CALE. CALE expert researchers joined the in-class sessions remotely to share their experiences and mentored the groups as they worked on addressing the critical issues identified at the beginning of the topic. These ranged from data physicalization for unders tanding climate change, interactive storytelling to promote cultural awareness, Scratch  based activity to understand the housing crisis in  the Netherlands, Scratch-based storytelling game to raise awarenes s against bullying, Scratch-based story to understand carbon emissions, physical sculpting to raise sustainability awareness, storytelling as a m eans of tackling loneliness amongst apartment residences, g ame enabling perspective taking on political matter s, and game focusing on water conservation.

Reflection and takeaways from implementation 2 The freedom to creatively express their CALE lesson s translated into diverse project topics and group artifacts. These dealt with deep issues that the master’s students found meaningful in their experiences. This was reflected in the topic evaluation survey (4 responses, response rate = 33.3%). The responses  on a scale of 1 (terrible) - 10 (outstanding) indicated that students found this topic good (mean = 7.5; SD = 1.7). They indicated – on a scale of 1 (great)  to 5 (terrible) – ‘meaningfulness of the interactions’ as positive (m ean = 1.8; SD = 1) and ‘level of challenge in the assignments’ as moderately positiv e (mean = 2.3; SD = 1.5). These two metrics indicat e a slight overall improvement in the students’ experience of the topic. The first author observed that the students were engaged in the topic, but this varied across the class with some team-members being more engaged than others possibly because the project was not challenging enough for everyone. This variation likely resulted in inequitable effort invested within the teams while creating the  project deliverables. The open-ended feedback from the students reiterated this observation. Some students  reported that “the assignment…felt too easy.”  They highlighted that while “it was good to think about this topic, … it was no t challenging enough. Constructing the lessons by filling in the form [CALE design templat e] felt like bachelor (sic), but making the format that we had to fill in should be masters degree (sic). However,  the format was already there.”   These indicate the need to increase the complexity of the assignment and provi de further agency to the students. They reported th at while the CALE design template was useful to think about the lesson design, it was restrictive and came acro ss as a simplistic fill-in-the-blank activity. They requested “space to think about how to teach critical thinkin g before giving the answer (CALE format).” This indicated the need to position the CALE design  template as a reference resource with room for deviation and creative explo ration. Further, the first author experienced tensi on in the classroom as the master’s students tried to use the ir own social issues to design lesson plans for school students since the needs of the (imagined) school students w ere ambiguous. Lack of an authentic setting and rea l students and real issues constrained the CAL experience of the master’s student. Implementation 3: CAL-toolbox 2.0-Nested model driven by community-engaged learning This implementation followed a community-engaged learning approach (Hou, 2014) and was spread across five weeks with four 90 minutes in-class sessions. The broad topic goals were to introduce students to the theory of CAL with a focus on understanding complex socio-scientific issues related to UN’s sustainability development goals, knowledge building, critical pedagogy, and studen t empowerment. A middle school teacher in the Netherlands served as the client having a broad educational challenge pertaining to her middle school students’ apathy and feelings of helplessness regarding vario us socio-scientific issues around them. The master’s student s had to collaboratively design a CAL lesson for this  teacher who was available throughout the design pr ocess to respond to queries and help understand the school context. We anchored our instructions and resources around the teacher’s needs and UN’s sustainable developmen t goals to provide an authentic context for the student educational designers. We included more readings focusing on theoretical foundations of critical action learning. In session 1, we covered theories of knowledge buil ding, epistemic agency, critical pedagogy, and crit ical action learning. In this session, the schoolteacher introduced the broad pedagogical challenge. Due to school commitments, the teacher could not appear in person for the first session, providing a self-recorded video with the challenge instead. This worked well during the sess ion since the video could be replayed to understand  and deliberate as a whole class on the broad and complex challenge posed by the teacher. Thirty-five student educational designers worked in teams (9 teams, 3-4 members per team) to understand the problem and create a scoped problem statement t hat they wanted to address. This was a challenging but valuable process since information gathering was ha ppening via email conversations with the schoolteacher. Although this asynchronous process slowed down the speed of communication, it created opportunities fo r the teams to critically think about their problem state ment and reflect on critical information required t o act on that problem statement. This slow but deliberate process transla ted into valuable questions for the schoolteacher to consider and reflect upon. The schoolteacher assisted this process by answering these qu estions promptly. These collective knowledge building processes resulted in the creation of a detailed and concrete collective problem case covering diverse interconnected issues that the teams were working on. The instructor (first author) documented the problem case and made it available as a persistent reference item via the course Canvas page. Between sessions 2-4, we introduced student designers to the importanc e of a culturally responsive design approach and va rious CAL approaches. The schoolteacher and CALE experts interacted with the student designers virtually for part of sessions 3 and 4. We followed a nested approach for designing the sessions.

• Layer 0 (core): Authors of this paper designed a lesson plan and learning materials for engaging student

educational designers (Layer 1) in critical action.

• Layer 1 (Student-level): Student educational designers used the lear ning materials from Layer 0 to

collaboratively work over 4-5 weeks for designing a critical action lesson for the client/teacher (Layer 2) and their student. The CALE design template (Figure 1ab) was used to guide student-work in this layer. In session 4, students received feedback on their C ALE design plan from the first author and the schoolteacher.

• Layer 2 (Client/School teacher-level): The school teacher uses the learning materi als designed by the

student educational designer (Layer 1) to inform their teaching practices and engage middl e school students in critical action learning.

After each session (weekly), students had to submit  a one-page individual reflection writeup focusing on thei r understanding of the research articles assigned for  that session, personal takeaways, and connection b etween the article and their team’s project. A detailed rubric for evaluating this reflection was provided. The open-endedness reflection prompt and page constraint made it challenging for the master’s students to write these reflections since they had little prior experience with such assignme nts. The first author gave feedback on these indivi dual reflections by the end of every week since critically reading 35 reflections took time. These reflections encouraged students to be concise and synthesize their understanding of the research articles and classroom discussions with the schoolteacher’s needs, identifying concrete con nections with their personal experiences and ongoin g team project. The first author observed personal growth of many students via these reflections as a by-product of their individual struggle with navigating through the uncerta in problem space and collaborative effort. Some stu dents also mentioned how they were considering transferri ng their knowledge of CAL to their personal/profess ional contexts outside the classroom.

All the nine teams designed unique CALE lessons for  the schoolteacher. These ranged from a dynamic/living newspaper owned by the middle school  students, inter-generational activities, digital eco-journal focusing on climate change, coaching programs for the teachers and students aimed at increasing awareness about climate change, student-centered activities using photography aimed at redu cing feelings of powerlessness and frustration, climate action fair to engage the community in relevant conversations, water footprint analyzer, social media video for influencing attitudes towards clima te change, and speed dating for engaging students c ritically on the biases in social media. The teams used the CALE design template to justify their design decisions explicitly making connections with the assigned research artic les, gave detailed enactment plans, and evaluation plans to help the schoolteacher adopt their designs. The schoolteacher reviewed all the final designs and was posi tive regarding their usefulness in the school for addres sing the broad challenge. In fact, the lesson with the living newspaper could be adopted immediately since the sc hool administration was already considering  a similar solution. Thus, the student educational designers successfully engaged in critical action at their level (Layer 1) by creating CALE lessons/artifacts that had the potent ial to facilitate critical action in the school. All nine CALE lessons provided elaborate guidance for the schoolteacher to inform their teaching practice and enabl ed middle school students to engage in critical action (Layer  2). The schoolteacher will now consider these lessons and artifacts while improving pedagogy for social impac t in the school curricula. This step is underway wh ile preparing this paper.

Reflection and takeaways from implementation 3 The nested approach anchored around the schoolteacher’s issues and UN’s sustainability development goals enabled the student educational designers to meanin gfully connect the theoretical articles on critical action learning with the practical requirements in the sch ool. While the broad social problem provided by the schoolteacher resulted in uncertainty amongst the student educati onal designers during the starting phase of the lesson, scaffolding via the construction of the collective problem case and CALE design template helped manage these uncertainties productively; an important part of the learning process (Kaur & Dasgupta, 2024). The process of creating the collective problem case required the teacher (Layer 2) to refl ect and respond to critical questions by the student educational designers (Layer 1). This reflective collective problem case creation process served as an emergent mirror for the schoolteacher (Layer 2). We call this ‘emergent mirror’ d ue to the dynamic nature of the problem case and emergent nature of the questio ns from the entire student educational designer classroom community that was actively engaged in understandin g the problem better. These questions encouraged th e schoolteacher to critically reflect on the broad challenge concerning her students and modify their practice. It also provided an opportunity to the first author to make  explicit connections with the research articles and customize the classroom discussion (Layer 0).

The ‘meaningfulness’ metric and the o verall satisfaction level of the topic was lower compared to implementation 2. The open-ended part of the student feedback provides further insight into these metrics. In contrast to feedback received on the previous implementations regarding the easy assignments, in this implementation, all the students reported that the challenge provided was hard to understand and posed  a lot of uncertainty. They found it especially challenging to determine exactly what needed to be designed. The instructor (i.e., first author) observed that this uncertain phase resulted in the teams engaging in critical thinking with their peers and asking insightful questions to the schoolteacher. The CALE template helped them notice key aspects of the problem that they needed more information about, thereby scaffolding the collective information ga thering process. Without this space, the concrete collective problem case could not have been built. Most importantly, the creation of the collective problem case reflected a whole-class collective inquiry process leading to the cre ation of a social product driven by the student educational designers; a key aspect of the knowledge building pedagogy (Zhang et al., 2018). The student educational design ers together with the first author and the schoolteacher experienced collective cognitive responsibility and  engaged in knowledge building discourse while crea ting this collective problem case (social product) (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2014). Thus, this social pr oduct mediated interaction between all the three layers.

The open-ended student feedback served as mirrors for the first author to understand how to modify the pedagogy (Level 0). While the students found the “[teacher’s problem] case [to be] greatly valuable and the weekly reflections help[ful] to stay on top of the content and make connections between the lectures/weeks”, they found the uncertainty faced while reducing the problem scope frustrating. The schoolteacher’s problem provided the desired authentic setting but the challenge of aligning schedules with an external client merits f urther discussions on how to balance  the need for authenticity with curricular time rest rictions; a tension that is quite common to educational settings but becomes central to the effective implementation of CALE curricula (Carvalho et al., 2022). Further, students reported that the feedback provided on the individual reflections was  valuable but “barely given in time”  before the submission deadline of the next reflecti on. This prevented them from implementing the feedback for their next reflection. The need for quick formative feedback is well doc umented (Shute, 2008) but its implementation in this context  was found to be challenging due to the large numbe r of students; another tension that merits further discussions. These tensions served as learning opportunities for Layer 0 to further improve the toolbox and become critical action educator (Carvalho et al., 2022).

## Conclusion

The three implementations highlight the complexity of becoming a critical action educator (Layer 0). We started with the research question investigating how to mea ningfully engage student educational designers in l earning how to design educational resources enabling critic al action. We found that the layered approach provi ded the space for various stakeholders (CALE educator, student educational designers, schoolteacher) in the CAL process to be meaningfully reflective on their experiences within their layer and also inspire learning across  the layers. While this cross-layer interaction invites logistical and procedural tensions and challenges, it creates opportunities to engage in expansive learning by blurring the boundaries between these layers (Engeström, 2016). The presence of the authentic context, collective problem case, emergent mirrors, and mirrors likely scaffold these  cross-layer CAL interactions. These, toget her with the CALE design template and other resourc es such as research articles provided in the third implementation comprise the CAL-toolbox 2.0 and  highlight one way to meaningfully engage student educational designers in learning how to design educational resources enabling critical action. We believe that our focus on building capacity amongst student educ ational designers using such a toolbox that balances their career interests – becoming researchers, designers, and consultants – with learning goals anchored to CAL is a novel contribution. Further, as a theoretical contribution, the nested model highlights the value of emergent mirrors and suggests further consideration within the CALE framework. The iterative design research presented in this pap er showcases that learning science researchers can cultivate a generation of designers capable of prod ucing educational technologies and resources that p rioritize equity, critical reflection, and social re sponsibility. The CAL-toolbox can be used as a resource by educators to practice CAL in their classrooms. When educational resources are created with a focus on critical action, they can effectively reach and influence a broad audience, a dvancing education systems that confront inequaliti es and foster an engaged, justice-oriented society; an important goal dur ing present times (UNESCO, 2021). This can have far reaching practical implications by creating a generation of students that can comforta bly navigate novel and uncertain circumstances.
