# Exploring Collaborative Teacher-AI Interaction for Rehearsing Responsive Teaching in Elementary Mathematics Education

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This proposal introduces TeacherCollab, an AI-supported simulated professional learning community designed to cultivate early-career teachers' (ECTs) responsive teaching in elementary mathematics. Rather than simulating students, TeacherCollab positions AI as collaborative teacher colleagues who model diverse pedagogical lenses while reasoning around authentic students ' work. Grounded in approximations of practice and computer-supported collaborative learning (CSCL) theory, TeacherCollab creates a multi-agent dialogue environment where ECTs interact with AI-generated personas that surface students ' relational dynamics, mathematical strategies, and areas of struggle. A pilot study with 16 ECTs showed that TeacherCollab fostered engagement, reflection, and awareness of multiple perspectives on student learning.

Responsive teaching, which requires teachers to notice, interpret, and respond to the substance of students' ideas, is a high-leverage instructional practice that early-career teachers (ECTs) find difficult to master (Sherin et al., 2011; Dyer & Sherin, 2016). To support ECTs, teacher educators have developed approximations of practice, simplified environments for rehearsing complex teaching moves (Grossman et al., 2009). More recently, large language models (LLMs) have been deployed to simulate student dialogue to support ECTs in eliciting and responding to students' thinking (e.g., Lee & Yeo, 2022; Dai et al., 2024; Lee et al., 2025). Early evaluations highlight clear affordances, including iterative and scalable rehearsal, more open-ended turns, and a str onger presence. However, they also surface limits that matter for transfer in actual teaching. For example, AI-simulated students can produce generic or implausible reasoning, struggle to offer productive beginnings for teacher uptake, and exhibit brittleness or repetition, which can nudge ECTs toward low-leverage moves (Zhang, 2024; Dai et al., 2024). These findings suggest reframing AI's role: rather than perfecting a single "AI student", simulations should be grounded in authentic student artifacts and l ayered with in-the-moment scaffolds that surface multiple interpretive lenses. In view of this, we introduce TeacherCollab, a teacher-facing simulation platform that moves from using AI to simulate students to positioning AI as collaborative teacher colleagues in a multi-agent, dialogic environment grounded in authentic student work. We introduce the TeacherCollab platform and explore the potential of an AI-stimulated platform to scaffold ECTs' engagement in the professional practice of making sense of students' mathematical thinking.

The TeacherCollab platform TeacherCollab simulates a teacher professional learning environment (PLC) that invites ECTs to practice noticing and making sense of student mathematical thinking, and to do so in dialogue with AI-generated simulated teacher colleagues who collaborate with one another and with the novice teacher around the same artifact (Klieger et al., 2024). ECTs are invited to participate in teacher-AI interactions about noticing and making sense of students' work and dialogue. Each AI agent persona adopts a distinct pedagogical stance, which helps ECTs construct a rich, humanizing read of students' work and dialogue. In particular, one agent examines social and relational aspects of student collaboration, particularly talk-move patterns, such as positioning and revoicing (Barron, 2003; Chapin et al., 2009). A second attends to students' learning strategies, identifying and naming students' mathematical thinking, positioning every learner as a capable thinker (Carpenter et al., 1999; Jacobs et al., 2010). A third focuses on identifying students' areas of struggle involving partial understandings and confusion, and proposes respectful follow-ups that preserve student ownership while nudging thinking forward (Kapur, 2014; Warshauer, 2015). These lenses invite ECTs to develop a multidimens ional, asset-oriented perspective they can apply to artifacts of student thinking, enabling responses that both advance mathematical ideas and humanize the learning process. Each session centers on a short lesson snapshot, a focused slice of classroom activity paired with the relevant students' work or dialogue, which anchors a brief sequence of guided dialogue. ECTs first engage one-at-a-time with an AI agent representing the respective interpretive lens (e.g., interactional dynamics, strategies, areas of struggle) (see Figure 1, left). The conversation then expands into a roundtable discussion, in 746 which multiple agents speak together with the user. Contrasting interpretations are made explicit, and the user articulates a justified next instructional step that reconciles or prioritizes the perspectives (see Figure 1, right). ## Pilot study and discussion

We piloted TeacherCollab with participants (n=16). We collected session records, including lens choices, dialogue transcripts, and timestamps per snapshot, and collected post-case reflections from participants, where participants shared positive impressions of this tool. They appreciated the clear and accessible user interface and found the experience engaging. Participants noted that TeacherCollab  prompted them to reflect on their own thinking and to consider diverse perspectives on student learning. 67% of participants reported feeling engaged throughout the process. In terms of learning potential, several participants highlighted that the tool enhanced their understanding of how children think mathematically and how social dynamics influence students' interactions. For improvement, participants recommended incorporating richer conversational variety as well as integrating multimodal student work, for example, using videos instead of only transcripts. TeacherCollab reframes AI's role from a simulator to a collaborative partner tha t scaffolds how teachers reason together around authentic student artifacts. The multi-agent design externalizes different interpretive lenses, creating productive tensions that prompt teachers to justify their pedagogical decisions. This aligns with CSCL's view of learning as knowledge building across multiple voices (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2010). By focusing on augmenting professional vision rather than automating it, TeacherCollab offers a scalable model for developing the complex practice of responsive teaching. While the current study focused on ECTs because they are at a formative stage in developing professional noticing skills and stand to benefit most from structured scaffolding around interpreting students' thinking, the multi-agent design is not inherently limited to this population. Experienced teachers may engage differently with the tool (e.g., drawing on richer classroom repertoires to challenge or extend the AI agents' interpretations).
