# Belonging Infrastructure: A Designable Construct for Cultivating Teacher Agency in RPPs

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Research-Practice Partnerships (RPPs) often rely on boundary infrastructure to explain how collaboration is organized, but this lens overlooks how teacher agency is cultivated. We introduce belonging infrastructure, the designable features of collaboration that legitimize participant contributions and make belonging visible. Evidence from a teacher co-design workshop demonstrates how this construct provides a new lens for intentionally designing partnerships that foster teacher leadership, build district capacity, and expand who feels authorized to teach computer science.

Research-Practice Partnerships (RPPs) are instrumental for improving education (Coburn & Penuel, 2016; Fishman et al., 2013), and the concept of boundary infrastructure, the routines, tools, and roles that mediate collaboration, helps explain how they function (Farrell et al., 2022). While a valuable framework, focusing on the structural elements, it offers less guidance on how to intentionally design for the relational dimensions within curricular co-design that enable teachers to move from being participants to integral partners (Gomez et al., 2022; Severance et al., 2018). This is a critical need when supporting educators teaching outside their primary expertise, a common challenge in elementary computer science (Ni et al., 2023; Yadav et al., 2016).

To address this design challenge, we introduce the construct of belonging infrastructure: the network of designed participatory structures  (e.g., co-created norms, teacher-led artifacts, public critique routines) that validate participants’ knowledge and legitimize their contributions (Figure 1). This infrastructure is what allows teachers to confidently engage with, critique, and create the boundary objects and practices of the partnership. This leads to our research question:  How does belonging infrastructure characterize the development of teacher boundary spanners in a research-practice partnership?

Figure 1 Belonging Infrastructure Mediates the Engagement of Boundary Spanners with Boundary Practices and Objects This study occurred in a new RPP among two local universities and a neighboring large, urban school district in the southwestern United States. We analyzed data from a four-day summer co-design workshop where four elementary STEM teachers and one district leader developed a third-grade physical computing unit  using the micro:bit, a small, programmable microcontroller designed for introducing computing to young children (Sentance, et al., 2017). The workshop included intentionally designed participatory structures (e.g., co-created norms, teacher-authored artifacts, public critique). We analyze how belonging infrastructure emerged through these structures. Each day balanced design tasks with participatory structures intended to foster belonging: Day 1 built community through icebreakers, norms, and a co-created teacher goals board; Day 2 focused on shaping the Boundary Infrastructure Boundary Spanners Boundary Objects Boundary Practices Belonging Infrastructure 632 storyline and guiding questions, with teachers reframing the unit design; Day 3 emphasized lesson development, live coding, and group reflection; Day 4 centered on design iteration and teacher-led critique of the unit structure. Data sources included video recordings of the entire workshop and all physical artifacts created. We conducted a hybrid thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2006), combining deductive and inductive approaches. Through the hybrid thematic analysis, we identified four observable dimensions of belonging infrastructure in practice: belonging to the computer science community  (e.g., use of CS concepts, adaptations for learners, selfefficacy statements), belonging within the RPP (e.g., use of “we,” expressions that ideas mattered, openness about uncertainty), agency (e.g., initiating or redirecting planning, proposing new goals or tools), and navigating discomfort (e.g., asking clarifying questions, reframing ideas after feedback). These categories guided our coding and interpretation of the workshop data.

These moments were enabled by designed structures that positioned teachers as contributors, including public artifact creation, flexible use of shared space, and opportunities to reorganize and critique the work. Our analysis shows how these designed participatory structures functioned as a belonging infrastructure.  These designed structures created the conditions for teachers to take intellectual risks, demonstrate agency, and ultimately shape the partnership's work. We present two illustrative cases that show this construct in action through these structures (see Table 1). In the first case, a teacher’s spontaneous drawing of a computer program became a new boundary object that advanced the group's collective understanding. In the second, a teacher demonstrated design agency by physically reorganizing the unit timeline, a mo ve that reshaped a core boundary practice of the RPP.  These moments, detailed in Table 1, illustrate how belonging was not just an internal feeling but was enacted and made visible through recognized contributions. The belonging infrastructure provided the means for teachers to author new roles and become integral partners in the co-design process. This study introduces belonging infrastructure to characterize the development of teacher boundary spanners  as increasing participation in shaping boundary objects and practices. It does so by illuminating the moment-tomoment interactions where teachers, through acts of design agency, create new boundary objects and reshape boundary practices. This construct extends the theory of boundary infrastructure by shifting focus from the mere existence of collaborative structures to the designable conditions that invite all participants to become integral partners. Unlike boundary infrastructure, which describes coordination structures, belonging infrastructure focuses on designing for visible participation and authorship. Ultimately, belonging infrastructure offers a critical lens for designing more equitable partnerships that cultivate teacher leadership by recognizing them as active contributors to system change and knowledge building.
