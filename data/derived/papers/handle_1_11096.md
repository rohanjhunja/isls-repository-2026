We developed an ontology of teacher feedback used in practice by teachers in a digital platform for math learning. ## Background and motivation

Feedback is an essential component of instruction t hat can enhance learning outcomes by providing info rmation to learners about their performance and how to improve it (Hattie & Timperley, 2007). However, not all feedback is equally effective in different situations. The e ffectiveness of feedback depends on various factors, such as the instructional context, the nature of the task, and the characteristics of the learner (Shute, 2008). T herefore, it is important to understand the different types and cha racteristics of feedback that can be more or less s uitable for different learning scenarios  such as math learning in a digital platform. Distinguishing between elaborative and nonelaborative feedback is one way of categorizing types of feedback  (Shute, 2008). Elaborative feedback tends to be lengthier and more detailed whereas nonelaborative feedback is often straightforward and  to the point and is generally thought to focus primarily on the learner’s response. Such a distinction, however, may oversimplify the rich information contained in, and the usefulness of, nonelaborative forms of feedback. Furthermore, understanding patterns in nonelaborative types of f eedback may provide a foundation for understanding more elaborative forms of feedback given that lengthier feedback may consist of a combination of multiple instances of non-elaborative feedback.  Given this versatility,  focusing on the characteristics of nonelaborative forms of feedback makes sense  in pursuing the  goal of creating a general ontology of teacher feed back. Creating an ontology of teacher feedback in a digital learning platform could support a foundational understanding of processes and mechanisms to facilitate student learning, which could subsequently be empirically tested. This knowledge also has the potential to inform the desi gn and development of innovative pedagogies and dig ital learning environments, particularly those that might leverage models of feedback for more authentic AI-facilitated interactions that have the potential to foster student learning at scale.

## Digitally mediated feedback within the context of a community of inquiry

Feedback in online or otherwise digitally mediated learning contexts can have a significant impact on student learning, motivation, and self-regulation (Lipnevich & Panadero, 2021). Understanding feedback provided by teachers to students is important because feedback is a powerful tool for enhancing learning and motivation. Feedback can provide information to students to help them monitor their progress, identify their st rengths and weaknesses, and improve their performance.

Several possible mechanisms support the efficacy of feedback in instructional contexts. One such framework is the community of inquiry (CoI; Garrison et al., 2010). The CoI framework is a theo retical model that describes the essential elements of a successf ul online learning experience. It was developed bas ed on the principles of social constructivism, and a central tenant of this framework is that lear ners must feel a sense of presence—or connection —to their context to learn most effectively (Woo & R eeves, 2007). The framework consists of three interdependent types of presence relevant to an online learning context: cognitive, social, and teaching (Garrison et al., 1999). Cognitive presence refers to the extent to which learners can construc t meaning through sustained communication and reflection. It involves four phases of critical thinking: triggeri ng event, exploration, integration, and resolution. Cognitive  presence is the goal of any ed ucational experience, as it leads to deep and meaningful learning outcomes. Social presence refers to the ability of learners to project their personal characteristics into the CoI, thereby presenting and feeling connected with oth ers through seemingly authentic interactions. It involves three aspects: emotional expression, open communication, and group cohesion.  Social presence is also important for creating a collaborative and supportive online learning environment as it facilitates cognitive presence by creating the affective conditions for positive social interactions. Finally, teaching presence refers to the design, facilitation, and direction o f cognitive and social processes for the purpose of  realizing personally meaningful and educationally worthwhile learning outcomes. It involves three functions: instructional management, building understanding, and direct inst ruction. Teaching presence is shared among teachers  and participants in the community. It enhances social and cognitive presence for improved learning. Learning in online and remote contexts, such as when using a digital learning platform, may not support the opportunities for creating a strong sense of presence for students, especially f or mathematics courses (Borba et al., 2021; Taley, 2022; Tran & Nguyen, 2021). Some students may pre fer to learn mathematics in a face-toface setting, where there are more opportunities to build a strong sense of teaching presence (Caskurlu et al., 2020) and social presence through student-student interaction given that visual contact tends  to be less frequent (Joksimović et al., 2015). There is growing recognition of the need to support K–12 mathematics instruction in online learning environments especially (Bakker et al., 2021), and some have called for providing more interpersonal supports to improve math learning outcomes (Hodge-Zickerman et al., 2021; Jessup et al., 2021). In such contexts, the quality of feedback provided by teachers to students via a digital learning platform could have great potential to influence students ’ sense of presence and engagement within the learnin g environment. As a source of information that arises through the students’ interactions with teachers or artifacts of teaching, feedback is strongly relevant to the CoI framework. Indeed, it is closely related to all three types of presence. Feedback can enhance cognitive presence by stimulating critical thinking, providing guidance, and promoting reflection (Hosler & Arend, 2012). Feedback can enhance social presenc e by fostering interpersonal relationships, encoura ging collaboration, and building trust (Tu & McIsaac, 20 02) and it can enhance teaching presence by clarifying expectations, offering guidance and diagnosing misconceptions (Wallace, 2003). Beyond contributing to an understanding of the mech anisms that make feedback more effective to support math ematics learning, knowledge of effective feedback practices  can inform the design of innovative digital learning and assessment systems to provide personalized and scalable feedback to learners (Lin  et al., 2023). Such systems can leverage the benefits of hu man-to-human feedback while overcoming its limitations in terms of time, cost, and quality. By incorporating feedback features that align with spec ific learning goals and cater to learners’ individual needs and preferences, digital assessmen t systems can offer more equitable and effective learning experiences to all students. High quality feedback is particularly vital in the K–12 math learning domain, where as noted, there is need to develop digital math learning environments where students have a strong sense of presence and connection to the content. Mo reover, digital assessment systems can also enhance  the motivation and engagement of learners by providing feedback that increases their interest and value in the learning task (Narciss et al., 2014). By delivering feedback that piques their curiosity and underscores the value of a task, digital assessment systems can make the learning experience more captivating. Such systems can also be used to facilitate dialogical interactions with teachers, peers, or simulated agents (Panadero & Lipnevich, 2022) to foster a deeper understanding of the subject matter and encourage active engagement with the learning materials. Research aims Despite the potential benefits of providing feedbac k at scale via digital learning platforms, there is  a critical challenge in capturing the complexity and diversity of feedback types and characteristics across different domains and contexts. This is where  the creation of an ontology of teacher feedback wit hin the digital learning platform becomes essential. An ontology, which is a formal r epresentation of concepts and relationships in a do main, provides a structured framework for reasoning and inference (Guarino, 1995). By creating an ontology of teacher feedback, researchers gain the ability to systemati cally analyze and compare different instances of fe edback provided by teachers in the digital learning platfo rm. This analysis can reveal patterns and identify gaps in the feedback practices of educators, which is invaluable information for improving the overall quality of instruction.

## Methods

The data used to develop this ontology was collecte d through the ASSISTments platform. Teachers can us e the ASSISTments platform to assign problem sets aligned with teaching standards to assess students. Students, in turn, use the platform to work on interactive math problems, including graphics and images, and receive instant feedback and scores for multiple-choice and fill-in-the-blank questions as they complete assignments (Heffernan & Heffernan, 2014). We are not able to report the demographic details o f the sample given this study involves a secondary data analysis and it was not initially co llected; however, we note that the ASSISTments plat form is widely used across schools serving diverse students (Feng et al., 2023; Prihar et al., 2021, 2022). The recent addition of QuickComments to the ASSISTme nts platform  (Bothelo et al., 2023), which harnesses AI to provide teachers with recommended feedback for students that they can then select or edit, renders it an excellent environment for investigating characteristics of teacher-provided feedback given the sheer volume of comments. The initial corpus, which consisted of  instances where middle and high school students primarily located within the United States provided answers to open-ended questions and which had feedback from students, included 198,245 instances of students attempting a problem. Of these, 53,730 instances had feedback comments from teachers that were three or fewer words in length from 1,053 different teachers provided to 13,659 different students. We were primarily interested in feedback consisting of three or fewer words, which we considered an indication of nonelaborative feedback, given that i t comprised the majority of the corpus.  In total, 293 instances of distinct feedback messages were reviewed by two trained researchers in developing the ontology. Though this may seem like a relatively small number, many of the instances of feedback were provided multiple times and in total covered 35,563 instances of feedback delivered to students. The instances of feedback reviewed included all that occurred more than once in addition to a randomly selected subset of instances that occurred only once. Developing the ontology We developed the ontology through multiple iteratio ns. In the first stage, we used an existing taxonom y of feedback from Shute (2008) to derive the initial co des. We began by looking at feedback that we initia lly categorized as nonelaborative. Such instanc es of feedback were three or fewer words in length.  We did this for several reasons. First, we noticed that the median length of the feedback instances was already relati vely small (median word count = 8). As such, even looking at instances of feedback that were three or fewer words in length reflected a substantial proportion of the data (27.1%) and reduced the likelihood that we were looking at instances that were truly exceptional. Second, we were intere sted in looking at characteristics of shorter feedb ack, hoping it would be possible to apply a similar ontology and coding scheme to instances of lengthier feedback,  even if it would involve making necessary revisions. Firs t, we added categories to distinguish whether the feedback was effort or outcome-directed within the broader provide praise and provide critical feedback categories. Effort-directed feedback subcategories applied to both pro vide praise and provide critical feedback categories. It indica tes that the feedback is directed toward the effort  or process of the student rather than the outcome or product. For  example, “nice try” or “keep at it ” are examples of effort-directed feedback. Outcome-directed feedback subcategories also applied to bot h provide praise and provide critical feedback categories. It indicates that the feedback is directed toward the outcome or product of the student rather than the effort or process. For example, “good answer ” or “wrong answer ” are examples of outcome-directed feedback. Instances that were either effort-or outcome-directed could be categorized as “other-directed.” This subcategory applies to both provide praise and  provide critical feedback categories. It indicates  that the feedback is directed toward another person or entit y rather than the student. For example, “???” or “show work” are examples of other-directed feedback. Previous research has found dist inct features of feedback varying in relation to its perceived function (Panadero & Lipn evich, 2022), with effort-directed feedback more likely to support students’ motivation and self-regulation and outcome-directed feedback more likely to directly influence students’ learning and performance (Özkale & Kanadlı, 2021). Second, we recognized that teachers appeared to reference several resources and supports that were either external to the problem (e.g., “see previous proble m”) or the platform (e.g., “see me”), and in some cases, such resources were discouraged. We subsequently  added a new category and set of subcategories  to reflect this. In addition, we added a subcategory called “use discouraged,” which indicates that the feedback discourages the use of certain resources or supports, especially in cas es of suspected cheating. For example, “do not copy ” or “no searching” are examples of such feedback.

Third iteration During the third and final iteration and round of coding, we were interested in determining an estimate of interrater agreement, and thus, a subset of some of the more common instances of feedback, in addition to some of the less common instances in feedback, was randomized and coded twice, once by each pair of reviewers.  In total, 293 instances of feedback were double coded by a pair of reviewers. Based on the coding of the text corpus with the third iteration of the coding scheme, we were able to establish evidence of acceptable interrater reliability (single score intraclass correlation =.79), with interrater agreement across all categories >.80. ## Discussion

The purpose of this study was to develop an ontolog y of teacher feedback used in the ASSISTments platf orm, a digital platform for math learning. We analyzed 53,730 instances of feedback comments from 1,053 teachers and 13,659 students who used the platform. We conducted a thorough qualitative analysis involving multiple iterations of coding to create an ontology, which allowed us to examine the feedback types and characteristics that teachers used. The main outcomes of this work include  (1) an ontology that is relatively comprehensive consistent with existing typologies of feedback and (2) evidence that teachers varied widely in their feedb ack practices  in a digitally mediated platfor m. Though there has been prior work done to examine sentiment and tone of teacher feedback (Baral et al., 2023) or to develop feedback ontologies for writing (Murray et al., 2018), to the best of our knowledge, there are surprisingly few prior attempts to develo p a system of categorizing teacher feedback to support students’ math learning in digital learning environments. The final feedback ontology provides a simple and practical  way of categorizing and describing the different types of feedback that teachers can provide to students in a digital platform. The final ontology consists of seven main categories, each of which have some basis in existing  empirical research literature: praise (see Burnett & Mandel, 2010; Skipper & Douglas, 2012), critical feedback  (see Fong et al., 2018), emotive communication (see Feidakis, 2016; Rowe, 2017), effort-directed (see Lam et al., 2011), mathematical (see Fyfe & Brown, 2018; Narciss et al., 2014),  support external to the immediate context of the pr oblem or platform (see Roll et al., 2006), and socioculturally responsiveness  (see Paul et al., 2012). Additional subcategories further specify the definition and examples of the feedback  type (see Table 2). One challenge in using an ontology such as this is the potential that it is limited to a sp ecific context of use case. However, given the wide  age range and breadth of mathematical problem  types (e.g., multiple-choice single or multiselect, constructed response, etc.), concepts, and grade-levels assessed within the ASSISTments platform (N. Heffernan & C. Heffernan, 2014), we are optimistic that a theoretically guided and empirically based ontology such as this one has the potential to be useful in other digital math learning contexts. Fur thermore, our focus on feedback utterances of three  or fewer words may help to better ensure the usefulness across a v ariety of online learning contexts given that longer instances of feedback may contain several categories of nonelaborative feedback simultaneously, and in addition, written feedback from teachers that is provided onl ine tends to be relatively concise (e.g., Borup et al., 2015). Given our analysis focused on a relatively small subset of the total instances of feedback provided, further research should attempt to determine the extent to which the  present ontology generalizes to instances of feedback from the text corpus excluded in the present investigation, as well as other digital math learning contexts.

## Implications and future directions

Beyond supporting research efforts, an ontology of feedback could hav e implications for teaching and learning. It could be used to support the development of inte lligent agents within digital learning platform s. Such agents could generate and deliver personalized feedback to learn ers, considering their performance and individual preferences. Feedback tailored in this way  is a powerful tool for optimizing the learning proc ess and fostering a deeper understanding of mathematical concepts. Furt hermore, an ontology of feedback has the potential to be used as a pedagogical tool, serving as a resource f or teachers. It equips educators with insights and examples of effective feedback tailored to different instructional scenarios thereby not only enhancing their feedback capacity, but also contribut ing to more meaningful and productive interactions with  students. In these ways, such an ontology could support the design of personalized, scalable, and engaging digital learning and assessment systems for math education, improving the quality of feedba ck provided to learners, and ultimately contributing to more effective and equitable learning experiences.

Building on the work of this ontology, future resea rch could identify whether certain characteristics of feedback are more or less effective in response to students’ work  and in which contexts. Future research could also examine variation based on the type of question or question content, the modes of feedback presentation, and to what extent the source of the feedback (e.g., teacher, AI-generated chatbot, peer, etc.) is associated with  the with receptivity to feedback and learning.
