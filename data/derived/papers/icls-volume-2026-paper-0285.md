# Reconceptualizing Imagination as Social Practice in Science Education

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In science education, imagination has largely been framed as a cognitive process, with comparatively less attention to its social, political, and ecological dimensions. This conceptual paper reconceptualizes imagination as a social practice that reshapes how we imagine and design possible futures. At the micro-level, I reframe imagination as a subjective and situated practice, shaped by individual histories and sociopolitical contexts. At the meso-level, it is explored as a relational act, co-produced through intergenerational collaboration, care, and the navigation of structural constraints. At the macro-level, an ecological imagination extends this practice to include socioecological relations with more-than-human partners, drawing on Indigenous and pluriversal ontologies. By analyzing imagination across these interacting levels, this paper offers a pathway for designing more just and thriving futures. Purpose: Why imagination matters in science education We often give the most attention to children's imaginative engagement in early childhood or elementary classrooms, usually linked to children's play. However, imagination as a pedagogical practice tends to lose legitimacy as kids grow older, especially in science education contexts. Building on existing cognitive and sociocultural scholarship, I propose theorizing imagination as a social practice that foregrounds its relational and sociopolitical dimensions. Recent work in the learning sciences has advanced speculative approaches to justice-oriented futures (e.g., Arada et al., 2023; Garcia & Mirra, 2023). My argument complements this work by focusing more specifically on imagination as the social and relational practice that undergirds such efforts. My work presents imaginative engagement not as naive dreaming, but as a powerful tool for re-worlding that involves complex ideological struggles against frameworks like neoliberalism. Here, I define imagination as a sociohistorically situated practice of world-making through which certain futures become thinkable, legitimate, and actionable. This practice is best pursued in community, including youth and more-than-human partners. Drawing inspiration from marginalized worldviews, imagination as social practice dis rupts tradition by asking new questions: What are the sources of our imagination, what values does it reflect, and whose imaginations are legitimized? Revisiting existing frames of imagination While important work has examined imagination from cognitive and sociocultural perspectives, imagination in science education remains underdeveloped from an explicitly socioecological and political perspective. Early sociocultural traditions help us understand that imagination is not created from nothing, but uses real-world experiences as building blocks. However, theorizing imagination solely within cognitivist or sociocultural traditions leaves certain political and structural dimensions underexplored. F irst, imagination is not a neutral cognitive skill but is situated in our historical, cultural, and political contexts. Second, these renderings fail to recognize the consequential potential of imagination in both micro-and macro-level systems. Theorizing imagination solely at the level of the individual risks overlooking the relational and structural dimensions that shape what becomes imaginable. In defining imagination as a social practice, I draw from Holland & Lave’s (2019) theory of social practice, which considers how participation shapes a person’s daily lives in relation to power and politics. Imagination as social practice does not seek to understand people’s actions as isolated individual acts, but always in relation to other people, practices, and systems. The remainder of this paper is organized into three sections in which I illustrate how imagination-as-social-practice can open up new o nto-epistemologies at the micro - (intrapersonal), meso- (relational), and macro- (ecological) levels. These analytic levels are not mutually exclusive but offer a framing to discuss key facets of imagination as we contend with political systems, cultural histories, and ideological frameworks. Micro level: The individual imagination From cognition to situated subjectivity 1917 The most prevalent theories around imagination largely establish it as a cognitive function contained within an individual’s mind, with some recognition of cultural contexts. Experiential perspectives acknowledge how children blend existing experiences to create new ways of thinking: Vygotsky (2004) discusses imagination as a cultural activity of dissociating and recombining elements of reality in creative ways. This individualistic framing is evident in Egan’s (2005) articulation of imagination as a set of cognitive toolkits divided by developmental stages. This cognitivist tradition has shaped how imagination is researched and implemented in education—in terms of an individual’s cognitive performance. Numerous psychological studies have since examined how imagination mediates learning, viewing it as a mental technique to enhance comprehension and retention. Nonetheless, these studies bypass explanations around how the individual imagination is shaped by our own histories, cultures, emotions, and relations. Cognitive views have also dominated how the field has conceived of disciplinary practices in science education. Kuhn’s (1977) concept of thought experiments, for instance, describes a decontextualized view of the individual imagination in scientific thinking. While both Kuhn and Vygotsky (2004) imply that the individual imagination consists of subjective renderings of a person’s experiences, a chief focus on the tangible, sensory, and cognitive elements could conceal the value-laden subjectivities that driv e our scientific sensemaking. Thus, an inquiry into the role of aesthetics and wonder in science education enables us to see both the personal and deeply social dimensions of our imagination. While aesthetic experiences are seemingly experienced in private and subjective ways (Wickman et al., 2022), science education is seldom theorized as a subjective experience, despite being full of such moments. What we find interesting, wonderful, or strange depends on the observer, whose experiences are deeply situated. After all, Egan (2005) reckons anything has the potential to be seen as wonderful if “seen in the right light” (p. 92). Then, I argue that changing the so-called “light,” or the subjective lens with which we view the world, can alter what we perceive as wonderful, beautiful, or worth imagining. Science as socially constructed Framing the individual imagination as a social practice also makes visible the sociopolitical conditions which have driven our collective inquiry. Disciplinary knowledge is produced through socially organized processes, rather than existing outside historical and cultural contexts. Latour (1987) frames scientific facts as a set of ideas that are socially constructed, contested, and settled through a community of scientists. In other words, what counts as science is socially and interactionally constructed a mong members of the science community. Thus, disciplinary knowledge consists of a fluid, socially-constructed body of knowledge rather than a permanent, rigid set of concepts. Similarly, Medin & Bang (2014) argue that scientific ideas are often “settled” t hrough a social process. They also speak to how science education reflects the values of its adherents, most often dominated by Western scientists. Establishing the disciplinary canon involves a collective process dominated by those who yield social power. Similarly, I view imagination as a dynamic process subject to power relations; whose imaginations get taken up and pursued are deeply shaped by our subjective judgments and the social positions of those making them. Recognizing the subjectivities of the individual imagination offers an entry point to examine how our politically-shaped values permeate our “scientific” imagination. Imagination is constituted through people’s histories, values, identities, emotions, and relations to place. Afrofuturist practices exemplify how individual imaginations can contest social norms to create new forms of culture. As a form of political worlding, afrofuturism demonstrates how the individual imagination can simultaneously engage in cultural critique, resistance, and counter-stories. Speculative storytelling can also prompt resistance by blurring boundaries of present-future and real-imagined (Tomin & Collis, 2024). An exploration of the subjectivities of our imagination at the intrapersonal level mandates that we challenge dominant onto-epistemologies that shape “current and possible meaning, meaning-making, positioning, and relations in cultural ecologies” (Bang et al., 2016, p. 29). Then, it becomes necessary to critically question how our imagination can marginalize perspectives we often need the most. While this section foregrounds subjectivity, imagination does not remain within individuals; it becomes consequential in relation with others and broader systems. Meso level: The relational imagination Relationality as ethical matters I now examine imagination as a social practice that is not only subjective, but relational. By “relational imagination,” I mean more than collaboration; it references to the co-production of worlds mediated by social norms and roles. I stress the importance of questioning how social hierarchies and dominant narratives shape how we imagine at the interpersonal level. Relational imagination is driven by care, agency, and affect. With whom we imagine is just as important as what we imagine. In line with social practice theory (Holland & Lave, 2019), 1918 social hierarchies are central to imagination. Bang and colleagues (2016) suggest that normative relations in learning environments can reproduce power and inequity. They employ the term “withness designing” to underscore a relational approach, proposing intergenerational engagement with elders and youth to constitute community-based change. The relational imagination emphasizes learning across generations and requires role remediation through re-negotiations of power. Therefore, with whom we work, design, and imagine becomes an ethical matter. Reframing children as co-visionaries Children have been historically positioned as naive and questionable sources of knowledge. Silin (2014) emphasizes the need not only to listen to children but also to dissolve the boundaries between adult-child relations. If we were to view people’s experiences and emotions from a collective perspective regardless of age, then science education should focus on empowering and healing young people. This ideological liberation of age segregation is best pursued through collaboration where adults and children inspire and fuel each other’s imaginations. While both adults’ and children’s ideas may reflect and reproduce problematic social norms, I argue that we are more likely to create radical visions in community. In this sense, relational imagination explicitly centers the co-production of ideas —how people imagine with and for one another. Chang & Philip (2025) illustrate such orientation as they cultivated a co-design space that disrupted normative roles in adult-youth partnerships. They critique co-design work that centers adult stakeholders’ needs; instead, they argue the valued outcome is the negotiation of existing social relationships as newly figured worlds. These relational shifts surface how affect, agency, and imagination are jointly situated in practice. Relational imagination amid institutional constraints These relational practices contend with institutional logics that privatize youths’ emotions. Formal schooling is seldom sufficient in meeting youths’ emotional needs (Vamvalis, 2023). Hence, youths’ abilities to navigate the present and shape their futures are constrained by social structures. This is unsurprising given that American schools were primarily established for socializing students to reward passivity and compliance. As such, schools enculturate students intoneoliberal systems rather than prepare them to critique and resist the status quo of science education. Hence, this raises a central tension: How can we design science teaching and learning to help us overcome structural limitations and foster imagination as a path to collective wellbeing? Macro level: The ecological imagination Ecologizing multiple worldviews Macro-level ecological imagination asks not only who imagines what, but with whom and to what ends. The macro level pushes us beyond our human partnerships, toward living well with the natural world, recognizing agency as distributed across human and more-than-human communities. However, these orientations must be accompanied by ontological shifts at the macro level, where multiple worldviews co-shape science education alongside more-than-human partners. I argue that diversity and heterogeneity alone cannot dismantle systems of power and privilege. Thus, the ecological imagination draws from Escobar’s (2018) pluriversal design calls for designing new ways of being in the world—ontologies —centered on culture and community. In particular, pluriversality challenges us to abandon Western assumptions of viewing the world that have jeopardized the fundamental human experience, such as frameworks of modernity (Escobar, 2018; Latour, 2013). Specifically, Latour (2013) calls for ecologization in place of modernization. Such aims necessitate that we interrogate the Western “scientific worldview” that is responsible for establishing subject-object relations. In fact, Latour (2013) contends that nature plays the role of the active subject, sharing agency with other subjec ts. This critique sets the stage for rethinking settled expectations in sciece as part of our ecological imagination. Extending agency to more-than-humans The relational imagination discussed ways to achieve more equitable relations between youth and science. Here, I shift to the absence of ecological onto-epistemologies in science education. Historically, the natural world has been objectified by and differentiated from human culture. This objectification captures the dominant narrative of humans’ exploitation of nature, rooted in science’s dominance over more-than-human beings. It has promoted inequitable extraction of Earth’s resources, and this cost is shared disproportionately among Black, Indigenous, and People of Color (BIPOC) groups (Monarrez et al., 2022). The troubling history of science has also made its way into classrooms—where the valuing of Western scientific practices denies Indigenous ways of understanding and caring for the natural world (Bang & Medin, 2010). In engineering education, technocratic discourses emphasize the design of anthropocentric solutions at the cost of natural resources (Gunckel & Tolbert, 2018). To 1919 explore the roots of our domineering relations with the natural world, I turn to Latour (2013), who challenges the myth of a nature-culture binary, or “bifurcation.” He calls us naive for deanimating natural entities, which calls for a re-distribution of agency across human and more-than-human actors. This work calls for a reconsideration of dominant Western ontological assumptions about nature and culture to take up relational and Indigenous perspectives on inhabiting an animate world. The work of Lees & Bang (2022) embodies such orientations by centering the Indigenous framing of humans as “a part of,” rather than “apart from,” the natural world. In their STEM summer program, youth explored biodiversity in tide pools while learning about their “beach relat ives.” By referring to the starfish as “he” rather than “it,” more-than-humans were imbued with a sense of personhood. Through this experience, children developed socioecological reasoning while acknowledging the agency of these creatures. In Warren & Rose bery’s (2011) study, Jonathan, a Black boy, challenges the Western classification system for living and non-living things. Jonathan constitutes the sun as a living organism based on its essential role in the ecosystem. His challenge of the canon’s binary classification allowed the teacher to reframe Jonathan as a competent science learner, illustrating the potential for the ecological imagination to disrupt dominant onto-epistemologies that pervade science education. Conclusion: Designing for pluriversality Across the micro, meso, and macro levels, we have reframed imagination as subjective, relational, and socioecological world-making processes. I use these levels as analytic lenses, informed by social practice theory, to trace how imagination operates across subjectivity, relational interaction, and broader socioecological systems. Imagination emerges through our lived histories and social relations, shaping how we interpret and imagine possible futures. Imagination-as-social-practice refutes individualistic notions of thriving, foregrounding collectivity instead.

Methodologically, that means we should collectivize care, remediate roles, and establish youths’ wellbeing as shared commitments. We should aim to design science education as sites where we dissolve powered relations and prioritize healing and critical hope (Duncan-Andrade, 2009). Directly and collectively engaging with the complexity of youths’ ideas, emotions, ethics, and uncertainties can yield generative opportunities for re-worlding. Moreover, prompts that elicit axiological inquiry (e.g., What is beautiful/true/right in this design?; Bang et al., 2016) and ethical deliberation (e.g., Should we move earthworms from the sidewalk?; Tzou et al., 2021) can make space to challenge taken‑for‑granted assumptions. In our pursuit to build new worlds, we must necessarily contend with broader social and ideological forces like neoliberalism and anthropocentrism. Scholars have also problematized discourses of militarism (Vossoughi & Vakil, 2018) and technocentrism (Gunckel & Tolbert, 2018) pervading science education. In afrofuturist thought, Womack (2013) demonstrates the power of afrofuturism to critique dominant culture as it “unchains the mind” (p. 15) from dominant ideologies that constrain our imagination. Afrofuturism exemplifies how marginalized communities have used science to liberate themselves from the ideological boundaries. Thus, it becomes important to invite heterogeneity and marginalized perspectives into science classrooms. Nonetheless, aiming for heterogeneity alone is insufficient in the ecological imagination. Therefore, this suggests the need to design in ways that multiple worldviews can fit in and thrive (Escobar, 2018). Such pluriversal orientations involve designing with and for more-than-humans as well. Altogether, this exploration makes a case for examining imagination as a catalyst for rethinking dominant assumptions that shape science education in our present moment. This vision consists of giving young people the tools for imagining alternative worlds and for sustaining life in the current one. As Kelley (2002) would say, this is precisely when we need to exercise our imagination: “But now is the time to think like poets, to envision and make visible a new society, a peaceful, cooperative, loving world without poverty and oppression, limited only by our imaginations” (p. 196).
