# “Both Are Useful”: Student Epistemic Navigation in a Co-Designed Science Unit about Traditional Chinese Medicine

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Science education in rural China often privileges Western epistemology, creating disconnects with students ’ community knowledge systems like Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM). This design-based research study addresses this gap through a co-designed three-lesson unit in a rural middle school in central China. The unit on Shanghuo (上火, internal heat) positioned TCM and Western science as complementary systems with distinct goals, reasoning processes, and evidence criteria. Using a Knowledge Integration framework, analysis of 40 eighth-graders' responses reveals how students navigated between correspondence-based and reductionist causal reasoning without devaluing community knowledge. Students shifted from binary epistemological stances (“TCM is superstitious”) to purposeful differentiation, recognizing when each system serves specific purposes. Three instructional routines supported this work: organizing family knowledge as analyzable data, using parallel comparison structures, and scaffolding dual justification. Findings demonstrate that rural students possess rich epistemological resources that Western-dominant curricula render invisible, offering design guidelines for culturally relevant science pedagogy.

Science classrooms present students with epistemological choices about what counts as legitimate knowledge and how to reason about natural phenomena (Hammer & Elby, 2003). Shaped by Confucian educational traditions and exam-oriented pedagogy, Chinese school science has historically centered teacher authority and standardized knowledge (Gao, 1998), typically privileging a Western scientific epistemology that centers causal mechanisms, controlled observation, and standardized criteria for evidence (Guo, 2007;  Hannum et al., 2011).  At the same time, many rural Chinese students participate in community practices informed by Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM), a knowledge system rooted in Traditional Chinese Science, organized around relational logics of balance, correspondence (recognizing connections across symptoms, lifestyle, and environment), and prevention. When classroom instruction treats TCM as cultural background rather than as a knowledge-making practice, students encounter an epistemological disconnect between school and home. TCM operates through three linked commitments (Needham & Ronan, 2012): Qi (气) as a dynamic substrate whose balanced flow sustains health; Yinyang (阴阳) as complementary properties requiring proportion across seasons and life rhythms; and Wuxing (五行, Five Elements) as correspondences guiding diagnosis and intervention. Together, these commitments produce a distinctive reasoning logic: rather than isolating a single cause, TCM reasoning is relational and correspondence-based, connecting symptoms, diet, emotions, and environmental conditions into a holistic picture of the body's balance. What counts as evidence in TCM is the recognition of recurring patterns across bodily systems over time, such as symptom clusters, seasonal rhythms, and generational ex perience, rather than controlled measurement of isolated variables. Interventions like herbs and acupuncture regulate relations and flow rather than target isolated causes. Rural Chinese classrooms remain underexamined despite parallels to contexts where standardized curricula sideline local practices. In many rural households, TCM is ordinary and trusted (Ren et al., 2020). Students encounter TCM at home while learning mechanistic models in school. The practical challenge is not adjudicating whether TCM “is science,” but moving across epistemic repertoires in ways that preserve home knowledge while supporting rigorous school reasoning. Building on work treating Indigenous and community knowledge as legitimate epistemic resources (Bang & Medin, 2010; Medin & Bang, 2014), I use epistemic navigation to describe how students move between knowledge systems, elaborated in the theoretical framework below.

This paper reports a research-through-design (Hoadley & Campos, 2022) study conducted at a rural middle school in central China. Through a research-practice partnership, we co-designed a three-lesson unit investigating Shanghuo (上火, internal heat) that positions TCM and Western school science in dialogue, making reasoning explicit and structuring comparison without forcing hierarchy. I ask three questions: RQ1: How do rural middle school students navigate between TCM and Western science when investigating Shanghuo? RQ2: Which instructional routines support productive coordination? RQ3: What design guidelines honor community knowledge while advancing disciplinary learning?

## Related work

Research in science education increasingly conceptualizes students' epistemologies as context-sensitive resources rather than fixed beliefs to be replaced (Hammer & Elby, 2003; Sandoval, 2005; diSessa, 2014). A complementary strand centers Indigenous and community knowledge as legitimate epistemic resources that classrooms can build on. Kimmerer (2003) illustrates how Indigenous and Western knowledge systems can converge productively; Bang and Medin (2010) demonstrate that community-based designs help learners navigate multiple ways of knowing; and Sánchez Tapia et al. (2018) show that co ntextualization principles can mobilize community repertoires while supporting disciplinary literacies. Together, these studies establish that honoring non-dominant knowledge systems is both equitable and epistemically productive. National policies in China push for inquiry-based science education and seek to preserve traditional knowledge as part of China's cultural heritage, yet these goals exist in tension. Zhang and Liu (2021) document how the 2016 Benchmark for Scientific Literacy triggered fierce public debate in mainland China over whether yin-yang and five phases theory belonged in science education at all, revealing that scientism remains dominant and makes it structurally difficult for traditional knowledge to be treated as legitimate on its own terms rather than evaluated through Western scientific criteria. Lee (2018) argues that science education should situate scientific knowledge in broader historical and cross-cultural contexts, treating non-Western technological tradi tions as legitimate contributors to science rather than cultural background. Empirically, however, progress remains limited: Chen (2025) demonstrates a recent mainland China example integrating local cultural practices into middle school chemistry, while Kiang and Szeto (2021) show that even when Traditional Chinese Science is taught in Hong Kong undergraduate classrooms, students initially adopted relativist stances that only shifted with explicit epistemological scaffolding. Together, these studies reveal a field where theoretical frameworks for integration exist but classroom designs that treat traditional knowledge as a legitimate epistemology on its own terms, analyze specific student navigation moves, and offer design guidelines transferable to exam-oriented contexts remain scarce. This study addresses that gap.

## Theoretical framework

I frame learning as the productive coordination of multiple ideas and ways of knowing across settings. Knowledge Integration (KI, Linn & Eylon, 2011) provides the instructional backbone of this study. Grounded in sociocultural views of learning, KI treats students' diverse ideas, including those shaped by community practice and lived experience, as productive resources rather than misconceptions to be replaced (Smith et al., 1994).  In this study KI is applied symmetrically: both TCM and Western science ideas are treated as legitimate starting points for integration, neither privileged as the default framework through which the other must be understood. Prior work has applied KI as a design framework in community-relevant science contexts, demonstrating that connecting scientific concepts to locally meaningful issues supports students in building more coherent understanding (Bradford et al., 2023). I extend this line of work to a rural Chinese context where the community knowledge system carries its internal logic and evidence criteria distinct from Western science. Epistemic navigation names the specific student work at the center of my inquiry: deciding what counts as evidence, supporting claims, and reconciling or keeping distinct explanations from different knowledge systems (Hammer & Elby, 2003; Bang & Medin, 2010). Navigation appears in moves like connecting correspondence-based causal reasoning to reductionist causal reasoning, cross-checking claims with another system's criteria, or marking boundaries between accounts for different purposes. Together, KI and epistemic navigation provide complementary analytical lenses: KI frames the instructional architecture that supports learning, while epistemic navigation names the cognitive and social work students do when moving between systems. Research-practice partnership (RPP; Coburn & Penuel, 2016) provides the theoretical commitment underlying how knowledge is produced in this study. Rather than treating community members as research subjects, this RPP positions teachers, TCM doctors, students, and me as co-producers of knowledge, each bringing distinct epistemic resources to the design process (Penuel et al., 2020). This commitment enacts the same principle this study asks of students: that multiple ways of knowing are more powerful in combi nation than in isolation. The specific co-design process is described in the Methods section. The design commitments in this study draw on culturally relevant pedagogy (Ladson-Billings, 1995) and place-based education (Sobel, 2004). Rather than serving as analytical lenses, these frameworks guided curriculum design decisions: anchoring inquiry in a  locally meaningful phenomenon with genuine community participation, and treating rural community knowledge as a legitimate starting point rather than background context. These commitments shaped what I designed and how I structured community participation, and are elaborated in the Unit Design section.

653 Positionality I am a community-insider researcher who grew up in the study region and maintain long-standing relationships with teachers and families from the local communities. This standpoint afforded access, linguistic fluency (local dialect and Mandarin), and sensit ivity to local norms; it also risked assumptions of shared understanding. To mitigate bias, the study used: (1) explicit decision logs for design choices, (2) member checks with teachers and licensed TCM doctors on curriculum wording and interpretation, and (3) collaborative coding with a teacher-participant for student data. Positionality shaped recruitment, participation norms, and reciprocity: the partnership committed to returning a teacher-usable curriculum kit (lessons, facilitation guides, Data Wall protocol, safety/ethics sheet) and supporting the school’s Teaching Achievement Award submission. Research–practice partnership Following research-through-design as conceptualized by Hoadley and Campos (2022), this study moved through four stages: establishing shared goals through foundation workshops, co-designing the unit, enacting and observing the unit in classrooms, and analyzing outcomes to produce transferable design knowledge. This work took place within a three-year RPP (2021-2023) among four partner groups: three 8th-grade science teachers, two licensed TCM doctors, student representatives (rotating pair per class), and the researcher. Although all four partner groups participated in negotiating shared goals, authority was not equally distributed. Teachers and I held more agenda-setting power through our control over alignment with China's national science curriculum standards, while students participated in rotating advisory role with less sustained influence over design decisions.

## Participants and community context

The study site is a public rural middle school that serves nearby villages sharing a regional dialect. The three rural teachers are longtime community residents  with deep knowledge of local practices: Xing (female, 24 years of experience teaching biology and physics), Sun (male, 30 years of experience, 8th grade science department lead), and Jun (male, 18 years of experience teaching chemistry and biology). All teacher names are pseudonyms. The two TCM doctors are from the region's TCM hospital, each with 10+ years of clinical experience in diagnosis, herbal prescribing, and acupuncture, and are actively involved in promoting TCM education in K-12 schools. Forty eighth-grade students completed the TCM pilot unit with teacher Jun. 80% of the students live at the school because of the long distance between the school and their villages; the remaining 20% live nearby. Evidence regarding family use of TCM comes from the pretest question ("Did you or your family use Traditional Chinese Medicine in recent years? Pl ease describe one example and why you chose it."); more than 90% of students reported using TCM herbs such as chrysanthemum tea or honeysuckle, or practices like cupping or gua sha, in recent years.

Co-design process The partnership started with foundation workshops introducing Knowledge Integration and culturally relevant pedagogy (Li & Linn, 2022). Teachers mapped locally meaningful practices to these national standards. Partners negotiated shared goals: teachers emp hasized standards alignment and exam constraints; TCM doctors emphasized epistemic integrity and safety; students emphasized relevance; the researcher emphasized analytic tractability. Partners selected Shanghuo (上火, internal heat) as a common, consequential phenomenon and agreed to focus on how people know within each system rather than adjudicating whether TCM is science. Co-design artifacts made assumptions visible and guided iteration: a Two Ways of Knowing comparison chart (with parallel rows for goal, reasoning, and evidence); a phenomenon map linking community symptoms such as mouth ulcers and sore throat to school sci ence constructs such as inflammation and immune response; safety and ethics guidelines; decision logs; and standards crosswalks. After drafting a three-lesson unit and pretest question, partners piloted a Data Wall activity, revealing students contrasted " slower/fewer side effects" (TCM) with "faster/more side effects" (Western) without criteria. This prompted adding explicit argumentation scaffolds (CER framework, Beyer & Davis, 2006; McNeill et al., 2006) and a Side-Effects Mini-Measure activity. After rehearsal, the unit ran; the researcher observed and archived artifacts. Subsequent meetings refined routines and produced a teacher kit.

Data sources and analysis I collected four types of data: (1) individual written responses to the pretest question ("Did you or your family use Traditional Chinese Medicine in recent years? Please describe one example and why you chose it."); (2) individual 654 written responses to the Wellness Plan task (“Your older brother is preparing for a big exam. He is staying up late every night, feels very stressed, and is eating a lot of spicy instant noodles. Now he has a painful mouth ulcer and a sore throat. How can we help him feel better?”); (3) small-group artifacts (Two Ways of Knowing charts; photographs of Classroom Data Wall entries); and (4) observational records (time-stamped field notes of wholeclass and small-group talk; short teacher debriefs after each lesson). All curriculum materials, instruction plans, and student work were in Mandarin Chinese. As a native Mandarin speaker, I translated student responses and classroom discourse into English for analysis.

To address RQ1, I analyzed students' written responses to the pretest question and the Wellness Plan assessment. The Wellness Plan posed an open-ended scenario (“How can we help him feel better?”) supported by a Mandarin CER worksheet that provided scaffolding language for addressing both TCM and school-science perspectives. This design allowed the KI rubric to capture the full range of student responses, from single-system reasoning to explicit c ross-system integration. To address RQ3, I synthesized across all data sources to derive design guidelines. I treated a guideline as warranted when it was (a) traceable to a specific design decision made during co-design, (b) reflected in classroom enactment documented in field notes, and (c) evidenced in student learning outcomes. This cross-source synthesis follows design-based research conventions for moving from empirical findings to transferable design knowledge (Cobb et al., 2003).

Unit design: “On fire (Shanghuo), two ways of understanding our bodies” The partnership designed a three-lesson unit that treats TCM and school science as complementary systems organized around different aims, reasoning practices, and criteria for judging ideas. Community goals, including youth health, continuity of practice, and recognition of rural expertise, guided the selection of the anchoring phenomenon (Shanghuo) and the products students created. School goals, meaning alignment to national standards and practice with evidence-based explanation, guided the choice of scientific ideas (inflammation, immune response) and routine structures (comparison charts, CER writing). The unit opens with a co-authored Safety and Ethics sheet by TCM doctors reminding students that classroom activities are not medical advice. To counter the long history of framing TCM as “superstition,” Lesson 1 begins with community knowledge as a first-class source. Students interview a family member about Shanghuo (causes, signs, remedies) 655 and bring notes to class. Together, they construct a Classroom Data Wall, a large chart with columns for Causes (diet, sleep, emotions), Signs (e.g., mouth ulcer, sore throat), “Hot” foods, and “Cooling” practices. The teacher’s talk moves (e.g., “Whose ex perience is this from?” “How many households reported this?” “Where do we disagree?”) position rural household knowledge as analyzable, not anecdotal. A 5-minute Herb Spotlight (e.g., chrysanthemum, honeysuckle) uses a simple chart (riddle, plant/morphology, everyday use, food/medicine example) to make community talk specific and safe without endorsing clinical claims. Lesson 2 adds school-science ideas and makes differences explicit without forcing a hierarchy. Across one three-lesson unit with 40 eighth graders, I organize results to address three questions about learning, instructional support, and design guidelines.

RQ1: How do students navigate between TCM and western science during inquiry? Before instruction, all 40 students reported recent TCM use, primarily herbal products such as chrysanthemum tea or honeysuckle (100%) and practices like cupping or gua sha (25%), yet over 80% stated they did not understand how TCM works. Students' justifications fell into two binary categories: authority-based acceptance (45%; "We use it because elders use it") and dismissal as unscientific (38%; "I think TCM is superstitio us. You can't see Qi, so it isn't science"). Only 17% offered causal reasoning grounded in either system's logic, and these were fragmented ("Maybe the herbs have vitamins?"). Over 70% framed TCM as supplementary to "real medicine" (Western medicine). This baseline reveals a gap between use and understanding: students participated in TCM practices but lacked language to position it as anything other than tradition or pseudoscience. By Lesson 3, students demonstrated varied capacity to coordinate systems in the Wellness Plan assessment, which invited students to propose and justify actions informed by TCM and school science for a scenario (older brother with Shanghuo symptoms during exam stress). Knowledge Integration scores distributed as follows: KI-1 (5%, off-topic), KI-2 (20%, vague), KI-3 (45%, single system), KI-4 (25%, both systems connected), KI-5 (5%, explicit differentiation).

KI-3 responses produced targeted reasoning within a single system, grounded in that system's own logic, but without integration across systems. Among the 45% of students who scored KI-3, the majority (30% of the 656 full sample) drew exclusively on TCM reasoning anchored in relational correspondence and balance: "Drink chrysanthemum tea and avoid spicy food to cool the internal fire " (Student 7). These responses invoked TCM's own criteria of restoring balance and regulating heat rather than simply citing family tradition. A smaller proportion (15% of the full sample) drew exclusively on school science, targeting symptoms with reducti onist causal reasoning: "He can also take vitamin C tablets because that helps the immu ne system fight infection " (Student 4). This asymmetry reflects two reinforcing factors: first, the unit was deliberately anchored in TCM, opening with community knowledge as an epistemic resource before introducing school-science frameworks; second, the Data Wall and family interview activities positioned TCM as a legitimate starting point for reasoning. Together, these design choices meant that even students who had not yet developed tools to coordinate across systems were more likely to reason from TCM than to default to Western science's institutional authority. KI-4 responses used sequential layering: proposing complementary actions without reconciling the causal logics of each system: "TCM: Eat less spicy food and drink chrysanthemum tea to cool the body. Science: Disinfect the ulcer with povidone-iodine to reduce bacteria. Using both helps you get better faster" (Student 17). Actions are justified and linked to different goals, but mechanisms remain parallel. KI-5 responses demonstrated purposeful differentiation, explicitly marking goals, evidence types, and temporal scales: "First, adjust the whole body with earlier sleep and less spicy diet to reduce internal heat, because TCM looks at overall balance over time. Then treat the local inflammation with antiseptic and good hygiene, because western science targets the bacteria ri ght now. Patterns and measurements are different kinds of evidence, so using both is safer. One prevents it from happening again, one fixes the current problem" (Student 29). This response shows metacognitive awareness of epistemic criteria, not just content knowledge. Comparing pretest to Wellness Plan responses reveals several patterns. Across all dimensions, responses moved from authority-based and binary toward evidence-grounded and calibrated, with students drawing on class language like “restore balance,” “target bacteria,” and “patterns over time”. RQ2: Which instructional routines supported epistemic navigation? I traced three routines across the unit that consistently appeared in higher-KI student work and classroom discourse: the Data Wall as analyzable evidence, the Two Ways of Knowing chart as a comparative frame, and dual-justification task structure. I analyze how each routine functioned. Routine 1: Data wall as analyzable evidence In Lesson 1, students interviewed family members about Shanghuo (causes, signs, remedies) and posted notes to a large classroom chart (columns: Causes, Signs, "Hot" Foods, "Cooling" Practices). Teacher Jun used specific prompts to position family knowledge as structured data such as "How many families reported chrysanthemum?" (frequency), "Do we see a pattern in what people call 'hot' foods?" (regularity), and "Where do our families disagree?" (variation). These moves treated household reports as having empirical structure: patterns, variation, sample size, without requiring them to fit school-science criteria like controlled variables or measurement.

Two moments illustrate how the teacher maintained epistemic boundaries. First, when Student 12 asked, "Is 'fire' the same as inflammation?" Jun deferred: "Let's hold that question for tomorrow when we compare systems. For now, what do our families mean by fire?" This deferral honored TCM's internal logic by preventing premature translation into school-science vocabulary. Second, when Student 23 proposed, "Maybe 'cooling' foods have vitamin C?" Jun responded, "Interesting hypothesis. That's a school-science mechanism. Tomorrow we'll see if TCM reasoning needs that mechanism or if balance is enough." This exchange shows boundary marking: the teacher acknowledged the idea without collapsing TCM into reductionist explanation. In subsequent work, students cited Data Wall activity directly. For example, Student 17's KI-4 response listed "spicy food" (from Causes column) and "chrysanthemum tea" (from Cooling Practices column) as justifications. The Data Wall made community knowledge reusable as evidence. S29: That's TCM evidence! It’s experience over time. For school science, we'd need to measure something.

The dual-justification scaffolding created the conditions for this negotiation; without it, students would likely have defaulted to school-science-only responses. RQ3: What design guidelines honor community knowledge while advancing disciplinary learning?

Synthesizing across baseline, learning outcomes, and instructional analysis, I derive three design guidelines for curriculum that honors home knowledge while advancing disciplinary learning: Position community knowledge as analyzable data with empirical structure Rather than treating TCM as cultural background or motivational hook, the unit made family practices the starting point for inquiry. The Data Wall organized reports into categories that students later used as evidence in justifications. Teacher moves that asked about frequency, pattern, and variation positioned home knowledge as having the kind of structure that supports reasoning without requiring it to conform to experimental design or quantitative measurement. This guideline addresses a common pitfall in multicultural science education: tokenizing local knowledge as "stories" while reserving analytical work for school science. Use parallel structures to make epistemic differences explicit without imposing hierarchy. The Two Ways of Knowing chart let students see goals, processes, and evidence criteria side-by-side. Crucially, the chart did not ask "Which is better?" but rather "What is each system for? What does each system attend to?" This reframed the question from adjudication (Is TCM science?) to navigation (When and how do I use each well?). Students' uptake of chart language in later writing ("patterns vs. measurements," "balance vs. target") indicates the representation became a durable tool for coordination. Scaffold dual justification in tasks that matter to students' lives The Wellness Plan scenario (helping a stressed sibling with Shanghuo) was personally relevant and scaffolded opportunities for both TCM and school-science reasoning. This task structure prevented default to school science alone and created demand for coord ination moves. Combined with CER scaffolding and safety guardrails (when to seek care), the task supported students to practice navigation in a consequential but bounded context. High-658 stakes exam pressure often pushes teachers toward single-system answers; this guideline shows how to build dualjustification accountability even in constrained settings. This study demonstrates that when curriculum honors Traditional Chinese Medicine as a legitimate knowledge system alongside Western science, rural students engage in sophisticated epistemic work navigating between them. While most students reached KI-3 or KI-4, only 5% demonstrated explicit epistemic differentiation at KI-5, reflecting two constraints: the unit spanned only three lessons, and most students had limited prior experience with CER-structured science writing. Producing written explanations that coordinate claims and evidence across two knowledge systems is a demanding literacy task that likely develops through repeated practice across multiple instructional sequences.

Students bring rich epistemological resources that instruction can organize and make visible. All 40 students participated in TCM practices before instruction, yet lacked institutional language to articulate its reasoning or defend its legitimacy. The shift from binary stances ("TCM is superstitious" vs. "elders use it") to purposeful differentiation ("TCM is for prevention, emergency needs fast treatment") reveals students already understood contextual appropriateness. They needed tools to name and justify what they knew, not to learn it from scratch. The Data Wall, Two Ways chart, and dual-justification tasks created discursive space for students to exercise epistemological sophistication previously rendered invisible by Western-dominant curricula (diSessa, 2014; Hammer & Elby, 2003).

Maintaining epistemological integrity requires resisting premature synthesis. Teacher moves deferring translation ("Hold that question for tomorrow") and sequencing community knowledge first prevented what Bang and Medin (2010) call "epistemological assimilation," the collapse of community knowledge into Western frameworks. When Student 23 proposed "Maybe cooling foods have vitamin C?" Teacher Jun responded: "That's a school-science mechanism. Tomorrow we'll see if TCM reasoning needs that mechanism." He honored TCM as complete on its own terms. The focal group exchange validating grandmother's experience as legitimate TCM evidence while recognizing it operates through different criteria shows students can hold multiple validity standards simultaneously when instruction does not force them to choose. This is epistemological sovereignty in practice: communities' right to have their knowledge systems understood through their own logics. Dual justification is a structural move toward epistemic justice. Without this scaffold, Western science's institutional power makes it the default. The Wellness Plan created genuine opportunity for both systems to contribute, positioning TCM not as "also valid" (tokenizing) but as necessary for adequate response. Student 29's reasoning that "using both is safer, one prevents recurrence, one fixes the current problem" articulates what rural families already practice: neither system alone is sufficient; comp etence means knowing when and how to use each. This challenges multicultural education framings where "inclusion" of non-dominant knowledge is a gift to marginalized students. Excluding community knowledge produces incomplete science learners who cannot reason well about phenomena in their own lives.

These findings have implications beyond rural China. Dominant narratives position rural students as deficient compared to urban peers, yet these findings invert this: rural students possess epistemological resources that Western-dominant curricula render invisible. Globally, students navigate between community knowledge and school science, whether through Indigenous ecological knowledge, locally meaningful cultural practices, or traditional medicine systems like TCM. The question is not "Is traditional know ledge science?" but "How can science education support students to use multiple knowledge systems powerfully?" The shift from adjudication to navigation reframes equity: from asking whether non-dominant students can learn "real" science, to asking whether science education can become accountable to the multiple ways of knowing that students and communities already use to make sense of their worlds. Future work might examine how teachers' own epistemological perspectives shift through sustained co-design, and whether extended units with more CER practice opportunities support students in reaching higher levels of epistemic integration across other culturally meaningful phenomena.
