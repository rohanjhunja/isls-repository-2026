# Tusher Chandra Mondol, James Whyte Effectiveness of School-Based ADHD Interventions: A Meta-Analytic Review

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD) represents one of the most prevalent neurodevelopmental disorders affecting academic achievement and social participation. This meta-analysis synthesizes evidence from N = 23 primary studies (k = 164 group comparisons) on the effectiveness of school-based interventions for children and adolescents with ADHD. A small to moderate overall effect was found (d = 0.31, 95% CI [0.26, 0.37]) with moderate heterogeneity (I² = 40.53%). Moderator analyses showe d that indicated prevention programs yielded stronger effects than selective or universal approaches, and structured educational settings (special or regular schools) were associated with more consistent effects than extracurricular contexts. Interventions with a clear theoretical foundation produced more stable results, and observational or teacher ratings revealed larger effects than self-reports. These findings underscore the value of theory-driven, context-sensitive, and symptom-specific interventions in promoting participation and learning in children with ADHD, while highlighting the need for further large-scale studies to strengthen evidence-based educational practice.

Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD) is a common neurodevelopmental condition characterized by persistent inattention, hyperactivity, and impulsivity that interfere with learning and participation (American Psychiatric Association, 2013). These difficulties are closely linked to impairments in executive functioning, including self-regulation and working memory, which are essential for sustaining attention and goal-directed behavior (Barkley, 2015). Consequently, students with ADHD often experienc e academic difficulties, reduced engagement, and challenges in peer and teacher relationships (Fabiano et al., 2010; Power et al., 2014), highlighting the need for coordinated, evidence-based support within school settings. School-based interventions are typically informed by three complementary theoretical perspectives: (a) behavioral learning theory, which uses reinforcement and contingency management to shape behavior; (b) cognitive models, which target deficits in self-regulation and executive functioning; and (c) social learning approaches, which emphasize modeling and peer interaction. These frameworks are expected to be effective because they directly address core mechanisms underlying ADHD, such as impaired attentional  control and difficulties with goal-directed behavior. Empirical studies reflect this range of approaches. Behavioral and teacher-mediated interventions (Cho & Blair, 2017), self-management strategies (Imasaka et al., 2020), multiprofessional school models (Karhu et al., 2021), show promising but variable effe cts. Despite growing evidence, effect sizes vary considerably depending on factors such as target population, implementation quality, and context. Only few reviews (e.g., Harrison et al., 2019; Bussanich et al., 2026) have systematically examined which intervention characteristics predict stronger outcomes. In this study, prevention levels are defined as universal (delivered to all students), selective (targeting at-risk groups), and indicated (targeting students with identified ADHD symptoms). School-based settings are defined here as interventions delivered within or directly linked to classroom participation, including regular and special education classrooms, school-based services, and structured after-school programs conducted by educational personnel on school premises. This meta-analysis synthesizes 23 studies to estimate the overall effectiveness of school-based ADHD interventions and to identify conditions associated with greater success. Based on prior research (Durlak et al., 2011), we hypothesized that: (H1) interventions would yield positive effects on behavioral, attentional, and learning outcomes; (H2) indicated interventions would show stronger effects than selective and universal programs; (H3) interventions in structured school settings would outperform  extracurricular programs; (H4) theoretically grounded interventions would demonstrate greater effectiveness; and (H5) observational and teacher/parent ratings would yield larger effects than self-reports. Across studies, outcomes included behavioral (e.g., disruptions, off-task behavior, externalizing symptoms), attentional (e.g., inattention, on-task behavior, ADHD symptoms), and academic outcomes (e.g., reading, math, homework, classroom performance). In addition, 1732 some studies assessed social-emotional functioning (e.g., prosocial behavior, peer relations, emotional problems) and functional impairment or organizational skills.

The systematic literature search followed PRISMA guidelines (Page et al., 2021). Searches were conducted via EBSCOhost (ERIC, APA PsycINFO, PSYNDEX, MEDLINE, Education Source, GreenFILE) to identify studies on school-based interventions targeting behavioral outcomes. The present study represents a subgroup analysis within this broader framework.

A two-step search strategy was applied: (1) broad school-related (e.g., “school-based intervention”, “inclusive education”), behavioral (e.g., “behavior problems”, “classroom behavior”), and effectiveness terms, and (2) refinement using specific intervention keywords (e.g., classroom management, behavior modification, emotion-focused and systemic interventions). Peer-reviewed articles (2000–2024) in English, German, or French were included, yielding 1,234 records.

Inclusion criteria comprised quantitative evaluation studies with pre-post designs (with or without follow-up), a comparison group (randomized, quasi-experimental, or controlled single-case), and implementation in school-based settings. Studies included sc hool-aged participants without cognitive impairment and reported behavioral outcomes using standardized measures or observations. Non-empirical, non-school-based, or insufficiently reported studies were excluded.

Screening was supported by Rayyan (Ouzzani et al., 2016). In 45.5% of cases, two reviewers screen ed studies independently (15.7% conflicts were resolved by consensus). After duplicate removal 441 studies underwent full-text review. A supplementary search of German-language programs added 63 studies. The initially high exclusion rate reflects the intentionally broad search strategy (Sedlmeier & Renkewitz, 2018). Full-text screening and data extraction followed a piloted coding protocol (Schlosser et al., 2013; Sedlmeier & Renkewitz,

2018) and were conducted collaboratively. Of 504 full-texts assessed, 212 were in cluded. Inter-rater reliability

was substantial (Cohen’s k = 0.83; Landis & Koch, 1977), with additional checks (PABAK; (Byrt et al., 1993)). ## Data analysis

Effect sizes from all included studies were expressed as Cohen’s d (Cohen, 2013), unless already reported. When necessary, transformations were derived from  reported statistics, such as η², r, or φ coefficients, descriptive data (means and standard deviations), or inferential values (t, F). To ensure comparability, effect sizes were interpreted following Döring (2023): values < 0.20 were considered negligible, 0.20–0.49 small, 0.50–0.79 medium, and ≥ 0.80 large. To correct for small-sample bias, all standardized effects were adjusted using the procedure by Lipsey and Wilson (2001, as cited in Döring, 2023). Effect sizes were weighted by the inverse of their variance, and the overall mean effect was calculated as a weighted average and tested for statistical significance. Heterogeneity was assessed using  Cochran’s Q test and the I² statistic (Higgins, 2003), indicating the proportion of variance attributable to true between-study differences rather than sampling error. As significant heterogeneity was consistently observed, all analyses were conducted using random-effects models, incorporating both within-and between-study variance  (τ²). To examine potential sources of variability, moderator analyses were performed using meta-regression models. These tested whether methodological or contextual study characteristics (e.g., design, intervention type, implementation quality, or setting) were associated with differences in effectiveness. Moderator significance was evaluated using the QM statistic. All analyses were implemented in RStudio with the meta package (Schwarzer et al., 2015).

Prevention level significantly moderated effects (QM (3) = 117.19, p <.001, I² = 45.13%). Indicated interventions showed the strongest effects (d = 0.48, 95% CI [0.36, 0.60]), followed by selective (d = 0.27, 95% CI [0.19, 0.35]) and universal programs (d = 0.25, 95% CI [0.08, 0.41]), indicating greater effectiveness for symptom-specific approaches. Implementation setting also moderated outcomes (QM (5) = 145.23, p <.001, I² = 32.60%). Effects were largest in special school (d = 2.12, 95% CI [−1.06, 5.30]) and mixed settings (d = 1.04, 95% CI [0.69, 1.39]); however, the estimate for special schools was highly variable and not statistically significant. Interventions in regular schools showed moderate effects (d = 0.39, 95% CI [0.29, 0.49]), while extracurricular programs showed smaller effects (d = 0.23, 95% CI [0.16, 0.30]). Overall, results suggest more consistent benefits in structured educational settings compared to extracurricular contexts. Theoretical foundation had a significant moderating effect (QM (2) = 113.64, p  <.001; I² = 41.14%). Theory-based (d = 0.31 (95 % CI [0.25, 0.37]; p <.001)) and non-theory-based interventions (d = 0.32, 95 % CI [0.05, 0.58]; p  =.019) showed similar mean effects, but the latter displayed greater variability, indicating less consistency.  Finally, outcome assessment method further explained variance (QM (3) = 121.39, p <.001; I² = 40.61%). Effects were strongest for observational measures (d = 0.66, 95% CI [0.38, 0.93]; p  <.001), followed by teacher/parent ratings (d = 0.30, 95% CI [0.24, 0.36]; p  <.001), while self-reports were non-significant (d = 0.08, 95% CI [−0.36, 0.53]; p =.716), suggesting higher reliability of external assessments.

This meta-analysis synthesized 23 studies on school-based interventions for children with ADHD. Across comparisons, a small to moderate overall effect emerged (d = 0.31), supporting H1 and confirming that such interventions can improve behavioral, attentional, and learning-related outcomes. This aligns with prior research highlighting the role of school environments in promoting self-regulation and participation (Barkley, 2015; Fabiano et al., 2010), as well as findings by Harrison et al. (2019), who emphas ized the importance of implementation quality and teacher involvement.  Consistent with H2, indicated interventions targeting children with existing symptoms showed the strongest effects, suggesting greater immediate impact of individualized approaches. However, universal programs also yielded significant, albeit smaller, effects, supporting their role within multi-tiered systems of support in fostering classroom climate, peer relationships, and self-regulation. Regarding H3, implementation setting moderated outcomes: interventions delivered in structured school environments achieved higher effects than those in extracurricular contexts, underscoring the importance of contextual alignment. For H4, theoretical foundation  showed more stable effects, with less variability than non-theory-based approaches, suggesting that theoretical grounding enhances consistency. This is consistent with Song et al. (2023), who demonstrated that theory-driven interventions can strengthen executive functioning in children with ADH D. In line with H5, observer and teacher/parent ratings yielded stronger effects than self-reports, likely reflecting more reliable detection of behavioral changes.  Overall, findings indicate that school-based ADHD interventions are most effective when theory-driven, contextually embedded, and symptom-specific, while also benefiting from integration within multi-tiered support systems. Several limitations should be noted. Many studies had small samples or single-case designs, limiting generalizability. Although extreme effect sizes were constrained, they may still have influenced results. Additionally, findings are preliminary and may change as more studies are included. Nonetheless, the results highlight the value of combining targeted and universal approaches within structured, supportive learning environments to enhance participation and self-regulation in children with ADHD.

## Implications for practice

The results highlight several practice-relevant points. First, interventions should prioritize students with ADHD-related difficulties, while maintaining universal, classroom-wide strategies as a preventive foundation. Second, implementation within regular classroom settings appears most effective, underscoring the importance of active teacher involvement. Third, interventions with a clear theoretical foundation enhance consistency and support understanding of change mechanisms. Finally, systematic teacher observation and feedback should be integrated into monitoring, as they most reliably capture progress. Overall, findings emphasize the value of embedding structured, theory-based, teacher-led interventions within everyday practice and integrating universal, selective, and indicated supports within a tiered framework.
