# Bridging the Gap: Using Structured Reflection to Connect Teaching Practice and the Science of Learning

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Teacher education programs often struggle to connect instructional practice with research-based explanations of how learning works. Teacher candidates frequently focus on mastering classroom routines without developing a clear understanding of how those routines influence student learning. At the same time, research from the science of learning is often presented in abstract forms that teachers find difficult to apply in practice. This paper describes a structured reflective framework, DIGPA (Desc ription, Interpretation, Generalization, Planning Action), developed within English language teacher education and later applied in other instructional contexts. We argue that structured reflection can serve as a practical mechanism for connecting instructional practice to research-based explanations of learning within Practice-Based Teacher Education (PBTE).

Teacher education programs often face a challenge: helping teacher candidates connect classroom practice with research-based explanations of learning. In many programs, teacher candidates (TCs) learn instructional techniques through rehearsal and observation, while exposure to research on learning remains largely theoretical. As a result, research knowledge does not always become usable knowledge for classroom decision-making. Practice-Based Teacher Education (PBTE) addresses this challenge by emphasizing t he development of high-leverage instructional practices through modeling, decomposition, and rehearsal (Grossman, 2018). However, critics note that a strong focus on instructional routines may risk reducing teaching to a set of techniques if the principles underlying those practices remain implicit (Zeichner, 2012). The challenge is therefore not simply to teach practices, but to help teacher candidates reason about how those practices affect student learning. This paper describes DIGPA, a structured reflection framework designed to support this kind of reasoning by connecting instructional experience with research-based explanations of learning. Reflective practice and the origins of DIGPA DIGPA emerged from reflective practice traditions within English language teacher education. Drawing on Dewey’s conception of reflection and Rodgers’ (2002) articulation of reflective cycles, teacher educators at the School for International Training (SIT)  developed structured reflection activities in which teacher candidates analyzed lesson segments with peers and trainers. Over time, these practices were refined into a concise framework—Description, Interpretation, Generalization, and Planning Action —that links classroom events to broader principles of learning. DIGPA operationalizes reflection as a structured process of reasoning about the relationship between instructional decisions and student learning. The DIGPA framework DIGPA guides teacher candidates through four stages: ● Description: What happened in a specific lesson segment? ● Interpretation: How did this affect student learning, and why? ● Generalization: Which research-based principles of learning relate to this event? ● Planning Action: What will be adjusted or explored in future lessons? This structure encourages teachers to move beyond global evaluations of lessons toward analyzing how specific instructional moves influence learning processes.

DIGPA within Practice-Based Teacher Education Within PBTE, instructional practices are often analyzed through decomposition, in which complex teaching routines are broken into component parts for study and rehearsal. DIGPA complements this process by extending decomposition to include analysis of learning consequences. By examining how instructional moves influence student learning, DIGPA helps teacher candidates connect classroom routines with explanations of how learning 2533 works. In this sense, DIGPA functions as a reflective scaffold embedded within the practice-based cycle of representation, decomposition, and approximation.

Principles of learning Because research on learning is extensive and often difficult for teachers to access directly, we developed a concise set of Principles of Learning  summarizing findings from cognitive science and educational psychology (Ambrose et al., 2010; Kirschner & Hendrick, 2024; Schwartz et al., 2016; Willingham, 2021). For example: ● Principle 9:  Memory and skill development are strengthened through strategies such as distributed practice, retrieval practice, interleaving, and elaboration. ● Principle 13:  Students require a psychologically safe environment to focus attention and engage productively in learning.

These principles serve as conceptual anchors during DIGPA reflection. DIGPA in practice DIGPA reflection is integrated into teacher training through workshops, micro-teaching, and reflective writing. Teacher candidates analyze segments of lessons they teach and write structured reflections using the DIGPA framework. For example, in one lesson, after a short lecture, a teacher candidate asked students to compare notes with a partner. During the discussion, one student raised a question about a topic not covered in the lecture. The teacher interpreted this interaction as evidence that students may feel more comfortable asking questions in pairs than in whole-class settings. The event was linked to Principle 13 (psychological safety) and Principle 9 (retrieval and elaboration), leading the teacher to plan short pair discussions following lectures in future lessons. Emerging patterns from implementation Across multiple teacher training programs, we observed a shift in how teacher candidates approached reflection. Early reflections often consisted of broad evaluative statements such as “the activity worked well.” Over time, many reflections became more focused on specific instructional events and included clearer cause-and-effect reasoning about student learning. These observations emerged through iterative review of DIGPA essays during feedback and revision cycles rather than formal coding. While preliminary, they suggest that structured reflection may support more principled reasoning about learning.

Mechanism of impact One possible explanation is that DIGPA structures the reasoning process teachers use when reflecting on instruction. By requiring teachers to describe events, interpret their effects on learning, connect them to principles, and plan future actions, the framework may help transform tacit experience into explicit professional knowledge. Rather than simply evaluating whether a lesson was successful, teachers are encouraged to reason about how particular instructional moves influence student learning. Teacher education programs often struggle to connect instructional practice with research-based explanations of learning. DIGPA offers a structured reflective approach that helps teacher candidates analyze the relationship between teaching actions and student learning. Structured reflection can therefore serve as a practical mechanism for operationalizing learning science within practice-based teacher education, supporting the development of teaching that is both skillful and principled.
