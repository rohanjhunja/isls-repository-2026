# “I Thought the Heart Was at the Center”: The Entwined/Entwining Threads of Care

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Challenging the hegemonic practices in STEM that privilege quantitative epistemologies, this paper advocates for a care-centered, relational approach within STEM education. We conceptualize care from a feminist lens and explore care practices in informal STEM learning environments/afterschool programs, aiming to highlight care as a set of relational practices that support positive science experiences. Through a partnership with Daphnia, a non-profit focused on student-centered science education for underserved youth, the study follows ethnographic methods to observe care integration in educational settings. The preliminary findings reveal the co-facilitation between Community Scientists and community center educators, prioritizing relationship-building and fostering students’ autonomy where relationality is valued. We argue that valuing care in STEM education disrupts the traditional power dynamics and contributes to a more equitable and inclusive learning environment.

## Introduction, theoretical framework, and methods

In STEM education research, scholars have emphasize d the importance of culturally responsive practices  and fostering belonging to counter the exclusion of marginalized communities by current hegemonic practices (Vakil & Ayers, 2019). Yet studies often focu s on historically marginalized communities' deficit s rather than their experiences with inequality (Calabrese Barton & Tan, 2009), and how they actively participate in creat ing more inclusive and just STEM spaces. Science in dominant discourse in the US has become naturalized as distinct and “real,” further devaluing relationality and practic es of care. In response, this paper challenges traditional STEM values favoring quantitative epistemologies, advoca ting for a relational approach co-created by adults and youth through care.

Within this paper, we conceptualize care through a “feminist approach, emphasizing care as a set of (ritualized) practices that are part of how people maintain and enhance relationships with political, social, and global implications” (Garcia-Sanchez, p.168). We also conceptualize care by unde rstanding that language is a mediating tool that showcases how children perform care. As García -Sánchez explains, “attention to the interactional–relational aspects of caregiving illuminates the quotidian processes through which care practices are organized, negotiated, and enacted at the intersect ion of the local and global” (176). Viewing care as crucial but often invisible labor for creating belonging, we use various perspectives to explore its role in forming educational spaces, particularly in an informal STEM afterschool program emphasizing belonging. We aim to highlight care's presence in this STEM program and its contribution to fostering a sense of belonging. Observing an aft er-school science program, we identified instances of care beyond traditional STEM scopes and investigated care practices in this context through the following question: What are the practices of care we see in this space? The preliminary findings were collected in the pilo t year of our partnership with Daphnia, a non-profit organization offering inquiry-based, student-centered science programs targeting underserved you th populations whose core value is creating a s ense of belonging. The organization aims to empower and give agency to youth by developing youth voices or empowering young people to express their perspectives and recognition by others of their position as social actors (Gonsalves, 2014; Rahm et al., 2014). The program consists of eight  90-minute lessons exploring animal bodies and the different b ody systems using microscopes and samples. In the study's pilot year, we conducted participant observations and collected ethnographic field notes. Our analysis is not “fully grounded” in the sense that our ways of looking and knowledge of Daphnia philosophies have oriented us toward practices of care. Our study brings the iterative design of lessons and pedagogical strategies together with analysis of learners’ activity and their relationships withi n and across communities to investigate how care se rves to legitimize and (re)position them as members of these spaces in dynamically unfolding interaction that is centered on affirmations, valuing personal lived experiences, and relational experiences.

## Preliminary findings

Despite not building the curriculum together, through their combined facilitation, the  Daphnia Community Scientists and community center educators interpret ed the  Daphnia curriculum anew. R, the lead Daphnia Community Scientist within the program, led programming and delegated responsibilities to his co-facilitator and volunteers. Ms. C, the community center educator wh ose room attended Daphnia programming each week, mediated between the curricula and youth participants. Across introductions, transitions, and activities, R and Ms. C were found side-by-side with their bodies angled toward learners, affirming one another’s words with additional anecdotes or head nods. Through their emergent co-facilitation of Daphnia programming, R and Ms. C modeled how educators may work together within a science learning environment without competing for physical or verbal space.

Regardless of the time allocated for an activity, t here appeared to be no urgency during these moments, illustrating Daphnia’s priority of building and deepening relationships th rough conversation between adult and youth participants alike. Equal amounts of time, if  not more, were allocated for youth participants to  write and share their reflections during sessions at Daphnia. As youth participants verbalized their ideas, eac h adult positioned themselves toward each speaker, asked fo llow-up questions, and engaged surrounding participants in their discussions. Throughout these interactions, a dults offered affirmations that focused on youths’ knowledge shared and lived experiences with the curriculum rather than the praise of memorization or replication of activities. The phrase “I see how far you’ve come” was used, in  particular, to dually acknowledge a participant’s current work and name the growth witnessed by surrounding adults over time. The flexible structure of Daphnia programming encouraged participants to lead their o wn exploration, engaging with materials and adults alike at their o wn pace.Towards the end of each session, adult faci litators introduced station activities with minimal instruct ions and no set amount of time to move through each  area. As participants moved about the space, adults position ed themselves in an area only after two or more you th participants were present. They would observe and only join an activity after participants angled towards them or asked them a question. During a session dedicated t o hearts, participants used stethoscopes at one sta tion to find each other’s heartbeats. One youth participant, K, told Community Scientist, J, “I thought the heart w as at the center.” J then invited K to try listening to the left side of  their chest underneath their collarbone. Though K did not initially locate the heartbeat, she was not penalized. Instead, she offered a check for understanding, which led to a conversation with an adult facilitator and the space to try again together.

## Discussion

Our preliminary findings build on previous research  in STEM education contexts by attending to underex plored aspects of care practices in STEM education in form ing positive science experiences. By valuing childr en’s autonomy, we disrupt the view of youth a s sole receivers of care and instead also see them as caregivers that foster a STEM learning environment where relational ity is valued. Our preliminary findings show how ca re practices contribute to a more equitable and inclus ive learning environment where  relationships and lived experience are honored and disrupt traditional positivist centered STEM environments.
