# The Process of Transforming Interdisciplinarity Perception Through Research Activities in an Interdisciplinary Department

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study examines how students' perceptions of interdisciplinarity transform through research activities in an interdisciplinary department. Through qualitative analysis of seven master’s degree students using the Trajectory Equifinality Approach (TEA), we identified two patterns of transformation: interdisciplinarity as strategic boundary-crossing while maintaining disciplinary depth, and as a paradoxical process where interdisciplinary engagement enhances disciplinary expertise. The results reveal that these transformations occur as students expand their networks of academic actors, deepening their understanding of disciplinary positioning. The findings suggest the importance of supporting students in repositioning their research questions across multiple disciplinary frameworks, contributing to our understanding of interdisciplinary thinking development.

## Introduction

Interdisciplinary higher education aims to develop boundary-crossing skills, such as interdisciplinary thinking. (Spelt et al., 2009). Boix Mansilla & Duraisingh (2007) define interdisciplinary thinking as " the capacity to integrate knowledge and modes of thinking in two or more disciplines or established areas of expertise to produce a cognitive advancement—such as explaining a phenomenon, solving a problem, or creating a product—in ways that would have been impossible or unlikely through single disciplinary means (p.219)." Interdisciplinary thinking comprises five essential components (Spelt et al., 2009): knowledge (knowledge of disciplines, knowledge of disciplinary paradigms, and knowledge of interdisciplinarity) and skills (higher-order cognitive skills  and communication skills).

Previous research has empirically demonstrated the significance of metacognition and epistemology in interdisciplinary thinking. For instance, Ivanitskaya  et al.  (2002) established that interdisciplinary learning contributes to the enhancement of students' epistem ological beliefs, critical thinking abilities, and metacognitive skills. Epistemics, the disciplinary ideas about what knowl edge is and how to use and produce knowledge, are part of the culture of a discipline  (Bradbeer, 1999). Consequently, the developmen t of interdisciplinary thinking appears to be contingent upon one's perception of m ultiple academic disciplines. However, Oudenampsen et al.

(2023) highlight a critical challenge: disparate disciplinary perceptions among students from different fields may

impede interdisciplinary collaboration, suggesting that learners have not yet achieved internal integr ation of multiple disciplinary perspectives.

Given these considerations, this study focuses on r esearch activities where perceptions of multiple academic disciplines are formed, particularly throu gh engagement. As global challenges become increasingly complex, the importance of interdisciplinary research in higher education has gained growing recognition. Within this context, integrating interdisciplinary undergr aduate research into bachelor's degree curricula ha s attracted significant attention (Sengupta et al., 2022). Bettencourt et al. (2023) studied STEM undergraduate students a nd found that their perception of interdisciplinarity evolved from viewing it as an extension of their own multi-faceted academic interests tonegotiating through both disc iplinary and interdisciplinary work and subsequent opportunities and challenges. H owever, the specific factors driving this transform ation in interdisciplinary perception through research activities have yet to be elucidated. This study examines the transformation of students'  perceptions of interdisciplinarity through researc h activities. The theoretical framework employed is A ctor-Network Theory (ANT) (e.g., Latour, 1987), which enables analysis of interactions among various actors (researchers, literature, theories, etc.) in research activities, with a particular focus on the formation of percept ions across multiple academic disciplines. Within t his framework, this study specifically focuses on the c oncept of translation in A NT (Callon, 1984). Callon (1984) conceptualizes the process of transformation in the  meaning and roles of different actors through tran slation, which consists of four stages: (1) Problematization, (2) Interess ement, (3) Enrolment, and (4) Mobilization. Accordingly, this study aims to elucidate how stude nts' perceptions of interdisciplinarity transform t hrough research activities in an interdisciplinary department. To achieve this aim, we established the following  research questions: H ow do students' perceptions of interdisciplinarity transf orm through  research activities in an interdisciplinary department?

## Participants

Our study focused on seven students from University  P's interdisciplinary department who completed the ir graduation research  (Capstone) and proceeded to master’s degree. The target department emphasizes Human Sciences and aims to integrate insights from multiple academic disciplines. The Capstone at our target university is characterized by three key elements: (1) it represents the culmination of four years of learning and research, (2) all students participate in small-group seminars from their third year through gradua tion research in their fourth year, and (3) it aims to promote inquiry that trans cends traditional academic disciplines to address c omplex contemporary societal issues.  In our study, all seven were chosen through purposi ve sampling: we recruited students who had completed their Capstone in this i nterdisciplinary department and continued onto the master’s program, thereby providing a longitudinal perspective on their academic trajectories.

## Procedure

We conducted two longitudinal semi-structured interviews, each lasting approximately 120 minutes,  in March 2022 and October 2023. These semi-structured interviews covered the process from the start of graduation research through research activities in graduate st udies. The interviews focused on six key topics: re asons for enrolling in the department, course selection ratio nale, seminar selection criteria, graduation resear ch theme selection, graduate research theme selection, and interdisciplinarity in their research. Analysis To capture the longitudinal and context-dependent nature of how interdisciplinarity percept ions transform, we employed the Trajectory Equifinality Approach (TEA) (Sato et al. 2016). TEA consists of Trajectory Equifinality Modeling (TEM) and Three Layers Model of Genesis  (TLMG). Based on cultural psychology (Valsiner, 2016), TEM identifies specific points as Equifinality Points (EFP) and maps multiple pathways leading to these po ints, revealing why particular paths were chosen at crucial junctures. Key TEA concepts include:

• Bifurcation Points (BFPs): Moments where participan ts could choose among multiple paths (e.g.,

focusing on a single discipline vs. integrating multiple disciplines).

• Obligatory Passage Points (OPPs): Events or conditions that all participants must navigate (e.g., selecting

a seminar, completing a Capstone).

• Social Guidance (SG): Forces nudging individuals to ward the EFP (e.g., supportive feedback,

mentorship).

• Social Direction (SD): Forces that create tension o r pull individuals away from certain paths (e.g.,

difficulty in communicating one’s research to broader audiences). TLMG analyzes the interplay between core values/beliefs (Layer 3), internalized frameworks (Layer 2), and observable behaviors (Layer 1), revealing not only what choices students made but also why they made them. This approach complements our Actor-Network Theory framework by helping trace how various actors influence the translation process in developing interdisciplinary perceptions, documenting moments when interactions with supervisors, literature, theories, or research findings catalyzed shifts in students' understanding.

Our analysis identified two main patterns of interdisciplinary perception transformation:

1. Interdisciplinarity as Strategic Boundary-Crossing: Some students utilized interdisciplinary resources to

deepen their primary discipline while strategically incorporating insights from other fields.

2. Interdisciplinarity as a Paradoxical Process: Other s found that engaging with interdisciplinary

perspectives enhanced their expertise in their primary field, reinforcing disciplinary depth. Additionally, the final bifurcation into two types of interdisciplinary perceptions among participants appeared to be influenced by their external and int ernal orientations and the resulting relationships with various actors (e.g., courses, research labora tories, supervisors). Consequently, these participants seemed to transform their perceptions of interdisciplinarity by partici pating in research groups from other disciplines ou tside their laboratories while following their own research interests rather than being primarily guided by actors such as their research laboratories or supervisors.

## Discussion

This study investigated how students in an interdis ciplinary department transform their perceptions of interdisciplinarity through research activities. Our findings reveal two distinct pathways of tran sformation in interdisciplinary perception. The first views inter disciplinarity as an act of transcending disciplina ry boundaries while maintaining deep disciplinary expertise, aligning with Klein's (2010) concept of "bridge building" between disciplines. The second perceives interdisciplinari ty as a paradoxical process where engaging with mul tiple disciplines enhances one's disciplinary expertise, extending Bettencourt et al.'s (2023) finding that students primarily view interdisciplinarity as bringing mult iple disciplinary perspectives into conversation ra ther than transcending disciplines entirely.

The results of this study reveal that students' transformation of interdisciplinarity undergoes a fundamental change through research activities. While Papendieck & Clarke (2024) showed how scientific articles as boundary objects facilitate interdisciplinary le arning, this study reveals that students' perception of interdisciplinarity transforms as they expand their network of academic actors, including scientific articles, and deepen their understanding of the positioning of their field of expertise. This process can be effectively interpreted through Latour's (1987) concept of “ translation.” As students research literature, participate in a cademic communities, and expand their research networks, they actively reconstruct their repositioning of interdisciplinary research through what Latour calls “translation.” The findings suggest the importance of supporting s tudents in repositioning their research questions across multiple disciplinary frameworks, contributi ng to our understanding of the development of interdisciplinary thinking. Regarding the support for the development of interdisciplinary thinking, mentoring by supervisors at bifurcation points appears to be eff ective in helping students maintain balance —neither overly focusing on a single discipline nor becoming excessively diffuse across multiple fields. However, while our study focused on students who proceeded to master's degre es within a single institutional context, potentially limiting generalizability, we should note another limitation. Our participants predominantly engaged in psychol ogical approaches in their research. This disciplinary ten dency may have influenced how they made sense of their interdisciplinary experiences. Despite these limitations, this study makes a valuable contribution to understanding how interdisciplinary researchers develop their understanding of interdisciplinarity through research activities.
