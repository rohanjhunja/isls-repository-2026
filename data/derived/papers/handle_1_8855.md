# Locating as a Temporally-Oriented Practice for Noticing Data for Improvement

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study examines how practitioners look at and make sense of improvement data through the lens of noticing. Conceptualizing noticing as a practice performed through talk, we examined data conversations in a teacher preparation networked improvement community and found that practitioners engaged in the noticing practice of locating data. In particular, we found that practitioners’ noticing practice of locating was shaped by their temporal orientations, where attention to past, present, and/or future guided where and how they placed data within their own contexts and settings. We also found that practitioners located data either under their own or others’ purviews or within the experiences of their students. One contribution theorizing noticing as a practice and applying noticing to improvement data as a material resource. Attending to how practitioners notice data can generate insight into the kinds of noticing practices that produce productive insight from improvement data.

## Introduction

Networked improvement communities bring practitioners together around shared problems of practice to generate solutions that are usable in local contexts. In the context of improvement, the collection and analysis of data serves an important role. Unlike data for research that seeks to answer questions to advance a scholarly knowledge base, data for improvement intends to develop usable measures that provide real-time feedback that enable practitioners to take action (Bryk et al., 2015; Shah, 2019). Whe n using data for improvement, the goal is to create  a small number of measures that can be collected frequently and shared in data displays to allow the network to examine if it is improving over time and enable decisions t o be made about how to improve. We focus on data us e for improvement because of the increased interest in education research to examine how practitioners (e.g. leaders of schools, districts, and other educational organizations) can engage in systematic inquiry into the performance of their systems (Coburn & Stein, 2010; Datnow & Park, 2014). While data plays a central role in the improvement process and has emerged as a tool for facilitating improvement, we know little about how practitioners  look at and make sense of data in the context of improvemen t efforts. In this study, we seek to understand how practitioners use data for improvement by conceptua lizing the sensemaking of data as being expressed t hrough noticing practices. The construct of noticing has been taken up to understand how teacher educators attend to and make sense of material resources (Amador, 2021; Kazemi et al., 2010). Traditionally, noticing has been taken up as a cognitive construct to examine how teachers look at and make sense of classroom video data (Sherin & Russ, 2015). We bring a noticing lens to help us understa nd how practitioners use improvement data, a different kind of material resource than classroom video. Additionally, we conceptualize noticing as a practice that is performed through action, of which talk is one kind. By exami ning practitioners’ noticing practice, we focus our study on how practitioners engage with data, as opposed to what practitioners notice about data. Our study takes place in the context of a teacher p reparation networked improvement community.  We study data use for improvement in this context for a few reasons. First, research in teacher education  is often isolated to one course, in one context, limiting the field’s ability to make progress in understanding whether and how particular pedagogies of teacher preparation prepare candidates for teaching (Cochran-Smith et al., 2015). In addition, current measures of teacher education are often used to make summative evaluations of program quality, such as meeting state credentialing or accreditation requirements (Bastian, 2018; Peck & McDonald, 2013). Such measures do not capture the process by which teache r education programs are designed over time to prep are candidates on particular aims.. Therefore, we know very little about how the organization of a program  and the sequence of coursework and field experiences prepare candidates to learn the practice of teaching. Though there is limited research on teacher education program data use, the limited body of work shows promise for using data to improve. Prior research on data use in teacher preparation shows how teacher education program stakeholders can use summative data, namely teaching performance assessments, to generate insight into program quality. For example, Peck and McDonald (20 13) highlight how one program successfully used the se summative data to learn about program quality via teacher education program staff’s participation in scoring their candidates’ teaching performance assessment portfolios. Further, Davis and Peck (2020), examined the v arious organizational practices that teacher education programs engaged i n that enabled regular and productive data ICLS2022 Proceedings 67 © ISLS conversations. The authors found that programs that were successful in using data for program improvement held regular meeting times to discuss data and integrated data conversations into existing routines. While this line of research offers insight into the organization of da ta use conversations, questions remain about  how t eacher educators look at and make sense of data-what do they focus on, how do they engage with data, and how does their engagement with data inform improvement effor ts. In this paper, we begin to explore these questi ons by illuminating teacher educators’ noticing practices to understand how teacher educators look at and make sense of improvement data. To do this, we focus on one notic ing practice that emerged as a dominant practice in  our analysis: the noticing practice of locating. We illuminate how studying this particular noticing practice a) enabled teacher educators to make sense of data, and b) revealed the different places they located data.

## Theoretical Framework

We draw on two broad lines of research to frame our  inquiry into how teacher educators work with and m ake sense of data: noticing and practice theory. Together, these bodies of literature situate teacher educators’ data use as a practice they enact to both attend to and reason about the problem of practice and the enactment of solutions. We elaborate on each framework below.

The construct of noticing captures what educators attend to, how they understand what they see, and the broad range of decisions they consider that inform the actions they take in practice (see Blomeke et al., 2015). In the context of teaching, researchers have sought to  understand the nature of teacher noticing and how it can be developed to support more responsive, equitable instructional practice (Sherin et al., 2010; Schack et al., 2017). As this work has developed, researchers have argued for the active nature of teacher noticing. For example, van Es and Sherin (2021) propose a framework that defin es teachers’ noticing as three interrelated actions  teachers take to gain deeper insight into student thinking i n the context of classroom instruction. This is con sistent with others who explain that noticing is driven by the activity system of which teachers are a part, where teachers are attending to and making sense of phenomena that is guiding their decision-making and thus provides new experiences for noticing (Erickson, 2011; Hand, 201 2). Together, this line of research suggests that n oticing is not passively observing, but rather, entails active ly seeking out information and trying to understand  the phenomenon under one’s purview. We extend this rese arch to propose that noticing is a practice that is  being performed moment to moment through action, primaril y discursive practices. Rather than conceptualizing noticing as a cognitive process, we position notici ng as an action that is shaped by activity systems and in turn (re)shapes those activity systems.

To understand noticing as a practice, we draw on practice theory. Practice theory conceptualizes practice as simultaneously situated and situating action, where actions produce context and context constrains and enables action (Feldman & Orlikowski, 2011). We used practice theory to see how teacher educators performed noticing of improvement data in data conversations through talk. In our examination of how teacher educators lo oked at improvement data, the practice of locating emerged, where teacher educators placed data in te mporally diverse ways. To understand this temporality in teacher educators’ locating of data, we turned to Emirbayer and Mische’s

(1998) conception of agency to understand how teach er educators were temporally oriented as they engaged in

locating data. Emirbayer and Mische view agency as a “process of social engagement” that is informed b y orientations to the past, present, and future (p. 9 63). For Emirbayer and Mische, actions and the capa city to act are simultaneously influenced by past, present, and future, where actors may be more or less oriented to one over others at any given time. This view of agency helped us understand and see the varied ways temporality shaped teacher educators’ data-locating practice.

## Study Context and Research Methods

The study took place in the context of a broader re search effort to understand the design and improvem ent of teacher education (cterin.ucop.edu). We developed a  networked improvement community (NIC) consisting o f eight teacher education programs, who are all part of a larger university system. The directors of these programs come together twice a year to share efforts to respond to state-wide policy mandates, to discuss local problems of practice and seek out support and advice on these i ssues, and to develop strategies to advocate for su pport for teacher preparation at their local campuses, across the university system, and within the state legislature. Because of their shared commitment to understanding and imp roving teacher preparation, this group became the s ite for launching a NIC.

The improvement project that is the focus of the s tudy includes nine campuses in the NIC who collectively identified the preparation of candidat es to support multilingual learners as the shared p roblem of practice (see Sandoval & van Es, 2021 for a description of launching the NIC). The measurement system the NIC developed included a survey consisting of 15 items that were both quantitative and qualitative in natu re. These items asked questions about whether candidates learned about their multilingual students’ backgrounds; whether ICLS2022 Proceedings 68 © ISLS candidates considered multilingual students’ experi ences and backgrounds in their instruction; and whe ther candidates navigated school systems to advocate for their multilingual students. The network established a routine for making sense of these data across the network and within campuses. This routine included teacher educators administering the data to teacher candidates in the ir program coursework; improvement facilitators gen erating data displays from the data, typically in the form of line charts that showed the percent of candidate s who said they engaged in particular activities in their placements (see Figure 1); and having teacher educators review and reflect on the displays.

Additionally, data includes artifacts that were generated for and from these conversations, such as data displays, spreadsheets where data were stored and manipulated, drafts of a nd notes on the survey, and our memos about these d ata conversations that we generated in the moment. For this analysis, we selected six data conversations across five different teacher preparation programs and four dif ferent campuses over three academic years. Collecti vely, 12 teacher educators participated in these data conversations. We chose these six data conversations from the 17 data conversations we conducted in this network because they represented a variety of campuses, programs, a nd moments in time.

Data analysis was qualitative in nature. Informed b y interaction analysis methodology (Jordan & Henderson, 1995) and research on noticing (van Es & Sherin, 2008; Sherin et al., 2011), we started by having the three authors collectively analyze one data convers ation together, where we each highlighted what teac her educators noted in the data-what they thought was interesting or noteworthy in the data displays and in candidates’ qualitative responses-as well as how they interpreted what they saw. As we reviewed the data, we observed that the teacher educators were engaging with the data displays in different ways, such as making requests to compare the displays to ones they viewed previously and tal king with each other about how the displays reflect ed what was happening in their programs at different points in time. We then added an additional focus  for ou r analysis that included capturing how teacher educators interacted with the data. We then viewed a second data conversation together, each capturing what teacher educators hig hlighted as noteworthy, how they reasoned about the se highlighted elements of the data and how they inter acted with each other and with the data. We each ge nerated analytic memos that captured the noteworthy ways they engaged with the data and summarized key observations and insights they generated from the analysis of data. We then compared our individual memos and discussed any discrepancies in our analyses. Using this same process, we each analyzed one additional data conversation—with the exception of the first author, who analyzed two conversations—along the three broad categories, generating analytic memos for each one. We then conducted a cross case discussion, among all the conversations we analyzed. At this point, we identified the practice of locating as something that all the teacher educators were doing as they ICLS2022 Proceedings 69 © ISLS interacted with the data. In the final round of ana lysis, we returned to the data to generate evidence  for locating and to characterize the various ways teachers engaged in this noticing practice in data conversations. We now turn to present our findings.

## Findings

Examining teacher educators’ noticing practices rev ealed that teacher educators engaged in the practic e of locating. Locating refers to how teacher educators’ situated improvement data within a range of contexts to make sense of it. TEs located improvement data within their own program and within their own or others’ purview. This practice of locating improvement data within varied  contexts was also influenced by teacher educators’  varied temporal orientations, where some TEs were more ori ented towards the past while others were more orien ted towards the future or present.

We begin by describing how teacher educators locat ed improvement data within their teacher preparation programs, such as program structures and activities in which teacher candidates engaged. We surface a series of exchanges in February 2020 among teacher educators at a teacher preparation program around improvement data from an item that read: “In the past week, I incorp orated some aspect of my multilingual students’ lan guages, communities, or families into an activity or lesson.” The data display had shown that 29 percent of candidates had responded affirmatively (“Yes, in my fieldwork” or “Yes, in my coursework and fieldwork”) in October 2019; 60 percent responded affirmatively in November 2019; a nd 68 percent responded affirmatively in February 2 020. It went from 29 to 60 percent, which is a huge jump, and then it went to 68 percent. Oscar: They're also taking two language courses. During that time during the fall, they’re taking the Language for the Chicano Child [course]. And th en in the winter quarter, they’re taking a course on [English Language Development].

Rachel: Well, and then for us, there also may have been the pulse-off thing going on. So if it was given in October in the second week of a pulse-off, then anyone who was pulsing off would say no. If given in the first week of a pulse-off, those people would have been in their classrooms the prior week. This has to do with “in the past we ek” language. Both Rachel and Oscar located the data within what program activities and structures candidates were experiencing as they completed the survey, evidence for what we argue is a pronounced orientation to t he past. For example, when asked about the changes in data f rom October to November and February, Rachel points  to the “pulse-off” program structure; TEs would later explain that “pulse-off” referred to a routine that  occurred throughout the winter and spring quarters where candidates worked in their student-teaching placements for two weeks before taking a two-week break. Rachel’s locating of data was influenced by her orientation to past program structures, evidenced by her recollection of what candidates were experiencing at the time that may have influenced the data. TEs’ locating of improvement data within program s tructures and activities were not always influenced by an orientation to the past, however. TEs also located data within program activities and structures in ways that were influenced by a closer attention to the present and future. Oscar and Maria located the data within program str uctures and activities—in this case, coursework, re sident teachers, and student-teaching placements—but with more of an orientation to present and future than past as was surfaced in the previous exchanges. This time, Osca r and Maria were imagining how candidates might respond and generating ideas for new follow-up items to understand what program structures were influencing how they might respond. We note that, in alignment with Emirbayer and Mische (1998), Oscar and Maria’s exchanges were at once temporally oriented to the past, present, and future. However, we argue that their exchanges are evidence of a more resonant orientation to a) the future, where TEs are imagining the possibilities for candidates’ responses, and b) the present, where TEs are generating ideas for items that can be added to the survey to gain i nsight into candidates’ responses. In this meeting, TEs engaged  in the practice of locating primarily to situate d ata within program activities, and their temporal orientation shaped how they made sense of data and the ways in which they responded to data. How TEs located data within prog ram activities and structures changed drastically b ased on whether they were oriented more towards past, prese nt, or future. In this case, TEs moved from finding explanations for why improvement data looked the way they do to ideating new items to gather more information about improvement data.

We turn to describe how TEs located data within th eir own or others’ purview, situating improvement data as someone’s responsibility to look at and res pond. We argue that this particular way of locating  was also influenced by TEs’ orientations to past, present, and future. We illustrate this first by examining segments from a data conversation with Alicia, a teacher educator primarily responsible for leading the single-subject program at her campus. During this conversation, Alicia located the data under her own purview, viewing data as something she was responsible for and could control. In April of 2021, Alicia reviewed improvement data and noted that 45 percent of candidates responded affirmatively to th e item “In the past week, I incorporated some aspec t of my multilingual students’ languages, communities, or f amilies into an activity or lesson,” remarking that  she “expected more” candidates to respond affirmatively. Following this remark, Acacia began to generate ideas for how she wanted to respond to the data and landed on changing a form teacher candidates are required to use and complete when preparing for observations from supervisors. And so, because they have 12 observations, this is something that I could change. I can add, you know something about, “How do you incorporate? Like, what was the question? I can add, “I am incorporating some aspect of my multilingual students’ languages, communities, or families by doing the following.” Alicia’s comment that the “long term goals are just not working with me” and that she asked herself what she can “do now” suggest that Alicia’s temporal orientation foregrounds the present by locating the improvement data as something she is capable of acting on and responding to “immediately.” Her focus on “something that [she] could change” was evidence that she located improvement data by placing it under her purview as something to which she ought to respond. We also argue that Alicia’s o rientation to the future and past were simultaneous ly influencing how she located improvement data. Alici a’s comment that changes do not “have to be voted o n by the steering committee,” and that she could make these changes immediately suggest that Alicia was attending to the future by imagining the trajectory of a “change” would be, while drawing on her past experiences with the process of making changes in the program. While Ali cia was at once simultaneously oriented towards the  past, present, and future, we viewed her comment as havin g a tone that was more aligned with a present orien tation, with an emphasis on making a decision about what she cando immediately. Janice’s comment that she did not “see myself doing that in [my inquiry class]” illustrates how she located improvement data as something over which she had no control. For Janice, data from this item is relevant to “other courses” and not so mething she would talk about in her course. We view ed these exchanges as being influenced by Janice’s pronounced orientation to the past, drawing on her past experiences of what her class does and does not emphasize. By orienting to how her class on inquiry has been conducted, Janice is locating data on candidates’ reflection on the enforcement of Standard English outside of her purview. While Janice is locating specific improvement data outside of her own purview, we are not making the claim that Janice is absolving herself of responsibility for the data  broadly. In these exchanges, Janice mentions that the topic of enforcing the use of Standard English would be a “huge” point of discussion in her teacher preparation program course on math methods. Additionally, in prior exchanges during this meeting, Janice reflected back on whether she led any activities related to the items and won dered why she did or did not do so, indicating she viewed improvement data as something for which she holds s ome responsibility. What we argue, instead, is that  TEs engaging in the noticing practice of locating improvement data served to place data within some people’s purview at particular times, and thus shaped how they looked at and responded to improvement data.

Our findings illuminate how teacher educators perf ormed noticing of improvement data and focused on one specific noticing practice that emerged as prominent as TEs looked at improvement data: locating. Teacher educators engaged in the practice of noticing in a range of ways, namely locating improvement data wit hin program structures and activities and locating data under some teacher educator’s purview and outside of others’ purview. Additionally, we argue that the practice of locating data was influenced by the temporal orientations of teacher educators, where orientations to the past, present, and future shaped where and how teacher ed ucators located data and what teacher educators did once they located data. Having illuminated how TEs located data and how that was influenced by TEs’ orientation to past, present, and future, we turn to discuss implications for teacher preparation and improvement data use.

## Discussion and Conclusion

This study is significant in three ways. First, our study stretches conceptualizations of noticing by viewing it as a practice that is performed through discursive moves and in relation to artifacts under one’s purview. Second, we contend that examining how practitioners notice data can enable those leading and studying improvement sciencedriven efforts to examine how data conversations ca n be led to engage in noticing practices that are p roductive ICLS2022 Proceedings 72 © ISLS for doing improvement. Finally, our study examines the use of data for improvement in teacher preparat ion, a field where data use, and improvement data use in particular, are not well-understood.

Our findings build on prior research to propose tha t noticing is not only a cognitive process, but als o a practice that is enacted as educators work with and  analyze artifacts of practice. Elsewhere, van Es a nd Sherin

(2021) describe how teachers actively work with stu dents’ ideas to shape interactions in order to gain  deeper

insight into students’ mathematical thinking. In do ing so, they position noticing as a process that is  mediated through interaction in classroom activity. Here, we  extend this research to conceptualize noticing as a practice that is performed, through talk, as teacher educators interact with artifacts of practice, in this case, data displays. By characterizing the ways teacher educators enact locating we see that noticing not only entails what and how individuals and communities attend to and reason representations of practices (e.g. video, student work) but also involves how individuals and communities interact with these artifacts in order to attend to and understand their meaning. By shifting our lens to how teacher educat ors engage with artifacts, we begin to expand theor y of construct of noticing in educational contexts.

We also contribute to improvement-focused research  by examining how practitioners make sense of and use improvement data. Although there exists numerous studies examining practitioners’ data use and sensemaking (e.g., Datnow & Hubbard, 2015; Horn et al., 2015), our study focuses on how practitioners use data des igned specifically for continuous improvement and situated within an improvement effort. Because improvement data differ from data for research or accountability (Bryk et al., 2015), we argue that understanding how practitioners make sense of improvement data is critical for enab ling productive data conversations. Unpacking the n oticing practice of locating helped to unveil how and where practitioners place data temporally and specific to their own contexts and settings. Understanding how teacher educators located data helped us to see that improvement data were enabling particular acts of locating. For example, for some teacher educators, reviewing improvement data enabled them to locate the data as being something for which they are responsible for, while enabling other teacher educators to locate data as someone else’s responsibility. We argue that attending to the varied performances of locating can help those leading improvement efforts design data conversation activities and protocols that enable certain kinds of locating.

Finally, our study adds to a small but growing bod y of research on data use in teacher preparation. T o date, very few studies have examined the use and sensemaking of data in the context of teacher preparation. Those that do examine data use in teacher preparation foc us on how teacher preparation programs can organize themselves to enable the collection and use of data  for program improvement (e.g., Davis & Peck, 2020). Our study gets inside data conversations to reveal how teacher educators engaged in the noticing practice of locating to situate improvement data within program activities, coursework, student-teaching placements, and instruction. Unveiling the varied ways that teacher educators lo cated improvement data help reveal teacher educator s’ conjectures about what program activities are pertinent to improvement efforts. Additionally, understanding how teacher educators make sense of improvement data can help to conceptualize efforts to make improvement data more useful relative to specific improvement efforts.

We conclude by arguing for closer examinations of how practitioners notice improvement data. We argue that doing so can enable those leading improvement efforts to examine how they organize improvement da ta conversations to make them more productive. Taking a noticing lens enables improvement-focused researchers to get inside data conversations to inform how they  organize data-driven improvement efforts. We argue  that examining how data conversations unfold is a critical step in improving how improvement work is carried out.
