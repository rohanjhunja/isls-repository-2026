# Clustering Cognitive Engagement Changes in Longitudinally Traced Discussion Data From a Graduate-Level Online Course

**Conference:** ISLS 2023

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Given the pervasive use of online learning, especially asynchronous discussion forums, ensuring students’ ongoing engagement is critical. Examining students’ cognitive presence throughout a series of discussions candisclose different engagement patterns. However, little research has leveraged longitudinal discussion data. The current study aims to identify learner engagement states and their trajectories in discussion forums using two-cohort data from a graduate-level online course. We used HMM (Hidden Markov model) to discover the hidden longitudinal engagement clusters. Results showed two clusters in the first cohort and three clusters in the second cohort. Students tended to remain in a similar engagement state, and their participation patterns depended on the discussion dynamics likely influenced by class size.

## Introduction

Computer-supported collaborative learning (CSCL) studies focus on a process of  online collaboration to build shareable knowledge through learners’ meaningful conversations in a learning community  (Kim et al., 2021; Carvalho & Santos, 2022). Students’ active participation in discussions is critical to achieving learning goals. We can ensure effective online collaboration by the design improvement based on the l earner engagement data over time.  However, unfolding longitudinal learner engagement trajectories represent ed in their discussions over a semester is challenging. The current study addressed two challenges: char acterizing students’ hidden cognitive engagement levels and grouping their engagement trajectories. To this end, we took advantage of advanced learning analytics techniques. The abundance of online data has sparked interest in using modern analytical techniques to understand, profile, and support student engagement in these environments (Kim et al., 2020; Barthakur et al., 2021). Research in  learning analytics has provided valuable insights into many key aspects of online learning, for example, engagement patterns, s tudents’ profiles, and the relationship between engagement and achievement (Henrie et al., 2015). Suc h insights have been based on diverse methods such as clustering, sequence mining, process mining, and predictive modeling (López-Pernas et al., 2021; Matcha et al., 2019). In light of the practical attention above, this study a ims to address the process of clustering the learners’ cognitive presence and learning trajectories. Th e following research questions guide the study design:

• What is the computational process involved in clustering cognitive engagement pres ences in

longitudinally traced discussion data?

• What are the trajectories of students’ cognitive engagement presence  over the online course, and what

are their defining features?

## Background

Cognitive engagement Fredricks et al. (2004) address that engagement is a multidimensional structur e that integrates behavioral, emotional, and cognitive subcomponents. Specifically, cognitive engagement reflects stude nts’ mental efforts, ranging from memorizing and trying to understand learning tasks, to investing thoughtful ene rgy to grasp advanced concepts or acquire complex skills. Cognitive engagement also includes  using self-regulated learning strategies, a preference for challenge, and positive coping in tough situations. C ognitive engagement refers to a learner’s knowledge construction level in a learning task (Fredricks et al., 2004, Roseli & Umar, 2015), such as using different cognitive strategies to comprehend and master knowledge and skills (Xu et al., 2021). In the current study, cognitive engagement means a hierarchical order of four different cogni tive presences from least to most engaging: triggering event, exploration, integration, and resolution (Garrison et al., 2001). Clustering longitudinal learner engagement Cluster analysis methods identify multiple heterogeneous groups to which an individual may belong based on a given set of cluster variables (Araka et al., 2022; Feng et al., 2022). For example, Saqr and López -Pernas (2022) CSCL 2023 Proceedings  © ISLS 298 examined student engagement during online courses taken over a full education pr ogram. They used Hidden Markov Models (HMM) to cluster the engagement states, resulting in in a mos tly engaged, an intermediate, and a troubled trajectory.  In this study, we use the seqHMM package in R to cluster learners’ cognitive presence over the online course. The seqHMM is a technique that uncovers the hidden sequence pattern in engagement trajectories. It can reveal hidden longitudinal patterns and summarize complex inf ormation (Helske & Helske, 2015).

## Context

The current study leverages the discussion data from a graduate-level online c ourse on learning technologies. A total of 27 students from two cohorts who enrolled in two different semesters parti cipated in the study. The two cohorts consisted of 9 and 18 students, respectively. The first cohort (N=9) had 4 male students and 5 female students, while the second cohort (N=18) had 5 males and 13 females. Students were tasked with participating in weekly asynchronous online discussions over 12 weeks. Students were requir ed to post a summary of weekly readings with 200-250 words each week. The summary was expected to include  a substantive question accompanied by the student’s commentary. The questions served to trigger whole group discussions each week. Over the online course, students were instructed to answer at least th ree of the other students’ questions and respond to their peers’ commentaries in order to obtain full credits. Data source and analysis We used text messages posted in weekly discussion forums from two diffe rent semesters. The total number of messages during the 12-week period was 427 and 933, respectively. Garrison et al. (2001) proposed the Practical Inquiry Model that defines a hierarchical order of four-level cognitive prese nce from least to most engaging: triggering event, exploration, integration, and resolution. Using the Practical Inquiry M odel, two researchers examined each post and determined cognitive engagement levels (0 = No Relation, 1 =  Triggering Event, 2 = Exploration, 3 = Integration, 4 = Resolution). The weighted Kappa values for the semesters were 0.90 and 0.94, respectively. We constructed student engagement sequences over 12 weeks from the manually coded data.  Each student had a different number of engagement states chronologically ordered ac cording to the frequencies of his/her discussion posts. We used seqHMM to cluster the sequence data into a group of engagement trajectories.

## Findings and discussion

The HMM resulted in several candidate models. Referring to the lowest Bayesian information criterion (BIC), we chose the two-cluster model for the first cohort and the three-cluster model for the second cohort (see Figures 1 and 2). Also, we examined each learner’s cluster membership and hidden states over the semester base d on the hidden path function of seqHMM. We used cluster membership to further examine the individual difference over the course.

For the first cohort, students’ initial membership showed cluster 1 (11%) and cluster 2 (89%), and most students remained at the same cluster with a transition probability of 0.002, fr om cluster 2 to cluster 1. Cluster 1 showed a larger portion of Integration along with Exploration, while cluster 2 is dominated by Exploration with Triggering Events  and Integration (see Figure 1-a). The index plots indicate that students in cluster 1 mostly engaged in weekly discussions, while cluster 2 students tended to participate in w eeks selectively (see Figure 1b). As seen in Figure 2-a, we found three clusters in the second cohort with the following initial membership: cluster 1 (44%), cluster 2 (6%), and cluster 3 (50%). Like cohort 1, student membership in cohort 2 did not change noticeably other than the transition from cluster 2 to cluster 3 (transition pr ob. = 0.037). Regarding the combination of cognitive engagement levels, cluster 1 and cluster 3 appeared similar to cluster 1 and cluster 2 in the first cohort’s model, respectively. Cluster 1 was grouped with E xploration and Integration, cluster 2 had Triggering Events as its main pattern, with Exploration and Integration, and clus ter 3 is mainly constituted with Exploration with some frequencies of the Triggering Events and Integration Level.  Referring to the index plots (see Figure-b), cluster 1 showed trajectories with much missing participati on compared to cluster 1 of the first cohort data. Cluster 2 seemed to be a troubled group with rare contributions to the discussions. Cluster 3 showed similar patterns as observed in cluster 2 of the first cohort. The engagement states and very low transition probabilities imply that students’ cognitive engagement may be somewhat invariant, perhaps determined by their pre-existing characteristi cs such as learning strategies, self-regulation skills, motivation, etc. In contrast, such learner difference s may interact with the design of the CSCL 2023 Proceedings  © ISLS 299 online discussion, including the class size. For example, students in a small er class (e.g., cohort 1) would track discussion threads and interact with peers easier than in a larger class, which enabled them to participate in most weeks and feel connected in a learning community (AbuSeileek, 2012).

## Conclusion

In this study, we clustered online students’ cognitive engagement states and traced transitions between the states from the two cohorts who enrolled in two different semesters. We identified sim ilar learner engagement states from the two cohorts. However, the details of their participation patterns differ ed depending on the group dynamics. This initial endeavor demonstrates that we can group learner engagement patterns using their longitudinal participation in online discussions. A larger class data can increa se the chance of unfolding more clusters. Also, future studies should investigate how other factors (e.g., individual differences) influence shaping engagement trajectories.
