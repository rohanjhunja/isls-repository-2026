# Design Thinking as a Structure for Collaborative Project-Based Learning in Multiple Disciplines

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study examines the design thinking process as a structure for collaborative project-based learning in undergraduate courses ranging from biomedical sciences to teacher education. Our aim in this design-based research study was to understand how design processes are experienced and to develop a set of design principles. Individually written reflection papers were coded in categories including design aspects that worked well as well as student struggles. Code co-occurrence correlations were calculated and a network map created. Findings in each set of relationships were translated into design moves. One important finding was that learning through design thinking requires truly collaborative work where team members work collectively throughout the design process. The learning experience should also be designed to emphasize building relationships of interdependence. The innovative analytical process used provides valuable insights to improve the use of design thinking for collaborative project-based learning in multiple disciplines.

## Background

Project-based learning and collaborative learning are generally acknowledged in the literature as evidence-based high-impact practices (National Academies, 2018). However, there are a number of issues educators face  when attempting to implement these practices in their ow n courses. A common issue is lack of scaffolding st ructure, leading to inconsistency of effectiveness, students trying to do all the work at the last minute, and breakdowns in interpersonal communication within student groups (Barron et al, 1998). Furthermore, many projects are structured such that the teacher determines the pro blem, process, and nature of the solution rather th an helping students develop skills in problem identification, creative problem solving and managing productive fa ilure through focused tinkering and exploration (Svihla &  Reeve, 2016). Finally, project-based learning in a cademia often involves designing artifacts or solutions whi ch are only seen by the teacher and peers within th e course rather than pushing students to interact with real-world stakeholders and design solutions for real-world problems (Kincheloe, 2008). To address these and other relat ed issues, we have been conducting design-based res earch study across multiple university courses in which students engage in the design thinking process. In the current study we are using the Design Thinking for Engaged Learning model (Donaldson & Smith,

2017) which consists of a 10-stage process broken down into 5 phases, with each stage intentionally crafted such

that students develop skills in designerly ways of knowing (Cross, 2006). For instance, in order to help students develop the “wicked problem framing” designerly way of knowing, small groups of students identify and frame a wicked problem within the disciplinary scope of the course (Donaldson et al, 2022). To help them develop the cognitive, affective, and conative empathy designerly ways of knowing, students go into real-world con texts in which their wicked problem exists and conduct obser vations and interviews to understand stakeholders’ experiences of the problem—especially the experienc es of those at the margins (Jamal et al, 2021). In order to facilitate truly collaborative (Chi, 2009) learning, groups are expected to complete all stages of the  process together rather than splitting up tasks to be accomplished by individual members (Odom et al, 2021). In this study, we were seeking to understand how such design proce sses are experienced by students and to develop a s et of principles for using design thinking as a structure for collaborative project-based learning in higher education.

## Methodology

This design-based research study (Sandoval & Bell, 2004) investigated the use of design thinking for collaborative project-based learning in the second iteration in u ndergraduate courses. Students in agricultural lead ership, bioenvironmental sciences, tourism studies, animal sciences, and teacher education courses engaged as small groups in the design thinking process over a period ranging from 10 to 14 weeks. The process was broken down into 5 design thinking phases. For analysis of the first iteration (not reported here) we used simple frequency analysis of weaknesses and strengths of the design found in 381 reflection papers. For analysis of thi s second iteration we developed an innovative analytical process through which to leverage multiple statistically significant ICLS2022 Proceedings 901 © ISLS correlations between student struggles and design strengths as a means of developing data-driven design moves for improving the third iteration. Throughout the second intervention, students individually wrote reflection papers which were collected for data analysis (163 documen ts). We coded the data in MAXQDA Analytics Pro for emergent themes in a number of categories including design aspects that worked well as well as student struggles, resulting in 174 codes covering 2,257 coded segment s. We then calculated code co-occurrence correlatio ns to create symmetrical matrices which we imported into the UCINET and NetDraw network analysis software to create a network map of struggles and design features that worked with correlations of p<0.001. We used GirvanNewman cluster analysis (Girvan & Newman, 2002) to identify clusters, calculated eigenvector values, and set node sizes according to betweenness centrality measures to identify the most salient strengths and weaknesses of the design. Because this is the second iteration in a design-based research study, we were primarily interested in crafting design moves to improve the next iteration. To do so, we selected the category of student str uggles to indicate aspects of the design that were weak and t he category of design aspects that worked well to s uggest possible solutions which leverage strengths to address weaknesses. We analyzed the relationships between each of the student struggles and the related design strengths, and translated findings in each set of rela tionships into design moves.

## Findings

In our network analysis of correlations at p<0.001 we found 3 clusters with Q=0.372 through Gir van-Newman analysis (see Figure 1) indicating the network of relationships between student struggles and aspects of the design that worked well. We set the node size such that nodes with higher betweenness centrality measures were larger. Diamond-shaped nodes are student struggles, and cir cles are aspects of the design that students experi enced as beneficial.

Figure 1 Network Map of Design Strengths and Student Struggles In the red cluster in the middle of Figure 1 we found that students who struggled with logistics (node 2-11) also tended to struggle with technology use (no de 2-7), understanding what to do (node 2-8), diver gent

thinking (node 2-12), idea generation and coming up with new ideas (node 2-10). What worked for these students included interacting and developing relationships w ith team members (node 2-1), collaborative document s and technologies (node 2-5), teamwork times (node 2-6-recurring meetings devoted to the team project), a nd idea generation (node 3-1). What these students also found beneficial was rehearsing and sharing projects with peers (node 2-2), and the development process (node 2-4). This suggested that a potential solution for the struggle with technology was to have teams hold workshop times with their teams during team work time where time was set aside specifically for troubleshooting problems. This design move was supported by the connection to t he team work times, which worked well. A final design move this analysis suggested was to address student struggles with ICLS2022 Proceedings 902 © ISLS logistics by providing students with opportunities to obtain feedback from outside perspectives on logistics of the projects.

There also appears to be a connection between students’ personal struggles (Figure 1-red cluster: node 2-9) and their struggles with divergent thinking (node 2-12), ideation (node 2-10), and collaboration, interpersonal interactions, and relationships in the group (node 3-9). These struggles were addressed with a design move of advising students to seek new perspectives and to b e open tonew perspectives outside of their group. Another design move that was developed and implemented was to emphasize the value of self-reflection and helpi ng students recognize their own assumptions regarding a challenging team, team member, or situation. For this we added a prompt to a reflective assignment that asked students to think about managing personal and interpersonal relationships for optimal teamwork.

In the blue cluster on the right of Figure 1 we found that student struggles with time management (node 3-7) were connected to struggles with collaboration and relationships (node 3-9), adapting to change (node 312), and following instructions (node 2-11). What worked for students who struggled with time management was interest alignment in teams (node 3-5) and increased communication (node 3-2). To leverage these strengths to address the time management weakness, a potential solution is to encourage students to include timing expectations and status updates in their existing communications with each other so that they can leverage their current strengths with communication frequency. Modeling this behavior may be necessary to augment assignment instructions as these students also expressed struggles with following instructions in general.

There is also a connection between those who struggled in time management and success in generating a large number of ideas (Figure 1-blue cluster: node 3-1). This suggests that they started the design process off well in generating many ideas (possibly because they had aligned interests within teams), but then struggled with collaboration and relationships (node 3-9), following instructions (node 2-11), and adapting to change (node 3-12). A design move and potential solution for this struggle was to add specific instructions for each of the team activities to instruct teams to spend only 40 minutes working collaboratively in the activity, complete the activity within the allotted time, and not delegate other outside individual work after the team meeting. In the small black cluster on the left of Figure 1 we found that student struggles in problem finding (node 1-3) were related to strengths in scientific and co nceptual understanding (node 2-1), and project fram ing and orientation activities (node 1-1). This led to the development of two design moves. First, the project framing and orientation activities were revised to include disc ussion of the scope (boundaries) delimiting what ty pes of problems were within the disciplinary scope of the course (guided by the course learning outcomes) and  what problems were outside the scope of the course. Second, the problem finding activity instructions were changed to ask groups to have discussions in which they narrow ed the scope of their problem while discussing the ways in which the range of problems they were considering t ied in with the scientific and conceptual material they had been studying thus far in the course and in previous courses in the discipline.

Using network analysis of correlations between stu dent struggles and design strengths (aspects of the design which students found beneficial) not only allowed us to identify the multiple interdependencies between struggles and strengths in order to leverage those relationships to develop design moves, but also all owed us to identify overall leverage points in the data. Because betweenness centrality measures indicate the degree to which a node is “important” in connecting other nodes, we were able to interpret larger betweenness centrality measures of particular nodes as leverage points. In other words, changing that aspect would impact many other aspects. The design feature of requiring groups to set up weekly  teamwork times (Figure 1: node 2-6) had the highes t betweenness centrality measure (79.73 with an eigenvector of 0.27). Interacting and developing relationships with team members (node 2-1) had the second highest betweenness value (65.74 with an eigenvector of 0.36). Coming up with many ideas (node 3-1) had the third highest betweenness (48.97 with an eigenvector of 0.34). These three leverage points were design strengths. One area of student struggle had a high betweenness value (46.78 with an eigenvector of 0.28): time management (node 3-7). T aken together, this suggests—as supported by the ea rlier data-grounded design moves described earlier—that design thinking projects will be most powerful if we  place an emphasis on building team relationships through regular working meetings in which all team members work collectively and collaboratively (rather than split ting up tasks and letting members work individually) over an extended period of time. This group functioning wil l further be optimized if we provide specific guida nce and structures for time management.

## Discussion and Implications

Design thinking can be used to structure collaborat ive project-based learning (Carroll et al, 2010). T his study investigated the details of how to optimize such st ructuring of the design thinking process in learnin g contexts through analysis of the second iteration of a desig n-based research study. We hope to gain deeper insi ght in ICLS2022 Proceedings 903 © ISLS subsequent iterations. One of our most important findings was that learning through design thinking requires truly collaborative work in which members of each team wo rk together collectively to engage in all aspects o f the design process, and that the learning experience should be designed such that it emphasizes building relationships of trust and interdependence within the team. We also found that it may be beneficial for design think ing teams to routinely share and discuss their work with peer s outside their teams, as well as with others outsi de the educational context with an emphasis on reflection,  perspective taking, and questioning assumptions. A nother strategy suggested by our analysis was that learning through design thinking may be most effective if teams are taught and encouraged to engage in frequent metacognitive practices such as reflection-in-action (Schön, 1992), so that their discussions are not only focused on c ompleting the task at hand, but also focused on rev iew and refinement of logistical and technical issues, processes, and ways of thinking about the task at hand. Finally, the innovative analytical process we developed provided  valuable insights grounded in the data, which we t hen leveraged to develop design moves to improve the im plementation of design thinking for collaborative projectbased learning. We believe that this analytical method may be applicable in a wide range of design-based research projects in multiple disciplines.
