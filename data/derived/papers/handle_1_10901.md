# Students’ Use of Cultural Practices in Astronomy Stories

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
We investigate how preservice teachers (PTs) express the ways science is enacted through cultural practices, by writing astronomy stories. Storybooks written by PTs in an introduction to astronomy course were analyzed. Seven themes were identified describing how PTs take up anti-deficit perspectives as well as enact traditional approaches to science.

Science as culture As with all sciences disciplines, astronomy is influenced by the people and places in which it is created. Our study took place in an introductory astronomy course for preservice teachers  (PTs), designed to engage PTs in astronomy as a practice that is developed through participation in cultural processes (Rogoff, 2003). We consider cultural processes as “ configurations of routine ways of doing things in a ny community’s approach to living” (Rogoff, 2003, p. 3). Our objective was to examine how engagement in an i ntroductory astronomy course, using culturally responsive pedagogy, supported PTs in taking an anti-deficit perspective on astronomy. Learning more about how these PTs take up anti-deficit practices is important to understanding their trajectory towards enacting social justice as teachers. Each PT wrote a children’s storybook as the culminating pro ject for the course. This medium was chosen because of the prevalence of children’s books in elementary science instruction (e.g., Pringle & Lamme, 2005). Writing a storybook also served as an open-ended platform for the PTs to demonstrate their thinking about the nature of astronomy, serving as a productive assessment tool. Our study was guided by the following research questions: In what ways do PTs write astronomy storybooks that  c onvey astronomy as embedded in culture and enacted through scientific practice? The ways in which science is enacted through cultur al practices differs across contexts. For example, Indigenous ways of knowing astronomy place people at the center of practice, emphasizing relationships with the sky, not just placing the sky as a distant physical construct (Lee et al., 2021). Reform-based efforts in schools seek to engage students in a scientific culture reflecti ng collaborative work that answers questions with e vidence. Yet these ways of practicing science contrast and even conflict with what Carlone (2004) terms “prototypical science education” (p. 394), including transmission models of instruction, a focus on prod ucing future scientists, and an emphasis on verification laboratories and memorization of defin itions. We focused our study on the broad range of sense-making practices that people engage with around sci ence, drawing on a theoretical approach that considers how people live culturally. Gutierrez & Rogoff (2003) emphasize the need to move beyond assumptions that “characteristics of cultural groups are located with in individuals”, in an attempt to “leave behind def icitmodel thinking, in which cultural ways that differ from the practices of dominant groups are judged to  be less adequate without examining them from the perspectiv e of the community’s participants” (p. 19). Everyth ing is cultured; there are no situations or experiences wi thout culture (Bang & Medin, 2010; Rogoff, 2003). T his framework helps us to shift the focus away from ste reotypical representations of science, such as thin king of science as a body of knowledge or activities performed by White men in laboratories.

## Methodology

Participants were students from two semesters of an introductory astronomy course designed for PTs at a large public research university in the northeastern US. All PTs in the course (N=47) provided consent to participate in the study. 91% of the PTs were in their 1 st or 2nd year of college. 79% were majoring in elementary education, with the remaining in middle or secondary education majors. PTs were primarily White (94%) and female (89%). The first author was the course’s professor. The final project was to write a children’s astronomy s torybook. In preparation, PTs shared their critique of a published children’s astronomy storybook with a s mall group of peers (all books focused on human characters of either mi noritized backgrounds or early women astronomers).  PTs were asked to author narratives—not informational texts—featuring human characters engaged with Solar System astronomy. Storybooks had an average length of 26.2 pages (SD = 4.6). We used thematic analysis to guide our analytical process (Braun & Clarke, 2006). We used a theoretical process to generate initial co des as we were guided by our research questions and the existing literature on science and culture. For example, we identified potential ways the PTs considered astronomy embedded in culture when stories presented astronomy as a family activity, thereby showing how astronomy exists within the practices of a community (Rogoff, 2003). After initially coding books together, we coded small groups of books separately, then discussed the coding until agreement was reached. We constructed themes by looking for patterns in the codes relevant to our research questions.

## Findings

The PTs’ stories present ways astronomy is developed through participation in cultural practices reflecting aspects of our astronomy course. (1) Stories conveyed the message that astronomy can  be done by women, people of color, people with disabilities, and other forms of diversity (74%). These stories challenge depiction of science as only practiced by White, able-bodied, men. (2) Stories conveyed that astronomy can be done as part  of a family (36%). PTs may recognize that astronomy can be part of one’s identity as a family  and can be done through collaboration and in community with others. These s tories may also reflect indigenous perspectives that people and relationships are at the center of doing scienc e. (3) Stories portrayed astronomy as dynamic, building on observations as evidence (17%). Presenting science as constantly changing and evolv ing provides students with opportunities to see themselves as part of a n ongoing process of sensemaking. (4) Stories also provided explicit views of astronomy as seen through the lens of non-Western cultures (9%). Some stories presented children from Western cultures learning about astronomy in non-Western societies. Other stories integrated astronomy in the lives of people of non-Western cultures (e.g., a Chinese American family relating observations of the Moon in the sky to their celebration of the Mid-Autumn Festival, also known as the Moon Festival). The PTs’ stories also reflected school science and popular science  culture. (5) Stories reproduced elements of prototypical science education, including depiction s of adults transmitting scientific knowledge to children and a focus on learning and/or repeating scientific facts and definitions (30%). For example, rather than showing the child protagonist conducting an investi gation to answer their scientific question, an adul t would provide the answers or solve their problem for them. By removing the child’s agency as a scientist, th ese stories reduced the impact of including people of color, female, or characters with disabilities. (6) Stories emphasize the importance of becoming an astronomer or astronaut a s the goal of science (23%). This may further reflect a prototypical science education viewpoint, by emphasizing the importance of producing scient ists rather than engaging with science out of curiosity or interest. (7) Finally, stories included elements of science fiction in their depictions of how astronomy is practiced (15%).  Several stories featured aliens —often in ways that enacted prototypical science education cultural processes, such as transmitting astronomy knowledge to children.

## Discussion

This study provides a window into PTs’ viewpoints on how astronomy is enacted through cultural proces ses in ways that move towards an anti-deficit stance. In particular, the PTs told stories that consider ways to b roaden participation in science through depictions of characters traditi onally underrepresented in astronomy. And by telling stories of families practicing astronomy together, they present a vision that science can be enacted through the cultural processes that children engage with at home.  The course was designed to help PTs recognize astronomy as something that is part of their own cultural processes, and part of other people’s cultural processes. In doing so, we work towards shifting these PTs’ views of astronomy —and science in general —away from something that is separate from human influence and  towards an understanding that astronomy is generat ed and transformed through its integration in cultural act ivities across time (Rogoff, 2003). As we move forward with this work, we wonder how to  further leverage our PTs’ epistemological resources (Hammer & Elby, 2003), especially as we work with PTs who enact prototypical science education practices and consider ways to engage them more deeply with broader views of astronomy as a cultural practice.
