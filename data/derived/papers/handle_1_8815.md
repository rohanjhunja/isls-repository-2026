# “Keep It in the Village”: A 25-Year Journey of Collective Concept Formation Transforming Financial Practices in a Rural Thai Village

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Community-generated concepts, namely “germ cells,” can be productive to address problem situations. Yet most literature on germ cells accounts for the initial development of concepts and how these create opportunities for learning and concrete application. This taps into only a small part of the potential power of a germ cell to be generative. In this paper we consider the ongoing development and application of a germ cell in a rural Thai village over 24 years, showing how through a series of learning intraventions, each involving self-analysis and reflection alongside concrete community actions, a germ cell slowly evolved, continuing to orient the village’s transformative work. In this process, the villagers’ family and community financial practices underwent dramatic, productive change. The ongoing unfolding and expanding concrete manifestations of the germ cell shed light on their generative capacity in community learning and transformation. Keywords: germ cell, formative intervention, intravention, activity theory, concept formation

## Introduction

Supporting collective community learning through concrete changes in practice is an underlying challenge in the learning sciences (Gutiérrez & Vossoughi, 2010) that requires forming collective concepts that catalyze change. One way to approach concept formation is through th e analysis of germ cells: powerful, underlying idea s (i.e., abstract concepts) developed through experimentation and self-analysis by a group of people to orient their actions toward productive, collective learning. In other wo rds, a germ cell is a simple explanatory concept th at people construct to transform their community practices in concrete ways to learn expansively (Engeström & Sa nnino, 2012; Gutiérrez & Penuel, 2014). In this paper we explore the community development of a germ cell that helped to orient dramatic improvement in community financial practices in one rural Thai village over 24 years. In the late 1990’s, like many subsistence agricultural villages, the northern Thai village of Ban Samkha faced crippling financial conditions that threatened its survival. They were collectively buried under unsustainable debt at high interest rates and, according to their  own descriptions, had no understanding of the math ematics of compound interest (or its influence on their debt) or how they accumulated such debt in the first place. Problematic practices added to their expenses, for instance, se lling all their crops at low prices and purchasing food from outside vendors at higher costs. While various gove rnment and nonprofit organizations initiated develo pment programs in prior years to help Ban Samkha and othe r villages, for instance providing an influx of cas h with a “village fund” or group lending initiative (Coleman, 1999), without a means to comprehend the underlying issues behind their financial problems these interventions worsened the village’s problems. Villagers expressed feeling incapable of understanding, much less tackling the concerns that overwhelmed them. The challenges Ban Samkha faced were not unique; they were (and are) common in villages throughout Thailand and the world (Chichaibelu, 2018). Althoug h transformative social change in villages and rura l communities is a valued ideal, supporting it is non trivial. Villages face multiple, interrelated obsta cles, from a lack of financial literacy to unsustainable agricul tural practices, to local ecologies actively being reshaped by climate change (e.g., Sayizonga et al., 2016). In t he face of these challenges, community participatio n and leadership are necessary for initiatives to succeed, yet most development initiatives in Thailand and across the world are designed with scant understanding of loca l contexts, community interests, or the histories a nd power structures in which problems are rooted (Gutiérrez & Vossoughi, 2010; Nuttavuthisit, 2014). Further, t he participatory practices necessary for community empowerment and transformation (Freire & Macedo, 1987), as well as the understanding of larger schemes of expl oitation (Freire, 1974), are usually absent from fi nancial literacy education programs (Blue & Pinto, 2017). H owever, the development of local agency is essentia l for community members to become “agents of change” and take action together (e.g., Gutiérrez & Jurow, 2016). Such agency requires conceptual development on the part of communities: the ability to analyze their s pecific, local challenges and develop means of organizing and acting toward transformation (Vossoughi & Gutiérr ez, 2016). In these contexts, community formative interventions, which include the development of germ cells, can provide a means for expansive learning and transformative agency (Sannino et al., 2016). Yet most literature on germ cells addresses only their initial development and application over the first few years of an intervention (e.g., ICLS2022 Proceedings 377 © ISLS Ivaldi & Scaratti, 2016), tapping merely a small bit of the potential power of germ cells to be generative for years and across multiple areas of community life.

In this paper we consider how Ban Samkha developed a germ cell— “keeping it in the village”—and how this germ cell helped spur the village’s learni ng through a truly remarkable series of intraventio ns over 24 years. The germ cell emerged through collective ana lysis of their financial problems and supported the development of many concrete actions in the village. Further, what began in the financial domain slowly expanded to other domains as the germ cell was incrementally developed over time. To study this, we drew on 37 hours of interviews across four visits over as many years in  addition to over 350 pages of documentation by the  village and a supporting foundation. We employ an iterative  case study approach, using chronological sequencin g and thematic analysis, and apply Engeström and colleagues’ (2012) framework of learning actions for ascending from the abstract to the concrete to address the following research questions: (1) How did the village develop a germ cell? and (2) How did that germ cell evolve over time through multiple, concrete village-led intraventions? In the discussion we consider implications of this work for community development alongside theoretical insights from the village’s markedly longitudinal and generative development of the germ cell.

## Background

Village learning, like other community learning, can be observed in changes in practices, as conceptual thinking occurs in action (Davydov, 1990). Davydov (2008) proposes that when learners engage on a particular task, they “complete a sort of microcycle of the ascent from the abstract to the concrete” (p. 124) in order to assimilate, in a Vygotskian sense, theoretical knowledge and form concepts. In these sequences of learning actions, which consist of operations that occur within the context of a le arning task, germ cells emerge as general concepts or activity units. Germ cells are abstract concepts created through iterative experimental and analytical examination of one’s own community’s practices (Gutiérrez & Penuel, 2014). The abstraction of the germ cell is developed in  the process of modeling a solution to a problem situati on and captures the genetic unit capable of creatin g new and concrete change (Falmagne, 1995; Davydov, 1990). Fo r Davydov, the proponent of the term, these concept ual cells work like biological cells in the sense that “a cell is what everything stems from and is reduced to” (Davydov, 1999, p.47). To wit, germ cells reproduce like biol ogical cells, generating new implementations and extensions of a concept as learners “ascend to the concrete” (Falmagne, 1995; Davydov, 1990) of concept formation  by mastering conceptual understanding through concrete changes in practices—further constructing the abstract idea by applying it in everyday situations.

Engeström and colleagues (2012) have a long history  of studying the development of germ cells in formative interventions where communities address c hallenges with the support of researchers. Building  on Davydov, they argue that concepts are formed as learners ascend from the abstract to the concrete where, starting with a problem situation, they model an abstract concept that is developed and becomes concrete when applied in practice. This process occurs in a sequence of epis temic actions that include (1) questioning, (2) ana lyzing, (3) modeling, (4) examining, (5) implementing, (6) reflecting, and (7) evaluating. In such a sequence, the germ cell itself is discovered, modeled, and examined experim entally and analytically by learners as they invest igate and transform their activities. Germ cells play an important role as the smallest unit of the complex totality of all the analysis and changes in practice, they carry the contradictions of the whole, and in practice can be ubiquitous to a point that they may go unnoticed (Ibid.). More importantly, germ cells are generative and can be extended in multiple applications and future developments. Yet while the generativity of germ cells is well argued for, most research on germ cells takes place in researcher-led formative interventions where the time limitations of research projects (i.e., 3-5 years) often curtail the evidence for the longevity and “multiple applications” of germ cells. In the learning sciences, most research on germ cells has focused on single learning sequences to study how germ cells are formed. For instance, Engeström and colleagues (2012) studied how care workers at an elderly home alongside elderly people at risk of losing physical mobility collectively formed a new concept of mobility over a period of two years. Through the simple idea of “standing up” they included regular mobility exercises in home visits and daily life. This was generative in framing physical mobility in terms of sustainabilit y and in generating innovative ways of using household objec ts to support mobility and autonomy. Other work has addressed the emergence of the “walk the talk” germ  cell in a formative intervention among volunteers at a nonprofit as a concept that in practice can help align volunteer work with organizational values (Ivaldi & Scaratti,

2016) or the “tighten knots” germ cell as a concept  that through concrete practices helped an interdis ciplinary

health promotion team come together and work toward s common goals when providing health services from complementary disciplines (Ibid.). These examples focus on germ cell emergence with out addressing germ cell generativity over time and domains.

This is not to say that there is no research that has focused on generativity beyond the initial conception and application of a germ cell. As an example, Sannino and colleagues (2016) describe how in a series of learning ICLS2022 Proceedings 378 © ISLS sequences over many years, a community in an urban Brazilian informal settlement developed, sustained and expanded a germ cell. The germ cell, that trash has  value and “trash is life,” emerged as the communit y was dealing with flooding where trash in the river affe cted its members’ health and safety. The community’ s questioning and analysis led to an intravention on waste management, where the germ cell “trash is life” emerged. Yet the generativity of the idea continued far beyo nd this initial development, and the community cont inued to learn, as evidenced in their changing practices ove r 3 years in related areas: waste management and po licies, flooding prevention, community safety, and even policy advocacy at a city level. Further, through the concept of “trash is life”, the community became aware of its rights and its power to transform and improve itself (Sannino et al., 2016). We find this work particularly inspi ring because it was not researcher-driven, but prim arily community-driven (illustrated with the term intraventions), in a sense a social design experiment (G utiérrez & Jurow, 2016) generated by and iterated on by the community itself. This is also a provocative example of a germ cell that the community applied in multiple ways, providing evidence of the promised generativity of germ cells. Building on this work, we analyze the community-led formation and generativity of a germ cell in Ban Samkha across a series of learning sequences in community-led financial intraventions over a period of 24 yea rs. We consider how the germ cell developed over time—evolving through iterative experimentation, practice, analysis, and reflection—and briefly describe how it became g enerative in other domains of village life through environmental, health, and educational initiatives.

## Context

Ban Samkha is a rural village in northern Thailand (~600 people) that today is known as a “model village” in the country (Bruneau, 2009) for its financial, environm ental, and agricultural sustainability initiatives,  including a community bank, a successful homestay program (~300 0 tourists a year), and a ban against herbicides and pesticides. By internalizing the learning philosophy of Thai Constructionism (see Blikstein & Fields, 2021; Fields & Blikstein, under review), the village developed a n established sense of agency with a learning proce ss they applied iteratively with perseverance. This paper i s part of a larger study on the development and pur suit of a unique implementation of Papert’s constructionism (1980) in Thailand (see also Cavallo, 2000), which over two decades was reshaped through local internalization into Thai Constructionism (Fields & Blikstein, under review). Elsewhere we have documented the transformative story of Ban Samkha’s journey to become “a village that learns” (Fields et al., under review). Here we focus on the  development and role of the germ cell—“keeping it in the village”—that helped drive their communal work in finances.

## Participants, data collection and analysis

We collected data during four research visits to Thailand from 2017-2020, including three extended research visits to the village. We interviewed 14 villagers and 6 people from other institutions that mentored and/or learned from villagers, totaling more than 37 hours of interview s, in addition to gathering over 350 pages of scann ed project documentation, including a book published by the village (Ban Samkha, 2005). Interviews focused on participants’ personal and collective narratives of struggle and change, as well as experiences learning with constr uctionism (the original topic of the broader study). Later interviews built on early analysis, soliciting specific details about what led to significant events and projects. Overall, we spent 1-5 hours with each participant, individually and/or in focus groups. Further, to develop a common understanding of research interests and IRB concepts of privacy and research protections, we spoke with translators  extensively before interviews to support more mean ingful translation and interpretation. Of note, the native language of Ban Samkha villagers is Lanna, and interviews were conducted in Thai, English, or a blend, depending on villager preference. We clarified meanings whenever greater understanding was needed.

The 14 participants interviewed, while a small sample, represented a range of people who participated in the constructionist efforts over the course of 24 years in different ways. Some were children at the time of the first intraventions and as of our last visit led various village committees. Others joined in very peripheral ways in the early years and became more involved 5-10 years lat er but are not considered core leaders of initiativ es. Still others did not even begin to be involved until 10 y ears after early intraventions in the village. Some  were the original core leaders. The villagers chose who we i nterviewed. We began with a few contacts in the vil lage, networked through a foundation that worked with the m since 2001. We requested to meet a range of peopl e involved in different ways, and our contacts chose people from a variety of ages, professions, and gen der, including local farmers, teachers, parents, homestay hosts, local monks, village committee leaders, etc. We were invited into homes as well as the village meeting hall and participants showed us their accounting forms, reflective documents, farm products, crops, fruit trees, check  dams, etc. The villagers we interviewed represent only a ICLS2022 Proceedings 379 © ISLS fraction of those involved in constructionist effor ts in Ban Samkha and of the village population at l arge (~600 people). While interviews were primarily based on memories, some 20+ years old, these were checked by villagers’ meticulous written records and reflections, for instance of every visit by mentors over 24 years (into the hundreds), records of village and family farm finances, and contributions (knowledge and monetary) by outside organizations. Most notable is a publicly available, village-authored book covering 1999-2003, including detailed explanations of the historical origins of village problems and sequences of early intraventions (Ban Samkha, 2005). To honor and reference Ban Samkha’s own written history as w ell as other native Thai authors who have researche d the village (e.g., Koanantakool, 2004; Sipitakiat, 2020), we identify the village by name. Consequently, w e seek to protect the identity of participants by keeping names and identifiers of individuals as anonymous as possible. This serves to protect villagers who could be easily identified by gender, age, or profession. In developing the case study, we conducted chronological sequencing, and thematic analysis (Merriam,

1988) to organize villagers’ accounts of events. Before our third data collection visit to the village, we created a

comprehensive timeline of key events. Then, during this visit, using an 8-foot-long version of the tim eline with movable post-it notes for events, we invited villagers in four different focus groups to help fill in missing events and confirm the order. Villagers checked each other’s accounts, and together we created a firm order of key events important to villagers which further prompted refle ctive, insightful conversations. Once we had a clea r understanding of the intraventions conducted in the  village we conducted thematic analysis (Yin, 2018)  of initiatives in five key domains of village life (financial, environmental, educational, agricultural, entrepreneurial) across all interviews. Through this process we reco gnized that villagers engaged with a germ cell that  we summarize in English as “keeping it in the village”. Further the meaning of this germ cell changed ove r time— evident in villagers’ explanations of their initiatives, reflections, and changed thinking. Following this insight, we applied Engeström and colleagues’ (2012) framework of learning actions for ascending from the abstract  to the concrete as an analytical grid to investigate how the germ cell was developed and identify the sequence of learning actions within each intravention. Then we compared how the model of the germ cell changed and evolved in practice to analyze its development and generativity over time. For external validity we engaged in “member checkin g” with feedback sessions at several stages of analysis and writing, including follow-up interview s, focus group interviews, and the timeline constru ction. In addition, two long-term Thai Constructionists with 15-20 years of experience with the village provided feedback on our findings to support analytical rigor as well as political and cultural sensitivity.

## Findings

To situate Ban Samkha’s challenges historically (Gutiérrez & Vossoughi, 2010), we begin where the village itself placed the origins of its current financial struggl es. In the mid-1900’s, as modern farming technologi es were advertised and better roads connected the village t o the provincial capital, farmers bought “iron buff alos” or walking tractors, started selling rice and produce to middle-people from outside the village, and began utilizing chemical fertilizers, pesticides, and herbicides (B an Sam Kha, 2005). Villagers took out high-interest  loans for farming, vehicles, and secondary education for children (Ibid.). But it was the 2540/1997 crash of Asian markets, which started in Thailand, that took village debt t o unprecedented levels. That year, the country’s ec onomy contracted 11%, and the cost of living increased by  40% (Hewison, 2000). Villagers returned from the c ities looking for work and the collective debt of all families summed more than 18 million baht (31 million baht or 1 million USD adjusted for inflation, ~12,000,000 USD converted per purchasing power parity) gathering interest at high rates (Koanantakool, 2004). Something had to be done. As one villager explained, they had to find ways to survive because “the entire village was in bad d ebt” (interview, 2018). Debt was a common problem a cross Thailand, as were government interventions to try to resolve it, including the 2512/1969 “village fund” (Coleman, 1999). However, in Ban Samkha and other villages, this initiative failed. As a villager explained, “because of our lack of knowledge, we used [the] money the wrong way… we actually used it for our daily expenses” (interview, 2020). However, in 2540/1997, the villagers approached their financial issues with a new and different  resolve: to understand them and seek knowledge. Thus began a series of initiatives—or intraventions—by the villagers to learn ways to address their financial problems, a process that led to rich financial literacy and further developments that the village could not have predicted at the time.

Keeping it in the Village: Forming the Germ Cell In pursuing financial health, villagers eventually created a new concept: “keeping it in the village.” This germ cell developed across successive and iterative intraventions. Here, we analyze how the germ cell was formed through a sequence of learning actions taken by the village. In 2540/1997, villagers began by questioning the nature of their debt. Unlike prior efforts, they rejected the idea that they could not understand their finances, sought help to learn about debt and organized to find solutions. I n a second learning action, with the support of a J apanese ICLS2022 Proceedings 380 © ISLS nonprofit, “fourteen smart people studied debt in depth” (interview, 2020), spending 3-4 hours daily over an entire year to inquire into the origin of villagers’ debt and analyzed it using spreadsheets to keep track of income, payments and debt owed. A village leader explained: We looked back to try to figure out how all the deb t was accumulated and how the problem might be solved. We realized that the villagers didn’t have savings, only debt. Saving is hard if the amount of money is small, it’s difficult to deposit it to the bank (interview, 2020). This initiative, which they called the Debt Researc h Group, was not a complete sequence of ascension t o the concrete as described by Engeström and colleagues (2012) and did not yet model a new concept or germ c ell. However, after the debt analysis villagers realized they needed to find a way of “creating savings within the village” (interview, 2020). The questioning and analysis con ducted during this intravention led to the first mo del of a plausible solution that same year (2540/1997), an intravention they called the Honesty Savings Group. The village council decided to mandate participatio n in the Honesty Savings Group,  with 40 families each contributing 30 baht/month (~1 USD/month in 2021 rates) to a collective fund that was then used t o make payments on the most urgent loans (like that descri bed by Martin, Goldman & Jiménez, 2009). It was her e that the first iteration of the idea of “keeping money in the village” appeared: villagers engaged in modeling the new idea, or germ cell, which provided the beginnings o f a plausible solution to their financial problems— saving money, paying off debt and working collectively. Th ey reflected and evaluated their work looking for possibilities to expand it: “we learned from creating the savings group. When we took out loans, the interest was high. So, we said to each other, let’s stop getting  loans from the bank. Let’s use our own savings for  the loans” (interview, 2020). In other words, villagers began to conceptualize the need to save money, pay off debt (even a little), and not take out further loans to pay off earlier loans. Villagers’ learning actions in these two initiatives set the stage for later work that evolved around the emergent germ cell “keeping it in the village”. Generativity of the germ cell The germ cell of “keeping it in the village” was co re in the development of several more concrete fina ncial initiatives that also helped to further refine the idea, leading to a cycle of generativity (see Figur e 1) where the village went from saving money with the Honesty Savings Group, to keeping it from “leaking”, to investing it. In 2544/2001, villagers extended their research into the nature of their debt in the Household Accounting Program, shifting from a village-level analysis of how much debt existed to family-level analysis of how debt was created. Support came from a Thai foundation that invited a janitor from a nearby industrial plant to share how he kept track of his finances using a simple spreadsheet that laid out income and expenses. After formal discussion, including opposition from some villagers, the village council decided to make the Household Accounting Program mandatory. Small groups of 6 households, each with a leader (usually a woman) completed household spreadsheets and met monthly to share insights from  their accounting and identify how their debt was c reated. The village decided to use the funding the foundati on provided for snacks to pay each household leader  (30 baht/month, ~1 USD in 2021 rates) for the effort of  accounting, adding to the potential to save money.  Thus, research on village debt evolved into research on family debt, which led to insights about over-expenditures that allowed for changes in family financial practices, namely, to keep money from “leaking” out and encourage saving within families. The Household Accounting Program was credited for many behavioral changes in people’s lives. For example, one man, who had been contemplating suicide because he did not understand why his debt grew the more and harder he worked, learned through his anal ysis of daily expenses that instant coffee purchase s were beyond his means; further, the more he worked, the more he spent on coffee packets (interview, 2017). Understanding his income and expenses helped him ch ange his habits and decrease coffee consumption (Ibid.). Villagers shared many other stories of how the insi ghts from accounting research helped change their s pending habits. The Household Accounting Program provides t he first example of how the germ cell “keeping it i n the village” began to expand beyond its initial concept ion to include preventing money from “leaking out,”  made concrete through the new layers of self-research and changed family practices. ICLS2022 Proceedings 381 © ISLS Other intraventions further developed the germ cell by creating a cooperative store and banking systems within the village that could keep profits, financi al transactions, debt, interest, and savings in the  village. The cooperative community store, opened in 2541/1998, provided reasonable prices for villagers, creating beneficial competition with the other stores in the village and allowing families to save more. At the same time a portion of revenue from the store went directly into village c offers, enabling the village itself to invest in co mmunity initiatives and infrastructure. Here, “keeping it i n the village” included price competition and profi ts. A second intravention was the Brain Bank, a youth-led bank t hat issued loans to villagers. It started in 2545/2 002 when children used savings from a fundraising event at an English language camp to provide a small loan to a cherished elder. In this bank individual villagers themselves  chose what interest they could pay, including unus ual forms such as providing expertise on raising crops or animals. Not all loans were paid back in a timely manner, leading to some difficult learning, but slowly profits built up. This was the first time that interest from loans (even in the form of animals or expertise) was kept in the villa ge rather than paid to an outside financial institu tion. This success, along with a growing understanding of interest and loans, led to the later development of a Community Bank (2545/2004) in partnership with a commercial bank in the provincial capital. One villager reflected on how one initiative led to another and to the creation of the community bank: Every family had debt and paid interest. We never talked about it, villagers just paid whatever interest the outside banks calculated. We saw the d ata, and the bigger picture: we were really getting taken advantage of. We told the villagers that we were going to build our own bank. We created our dream.  (interview, 2020) Some villagers attended a year of classes to learn how to manage the bank. Now, villagers earned more interest on their savings, paid less interest on loans, and the village itself benefited from loan payments. Other financial initiatives over the past 15 years focused on keeping money in the village by investing in small enterprises (see Figure 1). A younger generation of villagers, that were children and teens while the germ cell emerged, have recently started several enterprises including a rice paper plant, a dried mushroom factory, and a local tourism program. All the enterprises capita lize on the village’s resources (leftover rice leav es, foraged mushrooms, native knowledge, and people) at the same time as they prioritize hiring and paying people from their village and others nearby communities, keeping money and jobs in the region. The germ cell of “keeping it in the village” became so important that the village now teaches its children about savings and interest through a savings program in the primary school. All children entering the school get a savings spreadsheet to track how much money they save during primary school with interest calculated monthly. Every week children bring in allowance or earnings from small jobs like traditional dance performances  at the village homestay. “It’s become a tradition of savin g, everyone has to bring their own cash to school, it’s like creating a discipline and habit of saving for the kids,” (interview, 2020) a teacher explained. Over the course of 7 years (K-6), some children even save enough to pay for secondary school expenses. Generativity in other domains Over the past two decades the germ cell, “keeping it in the village,” has further developed as villagers implemented concrete intraventions beyond financial initiatives. After six years of work on debt and financial man agement (with parallel efforts in education and computers),  the village sought to study (and then invest in) t heir environment as a source of income: After the research of 2544 [2001], we knew that our income mostly came from the forests and mountains. When we knew where the money came from, we became interested in the environment. It started after the research; the research told us to do so. (interview, 2020) This led to the development of sustainable water and fire management intraventions (see Fields et al., under review) which extended the germ cell to literally keeping water in the village as well as protecting the natur al resources the village had. Other intraventions extended the germ cell to keep health in the village by banning pesticides and herbicides and promoting organic farming. In more r ecent years, village leaders have recruited young p eople (pursuing education and careers in cities around Th ailand) to return to the village. Young people have  filled positions leading the savings and loans programs, m anaging the homestay program, teaching in the schoo l, managing dam maintenance, and running fire prevention initiatives. The village is quite literally seeking to keep their young people in the village, embodied most fully in a young people’s branch of the village council. Though space prevents us from exploring these examples in more depth, the germ cell which the village developed through iterative learning sequences of ascending to concre te change has become extremely generative in the mu ltiple intraventions the village has conducted over more t han two decades, beginning in the domain of finance s but stretching to the environment, health, and urban/rural migration.

## Discussion

ICLS2022 Proceedings 382 © ISLS In this paper, we documented how the germ cell “kee ping it in the village” emerged through Ban Samkha’ s collective analysis of financial problems. Before their transformative series of intraventions, villagers struggled financially even when provided with outside support such as the early “village fund” program sponsored by the government. They did not understand their financial  problems and their causes. Through iterative analy sis and collective reflection, villagers developed the germ  cell: a concept they could comprehend and apply ac ross different initiatives. Indeed, the germ cell gave t hem the means to understand the underlying issues o f their problems, organize and transform their reality over 24 years, becoming a “model village” in the country (Bruneau, 2009). Our analysis demonstrates that the germ cell played an important role in community-driven learning and formative interventions as members used it to communicate concepts in practice with each other as well as with outsiders, explaining and modeling plausible soluti ons to problem situations. Like the Brazilian commu nity discussed by Sannino and colleagues (2016), intraventions in Ban Samkha and the development of the germ cell were not researcher-led, but community-driven, with  support from outside organizations, resulting in m ultiple generative, practical, and transformative intraventions by the community for the community. Iteratively modeling the germ cell was key as villagers developed empowe ring abilities (Freire & Macedo, 1987) to study and understand their problems, think critically, and en act change. The community-driven learning and decad es of work of Ban Samkha provides an alternative to the large (and often unsuccessful) efforts of other more top-down development initiatives that do not focus on local understanding and leadership. While iteratively developing the germ cell “keeping it in the village”, the villagers transformed the social and material settings of their community. They appl ied means of collective analysis and reflection, lo cally adapting methods (e.g., the philosophy of Thai Cons tructionism) and tools (e.g., spreadsheets, notational forms of household accounting, models of a community bank) that they learned from other people and organizat ions. One crucial element was the creation of village-wid e policies that mandated that everyone participated  in initiatives such as the Household Accounting Program or later banning all herbicides and pesticides from village farms. These tools and policies (mandated or otherwise) bring up the role of infrastructures in the village’s work. Engeström and colleagues (2012) recognize the importance of infrastructure in ascending from the abstract to the concrete by calling for “further analysis of the roles and potentials of mediational artifacts in actions and activities” (p. 304). Elsewhere we have begun to explore the se ries of policies that the village established that supported collective change even if some members were resistant (Fields et al., under review). While it is beyond the scope of this paper to fully account for and explore the role of representational and social infrastructures (Ma & Hall,

2018) that supported the village’s generative work,  we note this as an area for further research. Stud ying how

germ cells, or conceptual practices, evolve over lo ng periods and how, in the process of ascending fro m the abstract to the concrete, learners transform repres entational and social infrastructures that can supp ort the generativity of germ cells themselves, is important  for better understanding and supporting community-led transformative change.

Our analysis complements studies that focus in more detail on the first years of germ cell development, providing insights into how a community can evolve,  adapt, and iteratively apply a germ cell over time  and in practice. The “it” of “keeping it in the village” came to mean not just leaked out money, but investments, interest, loans, expertise, water, environmental resources, and people. The longitudinal lens of the study (achieved in large part through the detailed record keeping and collec tive memory of the villagers) provides insights int o the generativity of the germ cell and was essential in seeing its continued, iterative development. Ban Samkha constitutes an existence proof of the power of emancipatory collective action (Gutierrez, & Jurow, 2016; Freire, 1973) in which communities solve complex problems by learning, collaborating, and getting to the bottom of long-established oppression schemes. By reading and writing the world with a new and expanding lens— keeping it in the village—they transformed the village and transformed themselves.
