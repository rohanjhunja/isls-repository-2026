# The Making of Competent Outsiders: Using Epistemic Practices of Science to Enhance Student’s Critical Evaluation of Misleading Scientific Explanations in Classrooms

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Eroding public trust in science has become a critical challenge due to the proliferation of misleading scientific explanations in the media. Scholars recommend integrating scientific epistemic practices into classrooms to help students become competent outsiders—citizens who can critically evaluate misleading scientific explanations without being subject matter experts. However, limited empirical evidence exists on how teaching these practices influences students’ evaluation of misleading scientific claims. This study examines the performance of four high school students during a two-week inquiry unit where they used scientific epistemic practices to construct explanations for containing an epidemic similar to COVID-19. Students then evaluated misleading scientific explanations on three newspaper articles. Analysis of their pre-and post-responses revealed that students improved in identifying and critiquing erroneous representation of science in these articles. We discuss the implications of using epistemic practices to develop competent outsiders in classrooms.

## Introduction

Recent proposals to support youth in becoming competent outsiders suggest placing epistemic practices of science at the core of inquiry-based learning (Chinn et al., 2023; Duschl, 2020; Yoon et al., 2023). Epistemic practices of science refer to the agreed-upon methods and approaches that guide knowledge bu ilding within the discipline. These include both cognitive and metacognitive proc esses (e.g., determining what counts as high-quality evidence, identifying markers of accuracy and precision) as well as social processes (e.g., comparing methods and claims with other scientists to resolve disagreemen ts) (Duschl, 2020; Kelly & Licona, 2018). Put simpl y, these are the practices scientists use to construct reliable explanations about the world. Scholars propose that engaging students in the epistemic practices of science is l ikely to deepen their understanding of why scientif ic processes produce trustworthy conclusions, enabling them to m ake informed decisions  and appropriately defer to claims supported by scientific consensus (Osborne & Allchin, 2024; Osborne & Pimentel, 2022). For the last four years, we have collaborated with high school science teachers from the USA and India to co-design curriculum units that center scientific epistemic practices in classroom inquiry (Author et al., 2023).

In this paper, we focus on four students who partic ipated in an eight-day epidemic unit at a charter school in Northeast USA. These students used data from an age nt-based simulation to design experiments and generate explanations on which mitigation factor (e.g., mask ing, handwashing, distancing, lockdown and/or vacci nation) would help contain the epidemic. After the unit, students were assessed on their ability to evaluate c onflicting scientific explanations on unrelated topics (e.g., low-carb diets and ginkgo supplements), with assessment s administered before and after the unit. Through ana lyzing these assessments and student interviews, th is study aims to answer: How did students’ evaluation of misleading scientific explanations change?

## Background

Shaping competent outsiders Feinstein (2011) defines competent outsiders as individuals who are not scientists but can recognize when science is relevant to their personal or social lives. In situations where scientific explanations conflict, competent outsiders may critically assess these claims to make informed decisions. They rely on broader markers, such as the presence of multiple studies, coherence with scientific cons ensus, and the reputation of sources, to judge the reliability of scientific information.

An emerging debate in science education questions w hether focusing on epistemic practices of science can effectively prepare students to engage as compe tent outsiders, particularly when evaluating confli cting or misleading scientific explanations outside t he classroom. Some scholars argue that engaging stu dents in the iterative processes used by scientists to construct explanations is essential to fostering an informed trust in science (Covitt & Anderson 2021; Yoon  et al., 2023). However, some scholars question whet her teaching epistemic practices of science is sufficient to equip students to assess misleading scientific explanations (Osborne & Allchin, 2024; Osborne & Pimentel, 2024; Feinstein & Wadding ton, 2020). While Osborne and Pimentel (2024) acknowledge the value of emphasizing epistemic prac tices in science inquiry, they argue that students may struggle to fully assess the reliability of scienti fic explanations without addressing the influence o f their own epistemic beliefs and ways of knowing. Feinstein and Waddington (2020) argue that students must have opportunities in the classroom to critically examine the nature of scientific explanations and consider the broader societal contexts in which these explanations are produced and used. Without this contextual understanding, they state that students are likely to strugg le to apply epistemic practices effectively when na vigating complex or misleading scientific claims in everyday life. This  highlights a critical gap in preparing students to  navigate the complexities of evaluating misleading scientific explanations in everyday life. Conceptualizing epistemic practices of science in classroom inquiry In this study, epistemic practices of science serve d as the foundational instructional tool for develo ping students as competent outsiders. To address the dual goals o f science instruction —helping students (1) understand why science is reliable and (2) engage critically with science as competent outsiders—we draw on the AIR framework (Chinn et al., 2023). To operationalize these concepts in the classroom, the curriculum highlighted specific reliable processes: (1) accounting for multiple explanatory variables, (2) systematically evaluating evidence when addressing conflicting claims, and (3) resolving di sagreements by seeking new evidence.  As outlined in the introduction, this study examines student performan ce on assessments developed as part of a larger NSF-funded project focused on integrating epistemic practices into high school science instruction (Yoon et al., 2023). The epidemic unit consisted of eight 45-minute lessons designed to highlight the epistemolo gical practices that make science trustworthy. The unit also aimed to help students transfer their understanding of these practices to critically evaluate scientific information encountered on social media (Chinn et al., 2023; Yoon et al., 2023).

## Methods

Description of the newspaper articles The assessments consisted of three newspaper articles with conflicting explanations on two topics: the efficacy of ginkgo supplements in enhancing cognitive abilities  and the effectiveness of low-carb diets for weight loss. All three articles presented conflicting claims on the topics. The first article explicitly claimed that ginkgo su pplements or low-carb diets were effective, but the evidence was based on a single study which used a control  measure. The second article argued that the supplements or low-carb diets were harmful, supporting this claim with  an analysis of 38 different experiments. The article acknowledged ongoing progress in understanding the effectiveness of low-carb diets, highlighting that while evidence is emerging, definitive conclusions have yet to be reached. For example, it concluded w ith, Are low-carb diets safe and effective? The answer to the qu estion is getting clearer. Low-carb diets may promote weight loss, but not more than low-fat diets. The third article attempted to present both sides of th e argument, discussing studies that showed both positive and ne gative outcomes of low-carb diets. However, the author displayed bias in evaluating the quality of evidenc e, favoring a study with 320 participants as "very good" while casting doubt on another with 300 participants as " not very trustworthy" due to its smaller sample siz e. Additionally, the author relied heavily on their pe rsonal anecdote of losing 30 pounds on a low-carb diet, encouraging read ers to try it themselves. This reliance on personal  experience, combined with inconsistent evaluation of evidence, undermined the article’s credibility. These topics were chosen as counterbalance measures to minimize potential bias. Students were asked to read each article, rate its reliability, and justify their evaluations. The assessment instruments were developed as parallel forms to measure the processes students used to evaluate the claims consistently in both pre-and post-assessment conditions. Each  form featured similar contexts and conflicting claims on ginkgo supplements and low-carb diets to enhance face validity. The topics wer e counterbalanced to alternate across pre-and post-assessments, with students evaluating articles on g inkgo supplements before the epidemic unit and articles on low-carb diets after the unit. The assessments were designed by the research team in 2023 and underwent refineme nt through a Cog Lab (Willig, 2013)—a-controlled setting used to study cognitive processes such as perception, memory, attention, and decision-making.

## Participants

For this study, we examined the engagement of four students from Elara’s classroom. Elara taught the e pidemic unit in ninth grade as a nature of science unit in a private school in Northeast USA. The four student s examined in this study are: Joy, Anahita, Jess and Quinn. These students were nominated by Elara. She selected them as she identified the group represented students who could articulate their thinking and ask questions when needed. Elara was the only teacher who implemented lesson 8, whic h focused explicitly on evaluating conflicting scientific explanations in the popular media. This made her cl assroom an ideal setting for studying the role of s cientific epistemic practices in constructing and critiquing scientific claims. Additionally, these four students consented to participate in individual interviews where the research team revisited some of the same questions from the written assessments. The  interviews provided an opportunity to explore their  reasoning in greater depth, particularly regarding their rankings of the magazine articles, which help ed the researchers cross-validate themes emerging from the assessments.

## Data Analysis

The lead authors observed the classroom implementation of the epidemic unit, focusing on the interactions of four students who worked together to generate claims about how to contain the epidemic. We analyzed two main data sources: (1) pre-and post-assessments, and (2) individual student interviews. Each interview lasted about 45 minutes and included questions such as, what do scientists do when they disagree on claims? and how would you rate the reliability of the article on the low-carb diet, and why? We used a constant comparative method (Glaser, 2008) to analyze the data from these sources. The analysis began with an inductive review of the pre-and post-assessment responses by the first author, who identified initial themes separately for each datas et. In the pre-assessments, students often relied on the tone of the articles and personal anecdotes as indicators o f reliability. We also noted minimal references to the scientific epistemic practices targeted through the epidemic unit. These initial themes formed the basis for our coding, which we then compared with post-assessment responses to detect changes in students’  evaluation criteria.  Next, two additional research team members cross-checked the themes from the post-assessments against the interview responses. This triangulation helped ensure consistency across data sources and strengthened the reliability of our analysis. We identified recurring themes that highlighted changes in how students evaluated the articles from preto post-assessment. Finally, an external expert in scientific epistemic  practices, not involved in the initial coding, rev iewed the analysis. When disagreements arose, we consulte d this expert to validate our approach, resolving a ny differences through discussion. This process helped ensure the credibility and robustness of our findings.

## Findings

Students demonstrated a shift from superficial heuristics to markers that aligned with evidence evaluation when evaluating misleading scientific explanations Students exhibited significant improvements in their ability to evaluate misleading scientific explanations, shifting from reliance on superficial markers to a focus on the nature and quality of scientific evidence. In t he pre-responses, students often used unreliable markers s uch as tone and personal anecdotes to judge the rel iability of claims and rarely referenced the evidence mentioned in the magazine articles. For example, Anahita ranked article three as most reliable because of its “neutral tone”. She explained her rationale by stating that, “This article seems the most neutral when it comes to rating the ginkgo  supplements and provides the most evidence along w ith cautioning readers about possible misinformation wh en it comes to the evidence” (06/10/2023). In her explanation, she clarified that the article was mor e trustwo rthy than other articles as it was transparent abou t another study that had contested evidence. However,  she did not reference the inconsistent evaluation of the studies in article three. As stated in the methods section, while both studies were methodolog ically comparable, the author of the article evaluated them inconsistently, favoring one study over the other without clear justification. Similar to Anahita, Quinn rated article three as th e most reliable as well. He justified his ranking w ith “they [authors] have a logical order and used resea rch and were transparent of positive and negative” (06/10/2023). Both Anahita and Quinn, did not refer ence any details on how the claims were being estab lished though erroneous comparisons. They also ranked arti cle three, which referenced only two studies more r eliable than article two which presented an explanation fro m a scientific source that reviewed 38 different studies.  Jess, too, ranked Article 3 as most reliable. However, un like the Anahita and Quinn, she felt it was more re liable because the author of the magazine backed the claim  with their personal experience, in addition to the neutral tone. She justified her ranking by stating, “This is a good article because it tells you both sides. One study reports it works and the other it doesn’t. And the author m entions it worked for them. That is personal experience which should be counted” (06/10/2023). These initial responses reveal a reliance on erroneous markers that do not align with the critical evaluation skills necessary for competent outsiders. Instead o f examining the robustness of the evidence, students focused on superficial cues such the tone of the article (e.g., neutrality of the statement and the presenting of both sides of the argument) and menti on of personal experience with the topic by the aut hor to discern trustworthiness of the claims. This signals  to student’s use of erroneous markers to evaluate scientific explanations when presented with contesting claims.

In their post-responses, however, students demonstrated a marked shift toward engaging with the nature of explanation and quality of evidence backing thes e explanations. All students changed their rankings  of the magazine articles, ranking the article that  included a personal anecdote as less reliable due t o its potential to mislead readers, while ranking article two, which i ncluded a meta-review of 38 different studies, as the most reliable. This shift indicates a promising improvem ent in how students were  evaluating conflicting scientific explanations. For example, Anahita explained her revised ranking stating, “This article takes a lot of experiments and their results into account before compiling the ir own conclusion based on all the data” (06/24/202 3). Additionally, she re-evaluated article one —which she had previously ranked highest —and now rated it as the least reliable, explaining: This article is not good. First of all, it is kind of common sense for someone else's experience to not be evidence that something is good or not be cause those experiences differ from person to person. Additionally, they weren't very detailed about the results of the experiment or how it was conducted at all, so it is hard to understand t he possible limitations of the experiment to further our understanding of the diet. Therefore, t his article is too informal to be credible. (06/24/2023) In her post-evaluation, Anahita accurately critiques article th ree based on its lack of detail regarding the experimental design. She also begins by dismissing the personal anecdote, arguing that individual expe rience is insufficient as reliable evidence. This is a stark change from her pre-response. In summary, students’ postresponses demonstrated an increased capacity to app ly scientific epistemic practices in their evaluati ons. They accurately identified article three as misleading a nd consistently rated article two as the most relia ble due to its comprehensive evidence base. This progression under scores their growing ability to discern reliable ev idence, engage in systematic evaluation, and align their reasoning with the markers of competent outsiders.

## Discussion

The findings of this study indicate a promising shi ft in how students evaluate misleading scientific e xplanations after engaging with a curriculum that emphasized ep istemic practices of science. Prior to the inquiry-based unit, students often relied on sup erficial cues, such as the neutral tone of the arti cle or the presence of personal anecdotes, when judging the reliability of conflict ing scientific claims. In contrast, their post-responses demonstrated a notable change: students referred mo re frequently to the quality of the evidence and, crucially, acknowledged the value of aggregated data from multiple studies. This highlights a growing appreciation among students that the reliability of scientific explana tions often depends on the weight of evidence from numerous studies rather than isolated findings. This shift in focus—from superficial markers to evidence-based reasoning— marks an important step in student’s development as competent outsiders. Competent outsiders are characterized by their ability to evaluate the trustworthiness of scientific claims using reliable indicators, such as the aggregation of evidence and consistency across studies (Author et al., 2023; Feinstein, 2011). While students did not explicitly mention scientific consensus in their evaluations, their preference for explanations supported by broader evidence bases suggests an emerging understanding of the pri nciples that underpin scientific reliability. These  findings underscore the potential of curricula that emphasiz e scientific epistemic practices to cultivate critical evaluation skills, enabling students to engage with complex scientific claims in both academic and everyday contexts. The emphasis on aggregated evidence in this study provides a strong foundation for fostering competent outsiders, as it aligns with key markers such as reliance on multiple studies and coherence with broader evidence (Duncan et al., 2018; Osborne & Pimentel, 2024). Ho wever, developing a nuanced understanding of scient ific consensus requires more than recognizing aggregated evidence; it necessitates explicit instruction on the roles of expertise, bias, and the iterative nature of inquiry. Future curricula should expand on these elements by connecting epistemic practices to the broader societal and con textual factors that influence scientific explanations (Feinstein & Waddington, 2020). Such approaches align with calls to engage students in evaluating the credibility of sources, integrating diverse lines of evidence, and grapplin g with the provisional nature of scientific conclus ions. These instructional shifts could help bridge the gap betw een classroom inquiry and the competencies required  to critically engage with scientific claims in everyday contexts.
