# From Data to Meaning: Cognitive Resources for Interpreting CO2 Time-Series Graphs

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Investigating how individuals understand climate data is crucial for improving the instruction of climate change. The Knowledge in Pieces framework posits that individuals’ knowledge, including climate knowledge, is separated into pieces, which remain isolated or are connected in productive and unproductive ways. We investigated how an individual connects their knowledge about climate change to graphs representing atmospheric carbon dioxide (CO2) concentrations over various timescales. Our ongoing analyses suggest that individuals connected more recent rises in CO 2 to their cognitive resources about human induced climate change, in mostly productive ways.

Visualizations of CO2 over time, such as the Keeling curve (Keeling et al., 2001), powerfully communicate how CO2 levels have oscillated for hundreds of thousands of years, with a recent spike around the start of the Industrial Revolution (Nunes, 2023). Even though abundant data supports anthropogenic climate change, the issue remains highly politicized, and individuals often hold alternative conceptions (Chang & Pascua, 2015; Fleming et al., 2021; Wachholz et al., 2014). The Knowledge-In-Pieces theory posits that individuals have fragmented knowledge: small, context-dependent ideas (diSessa, 1993). Alternative climate  change conceptions may be derived from a fragmented understanding of climate, existing in isolated pieces rather than in a coherent system (Harlow & Bianchini, 2025; Zummo, 2024), e.g., interpreting a graph of CO 2 concentration as oscillating and thus attributing CO2 increases as a byproduct of natural cycles. Our aim was to qualitatively investigate individuals’ knowledge structures while interpreting graphs of CO2 concentration across timescales: the past 2 years, 65 years, and 800,000 years. How do individuals make sense of CO2 concentrations over time? What does this reveal about their understanding of climate change? Twenty-five (of planned 75) undergrads aged M(SD)  = 22.68(4.58) were recruited at a public university in the Los Angeles area. Each completed the study in-person on a lab computer for 45-60 minutes, receiving $15 USD. Figure 1 Graphs of Atmospheric CO2 Concentrations Over Time As part of a longer study, participants were presented with four graphs of CO 2 concentrations in the atmosphere over the past 2 years, 65 years, and 800,000 years (Fig. 1). The graph for the past 800,000 years was presented in two parts: one graph had the past 100,000 years removed for participants to fill in a continued trend (see Lee et al., 2023), and the next showed the actual data for the past 100,000 years. For each graph, participants were asked to describe the shape of the graph, then select from given shapes:  increasing, decreasing, concave up, concave down, flat, fluctuating, repeating, linear, random and unclear. Then, they described why they believed each graph had that shape, requiring them to pull from their climate knowledge. Our inductive coding approach identified cognitive resources (i.e. the smallest meaningful phrase expressing a unique piece of knowledge) within the supplied reasons for why the graph in question had a particular 2848 shape (see upper green nodes, Fig. 2). Two authors independently coded connections made by participants between the selected shape of the graph (orange nodes, Fig. 2) and these cognitive resources. Two others resolved any disagreements from the first two.

For this current study, we qualitatively analyzed the open-text responses for the first twenty-five participants. The reasons each participant supplied for each graph’s shape primarily fell under categories related to either humancaused or natural factors (see Fig. 2). Given space constraints, we focus here on the causes attributed to CO2 levels before the earliest known structures were built (i.e., 800,000 to 100,000 years ago, Fig. 2C) and after (i.e., 100,000 years ago to now, Fig. 2D).

The most common trends reported in the graph of the CO 2 levels from 800,000 to 100,000 years ago involved fluctuating (n = 22) and curved up (n = 18) patterns. Of the given reasons for these shapes, the most commonly articulated was non-human (Earth’s natural climate shifts), suggesting comprehension of the intervention material or productive use of cognitive resources. However, five participants  connected human activity to various shapes; since this graph describes trends from a time before early human st ructures (and well before the industrial revolution), this knowledge structure reflects the activation of a cognitive resource that is unproductive for this temporal context.

Strikingly different trends emerged from the interpretation of the graph that revealed the past 100,000 years. For this fuller timescale graph, the most common trend observed was to describe the graph’s pattern as curved up (n = 24) and increasing (n = 22), and these shapes were most commonly attributed to human activity (n = 16).  By contrast, non-human reasons became notably less frequent (n ≤ 2). This study provides insight into the kinds of cognitive resources individuals activate when making sense of climate data. Both human activity and natural fluctuations of CO 2 levels were supplied as reasons to support why a graph has a particular shape, in both productive and unproductive ways. Our results are preliminary, and we will gather and code more data from 50 more participants to gain a better understanding of the knowledge structures underlying interpretations of graphical representations of CO 2 concentrations over time. Additionally, we aim to further investigate the coherence and consistency of each individual’s knowledge structures across their interpretations of the four graphical representations. Understanding how individuals make sense of clim ate data is crucial in informing future instruction about Earth’s rapidly changing climate. Subsequent analysis can offer insights for future work related to designing interventions that help individuals better connect a graph’s visual pattern to a scientifically valid cause. Such interventions could be crucial in cultivating causal reasoning and graph literacy in climate science contexts.

We close by noting that Keeling et al.’s (2001) CO 2 measurements come in part from the Mauna Loa Observatory on the island of Hawai’i, built in the 1950s by the colonizing U.S. government. As climate change disproportionately threatens Indigenous people (Crook et al., 2018), including the Kānaka Maoli of H awai’i, it is essential that we seek restorative justice (Sproat, 2016) and emphasize Indigenous science for climate resilience (Frazier et al., 2024; Hernandez, 2022).
