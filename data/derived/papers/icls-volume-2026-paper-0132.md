# Creating Artifacts for Expansive Science Teaching through Art-Based Collaboration

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
The need for newer ways to enact human-nature relationships calls for educational practices to expand their boundaries of learning. Several educational initiatives have engaged in integrating arts-based practices with science learning to broaden the scope and aims of learning. However, the creation of artifacts that mediate the processes by which science educators can expand their pedagogical repertoire to engage with the emergent possibilities of arts-based practices has received less attention. This study describes collaborative processes between an artist and a pre-service teacher, Becca, which inspired her attempts to reimagine her teaching aims and practices, as part of a summer camp research –design partnership on a community urban farm. The study highlights how the evolution of pedagogical artifacts through collaborations made space for emergent learning anchored in embodied experiences.

Recent years have witnessed a growing need for STEM education to engage with complex  dimensions of socioecological issues in creative and innovative ways. Within STEM learning, efforts to create more expansive, critical opportunities have led to widespread movements to incorporate art practices as STEAM (Colucci-Gray, 2019). Art-based practices are creative pursuits that encourage the  suspension of habitual  thinking in favor of divergent responses,  opening up spaces to consider fundamental questions about nature, place, histories and possible futures (Graham, 2007). Within the field of Learning Sciences, a special issue edited by Halverson and Sawyer (2022) explored a range of art-based activities in formal and  informal spaces to describe the liberatory, agentic and rich sense-making possibilities made tangible through embodied, situated and collaborative arts practices. Seitamaa-Hakkarainen (2022) further argues that arts-based knowledge creation involves non-linear pedagogy as learners gather skills and ideas from diverse sources, often coming up with emergent outcomes rather than pre-determined content. Connecting the potential of art practices to foster expanded understandings of the nature-human relationship, Liukkonen et al. (2023) explore bio-art making as a way to interact with nature through expansive material, emotional and cognitive capacities.  Foregrounding the ways in which art and STEM are interrelated in everyday objects, Tsurusaki et al. (2024) conceptualize “STEAM objects” as a pedagogical activity to analyze how educators interact with material agency to make connections with land, their professions, and historicity. However, relatively few  studies have explored the evolution of artifacts and opportunities available to  educators to develop the required pedagogical fluidity, as creating these learning environments also requires a creative disposition among teachers, and the ability to embrace the uncertain processes marking such open-ended explorations (Turkka et al., 2017). Attending to artifacts of pedagogical practices In this study, we focus on the evolution of material artifacts accompanying and shaping pedagogical practices from a science teacher’s perspective to  design art-centred activities. Theoretically, we draw  on insights establishing the centrality of material and psychological artifacts in shaping learning experiences (Cole et al., 2014; Vygotsky, 1980) and how material engagement enables and scaffolds the development of social interactions. Socio-cultural theories of learning highlight the processual and relational significance of artifacts that emerge through interaction rather than pre-determined attributes. Various studies have explored the mediating and co-constitutive role of physical and ideational artifacts in embedding narratives that form the basis for collective learning (Cole & Engeström, 1993; Vossoughi et al., 2021). Focusing on collaborations between a science teacher and an artist, this study asks: How did artifacts of pedagogical practices evolve and open up spaces to challenge narrow definitions of knowing and understanding? This study describes a collaboration between a pre-service teacher, Becca and an artist, in her attempts to reimagine her teaching aims and practices as part of a summer camp research–design partnership on a community urban farm for racialized children and refugees focusing on Land-based (Styres, 2011) learning opportunities. 1144

## Context and methodology

We draw on methodological approaches from participatory design experiments that foreground the active involvement of participants as co-designers of educational practices and teaching-learning artifacts (Gutiérrez & Jurow, 2016). The approach helps in understanding the collaborative design process itself as a site of inquiry by positioning learning as a socially, culturally, and materially situated process. This paper is a part of a larger study based on a research-design partnership on a community urban farm (Takeuchi et al., 2021) called Soil Camp (SC; See www.soilcamp.ca), which began as a summer camp during the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020, in response to an invitation from a local refugee resettlement agency. The broad idea was to explore forms of landbased healing and learning for the children, most of whom have been forcibly displaced from their homelands and come from agricultural backgrounds. The project is currently in its fifth year of the partnership, and in the past five years, a total of 135 children (aged 5– 15 years) and  39 facilitators, including 21 teachers/teacher candidates, joined our program. Socioenvironmental visions for reconnection to the land, soil, plants, animals, and each other form the core of our network with teachers, community workers, researchers, youth, and children. This study focuses on the reflection of one pre-service teacher, Becca, who participated in SC as a facilitator in 2025. SC in 2025 included the participation in community-led events, seven designed sessions spanning 3-5 hours at the community farm, teacher professional development sessions, including a field trip to a site significant for the history of  settler colonialism.  We also collaborated  with a local science centre for the efforts of resurgence of Indigenous science knowledge, in which Becca played a key collaborative role. Becca facilitated three sessions, with other sessions facilitated by an artist, an Indigenous Elder, sustainability researchers and practitioners, and urban growers. All sessions were video-recorded, accompanied by photos and extensive field notes. For the study, we reviewed specific video episodes focusing on Becca’s facilitation (6.42 hours), along with the activity facilitated by Artist Chanda (4.07 hours), to explore how the interactions affected Becca’s subsequent design and pedagogical enactments. The selection of videos was informed by a detailed interview and informal conversations with Becca, in which she described instances that helped her critically rethink the content and artifacts used in her facilitation. Interviews (1.56 hours across two online meetings) and facilitation planning with Chanda also helped understand the pedagogical choices she made for the session. A WhatsApp group for facilitators was created to help with logistics, share photos, or post spontaneous reflections from different sessions. The WhatsApp chat logs were also analyzed to describe the collaborative nature of the pedagogical design, through informal discussions and information shared by other facilitators in the group. encounter Becca was chosen as a focal participant for the following reasons: She was one of the most engaged facilitators in 2025, having facilitated three out of the seven sessions. She joined the project team  in 2024, and was familiar with the space and broader values of the summer camp towards nurturing attentive, relational, and embodied Land-based experiences. She recently completed her pre-service teacher education courses, and thus was freshly inducted into the formal education system. She has trained to teach middle and high school science, with a masters in Biology prior to her teaching-training degree. It was thus particularly interesting to observe her views as a science teacher change over a period of time through encounters at SC. Becca was also open and comfortable reflecting on her experiences, including perceived doubts and insecurities, thus contributing to an authentic account based on mutual trust and respect.

The first session, facilitated by Becca before Chanda’s activity, was called the ‘underwear decomposition activity,’ in which children would bury cotton underwear in different spots on the land and discuss the level of microbial activity in the soil based on the extent to which the underwear had decomposed after two months or so. Initially, Becca had made a PowerPoint presentation to provide visual cues and planned to show it on a laptop. However, after conversations with the authors, it became clear that it would be difficult for children to see the screen in direct sunlight, as the activities were taking place out doors. Becca took large prints of the slides to support her explanation. Despite the richness of the content and the interesting analogies drawn by Becca in comparing the digestive system to the soil matter that decomposes organic matter, she noticed that children were losing interest in the lengthy explanations. The use of many unfamiliar terms, such as ‘microbes’ and ‘compaction’, also made the explanations somewhat difficult to follow in the absence of repeated, slowed-down, embodied, and emplaced  descriptions of key terms for the children, many of whom were multilingual learners with English  as a later-acquired language. The following week, she observed  the contrast  in children’s engagement with Chanda’s activity when she saw the freedom they had been given to explore with the medium. Commenting on the experience being an important reflection point for her she said, “I think it was day three where we did like the dyeing workshop and then just  seeing like how excited kids were for that and how everybody like all across all ages were like involved. It kind of made me think like, oh, OK, like this is really cool. Like how do I get like this sort of like level of engagement? And I felt like that lesson was really key for like changing like my approach.” 1145 The subsequent lessons designed by Becca in collaboration with other facilitators were based on using pH-sensitive natural colors. Chanda’s art focuses on experimental processes, material transformation and  thinking through making. Recently, Chanda has been doing creative placemaking in their  local community garden, working alongside gardeners and other  artists to find and distill colors from garden plants. She gradually got interested in developing inks from natural sources as  a way to connect with the culture and histories of the land. Chanda likes to make complex ideas more accessible through hands-on experiences, and believes that plants have a lot to teach us about the land, our histories, and ourselves. Given the resonance of Chanda’s approach with SC’s vision of connecting with Land, listening to silenced histories and attuning ourselves to the ecological dimensions of the place (see project website for more details), Chanda was invited to facilitate a session at SC, where she conducted an Indigo dyeing activity with participants. Chanda was very intentional about the materials used in the session, and had detailed discussions with the authors to deliberate over the implementation of the activity. The activity involved making print designs on recycled cotton scraps using clay-resist and immersing the cloth in a vat of indigo dye. The children would then be able to observe the dyed cloth turning green and then blue as the dye would oxidize in contact with air. Chanda was keen that participants are able to witness the change in color. She was careful to use natural materials even in the preparation of the prints, to ensure that the participants could freely interact with the medium. “the clay is totally safe for the kids. It just has Epsom salt clay and water and some gum arabic. Like, it's totally safe to touch. So, um, so that I would feel comfortable so they could really get their hands dirty literally with that, and they, they can go off.” Nitrile gloves were provided for older children and adult participants to dip the clay resist cloths in the indigo vat, as the dye is caustic and can cause skin irritation. However, the children were able to help in rinsing the dyed cloth in water to get the final color. While conducting the session, other facilitators were encouraged to participate along with the children. Spontaneous  conversations around dyeing and textile traditions from different countries grew, as many participants and facilitators are immigrants from other countries. Following Chanda’s session, Becca made noticeable efforts to make material interactions the focal points of her activities. Inspired by the materials used by Chanda, Becca explored the idea of creating natural dyes. In a conversation with another facilitator, she mentioned how Chanda’s activity motivated her to look for natural colors, and they discussed how the dyes can be further boiled to create paints too. In the first iteration, Becca wanted to get into more detailed explanations of pH change, but later allowed questions to emerge more spontaneously based on children’s experience with the natural colors, and let the observed phenomena guide subsequent learning. For instance, she went out of her way to make butterfly flower extract ice cubes that could 1146 be added to the lemonade, and the latter would change color, turning violet. She mentioned being inspired by the hot weather and outdoor settings to design a refreshing break that would also act as an opportunity to connect with the dyeing activities. The participants were fascinated by the change in color, and the children dubbed it “galaxy lemonade”, observing the swirls of violet forming as the ice cubes melted in the lemonade. The “why” and “how” of the phenomena became much more interesting for the older children once they were able to observe changes in real time. The first author shared pictures of the butterfly pea flower from her homeland, where it is a native plant, to help participants make a connection with the color source. Becca explained that she became more interested in following children’s curiosities and appreciating the affordances of the space rather than trying to control specific outcomes. The evolving openness then allowed her to explore pedagogical opportunities of unintended events. Commenting on the accidental spilling of a glass of natural color on the table during the third session, she said, “My favorite moment was just like when Farah was just like dipping her finger in the lemon juice and then drawing on the table and seeing it (blue butterfly pea colour) change… yeah that was like amazing I was just like oh my god like you know you don't plan for this in a lesson but it happened so naturally and you're like oh this is this is what I you know what I want as a teacher …” Similarly, an unexpected outgrowth of inky cap mushrooms on the land following the rains led to an impromptu foraging activity by the facilitators and participants. The mushrooms decompose into a dark liquid (hence the name). The color was used for the next iteration of facilitation along with additional naturally derived colors such as onion skin, red  cabbage, and cherry juice. Unlike other colors, Inky cap extract is not sensitive to change in pH, and Becca thought that it would be a nice contrast to other colors. Children were allowed to freely explore with different mixing strategies and make their own observations. The materials anchored spontaneous collective learning moments, with other facilitators labelling the inks in different languages. One of the unexpected observations during the third facilitation session was the attraction of flies to the sweet cherry juice extract, which created patterns as they landed on the portions painted with cherry juice. Reflecting on how her time at SC had pushed her to challenge the centrality of information dissemination, Becca remarked, “I think especially like with the art like me engaging with all these like art-based like workshops and SC it's made me realize like how you know a lesson just becomes more interesting when like kids are able to like connect to it and like bring themselves to it because I think often with science it's like we often we give information but we don't give a lot of room for kids to bring themselves to it you know” art-based collaborative environments Several studies have explored the integration of science and arts processes in education. However, teachers inducted into the practices prevalent within the formal schooling system are habituated to conveying canonical knowledge and find it hard to question or push the prescribed boundaries of textbook content (Dutta, 2023). This case study illustrates possible pathways for teachers to question their own norms and expectations through exploring the reflections emanating from Becca’s immersive participation in Chanda’s art-making session. The discipline boundary-crossing experience (between science and art in this case) created opportunities for co-design that were mediated by the artifacts and pedagogical perspectives introduced by Chanda. These explorations, grounded in the larger themes of SC towards connecting with the Land (Takeuchi  et al., 2021) allowed Becca to attend to the artifacts of facilitation, and slow down to favor deep engagement rather than providing information about an established body of knowledge. The intentional pedagogical slowness was reflected in Becca’s approach to sourcing and creating the materials as well.  The process then also legitimized and encouraged her to attend to the affective and aesthetic underpinnings of teacher learning (Jaber, 2021) through responding to the generative affordances  and uncertainty of the outdoor space. The gathering and designing of the artifacts themselves became an  anchor for collective learning among the facilitators. Becca explained that her gradual explorations and material experimentations, along with peers, have inspired her to create similar outdoor activities in her formal space of teaching as well. With a growing moral imperative to recentre educational inquiry along relational, interdependent frames encompassing humans and more-than-humans through networks of reciprocity and responsibility, reimagining teacher agency forms a crucial part of such visions. This study illustrates a possible pathway to support expansive pedagogies through art-based collaborations, which centre artifacts as constitutive of onto-epistemic experiences.
