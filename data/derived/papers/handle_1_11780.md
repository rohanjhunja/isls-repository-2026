# To Be or Not to Be: A Study on Shakespeare, Incarcerated Youth, and Future-Orientation

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This ongoing study examines how the Shakespeare Behind Bars (SBB) arts education program impacts future orientation development in five incarcerated youth aged 16-18. SBB engages participants in creating original artistic performances based on Shakespearean works, adapting them into modern contexts through podcasts, songs, plays, and films. Using a mixed-methods approach combining qualitative, quantitative, and ethnographic data, this preliminary analysis focuses specifically on how participants' future orientation emerges and evolves through engagement with the program's creative processes. This initial study begins a larger corpus investigating how interdisciplinary arts education influences incarcerated youths' socio-emotional development across multiple domains. Drawing on practice-driven and design-based research methodology, the analysis examines how personal narrative-based creative projects contribute to participants' ability to envision and plan for their futures in four domains of future orientation we have identified: Professional, Educational, Social Emotional, and Survival-Based Future Orientation.. Early findings suggest that artistic engagement creates unique opportunities for processing past experiences while developing future-oriented thinking.

## Introduction and background

In a midwestern youth prison, incarcerated teens breathe new life into Shakespeare by adapting classic works into podcasts, songs, and plays that mirror their own experiences. This creative work may spark transformative mental and emotional changes that cultivate new trajectories in their lives. Future orientation development proves both challenging and crucial for incarcerated youth, who often face multiple barriers to envisioning and planning for their futures. This multidimensional construct, originally conceptualized by Nurmi (1991) as involving motivation, planning, and evaluation processes, has been further developed by researchers such as Steinberg et al. (2009) and Seginer (2009) to encompass cognitive, motivational, affective, and behavioral dimensions. Many incarcerated youth have experienced significant trauma, disrupted education, and unstable family relationships, which Abram et al. (2017) identify as factors that impair future planning abilities. While these youth frequently demonstrate "survival-based thinking" focused on immediate needs (Ford et al., 2012), research suggests that developing future orientation serves as a protective factor against recidivism. This aligns with Oyserman and Markus's (1990) concept of "possible selves," which highlights how individuals' projections of their future identities can motivate present behavior. Steinberg et al. (2015) found that incarcerated youth with higher levels of future orientation show improved outcomes in education, employment stability, and desistance from crime, underscoring Cauffman and Steinberg's (2000) work on the relationship between future orientation and decision-making in juvenile offenders. Our preliminary analysis identifies four types of future orientation that emerged or evolved in the SBB program: “Professional Future Orientation” involves career planning and workplace skills. “Social Emotional Future Orientation” focuses on relationship building, self-expression, and emotional regulation. “Educational Future Orientation” encompasses learning goals and skill development. “Survival-based Future Orientation” addresses post-release safety planning and gang/risk management. It also addresses how the youth perceive volunteer programs and how they leverage and negotiate their participation and presence in these programs to get things they desire in exchange. These categories often overlap and intersect with one another. The intersection of arts education and juvenile justice has gained prominence, with Shakespeare-based prison programs showing particular promise in rehabilitation efforts since the 1990s (Shailor, 2011; ScottDouglass, 2007). These programs have demonstrated success in both adult and juvenile facilities, addressing emotional regulation, social skills, and personal growth (Warner, 2004; Pensalfini, 2016). The research site is a mid to max security juvenile prison that houses approximately 35 residents aged 13-20 and faces typical institutional challenges including security requirements, resource limitations, and gang dynamics (Cheliotis & Jordanoska, 2016). While previous research has focused on immediate behavioral outcomes of arts programs (Hughes et al., 2019), this study examines how SBB's combination of classical literature, personal narrative, and multimedia production influences and responds to the emergence, evolution, or regression of participants' future-orientation development.

## Literature review

Theater arts, particularly in correctional settings, can significantly influence future orientation through multiple interconnected pathways (Shailor, 2011; Warner, 2004). At its core, theatrical engagement creates a unique space where participants can simultaneously inhabit their present reality while imagining and embodying different possible futures (Balfour, 2004). Through role-playing and character analysis, participants develop the crucial ability to envision alternative life trajectories and understand the relationship between choices and consequences (Thompson, 2003; McAvinchey, 2011). For example, when exploring Shakespearean characters' decisions and their outcomes, participants practice the cognitive skills necessary for long-term planning and future-focused thinking (Pensalfini, 2016).

The collaborative nature of theater production inherently builds future-oriented skills. As participants work toward a final performance, they engage in project planning, goal setting, and time management—practical skills that translate directly to future-oriented behaviors (Hughes et al., 2019). This process is particularly significant in correctional settings, where opportunities for long-term planning and goal achievement may be limited (Cheliotis & Jordanoska, 2016).

Theater's impact on identity development is especially relevant to future orientation. Through character exploration and personal narrative creation, participants can safely experiment with different versions of themselves and their potential futures (Landy & Montgomery, 2012; Nicholson, 2014). This process challenges fixed narratives about personal identity that may limit future possibilities (Davey et al., 2015). As participants create podcasts, plays, or songs based on their experiences, they actively engage in reconstructing their personal narratives, incorporating both past experiences and future possibilities (Cohen-Cruz, 2010). The development of emotional competencies through theater work further supports future orientation. As participants process past experiences through artistic expression, they develop emotional regulation skills and resilience—essential capabilities for engaging with future challenges (Djurichkovic, 2011; Anderson & Overy, 2010). The creation of an emotional vocabulary through theatrical work enables participants to better articulate their hopes, fears, and plans for the future (Liebmann, 2008). Through these various mechanisms, theater arts can serve as a powerful tool for developing future orientation, particularly among incarcerated youth who may struggle to envision positive future possibilities (Hughes, 2005; Meekums & Daniel, 2011). The combination of practical skill development, identity exploration, emotional growth, and community connection creates a comprehensive framework for nurturing future-oriented thinking and behavior (Desmond et al., 2021).

## Research questions

We want to know if the Shakespeare Behind Bars (SBB) program can help incarcerated youth develop future orientation. To that end, we ask:

• What practices of SBB help students develop future orientation?

• What kind of future orientation are students developing?

## Research Design Overview

This study uses mixed methods to examine how SBB influences future orientation among incarcerated youth, enabling data triangulation for comprehensive understanding (Creswell & Plano Clark, 2017).

The study follows five male participants (Nate, Von, Will, Arnie, and Shane) at a juvenile jail. The study also looks at the approaches of SBB teaching artists Jen Sinnen (a white LGBTQI+ female theatre teacher), Jason Boulware (a black male documentarian), Kevin Aoussou (a black American/West African male who specializes in Shakespearean theatre) reporting to Ozivell Ecford (a black male with Albinism who specializes in music, theatre, multimedia design, and community outreach). Ozivell is the SBB Lead Teaching Artist and the Lead Student Researcher. He works under the advisement of his Principal Investigator: Dr. Michael Horn and SBB founder: Curt Tofteland.

Participant selection involved a three-stage process: facility screening, voluntary program selection after a "Demo Day," and study opt-in following an "Informed Consent" workshop. For minors, parental consent was obtained through a Leisure Time Activity manager, while participants 18 or older provided their own consent. Participants had to be enrolled in the SBB program to be in the study. The SBB met two days a week for two hours on each day. The study is ongoing, so this paper only analyzes the first six weeks of the twelve week program.

## Data collection

Data collection throughout the 12-week program employs diverse methodological approaches to gather comprehensive insights. These include conducting semi-structured entry and exit interviews to understand participants' goals and aspirations, collecting workshop surveys called "Introspection Sheets," gathering teaching artists' observations via “Daily Activity Reports” (DARs), analyzing creative artifacts, and tracking quantitative metrics such as attendance and earned "cut-time" upon program completion. The Introspection Sheets are two-part surveys completed at the beginning and end of each workshop. In the first part, participants describe one aspect of their current appearance, promoting self-awareness about how they present themselves to the world. They also record their emotional state, acknowledging that all emotions are valid as long as participants take accountability for them. Finally, they set a specific goal they believe can be achieved by the end of the workshop. The second part, completed at the workshop's conclusion, asks participants to reflect on whether they met their goal and why, identify something positive about themselves or others, and reassess their emotional state. Both sections are shared verbally during opening and closing circles, helping staff gauge the group's climate, build participants' verbal communication skills, track emotional patterns and their causes, and monitor participants' ability to set and achieve goals throughout the program. The "Daily Activity Report" (DAR) is completed by staff after each workshop, comparable to a theatrical "Stage Manager's Report." This document serves multiple purposes: it communicates workshop events to prison administration, allows staff members to share observations and insights with each other, and provides the research team with additional observational data to identify, confirm, and better understand phenomena that may or may not have been directly observed by researchers themselves.

## Institutional context: sentence reduction ("cut-time") policy

The juvenile facility operates a sentence reduction system, commonly known as "cut-time," which rewards program participation. For programs like SBB, the facility typically awards 6-7 days of sentence reduction per month of participation. However, some participants are required by law to complete at least 80 percent of their sentence depending on their conviction, making them ineligible for "cut-time." The research team and study have absolutely nothing to do with any part of “cut-time” allocation. Iterations and group structure adaptations A key program adaptation occurred when interpersonal conflicts required splitting the original group into two cohorts: "The workshops had to be split into two from one because some students in the group could not get along in their living quarters... various youths got into a physical altercation on their wing and had to be separated" (Ecford, Field Notes, Oct 27, 2024). This shift, while challenging, created opportunities for deeper observation of group dynamics, aligning with Hughes et al.'s (2019) concept of "the usefulness of mess" in applied theatre research.

The team also discovered participants expressed themselves more freely verbally than in writing: "They don't like writing, so they usually write one word where at least two sentences are required... However, when we share responses... they verbally express more than they write" (Ecford’s Preliminary Analysis Document, 2024). This led to emphasizing oral responses, supporting Warner's (2004) findings about the importance of multiple expression modes in youth corrections.

Assessment adaptations The research team adapted assessment methods to prioritize verbal expression while maintaining written documentation for institutional requirements. Teaching artists began recording more detailed field notes during verbal sharing sessions, capturing nuances that participant’s written responses failed to convey. As noted by teaching artist Jen: "Students were laughing during one run of the monologue but they were really trying to take it seriously and I think gained a new respect for acting during it" (Sinnen, J., Daily Activity Report, Sept 15, 2024). This kind of observational data proved more valuable than traditional written assessments from the youth participants in understanding participants' engagement and development. The SBB teaching artists adapted their methodology for personal narrative creation to address participants' literacy needs and preferences, a common consideration in correctional education settings (Abram et al., 2017). After reading and discussing the musical "Let It Go," participants demonstrated reluctance toward writing responses about choosing to "respond versus react." to life situations. This aligns with research showing that incarcerated youth often have disrupted educational experiences leading to varying literacy levels (Ford et al., 2012), which they may attempt to conceal due to shame or stigma (Warner, 2004). Rather than penalize participants for literacy challenges or resistance to writing-a practice that could reinforce negative educational experiences-SBB staff pivoted to become scribes and biographers for the participants' verbal and personal narratives.

The ability to maintain program efficacy while adapting to institutional constraints and participant needs emerged as a crucial finding, aligning with Balfour and Poole's (1998) research on successful prison theatre interventions. This adaptability reflects Hughes's (2005) assertion that responsive program design is essential in correctional settings. Furthermore, when SBB staff openly discussed and implemented program modifications in front of participants, they exemplified what Cohen-Cruz (2010) terms "transparent practice," where teaching artists' decision-making processes become part of the educational experience and model the same future-oriented thinking and teamwork skills they aimed to develop in participants.

## Data analysis

Our analytical approach integrates multiple methodologies to examine the relationship between theatrical engagement and future orientation among incarcerated youth. Qualitative data underwent thematic analysis following Braun and Clarke's (2006) six-phase framework, with particular emphasis on identifying emergent patterns in future orientation development, temporal perspective shifts, and transformative catalysts. All of which were identified either in interviews, field notes, DARs, or the “Introspection Sheets.”  We complemented this with narrative analysis of participants' creative works and documented verbal sharing’s via audio. To ensure research integrity and participant protection we ensured all research team members were CITI certified via the Collaborative Institutional Training Initiative program. We also implemented rigorous ethical safeguards. All research protocols received approval from the Institutional Review Board, and we employed trauma-informed practices throughout data collection and analysis.

## Context for preliminary analysis

This preliminary analysis of the SBB focuses specifically on future orientation development. This paper analyzes three pivotal workshops that demonstrated significant participant cultivation of future-oriented thinking and behavior that was emerging or evolving.

1. "Let It Go" musical workshop

Participants engaged with an original musical based on a true story of gang violence, retaliation choices, and maternal influence. The narrative, written by a former justice-involved student of Vell’s, centers on J-man's decision to break a cycle of violence following a near-fatal stabbing. While traveling to work in an “Uber Pool,” J-Man encounters the girl who stabbed him. The stabbing had taken place six months back and had happened while J-Man was involved in a physical altercation with the girl’s boyfriend, who was a rival gang member to J-Man. The girl stabs J-Man after he begins to get the best of the altercation. The girl had pleaded with J-Man to stop at a certain point in the altercation before stabbing him. J-Mandidn’t perceive the girl as a threat, so he foolishly ignored her. Now that he is face to face with her in a “ride-share months later,” he has to choose whether or not to retaliate. He confronts the girl about the stabbing and requests an explanation. She tells him she stabbed him because she was afraid he was going to kill someone she loved. J-Man chooses not to retaliate after reflecting on his promise to his mother. While he is in the hospital recovering, his mom made him promise that he would not let his pain dictate a plan to retaliate. This workshop aligns with Pensalfini's (2016) observation that incarcerated participants often engage more deeply with narratives that reflect their lived experiences. The teaching artists used this contemporary bridge to introduce Shakespearean themes of revenge and redemption, a strategy supported by Scott-Douglass's (2007) research on making Shakespeare accessible to incarcerated populations. J-Man’s story also prompted discussions and writings amongst the participants, about what it means to “grow-up”, and what it means to respond to a situation versus react to a situation.

2. “Romeo and Juliet” workshop

At the start of Act 2, Scene 2 of Romeo and Juliet, Romeo stands beneath Juliet’s balcony, hoping that she will step out so he may look upon her face. This is where readers get to hear the two characters confess their love for each other. Analysis of the balcony scene with the participants prompted unexpected discussions about love, sex, relationships, and emotional maturity. The participants started the discussion with the normal “macho man” posturing that the teaching artist expected. However, the teaching artist persisted in expressing that all humans need and experience love which does not equate to weakness. This prompted some youth to be more vulnerable in their expressions and the rest followed. This corresponds with Warner's (2004) findings that classical texts can catalyze contemporary personal revelations among incarcerated youth.

3. Personal narrative development

Four weeks into a 12 week program, two participants, Nate and Will, begandeveloping personal narratives, demonstrating what Shailor (2011) describes as the progression from engagement to authentic selfexpression in prison theatre programs. Their stories provide direct evidence of evolving future orientation through artistic engagement.

Both Nate and Will’s story was inspired by discussions and workshops we did with J-man’s story “Let It Go” and Shakespeare’s “Henry V” lead by Kevin. Will and Nate’s narratives depict ways in which they were forced to grow-up by the circumstances of their relationships and environments. The teaching artists' approach of connecting contemporary and classical narratives to participants' experiences reflects Davey et al.'s (2015) framework for effective arts-based intervention in correctional settings. Each workshop provided unique opportunities to observe and document different manifested domains of future orientation development among participants. ## orientation in SBB participants within four domains

Preliminary insights from early in this project reveal that SBB's arts-based programming impacted the development of participants' future orientation. While Steinberg et al. (2015) identify future orientation as a marker of psychosocial maturity in justice-involved youth, this analysis revealed four distinct manifestations of future orientation in the participants: professional (career planning, workplace behavior), social-emotional (relationship planning, emotional regulation), educational (learning goals, skill acquisition), and survival-based (post-release safety planning, gang/risk management). The data suggests these different types of future orientation developed simultaneously and interconnectedly, frequently starting with survival-based thinking and evolving into broader awareness.

In the "Let It Go" workshop, the narrative served as what McAvinchey (2011) terms a "catalytic moment," sparking crucial discussions about choosing to respond rather than react to situations. The SBB staff established a group understanding that aligned with Bandura's (1997) concepts of self-regulation: to respond means to be in control and able to make an informed decision, whereas reacting means being manipulated and acting impulsively. This distinction proved particularly relevant for justice-involved youth, who often struggle with emotional regulation and impulse control (Ford et al., 2012). The discussion organically evolved into explorations of what it means to "grow up" and be "grown," reflecting what Abram et al. (2017) identify as common themes among incarcerated youth: premature responsibility and traumatic acceleration of development. Participants began to articulate ways in which they had to grow up early, examining both beneficial and detrimental aspects of forced maturation. Von's response particularly demonstrated what Steinberg et al. (2015) describe as emerging future orientation, as he begandiscussing his two-year plan to acquire drug treatment for an earlier release date. His strategic planning represents what Cheliotis and Jordanoska (2016) identify as a crucial indicator of desistance thinking: the ability to envision and actively plan for an alternative future. I and my research team, identify it as professional and emotional futureorientation. Von’s potential recovery from drug abuse will effect his ability to regulate his emotions in the future and future professional opportunities.

Those eight participants who did not complete the program (57.14% of SBB participants) did so because they either quit, could no longer mix with the group due to gang relations, were transferred to another facility, or they were released. Out of the six that completed the SBB program, five participants (35.71% of SBB participants) chose to be in the study. Nates’ complex development through trauma and artistic engagement Nate's case illustrates how trauma can paradoxically accelerate Social Emotional Future Orientation” development while complicating Professional Future Orientation. His experience aligns with Ford et al.'s (2012) research on trauma's role in developmental processes. Through processing the loss of four cousins to gun violence in two years, while writing his personal narrative. Nate evolved from social-emotional suppression to heightened relationship awareness: "I remember I didn't cry when I found out my cousin Harie had been murdered. I was with the guys, and none of us knew how to take it. I was only twelve." (Nate's Personal Narrative). This instance of evolving social-emotional growth continued through expressing subsequent losses in program participation, most notably during SBB’s Romeo and Juliet workshop. In the Romeo and Juliet workshop, a complex interaction demonstrated both challenges and opportunities in Professional, Educational, and Social Emotional Future Orientation development. During script analysis, participants' initial reactions revealed ingrained patterns of expressing cynicism about love, but the moment evolved into a teachable opportunity: "The boys were analyzing the monologues of Romeo and Juliet during the balcony scenes. The young men were going back and forth verbally and em otionally on positive and negative thoughts about Romeo declaring his love for Juliet. They initially claimed he was a 'Sucker' and a 'Simp'. They stated he was only acting that way b ecause he was a virgin. This must have triggered Nate because: Nate suddenly starts referr ing to girls as "bitches.” He says something to the degree of, 'I used to crush on bitches like Romeo. But nowadays, these bitches...' Miss Mac is one of the jail staff members sitting in the room with us during the program. After hearing Nate use the slur for the second or third time, she  immediately and sternly asked him to stop. He did." (Ecford, Field Notes, Nov 10, 2024).

This moment exemplifies what Landy and Montgomery (2012) term a "critical incident" in this instance of observed “Professional Future Orientation” development. The teaching artist transformed this potentially disruptive moment into an opportunity for learning about professional communication: "Ozivell, then took five minutes to explain 'mixed company,' code switching, and why we sometimes have to alter our language. He explained that 'mixed company' could be a room where there are men and women present… (Ecford, Field Notes, Nov 10, 2024).

This interaction gains deeper significance when contextualized within Nate's broader developmental journey. His response to the Romeo and Juliet text reveals the complex interplay between emotional processing and professional development that Ford et al. (2012) identify as common in trauma-affected youth. Just as his grief journey showed an instance of evolution in socio-emotional suppression to expression - "I remember I didn't cry when I found out my cousin Harie was murdered" to later “ I cried and prayed to God for the first time when they told me that my mom died in the car shooting.. They were wrong. My mother survived but my cousin Mimi died.” His relationship perspectives demonstrate similar tensions between vulnerability and defensive posturing. The context from SBB peer interactions with Nate further illuminates this complexity: "Ori: Nate is just mad because I think he's going through it with his girl. She had Nate crying on the phone the other day.” (Ecford, Field Notes, Nov 10, 2024).

This revelation, juxtaposed with Nate's derogatory language about women, exemplifies what Hughes

(2005) describes as the "pendulum swing" between emotional vulnerability and protective behaviors. His ability

to immediately adjust his language when redirected by Miss Mac demonstrates emerging Professional Future Orientation, while his emotional response to relationship difficulties suggests ongoing development in Social Emotional Future Orientation.

The teaching artist's intervention-connecting code-switching to familiar relationships through the question, "Do you talk to your mom like you talk to your girlfriend?" - represents what Cheliotis and Jordanoska

(2016) identify as "bridging instruction," helping participants connect immediate behavioral choices to future

professional and social success. Nate's subsequent apology to the group demonstrates what McAvinchey (2011) terms "responsive adaptation," the ability to modify behavior based on environmental feedback-a crucial skill for engagement with all four domains of Future Orientation. This moment exemplifies the interconnected nature of future orientation development observed throughout the program. Nate's experience demonstrates how Professional Future Orientation often develops through emotional challenges. What makes this interaction particularly significant is its emergence from artistic engagement. The Romeo and Juliet text served what Pensalfini (2016) describes as a "triggering artifact," prompting both regressive behaviors (derogatory language) and progressive development (learning about codeswitching). This aligns with Shailor's (2011) assertion that arts-based interventions create unique opportunities for processing emotional reactions while developing professional skills. The moment also illustrates the program's ability to support multiple types of future orientation simultaneously. While the immediate intervention focused on Professional Future Orientation, the broader context-discussing love, relationships, and emotional vulnerability-allowed participants to engage with Social

Emotional Future Orientation.

This intersection of emotional processing and professional development continues to emerge in subsequent sessions. Similar to how Nate's grief journey showed progressive ability to express emotions, his professional communication skills demonstrate incremental growth through practice opportunities. However, as Warner (2004) emphasizes, such development rarely follows a linear path. The same participant who shows sophisticated professional awareness in one moment may struggle with emotional regulation in the next, highlighting the need for consistent support and multiple practice opportunities. These insights inform both immediate program implementation and broader research implications in several key ways. The ability to recognize and utilize spontaneous teaching moments while maintaining sensitivity to participants' emotional states reflects what Davey et al. (2015) term "responsive pedagogy." For example, the progression from Nate's defensive reaction to Romeo and Juliet, through the code-switching lesson, and finally to his ability to apologize to the group later on, demonstrates how multiple types of future orientation can be developed through a single, well-handled interaction.

Arnie’s social-emotional development through personal disclosure In Arnie’s case, the Romeo and Juliet discussion also catalyzed significant Social Emotional Future Orientation development through unexpected personal disclosures. When the conversation shifted from criticizing Romeo's naivety to discussing personal experiences with first memories of romance and love, Arnie demonstrated what Cohen-Cruz (2010) identifies as transformative vulnerability:"Initially, Arnie called Romeo a sucker... However, when prompted to recall his own first experiences with love, his demeanor shifted significantly. He leaned forward in his chair, moving from a disengaged posture of playing with his long curly hair to active engagement" (Ecford, Field Notes, Nov 10, 2024).

Arnie's subsequent disclosure about his first intimate relationship demonstrates what Davey et al. (2015) term "retrospective insight development" - the ability to reexamine past experiences through a more mature lens: "I felt like a man. It was my first time. I went and bragged to my friends about it... A lot of them claimed that they've had sex before,. I was telling the truth" (Ecford, Field Notes, Nov 10, 2024). The depth of his reflection expanded to include relationship development context: "No. We met at a party through mutual friends. We ended up flirting with each other the whole night. I remember when I was about to leave the party, she gave me a hug. It was a real hug—no side hug like with my mom or a teacher. I could tell she really had feelings for me." (Ecford, Field Notes, Nov 10, 2024). This disclosure represents what we identify as a crucial marker of Social Emotional Future Orientation: the ability to recognize and articulate the difference between superficial and meaningful relationship experiences. The progression from initially dismissing Romeo's emotions to sharing his own vulnerable experiences suggests emerging capacity for emotional authenticity, a key component of Social Emotional Future Orientation. Shane’s challenges and regression in future orientation Shane's case particularly illustrates what Ford et al. (2012) identify as "reactive regression" - the return to immediate gratification and confrontational behavior when faced with systemic delays. His resistance to future orientation first appeared during the "Let It Go!" discussion: "Shane was not having any of the peace talks. He said he disagreed with J-man’s response. He said he would have definitely retaliated against the girl in some way. He supported his argument with the fact that J Man almost died. He said it wasn't like a slap, which he could forgive and forget. It was a stabbing" (Ecford, Field Notes, Oct 27, 2024). This pattern of prioritizing immediate reaction over future consequences escalated in his confrontation with institutional leadership in a workshop one day.: "That day Shane verbally confronted Mr. Dryb, Mr. Dryb had come to the workshop to say hello to the staff. Shane told Mr. Dryb that he had heard that Mr. Dryb was going to transfer him to another facility for his behavior throughout the facility. Mr. Dryb did not back down and affirmed Shane's suspicions. He told him that if his behavior didn't change, he would definitely be transferred to another facility. After the interaction, the SBB staff tried to tell Shane that the way he handled the situation was more likely to ensure the transfer rather than stop it. But Shane didn't care. He cared more about the opportunity to confront Mr. Dryb in front of his peers and attempt to get his way through intimidation" (Ecford, Field Notes, Oct 19, 2024). The culmination of Shane's regression manifested in his decision to leave the SBB program: "Shane has decided to leave the SBB program. He is upset with the facility for not giving him cut time he felt he had earned in a timely manner. he did earn the cut time and the facility was going to give it to him, just not in the expedited timeline that he wanted. He was hoping to go home by Christmas 2024. He needed the cut time allocated from all the programs he completed before Dec 4, 2024. When he found out that wasn't going to happen, he quit" (Ecford, Field Notes, Nov 20, 2024).

Shane's case illustrates what Cheliotis and Jordanoska (2016) call "institutional trigger points" - where systemic constraints clash with immediate desires, derailing future-oriented thinking. While intervention strategies were attempted, Shane’s impulsivity and resistance to support, possibly linked to undisclosed mental health factors, shows how Survival Based Future Orientation (seeking early release) can paradoxically hinder long-term development when immediate results aren't achieved.

## Conclusion

This preliminary analysis indicates arts-based programming can significantly influence future orientation development in incarcerated youth through multiple interconnected pathways, though the process varies among individuals.

Shakespeare Behind Bars' method of integrating classical texts with contemporary narratives creates distinctive opportunities for personal growth and future planning. The program transforms challenging moments into learning opportunities, demonstrating how arts-based interventions can simultaneously support multiple domains of future orientation development.

Effective future orientation cultivation requires programming that addresses both immediate survival needs and longer-term emotional and professional growth. The SBB program's arts-based approach appears to provide a framework that accommodates this dual focus, though longitudinal study will better reveal the lasting impact of these interventions.
