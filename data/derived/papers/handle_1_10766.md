# “I'm with Peppa”: University STEM Faculty Listening to Student Reasoning

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Prior research in K-¬12 STEM education has focused on cultivating teachers’ abilities to notice, interpret, and engage with the substance of student thinking. There has not, however, been similar attention in higher education professional development (PD). We present data from a session of The Listening Project (LP), designed to support university STEM faculty attention to student thinking, based significantly on prior work in K-12 with a key difference: LP faculty work across disciplines. Participants examine and discuss student thinking both within and outside their own disciplines. We argue that interdisciplinary community plays an important role in faculty developing “epistemic empathy”.

Learning to listen in higher education While there has been a surge in efforts in higher e ducation to create “active” learning environments t hat center student thinking (e.g., Wright, 2011), there has been little research on how faculty learn to notice and interpret the thinking they elicit (Gehrtz, et al., 2022). To begin to address that gap, we draw on dat a from The Listening Project (LP), which for six years offered  university STEM faculty PD focused on examining student thinking. We present excerpts of an LP session in which we saw participants listening to illustrate listening and our approach to analysis to try to understand what contributed t o productive moments of listening. Following prior work, we analyzed the data in terms of framing, positioning, and epistemic empathy (Jaber, 2021). Framing, positioning, and epistemic empathy Framing is “an active, dynamic, and multilayered process”  (Jaber et al., 2021, p.227) of individuals and groups interactively negotiating understandings of what is valued and appropriate in a situation. Research in K−12 STEM education has argued that what teachers notice and value in students’ thinking affects and is affected  by their framings (Levin et al., 2009; Russ & Luna, 2013). Positional framing is “the way in which participants understand themselves and one another to be related to one ano ther in the interaction” (vande Sande & Greeno 2012, p. 2). We use “ positioning” to emphasize the dynamic relativity of roles and power in social groups. Watkins et al.

(2018) showed that positioning as “not-understanding” was productive for scientific sensem aking in a science

class. Teachers’ epistemic empathy is teachers’ “affective attunements, including… curiosity about student ways of being and feeling” (Jaber et al., 2023). Jaber and colleagues found evidence of empathy contributing to teachers’ attention to—and asset-based interpretations of—student thinking (Jaber et al., 2018; Jaber et al., 2023).

## Study context: The Listening Project

LP was designed to promote instructors’ inclinations and abilities for attending to the substance of their students’ thinking—closely, respectfully, and with the aim of identify ing productive beginnings of disciplinary thinking. Project sessions took place biweekly over the academic year. Each session lasted 90 minutes. In a typical session, participants collaboratively examined student think ing from a participant’s course, and facilitators g uided attention towards interpretation rather than evaluation. LP was based on prior work in K-12 PD (e.g., Sherin & Han, 2004; Hammer & van Zee, 2006), with a key difference that the participants in LP were all STEM professionals. The PD for K-12 is generally designed to accommodate teachers who have limited experience in STEM fields. One aspect of the LP design was to connect faculty across fields, such that every meeting incl uded both experts and (relative) novices examining stude nt thinking. As we argue below, the evidence suggests this facilitated empathy and respectful engagement. We find Demi’s main interest to be the students’ experience: first what it might be like for Peppa to be considering this complex and somewhat strange chemical system and then what Peppa might be thinking when she talks about “electrodes” (S15), “electron poten tial” (S16−S17) and a “chart” (S17). In the latter, Demi looks  past Peppa’s specific words (or at least the precise canonical m eanings of those words), by reading Peppa’s “electr on potentials” as “reduction potentials.” Demi recogni zes and values Peppa’s general approach of considering “materials” and its sense: “That’s why they are looking for a chart... It’s all interrelated.” Like Bruno, Demi evidently frames the shared work o f the session as focused on interpreting the studen t’s thinking, including to find epistemic empathy. She participates, however, from the position of an expe rt; she is not herself confused as she can recognize “we” are. Her disciplinary expertise seems to support her in this effort, in recognizing particular ways Peppa may be mixing terminology as well as in recognizing aspects of the system that are unusual and possibly difficult for a learner.

## Discussion and implications

If this is a new sort of activity for the STEM faculty participants, working to make sense of a student is thinking, it is evident they have resources for engaging in it, as prior work has argued for K-12 teachers (Levin et al., 2009; Russ & Luna, 2013; Louie et al., 2021). We saw Bruno and Demi frame what was taking place in the LP session as their working to understand Peppa’s thinking.  Adopting a multi-disciplinary model for listening-focused PD seems to have supported epistemic empathy and recog nition of the substance of students’ thinking: Bruno’s participation, in positioning himself “with Peppa,” supports a stability of respect for the student perspective. In the LP broadly, we have observed how, at times, disciplinary expert ise can impede listening. When faculty hear errors or ambiguities in students’ rea soning, it cues an older and generally more stable framing of instructing with correct information. We did not see that in Demi he re, perhaps reflecting stability within her of attention to students, perhaps reflecting stability  at the level of the group. Demi leverages her disciplinary expertise to make sense of a confusing context and see past Peppa’s words to recognize a focus on materials that she sees as relevant to understanding the problem. Demi uses her expertise to see Peppa’s confusion as appropriate and to re-interpret—as a productive way to think about the problem—what others could consider irrelevant. Ultimately, we hope to see PD in higher education s upport faculty in their roles as instructors framing interactions with students as noticing, attending, and engaging with student thinking. In this way, we  believe, instructors will be inclined and able to make meani ngful use of student thinking they elicit in “activ e learning” approaches to instruction.
