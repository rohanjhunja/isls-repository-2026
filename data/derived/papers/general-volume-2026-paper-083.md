# Epistemic Engagement with Epistemically Elastic Content

**Conference:** ISLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This dissertation aims to investigate how non-experts engage with content that presents itself as apparently sound and structurally logical, but dismisses larger contextual factors that make it problematic. I describe how I take a mixed-methods approach to propose a study to be implemented with youth and young adults in Rio de Janeiro, Brazil. Additionally, I present preliminary findings that result from the first phase of this research project. Project motivation and research goals Imagine someone holds a controversial stance such as “raiding an official building is legitimate as long as this is done to rescue a country from collapse.” Although that may sound contradictory, to many people that is the result of a well-structured reasoning chain. In fact, research has sustained that a sort of public pedagogy is in place to “educate” people so that holding such views actually feels part of becoming a literate citizen (Greenhalgh et al., 2021). In my dissertation, I am interested in studying the intricacies of the processes through which people construct knowledge in ways that seem agentic but result in controversial stances. I am especially intrigued by how processes of agentic knowledge construction find support in ostensibly reasonable argumentation. As Duncan and Chinn (2025) put it, those epistemically fragile arguments are “structurally sound”—that is, they pass many criteria of formal analysis of argumentation, such as relevance, (superficial) sufficiency of evidence, and nature of justification—but are built on weak epistemic processes and on suboptimal evaluative criteria. From that motivation, I arrive at the core guiding inquiry for this dissertation: non-experts construct meaning from content that is epistemically elastic but enveloped in structurally sound arguments; how does that construction of meaning take place in highly social settings that are information-heavy, emotionally-charged, and technology-infused? Examples of those settings are instant messaging apps and social media, spaces that enable a distorted form of learning—that is, learning that is effective but used as a springboard to advance harmful political agendas (Russo et al., 2024). My motivation stated above drives the two components that I intend to address in the Doctoral Consortium: my Methods and Findings. I operationalize that motivation as one main research question and two subquestions that lay the groundwork to answer it: ● (RQ) How do non-experts engage epistemically with epistemically elastic content, that is, content that presents itself as apparently sound and structurally logical, but dismisses larger context that makes it problematic? ○ (RQ 1.1) What epistemic criteria and processes do non-experts rely on as they construct meaning from that type of content? ○ (RQ 1.2) How do pieces of intuitive knowledge and naturalized axioms (Philip, 2011) support those non-experts’ understanding of that content, scaffolding further knowledge construction? In formulating these questions, I refer mainly to literature on epistemic cognition (Sandoval, 2017), particularly the AIR framework proposed by Chinn et al., (Chinn et al., 2020); supplementarily, I refer to literature related to Knowledge-in-pieces (KiP) by diSessa and colleagues (diSessa et al., 2015; Philip, 2011) to investigate how epistemic processes operate on intuitive knowledge and assumptions about the social world.

Qualitative data will include video recordings of task-based interviews which I will use to explore students’ criteria and processes in detail and to understand how they recruit pieces of intuitive knowledge as part of their interpretation of evidence. Once I finish analysis of qualitative data, I will use the insights to design the cognitive task.

## Anticipated findings based on interview data

Here, I refer to interviews conducted with a convenience sample of 14 Brazilian participants (age 16-23, Mdn = 19; 11 undergraduate students) recruited in Rio de Janeiro in October, November, and December, 2025. Participants read a scenario in which a fictional council member asks his social media audience to help him take a stance: should he vote for or against a bill to ban non-local informal beach vendors? Participants were then asked to mark their stance on a pro/against continuum. Next, they were presented 4 arguments, one at a time; after reading each argument out loud, they could either keep their stance or change, and were asked to explain why they chose so. Finally, they were asked to rank the arguments according to relevance and to reflect on possible effects of the bill. Although the sample and setting represent limitations for my study, the type of data I obtained revealed to be appropriate for my analytic approach. Below, I present snippets of preliminary analyses organized by my two supporting questions.

In answering RQ1.1, I draw on epistemic cognition literature (Chinn et al., 2014) to investigate the relevance participants assign to each type of argument. Preliminary analysis showed that testimony was the most relevant source for participants and that drawing on personal experience played a key role in their integration of the evidence presented, more so than other criteria like source trustworthiness. In answering RQ1.2, I am interested in the knowledge elements that students “recruit” to support their interpretations. In my analysis, I draw on KiP to go back and forth between the data and an emerging theory I develop to help explain the role of those elements in students’ interpretations. In two examples in my pilot data, ML (Female, 22, Law major) and AN (Male, 18, recent high-school graduate), both participants recruited the knowledge element people migrate to search for opportunities. However, throughout the interview, each participant developed different stances as they progressed in their reasoning: whereas ML sustained her support for immigrants, AN considered more restrictive policies, contrasting that knowledge element against new evidence and arguments.

Expected contributions With my dissertation, I hope to examine in depth the intuitive knowledge and epistemic criteria non-experts employ as they engage with ideas that have consequential societal implications. I anticipate that deeper examination can inform designs that prepare students to navigate “epistemically messy” settings (Duncan et al.,

2018) where evidence of poor quality is presented and form the basis for structurally sound and consequential

stance-taking. I also expect to add multidisciplinarity to epistemic cognition scholarship that has already made significant contributions to the natural sciences. The topics I aim to address in my studies have deep moral and cultural dimensions, and I intend to incorporate dimensions that relate to other literacies (e.g., data, media). Additionally, my dissertation can expand the international reach of the learning sciences by working with Brazilian participants. This adds to our community’s search for epistemological pluralism and, importantly, helps illuminate how diverse populations navigate the challenges that motivate this work. Chinn, C. Micro-and Macrocosms in Accessible Reality: VR/AR Glasses as Partners in Learning This work presents a visual narrative of immersive learning through affordable smartphone-based VR/AR technology. The photographs capture a student exploring two scientific domains: the vast scale of the solar system and the microscopic structure of molecules. Using simple VR glasses and a smartphone, the learner navigates planetary space and constructs molecular models atom by atom. The images highlight moments of curiosity, concentration, and discovery, emphasizing the human experience within digital environments. By focusing on accessible tools rather than expensive laboratory equipment, the work invites viewers to reflect on how emerging technologies candemocratize science education. The series connects artistic documentation with learning sciences perspectives such as embodied learning and situated cognition, illustrating how physical interaction with digital worlds candeepen understanding of abstract scientific concepts.

Oleksii Voronkin Links: Conversations of Sound and Body Links: Conversations of Sound and Body is a live, improvisational dialogue between music and movement. Beginning with solo piano and expanding through spontaneous participation, the piece explores how learning emerges through relationships between sound, body, and attention. Grounded in constructionism and arts-based research, Links dissolves the boundary between artist and audience, inviting participants to co-create meaning and experience partnership as presence-fleeting, relational, and alive.
