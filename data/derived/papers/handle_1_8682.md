# Elementary Students’ Epistemic Ideas Within Design-Based Learning Projects

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Students’ epistemic ideas influence how they engage in science practices. This study employed the Epistemologies in Practice (EIP) framework to explore elementary students’ epistemic ideas within design-based projects in a science classroom. Data were collected from multiple sources and analyzed qualitatively. Findings indicated that students meaningfully used their epistemic considerations while engaging in their design projects. It was also found that contextual factors such as task settings influenced how students prioritized their epistemic ideas.

## Background

Scientists use their epistemic knowledge to evaluat e the validity and accuracy of scientific products such as models and arguments to generate and advance scientific knowledge. In science classrooms, it has been suggested that students’ epistemic ideas influence how they e ngage in science practices, such as scientific inqu iry and problem solving, and understand the nature of science (Pluta et al., 2011). Therefore, science educators have been supporting the development of students’ epistemic understandings by promoting their meaningful engagement in science (e.g., Ke & Schwarz, 2021; Kelly & Cunningham, 2019; Ko & Krist, 2019). To contribute to this issue, this study aimed to explore elementary students’ ep istemic ideas within design-based learning projects  and investigate how contextual factors influenced students’ epistemic considerations in action. In this study, we employed the Epistemologies in Pr actice (EIP) framework (Berland et al., 2016) to understand elementary students’ epistemic ideas within their design-based learning projects in science. The EIP framework emphasizes that “one’s epistemology is made up of a flexible network of [epistemic] understandings that can be drawn from based on understandings of w hat is most appropriate and relevant to one’s goals  in the current setting” (Berland et al., 2016, p. 1085). F rom this perspective, we view students’ epistemic i deas as contextualized and tied to specific content and context. The EIP framework focuses on four considerations that are not only meaningful to scientific communities, but also to the knowledge building science classrooms (Berland et al., 2016). They are: ¥ What kind of answer should our knowledge product provide? (Nature): Students’ considerations to this question range from detailed description of what happened  to causal mechanisms to explain the How and Why.

¥ How does our knowledge product relate to other scie ntific phenomena and ideas? (Generality): This is related to students’ understandings of specific exp eriences or phenomena with one another and with general abstractions. Students’ considerations range from specific scientific phenomena do not relate to one another or to the generalized science ideas to knowledge products are created from and should explain a range of phenomena.

¥ How do we justify the ideas in our knowledge products? (Justification): Students’ considerations range from noneed to justify tonecessity of constructing, evaluating, and justifying the knowledge product with interpretation of the available information.

¥ Who will use our knowledge products and how? (Audience): Students’ considerations range from our knowledge product is for the teacher to evaluate our understanding to we collaboratively construct and use our knowledge products with our audience.

## Curriculum Context and Research Methods

This four-month-long research was designed as a qualitative case study (Merriam, 2016). This study took place in a Grade 5/6 science classroom in a public school  in Canada. Nineteen students and their science tea cher participated in this study. During the research period, the students engaged in a design-based learning project on the topic of astronomy. In elementary classrooms, astronomy topics could foster curiosity and imagination and is an effective method of inspiring student motivation in science (Plummer et al., 2010). Despite attracting children’s interests and affecting their everyday lives, research indicates that some astronomical phenomena are difficult for students to understand and challenging for teachers to teach (Plummer et al., 2010). To support their engagement in exploring other planets and their moons in the solar system, this unit was organized around a project: Design to further space exploration. In this project, stud ents worked individually to design a new technology  to further ICLS2022 Proceedings 1998 © ISLS the current space exploration. Students knew from t he beginning of this project that they would make a presentation and that their technologies would be evaluated by their classmates. Their designs were to be based on their experiences, knowledge learned in this unit, and information from online research on their topics. Multiple methods were employed for data collection.  For this paper, we mainly analyzed the data collected with classroom observation and student in terviews. These data were analyzed qualitatively fo r thematization to understand students’ epistemic considerations within their design projects. Our data analysis was informed by the EIP framework focusing on the four considerations discussed in the previous section. W e first analyzed the data separately. Then, through critically comparing and examining each other’s interpretations, we achieved collective data interpretations.

## Findings and Discussions

In this section, we present preliminary findings on  elementary students’ epistemic considerations duri ng their design projects. It was found that students in this study meaningfully used their ideas related to the four epistemic considerations in the EIP framework.

“What kind of answer should our knowledge product p rovide?” In this study, we found many of the student participants thought they should explain the why and how in their knowledge products (i.e., presentations). For example, one student shared during the interview “… this is going to happen, but how? We need to e xplain the how part, then people will understand what my t echnology is.” Some students also related their epi stemic considerations to the task requirements by comparing this design project with their previous ones: “we’ve never done this before. In this project, you really need to persuade … it is not like the trees [project] which you can only list what you found online … this one you really need to explain the why to persuade.” “How does our knowledge product relate to other sci entific phenomena and ideas?” In this study, we rarely found students addressing generality in their presentations. Most of the students’ desig n did not include connections to others’ ideas. It is conjectured that it is due to the specific task setting in which students designed their own innovative technology. This finding confirmed that epistemic ideas are situated, contextualized and tied to specific content and context.

“How do we justify the ideas in our knowledge produ cts?” All the students in this study realized that they needed to justify with evidence, yet their und erstandings about evidence were varied. For example, one student shared during the interview “we need to per suade with information, much information … the more information you have the better.” Another student i nsisted “I want to keep mine concise … you do not n eed to talk a lot, a few pieces, but really good, is more powerful.” They both realized the necessity of justification with evidence but had different ideas regarding importance of the quantity or quality of evidence. “Who will use our knowledge products and how?” In t he study, classmates were introduced as the audience who would evaluate students’ design by examining whether the design would contribute to current space exploration. Instead of collaboratively constructing knowledge with the audience, students viewed their audience as ones who are consumers of their designs. This fi nding also supports that context impacts students’ epistemologies that are in practice. Based on under standings of the context and goals in the current s etting, students prioritize epistemic ideas they think most appropriate (Berland et al., 2016).
