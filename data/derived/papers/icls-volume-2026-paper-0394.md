# Cultivating Critical Transformative Teacher Agency through Co-Design with Families toward Climate Justice

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Using participatory critical design ethnography, we investigate how a minoritized teacher developed critical transformative agency by collaborating with Arab American immigrant families to co-design and enact climate justice education. Findings show this agency as a hesitant yet protective stance, growing critical consciousness, and action. It emerged through engagement with community epistemologies, relational and cultural anchors, and home-school alliances. We argue that cultivating such agency is essential for advancing climate justice education and community collective thriving.

"I didn't even know how I would be able to make that conversation happen." (Ms. Hened, Arab American science teacher) Anthropogenic climate change is approaching critical tipping points, with catastrophic effects that disproportionately impact people of color, immigrants, and ethnic minorities (Trott et al., 2023). Yet science education often fails to prepare students adequately. Curricula tend to emphasize scientific content while remaining “silent” about the social disparities underlying climate change (Lee & Grapin, 2022). By privileging dominant epistemologies, such approaches marginalize the lived experiences and knowledge of communities most affected by environmental harm.

Teachers can play a key role in advancing climate justice education, but they often lack institutional support and curricular guidance (Clark, 2024). This  especially affects minoritized educators, who are often “stripped of political voice and expected to follow the curriculum as written” (Miller et al., 2022, p. 4). Ms. Hened’s uncertainty about “how to make that conversation happen” reflects how science curricula often omit the social and political dimensions of climate change, leaving teachers to navigate them on their own. Advancing climate justice education in schools requires reimagining curriculum and pedagogy, with attention to the role of teachers’ critical, transformative agency.  We understand this agency as the capacity to foster critical consciousness, connect science learning to local realities, and imagine new possibilities through collective engagement with families and communities (Stetsenko, 2020). This study examines how a minoritized teacher developed such agency while co-designing a climate justice unit with Arab American immigrant families. We ask:

1. How is the critical transformative agency of a minoritized teacher shaped by her engagement in co-designing climate change learning with families?

2. What shifts in the agency trajectory of this teacher occur, and why?

## Theoretical framework

Critical transformative agency involves “breaking away from the given frame of action and taking the initiative to transform it” (Virkkunen, 2006, p. 49). Unlike individualistic views of agency, this perspective emphasizes collective, relational, and justice-oriented action. We use the term “critical” to foreground how agency is shaped by power, place, identity, knowledge, and context. In science education, this means using scientific knowledge alongside community expertise to identify and address injustice (Calabrese Barton & Tan, 2020). Torres-Olave et al. (2025) describe this process as reading the world to recognize injustice, imagining untested possibilities, and transforming oppressive structures in science education. Such agency develops within what Holland et al. (1998) call “figured worlds,” socially and culturally constructed spaces where certain identities, actions, and outcomes are recognized and valued. These realms develop through collaborative activities and discussions, with participant expectations guiding action and shaping experiences. Thus, transformative agency is not linear; it develops through negotiation, resistance, adaptation, and collective possibility-making.

2608 Drawing upon participatory methods, we collaborated with one teacher, five 6th-grade classrooms of immigrant students, and families to co-design and implement a climate justice unit. Our project took place in an urban public school, located in an industrial area surrounded by factories. The school serves a vibrant Arab community with ongoing migration from the Middle East. The teacher, Ms. Hened, shares a common cultural background with her students. Across the classrooms, 120 students participated, 89% of whom are in economically disadvantaged. Through FamJam, participants explored familial capital, co-designed activities, mapped local climate experiences, monitored CO₂, discussed climate vulnerability, and examined tree equity. Data included field notes, video/audio observations, weekly teacher feedback, and interviews. Analysis used constant comparison and memo writing, focusing on climate justice figured worlds, teacher transformative agency, and solidarity with mothers through epistemic, relational, and home-school alliances.

Our findings reveal how Ms. Hened, an Arab American science teacher, developed critical transformative agency through a trajectory of hesitant protection, growing critical consciousness, envisioning new pedagogical possibilities, and taking action. This de velopment occurred through solidarity with mothers and students within climate justice “figured worlds,” where community epistemologies, cultural anchors, and home-school alliances supported justice-oriented teaching and community thriving. Initially, Ms. Hened approached the unit with trepidation, adopting a protective stance to ward herself, students, and community from the fraught contexts such a unit introduced. She explained, “I felt, for the longest time, how are we going to deliver this unit on climate justice, the section on tree equity, and the one about living by the factorie s.” She felt unprepared to address socio-political dimensions of climate change. Yet because environmental injustices were “so close to home,” she realized “we couldn’t deny it.” Thus, her protective stance did not lead her to avoid injustice; instead, she asked questions, observed  responses, and foregrounded neighborhood strengths before discussing challenges.

Her agency deepened through three forms of solidarity. First, mothers’ climate stories foregrounded community epistemologies. Their accounts of flooding in local and homeland  contexts helped students connect climate change to unequal vulnerability. Ms. Hened viewed these stories as an unexpected doorway into climate justice education: “the moms brought such an important component. They made it real.”  Second, climate mitigation became relational and cultural. When discussing trees’ role in climate mitigation, mothers shared memories and cultural connections to trees, positioning them not only as carbon mitigators but also as symbols of family, history, and sustainability. Ms. Hened reflected, “If I taught photosynthesis, I never really made a real-life connection with the tree.” This marked a shift toward decolonizing dominant science by connecting scientific concepts to culture and lived experience. Third, climate causality became embedded in everyday practices through home-based CO₂ monitoring. Mothers and students measured CO₂ levels in their homes, connecting emissions, and cooking to daily life. Ms. Hened legitimized families’ data collection as scientific inquiry and positioned herself as a co-learner, noting, “There’s actual science behind it.” This disrupted traditional home-school boundaries and redistributed authority in knowledge construction. Based on these experiences, Ms. Hened enacted transformative agency by opening new possibilities and imagining advocacy beyond school as a form of mobilization for collective thriving.

This study advances understanding of how teachers cultivate critical transformative agency by engaging in solidarity with families to address social and political dimensions of climate change education. Such agency empowers teachers, particularly those from minoritized communities, to challenge systemic inequities and prepare students to navigate complex socioscientific issues. It emphasizes the need to disrupt traditional boundaries between home and school learning. Strengthening partnerships between educa tors and families legitimizes community knowledge as an essential educational resource and supports teachers’ agency in providing meaningful, real-world learning experiences.
