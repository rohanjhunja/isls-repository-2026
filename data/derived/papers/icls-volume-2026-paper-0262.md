# Identity Play through “Creek-Science” as Liminal Practice

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Us ing identity play as an analytic construct, we designed a critical, place-based summer camp for youth to have multiple opportunities to try on heterogeneous science identity modes. Unexpectedly, scientific studies of the creek (e.g., hydrology data collection, macroinvertebrate studies) we feared would suppress identity play were reported by youth as one of the most impactful and novel activities. We conceptualize the creek as a liminal space between youths’ playworlds and the cultural world of disciplinary science, and 1) show how grounding served as a “ceremony” to refigure the creek as liminal space, 2) illustrate how the liminal creek-science practice of treasure-hunting enabled youths’ disciplinary identity play.

In a study of youths’ identities in an environmental education camp, we theorized STEM identity play  as provisional self-making aimed at curiosity and enjoyment in novel, horizon-expanding spaces (Carlone & Mercier, 2026). That work established identity play as an analytic construct, distinguishable from identity work.

In this paper, we show how specific practices prompted identity play. A guiding premise is that “social practices of ‘acting otherwise’ become the grounds for our ‘thinking otherwise’” (Holland et al., 1998 p. 236). We designed a critical place-based summer camp for identity play, offering youth multiple opportunities to try on science identity modes (investigator, connector, environmental advocate, creative, tinkerer, futurist). We expected novel activities like meeting activists, producing podcasts, and art gallery presentations to nurture  identity play. In contrast, we worried creek-based science was too familiar for our nature-based charter school students, and that “school-like” studies might suppress identity play’s “let-me-see” ethos. End-of-camp card sorts told a different story: most youth placed “scientific studies of the creek” in the high-novelty/high changed-my-thinking quadrant. This surprise led us to conceptualize “creek-science” as a liminal practice that refigured a familiar play-space (the creek) as a site where improvisatio n and inquiry co-existed, and to examine emerging identity play in that in-between space. Accordingly, we asked: 1) How did key creek-science practices create liminal conditions? 2) How did those practices afford episodes of disciplinary identity play?

## Conceptual framework

Cultural worlds, playworlds, and disciplinary identity play Playworlds have been underexamined in the identity studies literature, yet Holland et al. (1998) describe them as “cultural resources that help us envision new identities” (p. 250). In play, everyday scripts and positionalities loosen, opening room for improvisation and agency. Experiments first tried in play can seed new cultural forms that later reshape selves and collective life. Playworlds enable “unlearning” of habitual practice and a “return to the everyday, perhaps, with an altered subjectivity, an altered sense of who we are” (p. 238). This framing motivates our account of disciplinary identity play. We use disciplinary identity play to name moments when youth experimentally try out roles, stances, and ways of knowing associated with expansive science on a provisional, curiosity-led basis rather than for compliance or recognition (Carlone & Mercier, 2026). Identity play is organized by “let-me-see” aims, enjoyment, and trials at the edge of one’s habitual self, rather than a commitment to a stable or aspirational identity. We distinguish disciplinary identity play from identity work, even as they co-occur. While identity work’s telos is directed toward fuller participation and increasing competence with a cultural group’s norms and practices (Lave & Wenger, 1991), identity play’s telos is wayfaring, with outcomes that are not fully predictable nor necessarily sticky, with a purpose of trying out and discovering new aspects of self. Liminality Liminality is the middle phase of a rite of passage, after separation from a prior role and before re‑entry with a new status (Turner, 1969). Aware of its colonial anthropological lineage, we use the construct cautiously, pulling 1792 threads with exploratory value and pairing it with decolonial scholarship. In our work, liminality names the threshold between playworlds and cultural worlds, where learners candraw on the resources of each. A contemporary analogue is the summer between high school and university, where one belongs fully toneither world. Our summer program, Teens (Re -)Storying the Creek with STEM (T-ReCS), was similarly liminal– not fully play and not fully school, but including logics of both. The creek, however, was squa rely a part of these nature-school students’ playworlds, which was one of our design challenges. As a liminal site, we designed for the creek to be a relational partner in joyful, knowledge‑generating processes (Marin & Bang, 2018). Liminal science practices can serve as resources for identity play by reducing stakes, introducing multiple interest hooks and pathways, and encouraging trying out provisional selves (Carlone & Mercier, 2026). In this relational in‑between, scripts of schooling and disciplinary science loosened, boundaries around expertise blurred, and improvisation and inquiry coexisted in ways that made possible acting–thinking otherwise (cf. Zander, 2024). ## Research Context

In partnership with a nature-based charter school in the southeastern U.S., we co-designed and co-facilitated a three-week, place-based summer camp, T-ReCS, for 28 middle schoolers (ages 10-12) focused on Willow Creek (all proper names are pseudonyms). We designed the camp curriculum to nurture youths’ connection with, responsibility for, and investigation of the creek, interrogation of equity and justice within histories of the socioecological system, imagination and futuring, and the creation of digital stories (zines, podcasts, augmented reality) shared with the public at a local gallery. During camp’s first three days, we explored upstream through creek play, creek walks, scientific studies (hydrology, macroinvertebrates, water chemistry), a litter surve y, storytelling from local advocates and community members, and “grounding” activities to encourage youths’ attunement to place. The work we report on here focuses on the grounding activity as “ceremony” and the scientific studies of the creek, which we called “creek listening missions” to emphasize more-than-humans’ agency in telling stories and our responsibility to listen to and read the stories (Marin & Bang, 2018). We draw on end-of-camp interviews, fieldnotes from end-of-day reflections on Days 1-4, and GoPro video from creek-science on Days 1-3. We began by coding end-of-camp, card-sort interviews. “Scientific studies of the creek” was the only card most youth (n =  18 of 21) identified as both “new experience” and “changed my thinking,” which guided purposeful sampling of creek video episodes (Derry et al., 2010). We created time-stamped content logs and used constant comparative methods (Glaser & Strauss, 1967; Charmaz, 2014) with domain and taxonomic analysis (Spradley, 1980) to identify practice families (“ways to do creek-science”: 1793 connecting, treasure-hunting, placemaking, and care/ethics) and the included practices within each. For RQ1, we did a componential analysis to specify characteristics of each practice (e.g., joint attention; porous expertise; youth-led improvisation), and how the creek was positioned as an epistemic partner. For RQ2, we coded for identity play cues (see Table 1), and examined co-occurrences of characteristics and cues within episodes and across student artifacts and fieldnotes from end-of-day reflections. We first describe how the practice of grounding served as a ceremony which pulled the creek from youths’ playworlds into a liminal space. Second, we tell the story of how a hydrology activity became fertile ground for disciplinary identity play in this liminal space through the practice of treasure-hunting. Grounding as “ceremony” to refigure the creek as liminal practice Grounding became a key practice to shift the creek from playspace to liminal space; indeed, we came to think of it as “ceremony-lite” in the Turner, rites-of-passage sense (1969). Although most T-ReCS campers were comfortable outside given their nature-based school experiences, they saw the creek mainly as a place for free, lively play. On the first day of camp, many rushed off the bus and made a beeline directly into the creek, screaming, splashing, and laughing despite Dr. Heidi’s plea to “Wait until you get invited into the creek!” Within this context, grounding became an essential marking of the creek as something new, rather than a site for boundless and frenzied free play. We use “grounding” to describe activities involving direct physical and spiritua l contact with the Earth, often experienced as calm, meditative moments (Song, 2019). Where Western science might dismiss grounding as unscientific, our camp team embraced it as a meaningful, longstanding practice that connects people to place. Youth participated in grounding practices on the first three mornings of camp to ready themselves for a careful “creek listening” rather than anthropocentric play or formal science. On day one, Mr. Jacob, a teacher and local Indigenous community member (tribe blinded for anonymity), led our first grounding activity based on his tribal language and culture. He explained that directions in his language, unlike in English where we give directions relative to ourselves (e.g., “on my right”), are always relative to more-than-human landmarks (e.g., “east of the bend in the creek”). After orienting campers to the cardinal directions, he instructed them to rotate in a circle, observing each di rection as he played for them on his wooden flutes. David, Bruno, and Pablo, who had been kicking a ball during most of the activity, were still. Though they were clearly eager to get into the water, gradually the whispers and fidgets gave way to calm. On day two, we repeated the grounding activity, adding sense-by-sense observations to the stillness and reorientation of day one. Another teacher, Mr. Stanley, asked campers to close their eyes to “hear a story with your ears” and to “squeeze a story out of what you smell.” This ceremony-like practice legitimized listening as a valued practice and attuned youth to the agency of more-than-humans; Amelia reported, “I felt the grass trying to lift up on my hand!” The creek became an epistemic partner, not merely an object of study or a place to play. On day three, Mr. Stanley moved grounding into the water. Together, they connected grounding to disciplinary science by finding a favorite pebble and hypothesizing how it got its shape.

Youth repeatedly returned to grounding and its effect on their relationships with the creek. Vanessa reflected on day four, “Yesterday I was sitting in the grass looking at my surroundings while eating,” adding, “I connected with the water by being in it repeatedly.” Carlos and Michael created a podcast about grounding, inspired by their creek experiences and Dr. Christine, a visiting nursing professor who prescribes grounding as a health practice. Carlos says: “I think grounding does help with health…when I’m outside, I have less headaches and feel less worried” They concluded the podcast with a guided meditation, set to Mr. Jacob’s flute music: “Close your eyes and stand still, you’ll hear the sounds of Willow Creek and what would be in the water.” The ceremony of grounding opened up liminal conditions for the T-ReCS campers by: 1) shifting the tempo by both slowing frenzied play and delaying the “task-driven” urgency of school science; 2) settling bodies, to expand youth attention and noticing; 3) legitimizing listening, noticing, and sensory learning from more-thanhumans; 4) reducing anthropocentrism by refiguring the creek as an epistemic partner; and 5) buffering risk by providing a calm, guided entry into the space and activity. Refiguring the creek as a liminal space involved prompted “acting-otherwise” behavior, from screaming, stomping, and splashing to slowed observations attuned to the creek (aesthetic attunement). This opened opportunities for perspective-taking, like Alexis, whose view of itchy bug bites changed: “Bugs always have a certain reason when they bite you, you shouldn’t be afraid.” “Listening” expansively with multiple senses, like noticing shifts in water temperature or slimy rocks, sparked questions that guided exploration without fixed outcomes. The energy shift, from stomping through the creek to purposeful seeking, allowed improvisation and playful goals, such as Clara’s urge to explore “where humans haven’t been yet.” Legitimizing sensory appreciation of the more-than-human world encouraged lingering, bridging to scientific inquiry. 1794 Treasure-hunting in hydrology as liminal practice Youth engaged with Dr. Chris, an expert in hydrology and soil science, to collect water quality data. Armed with kid-friendly infrared (IR) probes to measure temperature and Dr. Chris’ “fancy” multimeter for dissolved oxygen and pH, youth entered the water with data tables and clipboards, aiming to identify patterns across the creek. Initially, this activity seemed rooted in traditional field science –e.g., learning to use Western scientific tools to collect and organize quantifiable data. We imagined these data as crucial for comparison studies downstream. Yet, the hydrology activity emerged as a key example of creek-science as liminal practice. We highlight treasurehunting as an investigative practice to illustrate youths’ adventurous following of curiosity. Dr. Chris did not bring in a fixed hypothesis. Instead, he framed the activity with flexible, exploratory aims alongside scientific rigor. He asked, “What’s interesting to you?” and “Where do you want to put the probe [in the water]?” Youth eagerly embraced these questions. The pre-printed data table offered a space to record data about one spot in the creek, but they quickly realized they needed room for myriad locations that piqued their interest. After briefly discussing options like adding columns, the group flipped the worksheet and sketched a creek map, marking collection sites with X’s bearing remarkable resemblance to a treasure map. The energy was playful but investigative. “Measure the algae!” Jesse shouted, pointing to a sunny patch. “It’s really deep here!” Amelia shouted as her ankle sank into the creek bed, IR probe raised. Meandering detours followed, with youth sharing discoveries of a fish, snails, a turtle, and delighting in others’ finds. They asked Dr. Chris “what-if” questions: “If I point [the IR probe] up and it keeps going, could I blind an alien?” The big “find” was a ground spring Amelia found, squealing as she stepped into a patch of “freezing” cold water, joined by others’ excited cries. They observed with senses, then measured with probes. Data poured in: temperature dropped abruptly, dissolved oxygen plummeted. “It’s 67.5 degrees!” “The dissolved oxygen’s still falling!” “What is it now?” “43%!” Dr. Chris and Dr. Heidi explained the numbers and why they fit a ground spring. Dr. Carl exclaimed, “Isn’t that cool?! We found something cool!” Dr. Heidi added, “Wow, guys, this is a big discovery! I bet nobody else found something this hard to find.” The lore spread through the camp, noted in journals, reflections, and interviews. It even got featured in a group podcast. Treasure-hunting created liminal conditions by: 1) authorizing children’s curiosity, framing the activity between play and protocol; 2) improvising data recording from rigid tables to messy, treasure-like notes shaped by discovery; 3) learning-on-the-move, keeping investigation dynamic and inviting pattern-seeking across locations; 4) verifying sensory observations with scientific tools encouraged evidence-making while preserving agency; 5) welcoming flexible learning targets and adjacent finds, preventing closure on joyful discovery. Liminal conditions supported disciplinary identity play. A “let-me-see” ethos pervaded — “Let me see your probe,” “What did you find?” These low-risk, curiosity-driven moves allowed them to try out new roles as investigator, tinkerer (improvised maps), and c reek listener (attending to creek features). Clara described hydrology as completely novel, and several youth self-named as “investigators” or “futurists” based on data collection, the spring discovery, and talked about how water quality “could affect the future.” Jesse named herself a “connector” because she “connected data to words” to communicate their findings. Taken together, these accounts reveal acting-thinking otherwise about the creek and themselves. Almost all youth described scientific studies of the creek as particularly novel and eye-opening: “I used to think of the creek as just a place to hang out [but] I had a change of heart” (Lucia). “I’ve never really gone to the creek and looked at it in a scientific way” (Rupa). Novelty and liminality created fertile ground for acting-thinking otherwise. The temporary suspension of normal routines and structural arrangements of everyday life loosens evaluative pressures that typically demand youth conform to a “certain” identity. In school science, celebrated identities are often narrowly defined, racialized, gendered, and classed. Most youth were from nondominant surveillance practices of correctness and related forms of disciplining narrow possibilities for Black and Latine youth to fully show up and be granted epistemic legitimacy (Bang et al., 2015; Brown, 2019; Miles & Roby, 2022; Morton et al., 2022). Identity play can create a sense of freedom, de-disciplining youth to explore different aspects of themselves in supportive conditions. In this way, identity play can be liberatory. Identity play is especially useful as an analytic construct for nascent or emerging identities. Rather than aiming to immediately solidify or strengthen science identities, encouraging science identity play may be a more fitting approach for newcomers exploring “not-yet-me” identities. Middle school is a critical time for trying out different versions of self socially and emotionally, yet opportunities for low-stakes experimentation with disciplinary identities are limited. Viewing these moments through an identity play lens allows researchers and educators to better understand and support how youth navigate these shifts, helping create spaces that affirm who they are and encourage expansive self-discovery.
