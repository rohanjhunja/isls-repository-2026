# Fostering AI Literacy through Opening the Black Box of Foundational Text Generation Models

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This pilot study investigates the impact of an intervention aimed at demystifying foundational text generation models to enhance AI literacy among ninth-grade English Language Arts students. Pre-and post-surveys revealed significant gains in self-assessed performance, while qualitative analysis showed improved understanding of concepts like predictive modeling. However, students struggled to connect foundational knowledge to advanced AI systems like ChatGPT. The findings highlight the importance of teaching foundational models and developing strategies to bridge this gap, supporting comprehensive AI literacy in K-12 education.

## Introduction

Fostering AI literacy is crucial in today’s digital  age, equipping students with the skills to navigat e and shape an AI-driven society (Long & Magerko, 2020). Effective AI  literacy combines technical knowledge, practical application, and an understanding of ethical and social implications (Zhang et al., 2023; Ng et al., 2023). However, students often struggle to grasp how AI systems wor k and engage meaningfully with them (Greenwald et a l., 2021). This pilot study explores how text-generation models can introduce AI concepts in English Language Arts classrooms. By using Markov Chain models as a gatew ay to advanced systems like ChatGPT, the study aims  to build foundational knowledge and self-assessed performance. As the initial phase of a thr ee-year project, t his work lays the groundwork for scalable, practical ap proaches to bridge theoretical understanding and ap plication, guiding future curriculum development.

## Participants and Learning Context

This pilot study, the first phase of a three-year design-based research project, explored how Markov Chains could introduce probabilistic text generation concepts to  ninth-grade English Language Arts students. Markov Chains were chosen for their foundational role in probabilistic modeling and their simplicity, enabling students to observe key mechanisms like state-based predictions and transition probabilities (Neal, 1993). This approach served as an accessible entry point to AI text generation principles, setting the stage for understanding more advanced models. Figure 1 Markov Chain Text Generation Demo The study involved 42 ninth-grade students at a public high school in the north eastern United States, predominantly Black/African American, with two-thirds identifying as female. While most students were familiar with AI through social media (77%) and movies (44%), 62% had no prior exposure to Machine Learning. Nearly 89% expressed aspirations for higher education in h igh-salary careers. The five-hour pilot study tested five educational activities: 1) AI is Everywhere: exploring societal impacts of language-based AI; 2) Who Wrote It: comparing student and AI-generated writing; 3) Writing by Prediction: understanding language prediction; 4) Markov Chains: hands-on exploration of text generation, and 5) What is Art: examining AI art’s implications for creativity. Students worked with an interactive text generation  demo using Markov Chains that visualized interconnected word states and probabilities (Figure 1). The demo allowed students to either manually draw states and transitions from a text or automatically gene rate models, modify transition probabilities, set p arameters like starting state and maximum length, and observe the generation proc ess step-by-step. We collected pilot data through pre-and post-intervention surveys (N=35) that included two 4-point Likert-scale items where students self-reported their competence regarding text generation models, along with open-ended responses explaining these models, and knowledge assessment of Natural Language Generation (N=42), in which five closed-ended questions were evaluated pre-and post-intervention, and then combined to assign a score f rom 0 to 5. We conducted initial analyses through paired t-tests on the self-reported competence items and qualitative coding of the explanations, categorizing them based on key co nceptual elements (e.g., training data awareness, m odel mechanics understanding, real-world applications).

## Results

Self-Assessment of Students’ Confidence Pre-post self-assessment (N = 35) indicated significant improvements in both measured  constructs, with the largest gains observed in students’ ability to describe the limitations of AI text generation, followed by their ability to explain how AI text generation works. Students’ self-rated ability to explain how AI text generation works improved significantly from pre-test (M = 2.63, SD = 1.00) to post-test (M = 3.11, SD = 0.58), p =.011*, with a small-to-moderate effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.46). Similarly, their ability to describe the limitations of AI text generation showed a significant increase from pre-test (M = 2.26, SD = 0.98) to post-test (M = 3.11, SD = 0.72), p <.001**, with a moderate effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.61). Knowledge Assessment of Natural Language Generation Scores showed a significant improvement from the pre-test (M = 2.14, SD = 0.98) to the post-test (M = 2.88, SD = 1.01),  with p < 0.001. The effect size, calculated using C ohen’s d, was moderate (d = 0.61), indicating a meaningful gain in understanding following the intervention. These results, while promising, remain exploratory and warrant further investigation. Gender-specific analyses revealed that both female and male students exhibited significant score improvements, with females demonstrating slightly greater gains. Among female participants (N = 26), the mean score increased from 2.31 (SD = 0.84) to 3.12 (SD = 1.11), p <.01. For male participants (N = 15), the mean score increased from 1.93 (SD = 1.16) to 2.60 (SD = 0.63), p =.036.

## Students’ Perceptions of AI Text Generation and Its Limitations

Qualitative analysis of open-ended responses revealed a progression in students’  understanding of AI text generation. Pre-intervention, many students identified AI’s relianc e on training data but offered limited explanations, often describing text generation as random or intuitive, such as “choosing the next word that makes sense” or “depending on chance. ” Post-intervention responses demonstrated a deeper unders tanding, with references to concepts like Markov chains and predictive mechanisms. Students also highlighted limitations, such as memory constraints (“AI can only generate text that is relevant for so long”), lack of creativity (“AI doesn’t possess the ability to be creative”), and inability to handle personal or novel topics  (“AI wouldn’t be able to write a paragraph about my life”). Others noted its failure to replicate human emotions and experiences (“AI cannot capture the feelings and emotions of a human being”).

## Discussion

This study demonstrates that demystifying foundatio nal text generation models (in this study, Markov c hains) enhances students’ self-assessed performance in understanding language-based AI technologies as well as their content knowledge of language-based AI technologies. Through exploring Markov chains, students gained insight into text generation mechanics, particularly regard ing predictive modeling and how probabilities influ ence the sequence of words in the generated text. Meanwhile,  our findings reveal a gap in students’ ability to apply this foundational knowledge to sophisticated AI technolo gies like ChatGPT. These results provide critical g uidance for the next steps in curriculum development. Futur e iterations will focus on bridging the gap between foundational models and sophisticated AI systems. One  potential approach is designing instructional stra tegies that explicitly link Markov chains to advanced AI technologies by examining enhancements such as incorporating longer memory, leveraging large datasets, and manag ing complex contexts. These connections can help students understand how foundational models act as the building blocks for the development of advanced AI systems like ChatGPT (Michaeli et al., 2022).
