# The Beauty in Science: Understanding the Role of Art in Youth STEAM Identity Development

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In this paper we expand on discussions of STEM identity to make space for including discourse on creativity, art, and making within science spaces and further normalize the use of STEAM (STEM plus Art) in academic discourse, and within science practice. ArtScience provides a space for youth and adults in a STEM project setting to honor the appreciation of aesthetics within science practice and further our understanding of STEAM identity development. We present findings from a participatory design research project involving high school youth and educators working alongside scientists to further their interests in STEM areas.

## Introduction

The impact of Science, Technology, Engineering, and  Math (STEM) programming continues to be a topic of interest among researchers as we further understand how STEM influences educational experiences, increases the number of individuals entering STEM careers, provid es equitable learning opportunities, and supports l earner identity development (Eisenhart et al, 2015; Nasir & Vakil, 2017). It is also becoming acceptable to include art in STEM discourse. STEAM (STEM plus Art) and ArtScience (Bang et al., 2014) have been explored in the learning sciences as spaces where creativity and science int ersect. In this study we analyze an episode where a rtistic appreciation is honored and explored within the con text of a larger participatory design research proj ect focused on data science and community-engaged STEM research.

## Conceptual framework

Barajas-Lopez and Bang (2018) discuss the “purposes, relati onships, and identities afforded by and structuring interaction and meaning-making in” STEAM practices as central to equity-building in STEAM programming (p. 7). Bang et al. (2014) use the term ArtScience to refer to approaches that consider what types of making, creating, or art are present within other domains of STEM, specifically science. Within this context of ArtScience, identity development can be viewed “as the ongoing generatio n and negotia tion of relations  within a multiplicity of possible trajectories” within STEAM practice (Bang et al., 2014, p. 1440). The STEM Learner Identity Model (Hazari et al., 201 0) identifies components of STEM identity, including competence in one’s ability to understand  STEM content, recognition of oneself and others as  persons who do STEM work, performance, or a belief in one’s ability to do STEM tasks, and interest or curiosity in STEM ideas. Building from this model, we consider what A rtScience affords us conceptually as we explore the  ways youth participate in an out-of-school learning environment focused on data science and STEM research. We ask: What is the role of ArtScience in shaping youth identity development in STEM in the context of our program?

## Research context

The current study is part of a larger participatory design research project (Bang & Vossoughi, 2016) called Seeds 2 EMPWER (S2 EMPWER) aimed at engaging high school youth and educators in data science and STEM research at a university over the course of one yea r. Five youth and two educators work alongside university toxicologists, environmental scientists, and learni ng scientists to collect particulate matter (PM) da ta on campus and in their communities, analyze the impact of environmental toxins on human lung cells, and develop research questions based on individual and shared interests.

Participants were recruited through social media and word of mouth through existing school district relationships. The PIs focused on inviting science teachers and students from schools situated near ar eas known in the local area as being affected by poor air quality and high traffic patterns, based on cur rent research led by the geospatial scientist on the project. Prior to t he initial workshop, youth and educators participat ed in focus groups or individual interviews to better understand their interests and reasons for participating in the project. While S2 EMPWER is a year-long program, we focus here on the first two-day workshop that took place over a weekend at the university. Participants collected PM data on a campus walk and discussed their findings to hypothesize causes of spikes in PM. They then went into the environmental science wet  lab to introduce diesel environmental pollutants (DEP) to human lung cells to observe the effect.

## Data collection

Audio data and fieldnotes were collected during ind ividual and focus group interviews with participant s prior to the first workshop meeting. Two youth participants were interviewed via Zoom and three youth participants were interviewed as a group at a coff ee shop near their homes. Interviews typically last ed 25-45 minutes and focused on youth interests, their STEM experiences, and wha t they hoped to do during the program. These two “g roups'' of youth attend two different high schools in schoo l districts, about 16 miles apart. All are juniors (11th grade). During the workshops, video, audio, and fieldnote data were collected during all activities by two members of the research team. Over 14 hours of video data were col lected for this initial analysis. Althoug h data collection is ongoing during the Fall of 2023, we focus on these existing data sources in the present analysis.

## Data analysis

A content log was utilized to capture moments in th e video in five-minute increments. Author 1 then conducted preliminary coding of these logs. Initial coding in cluded noticings on identity, power negotiations am ong participants, mathematical sensemaking, and artistic appreciation based on conjectures guiding the project design.

Using the STEM learner identity model (Hazari et al., 2010) initial noticings of identity work were re-coded for competence, recognition, performance, and interest.  However, instanc es of honoring the beauty in science emerged during this initial analysis, which were no t captured through this model. We began referring t o these moments as appreciation and coded for these instances.

For the present paper, a three-minute video clip of one youth, Everett (a pseudonym), loo king at mouse brain slices on a microscope with a university toxi cologist, was analyzed using interaction analysis (Jordan & Henderson, 1995). Several instances of artistic app reciation were noticed in th e data, which warranted a follow up interview with Everett for member checking to oc cur. We include some of Everett’s statements from t his interview for clarity in our findings section.

During the initial two-day workshop, youth and educator participants engaged i n science sensemaking while collecting PM data on a university campus and in the environmental science wet lab with toxicologists, Dr. L and her graduate student, Tyler, examining the effects of DEP on human lung cells. In the wet lab, participants rotated through statio ns to practice using pipettes to accurately move dy e from one well to another to prepare them for the fo llowing day in which they would be applying DEP to human lung cells to observe the effect on the cells. Of note is that Ev erett appeared more comfortable and confident completing the activities thandid the educators. Figure 1 captures some of the pivotal moments in th is interaction. In image one Dr. L pushes her chair back and swivels toward Tyler. In the second image Tyler is reaching over to the controls of the micro scope to demonstrate how to capture an image (which Tyler la ter admits he uses as a wallpaper image on his lapt op). In image three Dr. L is standing to allow Tyler to step in and Everett is taking photos with his phone, and in the final image Tyler has taken Dr. L’s spot, added an overlay on the data display, and created a clearer image of the cells being analyzed. Everett is once again taking photos with his phone. All of these moves construct ArtScience as being wo rthy of time and attention. The two adult scientist s supported STEAM identity through cultivation of Art Science practice. Everett audibly extended the word “galaxy” (see Table 1) confirming his identity as a  “see-er” of STEAM  phenomenon through cultivating ArtScience visual practice. The moves made by Dr. L  and Tyler honor the interest Everett showed within  the domains of science and art, and the beauty within science and contribute to the shaping of STEAM identity. In the lab the following day participants viewed hu man lung cells dosed with the DEP, and the conversation once again turned to the mouse neuron images displayed the day before, and how they slice the tissue to create slides. Dr. L says, “It’s kind of an art,” to which Tyler replies, “The artists always really like it [cutting].” When Tyler displayed an image not seen before, Everett said “Ooooohh,” and made a waving motion at the screen. Eventually, Everett took control of the microscope to look through different slides and took photos with his phone. In a follow up interview with Everett, Author 1 ask ed him to talk about this clip. He shared that he h ad been so passionate in that moment and even thought “this is what I want to do with the rest of my life.” He mentioned that what he was seeing was “real ly pretty.” When I asked him to explain what he mea nt by that he stated, “I’ve always been a very artistic person. I’ve always found, like really cool, artistic and nuanced things in science, and that keeps me interested in it.” He talked about the creativity involved coming up with hypotheses in science as requiring a “deep and innate creativity.”

## Discussion

While Bang et al. (2014) notice the science within art practice, our illustration depicts art within science practice. This study deepened our understanding of STEAM iden tity development and the role of ArtScience in the doing of science. Specifically, we saw how noticing the beauty in science reinforces i nterest in science discovery, and deepens the way scientists think about science conc epts. These findings support previous work drawing on the STEM identity model and ArtScience in demonstrating the significant role of creativity in science identity work. Future research with this project should continue examining how artistic appreciation shows up in science spaces and what role it plays in STEAM identity development.
