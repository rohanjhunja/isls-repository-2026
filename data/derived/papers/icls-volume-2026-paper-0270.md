# Promoting Students’ Critical Data Literacies Through Their Creation of Dynamic Visualizations of Real-Time Biometric Data

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Creative, dynamic data visualization offers opportunities to develop learners’ critical data literacies —the skills and understanding needed to evaluate and use data to inform decisions. We describe You:Quantified, a web app and curriculum for middle and high school students to visualize real-time biometric data through dynamic displays. Individual and focus group interviews with nine teachers reflecting on their program implementations with their 67 students suggest that creative, dynamic data visualization can spark students’ awareness, curiosity, and experimentation with data. At the same time, it can pose challenges due to the need to build fluency in, and understanding of how physiology, technology, and data intersect to capture experiences that are biologically, socially, and environmentally complex. This study adds to the literature on pedagogical approaches for building students’ critical data literacies. It especially highlights opportunities and challenges in using real-time biometric data visualization in classroom settings.

Advances in wearable biosensing technologies fuel an increasingly popular Quantified Self movement, and present opportunities for learners to develop critical data literacy—that is, the skills and understanding needed to use and evaluate data to make infor med decisions (Wilkerson & Polman, 2020)—through curiosity about their own bodies, behaviors, and emotions (Hernández -Mustieles et al., 2024). Biosensors that capture neurophysiological measurements such as heart rate, respiration, facial movement, eye blink rate, galvanic skin response, and electroencephalography (EEG), are especially relevant in classrooms, as these data can be used to infer cognitive states relevant to attention, emotion, and stress (Davidesco et al., 2021). Opportunities to work with th ese data can allow learners to understand the biological, environmental, and social contexts of their wellbeing, and to develop the skills and understanding necessary to use data to answer questions, address problems, and inform decisions (Wolff et al., 2016). Moreover, engaging with such data can enable students to critically examine the possibilities and constraints of data-driven insight, the behavioral consequences of data tracking, and the ethical dimensions of managing personal data (Hernández-Mustieles et al., 2024). At the same time, data visualization has become ubiquitous, shaping how individuals and communities interpret patterns and trends that inform decisions —from personal wellbeing to the collective management of resources. Creative, dynamic data visualizations  in particular, as seen in information science, journalism and art (The New York Times, n.d., Yau, n.d., Mieback et al., 2022), offer novel ways to engage broader audiences with data, and to understand the intangible aspects of human experience. By bridging scientific, artistic, and civic goals, creative data visualization offers opportunities to discover patterns and trends, and to convey evidence-based arguments that persuade diverse audiences (Jiang & Kahn, 2020). In contrast to static visualizations, dynamic visualizations allow the depiction of data changing over time. Biosensors themselves detect analytes from the body (e.g., glucose in sweat, light absorption by one’s fingertip, 1837 muscle movement), and convert these into electrical signals that are then displayed in real-time data streams (Bhalla et al., 2016). These raw data are often noisy (e.g., Fig.1 Panel B), but can be re-represented in interpretable formats, such as heart rat e, step count, or even other dynamic real-time visualizations (e.g., Fig 1. Panel D). Because of their unique affordances for engaging public audiences, and communicating information that impacts society (e.g., in terms of resource distribution, climate change policy) (Jia & Sundar, 2024), and because dynamic visualizations, compared to static ones, can tend to overwhelm and confuse viewers (Hegarty et al., 2003), it is critical to build learners’ dynamic data visualization literacy: Their abilities to understand the relationship between data and visualizations, appreciate their non-neutrality, and infer implications from their interpretation (Bach et al., 2023; Ge et al., 2023).

To address the dearth of programs that center students' real-time self-data (Hernandez-Serrano, 2024), we present a curriculum and web-based application for students to visualize real-time biometric data; and explore teachers’ observations of students’ development of critical data visualization literacies. Program and curriculum Our team of learning scientists, neuroscientists, educators, and developers, created You:Quantified [youquantified.mindhive.science], a web-based application and curriculum that engages students in exploring data generated by their own brains, bodies and behaviors. Students use biosensing devices including portable EEG headsets and EmotiBits (Montgomery et al., 2024), and a custom-built, online coding environment, to visualize real-time biometrics such as heart rate, tone of voice, facial movement, and brainwaves. To ease students’ entry into coding visualizations, the app provides a library of already-coded creative visualizations set up to map onto different data sources (e.g., a flower, on which petal sizes and colors can change with heart rate or tone of voice). Students can modify these visualizations, or create their own. Meanwhile, the 3-4-week long curriculum (2 class sessions/week) covers foundations in data, creative data visualization, data ethics, and careers involving biometric data. It leads students through data capture demonstrations and discussions, and finally, guides students’ independent inquiry into questions of their choice using their own biometric data. ## Participants

Nine instructors each integrated the You:Quantified app and curriculum into their middle school or high school teaching, with 67 students in total. Instructors were 5 science teachers, 2 math teachers, and 2 after-school facilitators from four public schoo ls, and one private school across two large urban cities in the Northeastern United States. The instructors each had around 10+ years of teaching experience, but all were new to the program, 1838 and to using biometric data. Instructors decided to participate because they wished to engage their students in inquiry, analysis, and interpretation of data connected to real life. Our team offered initial professional development sessions for teachers, and technical support throughout the program implementations. Data and analysis We conducted six 30-minute long individual interviews with teachers following their classroom implementations, and one 90-minute long focus group with eight teachers (who participated based on scheduling availability) to compare and contrast their experien ces, and to inform program modifications. Focus groups and interviews took place on Zoom, and asked teachers, among other things, to reflect on challenges and surprises in teaching with the app and curriculum, and their observations of student learning. This study focuses on our thematic analysis of teachers’ interview and focus group transcripts (Squires, 2023). To complement our interpretations, we referred to data from classroom observations conducted by our team, student artifacts (e.g., inquiry questions, written reflections, finished projects), and responses to a pre and post survey, which prompted students’ explanations of concepts such as data and data quality, and to rate their curiosity about, and interests in engaging with data. Data excerpts below illustrate teachers’ perceptions of learning opportunities and challenges, focusing on evidence of participants’ critical data visualizations literacy practices, including their appreciation for, and understanding of the nature, purpose, and implications of biometric data visualizations. Curiosity, experimentation, and application Overall, students expanded their views of data, coming to understand that data is being generated by everyone, all the time. Based on their responses to the pre/posttest question, “What is data?”, some students went from noting broad kinds of data and their applications (e.g., “reports on weather from the past can help people understand what the weather might be like in the future, or study climate change. Reactions to stimuli could be used to understand behavior”), to specific kinds of data and their applications (e.g., “The frequency of brain waves is data that people can use to determine reactions to certain stimuli. Heart rate is data that can be used to track if someone is healthy, if they are exercising, and how much oxygen their body might have.”). Students expressed curiosity about biometric data and its applications. One teacher remarked that the tools:“allowed students to feel [...] like they were transported to the neurological department at a research hospital and they loved it.” Another teacher commented that her students “thought it was like a superpower that technology could detect your emotional state, and that someone could act on [this].” Teachers reported moments of discovery, as students, most of whom had “never heard of an EEG before [...] were able to see how their brain is always working [...] and how certain parts of the brain controlled certain parts of their bodies.”  These data led students to reevaluate assumptions, for example: “Students learned they do not always interpret other people’s emotional data accurately.” Moreover, these data encouraged students’ curiosity and experimentation, and drawing of actionable implications from their observations. For instance, one teacher described an initially disengaged student who became interested on observing that: “My coping skills have been all wrong. When I listen to music, my theta doesn't go down, and my alpha doesn't go up. But when I breathe, my alpha goes up and my theta goes down. And then when I breathe and listen to music, it does the same thing as when I'm breathing. The idea that I put on music to calm down is crap [...] and I need a new strategy of deep breathing before I start the music, because otherwise it's not going to do anything for me.” Issues with interpreting dynamic data visualizations While dynamic displays offered engaging entry points into understanding biometric data, they also posed challenges for both students and teachers. One such challenge was in understanding the functioning of biosensing devices. Regarding an exercise in detecting movement from facial landmarks, one teacher commented that “My kids had no experience with sensors [...] so they had never seen data with noise. They didn’t know how to extract meaning from noisy data and they assumed the fault was in the way they set  up the devices.”  Another noted that “Students were confused about when data was recording or not.” Occasionally, these challenges of understanding biometric data, combined with creative data visualization, led to misinterpretation and overinterpretation. For instance, one sample visualization that we made available in the app visualized a sun, the rising or setting of which mapped onto levels of theta and alpha brainwaves. We chose this visual because theta and alpha waves relate to states of reflection and relaxation, and sunrises and sunsets are commonly associated with a sense of calm. However, this m etaphor proved to be 1839 ambiguous. One teacher, wishing to demonstrate the stressful impacts of social media, asked a student to open the TikTop app on his phone, and “pick something that calms you down.” As the teacher recounts, the student: “opens it up. Boom! Theta goes through the roof. I was like, ‘Picture the [statewide standardized] test: BOOM. Same result. How did the test that you hate give you the same result as your Tiktok?’ Students were amazed Tik Tok did not calm them down because  it is often what they go to when they are upset and trying to get away from stress.” While this teacher aimed to show that TikTok can be as stressful as a standardized test, she had in fact confused theta with beta waves (as did the student quoted above on his coping skills), only the latter of which are linked to states of anxiety. Moreover, such inferences are in practice based on multiple neurophysiological signals, such that each can offset the limitations of the other. This case demonstrates the challenges of interpreting creative dynamic visualizations, particularly when these concern data new to learners (brainwaves), when spatial mappings have already strongly associated meanings (rising and falling, more and less), when the significance of visual metaphors is subjective (a calm vs. a blazing sunrise), and when the subject matter may  make viewers prone to confirmation bias (the narrative of social media’s harm on mental health) (Li et al., 2025). Issues with designing dynamic data visualizations Another challenge was with regard to designing visualizations, students struggled to move beyond conventional representations, toward more creative designs. As one teacher described of an activity in which students were to design visualizations of data of their choice, many students chose to visualize their phone screen time: “and we [teachers] were trying to push them: ‘Well, you could make it like a flower and make like this petal bigger, because, like you use that app more’ But [...] they just all made a bar graph of their data.” Concurrently, students were compelled by the rhetorical capacity of visualization. As one teacher reflected, many of her students tend to see “clear delineation between art and science.”  However, they were interested in “the idea that you can [...] manipulate people with data visualizations,”  that is, “make someone feel something, and [...] then have the extra expertise [...] to go back and say, ‘This is actually based on something that [...] I collected using scientific methods’” This teacher explained how she had her students reflect on the 1968 presidential election “because the polling and the data visualizations of the election maps were saying something else. And yet the result was [...] completely different.” In class, students discussed “how much [data visualizations are] trying to shape people's behavior? And in what way are the data visualizations they're seeing on the smartwatch trying to influence their behavior?” Students moreover demonstrated their awareness of rhetorical intentions when critiquing one another’s visualizations. For instance, one student group completed a “lying study,” in which they sought to detect whether a peer is lying based on their brainwave  activity during conversation. During peer feedback,  “the other student who did the other lying study, who had inconclusive results [...] was really challenging them on the way that they presented the data visualization,” because, as the teacher described, the manner in which they visualized their data “was to make the correlation look stronger than it was.” Together, these findings show how students attended to the non-neutrality of data visualizations. In coming to recognize how these can be designed to persuade and shape attitudes (Jia & Sundar, 2024), students came to question designers’ intentions, which is an important skill in critical data visualization literacy. This study highlights the challenges and opportunities for building students’ critical data literacies via their design of creative, dynamic data visualizations of real-time biometric data. Overall, students expanded their views of what can be considered data, and demonstrated curiosity and inclinations to inquire into, and apply interpretations of data to inform adjustments to their behaviors. In working with real-world, messy data, students came to understand how the process and tools for data collection figure into the quality of data. In designing their own data visualizations, students moreover came to appreciate that representations have rhetorical intentions that must be considered in tandem with data quality, when interpreting meaning from visualizat ions Simultaneously, both students’ and teachers’ sensemaking with real-time biometric data visualization appeared to be complicated by needing for understanding at the convergence of domain content, technology, and data: They had to at once make sense of novel  content (the physiology behind brainwaves and other body signals are generated), of novel technologies (e.g., how wearable devices capture and process biometric signals), and of novel data representations.
