# Centralizing the Multispecies Margins: Learning with Wild Barnacle Geese in a Zoo Day Camp

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
To recognize education as more-than-human, this study examines how wild barnacle geese participated in learning practices at a zoo day camp. We focused on the open-ended assemblages of various materialities and lifeways with multispecies ethnography. Although the geese were not the main educational focus, their presence shaped the camp’s atmosphere and required embodied adaptation. Attentiveness emerged during mundane activities, highlighting the need for pedagogies and research to attend to vibrant multispecies margins of education.

## Introduction

In this paper, we examine environmental education i n a zoo day camp from a multispecies perspective, f ocusing on the role of wild barnacle geese in shaping pedag ogical activities. Our approach is inspired by the animal turn, an emerging scholarly focus that challenges human exceptionalism by emphasizing the agency, experiences, and contributions of nonhuman animals to social, cultur al, and educational practices (Weil, 2010). Highlighting such relational ontologies requires rethinking education as a more-than-human practice (Rautio et al., 2021) Researchers have called for educational practices to recognize humans as part of the world and consider how to foster learning with the world rather than about it (Taylor, 2017). This raises a need to consider animals as more than mere objects of learning or pedagogical tools designed for human benefit; they should be recognized as participants with intrinsic value and agency (Spannring, 2017; Tammi et al., 2023). Aligned with this goal, we focused on how other-than-human animals become part of the learning activities on their own terms. Our attention was drawn by the active presence of wild barnacle geese, which nest on the same island where the zoo is located. We asked: How do the geese shape and are shaped by the pedagogical practices of the zoo’s day camp? What kinds of attunements and becomings emerge from these encounters?

## Context of the study: Sharing a place with barnacle geese

While this study focuses on one four-day camp, we have been conducting multispecies ethnography with barnacle geese in the zoo and surrounding urban areas  for three years. The rapidly increasing presence of  barnacle geese in Finland (Yrjölä et al., 2017) has sparked public discussions emphasizing conflicts and problems. Also, the zoo has had to balance two sometimes conflicting aims: ensuring nesting peace for the geese and providing an enjoyable experience for human visitors. The polici es adopted by the zoo include guest education, cord oning off nesting areas, and obtaining special permission to relocate and destroy the geese's nests. The ethical complexities of these practices illustrate the non-innocent aspects of more-than-human care (Puig de la Bellacasa, 2017). The camp took place in June 2023, involving ten childre n aged 10 to 12, two environmental educators, and two teacher assistants aged 15 to 16. During the camp, children observed zoo animals and engaged in handson activities such as crafting enrichment, cleaning enclosures, and gardening. In June, few geese were nesting, and many of the goslings had already hatched and the geese’ families spent their time grazing on the lawns of the island.  Since the geese were not the pedagogical focus, enc ounters with them occurred mainly in the mundane moments around these planned events, such as walkin g between locations and informal conversations duri ng breaks.

Multispecies ethnography and storytelling Multispecies ethnography (Ogden et al., 2013)  allowed us to foreground lifeways and materialities  often marginalized in educational research. We employ ed the concept of assemblage (Tsing, 2015) to focus on open-ended gatherings and examine how species are brought into being through relations (Vandooren et al., 2016). As educational researchers focus on other-than-human animals, it was a novel approach for us and included a process of unlearning the anthropocentric inheritances of educational research. In the field, we dedicated time to slowness and engaging other senses to expand beyond verbal and visual thinking (Hohti & Tammi, 2024). For example, we listened to the soundscapes of the island to recogn ize actors participating through sound. One of us e ngaged in educational practices while the other remained on the sidelines. This distance allowed us to notice the more-thanhuman life taking place around the humans. The empirical material consisted of four hours of video recordings, handwritten notes, photographs, and interviews. Our method of analysis relied on narrative methodol ogies, especially on the practice of re-telling (Haraway, 2015; Tsing, 2015)  and acknowledging the inherently multispecies nature of  educational practices. Multispecies storytelling allow s us to focus on situated detail, to suggest a more modest position for the human protagonist, and to engage with hesitant ethics of knowing (Hohti & Tammi, 2024). Video recordings served as a tool to challenge our assumptions and deepen our understanding (Charteris et al., 2020). Many subtle actions and inconspicuous actors that escaped notice amidst mor e attention-grabbing events in the field emerged through the capabilities offered by video technology  (Valasmo et al., 2023). Ethnographic field notes and interviews helped us to understand shared emotional experiences and atmosph eres when we sought connections beyond the immediately perceptible assemblages toward broader phenomena (Hohti & Tammi, 2024). ## Findings: Learning with barnacle geese

As preliminary findings, we present four stories: a narrative of aggressive geese, negotiations of sharing the space, the affective power of the geese, and atmospheres.

The public discourse in Finland emphasizes the aggressiveness of geese, a perspective also central to the zoo's communication about geese, such as in guideposts (Figure 1). However, during everyday practices the geese got the opportunity to be seen as  individuals rather than merely as representatives o f their species (Vandooren, 2019). Since the goslings had already hatched, the geese h ad noneed to protect the nests. While moving around with the goslings, they acted mostly peacefully and avoided human contact. The instructors pointed this out and thus unraveled the narrative of aggressiveness by guiding  the children to interpret the geese's body language: “Since the parents aren't showing any tongue or hissing, they're not angry or aggressive toward us in any way.” Humans and geese had tonegotiate sharing the island by embodied means. The geese negotiated through protective behavior around nests and steering gosli ngs away from places dense with humans.  The humans’ embodied negotiations included teachers instruct ing the children to slow down in the playground so as not to disturb the geese nesting nearby. The teachers also helped the children to  learn to respond to geese with calming gestures. However, part of the negotiation process extended beyond the camp, including the removal of nests from busy areas. Such complex and non-innocent practices aimed at maintaining distance exemplified how multispecies relations are not merely harmonious (Nxumalo & Pacini-Ketchabaw, 2017) and carry uneven power relations. Even though the geese mostly remained on the margin s of summer camp activities (Figure 1), they possessed the power to influence them. Their presen ce sparked the children's curiosity, making them st op to ask questions or admire the cuteness of the goslings. While balancing with the schedules of preplanned activities, the teachers discussed the geese's ways of life with the childre n. These moments offered possibilities for cultivating the arts of attentiveness: the ability to notice and respond to other species (Vandooren et al., 2016). T hese encounters illustrated that not all learning opport unities need to be pre-planned; instead, they can emerge from the everydayness of multispecies relations and the act of giving space for other species to affect. The geese influenced the atmosphere of the camp; their honks entangled with teachers' talk, and they were constantly seen moving at the edges of camp ac tivities. On the other hand, there were also the ge ese's own atmospheres (Lorimer et al., 2019). When we spent time near their nests, the children's voices and colorful clothing appeared as occasional flows  in the background. Multispecies ethnography allowed  us to notice how while the geese were situated at the margins of the education al practice, so was the educational practice situat ed at the margins of the geese's lifeways.

To sum up, r ecognizing multispecies relations emerging at the e dges of pedagogical activities can provide important insights into how these relations  shape educational practices and how new forms of c o-living and attentiveness might arise in these margins.
