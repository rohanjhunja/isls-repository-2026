# Care and Complexity: Designing for Holistic Understandings of and Within a Pandemic

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
The COVID-19 pandemic spans multiple systems and dimensions – social, scientific, emotional, and axiological. This paper describes the collaborative design process of an elementary-age program addressing COVID-19, systems thinking, and photography from this multi-dimensional perspective. With the COVID-19 pandemic as both curricular content and design context, an educational design emerged that includes care as the centerpiece of both design structure and process. Using an ethnographic approach, we outline the evolution of the curricular theme – from relationships, to protection, to care – and how this theme is realized in our design. Through a perspective of care, we consider what it means to include socioemotional elements in systems thinking, specifically axiological considerations. This paper contributes to the need for educational designs that consider COVID-19 from multiple dimensions, to understandings of how collaborative processes can shape design, and to (re)theorizations of systems thinking in the learning sciences.

## Introduction

The effects of COVID-19 have cascaded throughout ne arly all aspects of our lives – some anticipated, o thers unpredictable. Global and local systems are respond ing to the pandemic’s effects on the economy, healt hcare, education, and individual well-being.  To think critically and adapt in the pandemic necessitates systems thinking to make sense of the interrelated and emergent phenomena arising from COVID-19. The pandemic reaches into social, scientific, emotional, and, we argue, axiological (i.e., pertaining to ethics and values) spheres. This paper describes the collaborative design process of an elementary-age (8–11-year-old) informal curriculum addressing COVID-19, systems thinking, and photography from this multi-dimensional perspective. The Care and COVID curriculum attempts to weave to gether multiple dimensions of the COVID-19 pandemic – spanning from individual to global syste ms. Care and COVID was designed during the pandemic, almost entirely over Zoom, a context that fostered unique perspectives for design that shifted with th e collaborators’ personal reflections of relationships, protection, and care in the pandemic. The inclusion of zoonotic disease science concepts, multi-dimensional systems thinking, and photography intends to engage learners with concepts of agency, ethics, and care. Care and COVID underwent three thematic shifts in our collaborative design process, outlined in this paper, that refocused the curriculum from what and how we protect from COVID-19 to why we should protect ourselves and others from COVID-19. The curriculum has not yet been implemented with students, with plans to do so in Spring and Summer 2022. The goals of this paper are to describe th e evolution of the Care and COVID design, how the contribution of multiple voices led to the theme of care, and to describe our emergent design conjecture, that attending to care in design may bring socio-emotional, and axiological dimensions into systems thinking.  This paper presents the design process and elements of our first iteration of Care and COVID that reflect a multi-dimensional approach to systems thinking, offering a possible (re)theorization of systems thinking within the learning sciences as well as an understanding of how collaborative processes may shape such design. Theory The COVID-19 pandemic and our potential entry into a “viralocene” (Kristensen, 2020) present urgent imperatives for us to make sense of the complex rel ations and interconnections involved in emerging di sease systems while imagining new forms of resilience with/in them. The pandemic reminds us of the intricate, mutual inextricabilities among environmental destruction, viral mutation, and multi-species relations, to name a few. The learning sciences have long attended to such forms of biological complexity and relationality in terms of systems thinking. Diverse approaches to systems thinking within the  field share a general commitment to investigating and supporting reasoning about complex phenomena in ways that attend to multiple factors interacting a cross a wide variety of micro-and macro-scales (e.g., Hmel o-Silver & Azevedo, 2006; Penner, 2000; Resnick, 20 06; Sabelli, 2006). Most approaches to systems thinking focus on rational-cognitive forms of understanding emergent processes (Resnick, 1996), system elements and dynamics (Chandler & Boutilier, 1992), and structure-behaviorfunction relations (Hmelo-Silver & Azevedo, 2006). While these remain important, the lived poignancies of the current pandemic – as both context and content of o ur design project – have made visible some of the a ffective and axiological lacunae within predominant approaches to systems thinking. The pandemic’s impacts on education have b rought into frame fundamental questions about the relationship between learning and what it means to care for one another. Because of this, we also theo rize our ICLS2022 Proceedings 1165 © ISLS emerging curriculum design in terms of care as described by Hobart and Kneese (2020) as “strategies for enduring precarious worlds” composed of an “affective connec tive tissue” formed by “a relational set of discour ses and practices between people, environments, and objects  that approximate… ‘empathy,’ ‘sympathy,’ or ‘fello w feeling’” (p. 2). This turn toward theorizing care beyond the strict locus of the individual to “span a breadth of localities: selves, communities, and social worlds” (Hobart & Kneese, 2020, p. 2) resonates with the cross-scalar holism of the systems turn in learning sciences whi le better disclosing the socio-emotional and axiolo gical dimensions of complex systems.

## Methods

The designers of Care and COVID co-developed the curriculum within the context of a broader participatorydesign partnership (Bang & Vossoughi, 2016; DiSalvo  et al., 2017). The partnership includes racially, geographically, and professionally diverse formal and informal educators serving rural, historically non-dominant communities, as well as university educators and researchers. We aim to contribute to the diverse and emerging approaches to participatory work in the learning sc iences by responding to the research question of ho w the curriculum’s core thematic focus has developed and refined over time through co-design. We aim to provide an ethnographic account (Erickson, 1984) of the curric ulum’s history (and imagined future) that interrela tes: (1) analysis of records and artifacts from our design process, (2) our own evolving theoretical perspectives on what it means-or could mean-to support the development of systems thinking about the pandemic, and (3) reflections on the evolution of our own collaborative relationships and processes. Our data include collaboratively produced notes and written reflections spanning nearly a yea r, as well as fieldnotes and Zoom recordings of des ign meetings.

Members of the Care and COVID design team met for one hour weekly between Februar y 2021 and April 2022 and completed asynchronous tasks outside of meetings. Early stages of design included consultation with teaching artists, medical practitioners, and advisory members. Over this period, the design team was tasked with developing a 10-hour curriculum. From initiati on, the design team believed that centering the cur riculum around a systems-focused theme may support elementa ry-aged learners to elucidate systems thinking. The development of this theme – beginning with relationships, then evolving to protection, and ultimately coalescing around care –  highlights a shift in our design team’s thinking ab out the intersection of photography, systems thinking, and the COVID-19 experience. We describe the evolution of our design below, a process that leads us to conjecture that attending to care may bring socio-emotional and axiological dimensions into systems thinking and that photography may engage students in acts of caring. Design evolution: Relationships, protection, and care Relationships Early design sessions of Care and COVID, beginning in February 2021, included open brainstorming aroun d photography, visual literacy, and the COVID-19 experience. The social heteroglossia (Bahktin, 1981) was evident as the six initial design team members had a wide variety of professional and educational backgrounds, spanning K-12 education, adult education, science and art education, research, and healthcare. Participants were excited to leverage the arts to engage learners in acts of storytelling, especially in regard to the inequities of healthcare and COVID-19. Each weekly meeting was abuzz with ideas,  constructed from the diversity of voices and perso nal pandemic experiences. Challenged to include COVID-19 disease science, photography, and systems thinking in the curriculum, the design team grappled with how to connect these topics in a way accessible to elementary-aged learners. Our own emotional and relational experiences with COVID-19 surfaced and revealed the many ways we tried to relate to one another. As a result, relationships became the beacon not only for our work as a team, but for our curriculum as well.

We saw the theme of relationships as one t hat could bridge curricular needs across photography, systems thinking, and COVID-19. We viewed relationships as a potential way to include multiple dimensions of COVID19 systems, including socio-emotional perspectives. Specifically, the design team included a focus on students’ relationships with their own bodies, families, and communities, as well as their trust and distrust of healthcare and information. We then employed photography as a way to elicit and communicate relationships and emotions. By March 2021, initial drafts of the curriculum included three segmented foci – relationships with self, relationships with communities, and relationships globally – spec ifically attending to how COVID-19 interplays withi n and across these scales. This curriculum structure-having students consider relationships at varying scales-became a fixture in Care and COVID that remained throughou t design. At each level, photography became the anc hor ICLS2022 Proceedings 1166 © ISLS through which learners could explore COVID-19 relationships in both affective and scientific ways, and a medium to express, compare, and contrast their lived exper iences with their peers. An activity at this stage of design prompted students to take photographs of playground s in the pandemic to explore their personal and the ir community’s relationship to these shared spaces.

Protection In June 2021, the Care and COVID design team shifted from six to four members as tw o community educators opted not to extend their involvement beyond the initial commitment. The remaining four team members included the principal investigator, two graduate students, and one community educator with expertise in arts e ducation. At this time, the Care and COVID curricular theme shifted from relationships to an agentic focus on protection. The shift to protection was prompted by a desire to  move into action surrounding COVID-19, rather than  only analyzing COVID-19 connections and impacts. We want ed to empower students as agentic members of their communities and society with valued stories about protection to be shared through photography. For example, the design team co-designed a lesson positioning students as community protectors. Here, students take self-portraits as protectors in their community, tapping into their own stories and understandings of what protection looks like. Prior design work around relationships ensured that the theme of protection was not exclusively scient ific (e.g., learning about how masks reduce transmission), but also included social elements of resilience around COVID19 (e.g., examining healthcare access in rural communities). We viewed protection from COVID-19 as relational, encompassing the previous theme, with individuals relating to themselves, their communities, and society through protective action. We embraced protection as an expansive concept, reflected in our design by moving s tudents from activities about individual protection, to community protection, to societal protection – keeping the structure originally implemented with the theme of relationships. For example, a curricular feature on the societal scale has learners create photo narratives of protection (or lack thereof) to tell stories of systemic assets or  barriers to protection. These stories come from their own notions and ideas of what protection can and should be, and allows for the overlapping of individual, community, and social protections. Care In October 2021, Care and COVID underwent its most recent thematic shift when the design team collectively reflected on the essence of the overall curriculum.  Initially focusing on relationships, and then prot ection, the notion of care ultimately solidified as the curricular theme. We view the current theme of care as a t ransition to not only explore what and how we protect, but also why we should protect ourselves and others from COVID-19. This axiological shift in the curriculum encourages students to value their own lives and the lives of others and to consider which actions represent care. For example, one activity has students write, act out, and photograph skits depicting what it looks like to care for the preciousness of their lives, specifically the preciousness of their health, bodies, and families. Throughout the curriculum, students explore acts of care through theatrics, self-portraits, and photo narratives. Care and COVID positions art as an empowering, accessible, physical, and emotional way to care for others, to engage in multi-scalar systematic care through photography and storytelling. The design team also notes care as integra l to our co-design process. The design of Care and COVID has occurred during the COVID-19 pandemic; care for ourselves and for each other has been imperative. We ask and listen to each other, we adapt to the needs of team members, and we bring empathy to the forefront of our design conversations. Maintaining the heteroglossia of our team has become a collective act of care throughout our design process. The focus on care within our design team h as allowed each of us an opportunity to voice our p assions and diverse expertise in design.

## Conclusion

The evolution of Care and COVID ’s design theme, from relationships to protection t o care, represents an expansion from solely cognitive to also include soc io-emotional and axiological dimensions of COVID-19 systems. Our team is exploring how these dimensions, when integrated into systems thinking, can give learners a holistic understanding of a phenomenon, in this case, protection from COVID-19. Through our co-design process, we identified care, focusing on the protection of oneself and others on multiple scales, as one way to  bridge the many dimensions of COVID-19. Using photography, we are exploring the integration of the arts as an act of care; we are exploring how to support learners with tools  to personally make sense of COVID-19 and to care f or themselves, and others, through their art.

Future work will investigate the implement ation of Care and COVID. We will examine how the curriculum does or does not support learners in con sidering COVID-19 systems from multiple dimensions. We ICLS2022 Proceedings 1167 © ISLS wonder: How does the inclusion of care in design support multi-dimensional understandings of systems thinking across socio-emotional and axiological spheres? What role does arts-integration play in expanding the dimensions through which students understand systems? We conjecture that design centered around and within care, utilizing strategies of arts-integration, may support students in developing holistic understandings of systems on multiple scales and in multiple dimensions.
