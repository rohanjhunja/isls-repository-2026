# Disciplinary Dynamics in Interdisciplinary Learning: Comparing STEM and SHAPE Student Outcomes in Team-Based Education

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Interdisciplinary education equips students to address complex societal challenges by integrating diverse perspectives. This study examines how disciplinary differences influence knowledge transfer and integration in an interdisciplinary curriculum. Mixed-method analysis of 2,623 STEM and SHAPE students showed parity in Horizontal Knowledge Transfer outcomes, with differences in discourse: STEM students favored cross-domain application, while SHAPE students emphasized abstraction. Knowledge Integration was shaped by team structure and educator facilitation, reframing differences as epistemic orientations rather than gaps.

Interdisciplinary education is a core concept of modern higher education, necessitated by the multifaceted, contemporary challenges of today (McCune & Entwistle, 2011). Current collaborative learning research heavily emphasizes the concepts of Knowledge Transfer (KT) and Knowledge Integration (KI). KT is further distinguished into Horizontal KT (HKT) tasks, defined as the application  of concepts across structurally similar contexts, and Vertical KT (VKT) tasks, defined as movement across levels of abstraction  (Engle, 2006; Lobato, 2012). Interdisciplinary KI is defined as the reconciliation and synthesis of ideas across multiple fields to achieve a coherent understanding of a given subject matter (Linn, 2005). In a collaborative context, this extends beyond the mere combination of individual disciplinary contributions, requiring that students negotiate the differences in epistemic framing and task abstraction.

The differing epistemological and pedagogical practices native to a student’s discipline may shape their approach to KT tasks and ultimately, KI in an interdisciplinary setting. There is limited work exploring how these differing disciplinary epistemic orientations interact with collaborative processes to shape KI in interdisciplinary teams. This study seeks to address this gap by investigating the se differences between  Science, Technology, Engineering and Mathematics (STEM) and Social Sciences, Humanities, and the Arts for People and the Economy (SHAPE) students in an interdisciplinary, team-based environment. To investigate the KT preferences and KI objectives of interdisciplinary teams, the study was conducted within a semester-long undergraduate course requiring students to work in interdisciplinary teams of 5 –7 members to address real-world challenges through collaborative discussions.  Throughout this course, the student’s understanding of course content would be assessed through weekly I ndividual Readiness Assessments (IRAs), where students worked individually,  and Team Readiness Assessments (TRAs), where in tra-team discussions were allowed. Beyond this, students were also tasked with proposing interdisciplinary solutions to a weekly topic, assessing team-based learning (TBL) abilities. This course was repeated over the course of 4 semesters, with similar content maintained for each semester, with the exclusion of one topic from the first semester for comparability. The test scores of a sample of 2623 students were analyzed, of which 1539 were STEM students, 1084 were SHAPE students.

Quantitative HKT performance was measured through the IRA and TRA quizzes; Structured team were also tasked with collaborating in interdisciplinary teams to develop a final proposal  containing a holistic solution to address a contemporary challenge, designed to examine KI proficiency. Qualitative insights were extracted through semantic analysis carried out on student reflections written at the end of the course. Sentiment analysis was used to elucidate general sentiments and themes from student reflections written at the end of the course. A total of 32 students were also interviewed in focus groups of 10-13 students each. Epistemic preferences diverge in discourse rather than outcomes Based on the most salient terms of the top five topics in  STEM student reflections, we find a stronger focus on cross-domain application language. Phrases like “different fields” and “new technological” suggest STEM students focus on applying technical knowledge across related contexts to solve analogous problems. Conversely, SHAPE student reflections place an emphasis on abstraction and perspective-shifting language. Key phrases like “thought process(es) ” and “cognitive transformation” hint at a focus on deconstructing theories  and learning processes rather thandiscrete outcomes. SHAPE students  also exhibited greater individual variability in IRA performance, with a higher kurtosis of 3.2 on average. This wider range of scores reflects more diverse cognitive approaches in SHAPE students, resulting in a pronounced difference in conceptual understanding and task interpretation.

Knowledge integration is mediated by structural equivalence and facilitation Both STEM and SHAPE students emphasized that increased heterogeneity within a team  increased cognitive interplay among members, fostering a richer intellectual environment. However, it was also noted that in teams where an individual was the sole representative of that discipline, role pigeonholing was a common occurrence.  A student, who was the only Computer Science major in his group, reflected:  “They see me as the tech expert of everything. So sometimes it felt quite overbearing to be the only subject matter expert. Like I didn't have anyone to turn to when it came to like a very specific problem in our scenario”.  Such teams demonstrated a lower level of structural equival ence and reflected limited cognitive diversity. Conversely, teams with a higher degree of structural equivalence were found to perform better, with roles and responsibilities being better balanced among members, allowing for the effective co-construction of knowledge through distributed cognition, bridging epistemological gaps and overcoming the limitations of individual understanding (Akkerman & Bakker, 2011).

Students further reflected that in such scenarios, the role of the educator needs to extend beyond didactic instruction to one of a facilitator, creating an environment for critical dialogue. We find that a capable educator can push the threshold on team heterogeneity by ensuring structural equivalence within teams by ensuring balanced participation and equitable contributions from all members. This study advances research in the learning sciences by distinguishing disciplinary epistemic orientations from performance in interdisciplinary curricula. Instead, while STEM and SHAPE students do diverge on KT tendencies, these orientations ultimately did not predict KI outcomes. Instead, effective interdisciplinary KI is dependent on structural equivalence within a team, with balanced participation, flexible role distributions and facilitative mediation serving as the bedrock of boundary-crossing dialogue essential to distributed cognition.
