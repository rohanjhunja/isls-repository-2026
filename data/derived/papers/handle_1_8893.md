# Encouraging Children to Envision Futures Through Learning in Career Development Classes: Focusing on the Case of Japanese Elementary Schools and “Social Scaffolding”

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study analyzes what children in Japanese primary schools learn through career development classes carried out by volunteers who continuously participate in schoolcommunity partnerships, clarifying the influence of these classes. We analyze children’s compositions describing their impressions of career development classes, utilizing a text mining tool (Tiny Text Miner βversion, or TTM) to analyze our data. Using TTM, we divide text data into words or phrases and analyze tendencies of word usage. Children, as a result, learn how long they will work during their lives, how society at present is constructed, what their future will be like, and what they work for in career development classes. Most children imagine what will happen in their future and future society, so these classes might provide them with the triggers to think about their future and offer “social scaffolding”.

Major issues addressed Education in schools is connected loosely to society, and children are always incorporated into it. Although many of them will work on it after graduating from high school or university, they cannot sufficiently learn the processes of viewing their future, thinking about their dream s, and realizing these future ideal perspectives. A ccording to an investigation by the Japanese Cabinet Office, 60.6% of Japanese young people answered they had brig ht expectations for their future, which is the lowest rate among investigated countries (Japanese Cabinet  Office, 2018). These results signify that it is difficult for Japanese young people to envisage a bright future and to form expectations about their future. To tackle these difficulties, in 2013, the Japanese government presented “4 Basic Directions about Educational Administration”, and one of these directions signifies a “nourishing capa bility for surviving present society”, which promotes career d evelopment. Career development in Japan, in general, is recognized as a way to nourish the ability to live.  At the same time, career development aims to gener ate selfesteem and a sense of work based on children’s curi osity, and to promote children planning their futur e and considering how they want to spend their lives.

Observing research on career development all over t he world indicates that career development in the educational field can be seen to provide effective ways of nourishing children’s perspectives on the f uture (e.g. Peila-Shustera et al., 2019). Previous research mentions that children must acquire the basic perspectives of career adaptability to envision their future and make educational or vocational decisions (Hartung et al., 2011). There is, however, not enough research on childhood career de velopment, especially in non-Western cultures, desp ite studies on adolescence and adulthood (Liu et al., 2 014). Career development is regarded as a way to pr omote children understanding their world, considering alternative goals, and planning courses of actions to meet goals (Magnuson & Starr, 2000). Children’s aspirations and expectations for an occupation change dramatically during the elementary years (Auger et al., 2005). These re sults, however, do not specify how children concretely think or learn from career development classes, and they cannot clarify how children learn alongside career development providers or through comparing themselv es at present to themselves in the future. Moreover, although the importance of connecting schools and s ociety is recognized widely in Japan, there is not enough research about career development and about what children are learning through it. Regarding career development in elementary school, it is not an exaggeration to state that there is no analysis. This study thus seeks to analyze what children in Japanese primary schools learn through career development and to clarify the influence of it.

## Participants and data collection

In this study, we investigate career development in Japanese public primary schools (called A school and B school). One volunteer in his 70s (called Volunteer O) engaged in conversation with A school and B school’s principals, and they decided to hold career development in their schools. Volunteer O also participated in other activities held ICLS2022 Proceedings 913 © ISLS in these schools with fellow volunteers, so he cont inuously kept in touch with these schools, and he a nd the children knew one another. Ninety 5th grade children at A school and Fifty-eight 6th grade children at B school took career development classes in July 2021. These classes were held in such a manner as to prevent transmission of COVID-19, so Volunteer O consciously stood in front of the children while maintaining social distance, using PowerPoint and video (see Figure 1). He had been working at a company relating to communication, so he made presentations about 1) what his job was like, 2) why he began to work at that company, 3) how long we have to work, 4) what we work for, 5) how learning in schools can connect to our jobs, and 6) how to broaden our curiosity. This class contained Volunteer O’s talking and videos relating to his jobs, showing a drone so that children would not be bored (see Figure 2). After finishing classes, teachers encouraged children to write what they learned when they participated. This study analyzed these children’s compositions describing their impressions of the classes. ## Method of analysis

Words and phrases that children often use can be cl arified through text mining tool called Tiny Text M iner βversion (TTM), developed by Naohiro Matsumura and Asako Miura. Papers describing the impressions of career development classes of 148 children are analyzed. B efore analyzing word usage, we designated the words  that should be treated as noise in order to remove words  not directly relating to children’s impressions of  career development classes. We also specified some words a s synonyms that are combined through the analysis. We consequently filled the boxes of nouns, adjectives,  and adverbs to designate objects of analysis. As p art of the procedures stated above, we clarified 1) the tendency of word usage, and 2) relations between modification and re-modification through dependency parsing that can  be ascertained by TTM. After clarifying the tenden cy of word usage and co-occurrence relation among words, we picked up some words or phrases from the analyze d results and observed concrete whole sentences containing these words or phrases. Through the above analysis, we tried to clarify how children learned and felt through career development classes.

We then analyzed relations between modification and re-modification through dependency parsing. The results of analysis are as follows in descending or der: “life course + half”, “future + dream”, “futur e + movie”, “drone + manipulation”, “class + interesting”, “mov ie + interesting”, “future + development”, “drone + interesting”, “job + like”, and “job + money” (see Table 2). The word “future” qualifies “dream”, “movie”, and “development”, so c hildren might imagine future developed society or t heir future dream through watching the movie, which was provided by Volunteer O, relating to future technol ogies. These children also thought “class” or “movie” was “interesting”, so they might experience a degree of interest, though there is the possibility that the impressions stated here are somewhat superficial. T he word “job” is used in the same context as “like”  and “money”, so children may mention future jobs relating to their preferences or monetary conditions. ## Conclusion

Children who participated in career development classes, in this study, were surprised at the reality of what a job entails, and were impressed by the reason for work. This very simple awareness can be said to be chara cteristic of career development in elementary schools because children in this age rage have a tendency to look to the near future according to development stages. These children's purity of perspectives about the future may enhance their feelings that these classes were very interesting. Children, as a result, learned how long they will w ork during their lives, how society at present is constructed,  what their future will be like, and what they work  for in these classes. Most children imagined what will happen in their future and future society, so these classes might provide them with triggers to think about their future perspectives, which inevitably contain positive and a few negative images of the future. Some tools used in these clas ses—for example, movies or drones—could offer a med ium for children to look at the future. In addition, this study signified the possibility of collaboration between teachers, school counselors, and volunteers to realize career  development classes, which broadens the range of j obs that children can learn.

Of course, the career classes analyzed in this study could not provide children with instructions on how to realize their futures; these classes could, howe ver, illuminate children’s futures and potential li fe courses. Providing young people with role models in the futu re encourages them to produce their ideal futures o r to consider ways to realize their future. This process  can be called “social scaffolding” in this study a nd may be related to intergenerational learning (Nakano, 2021). Children learned through relationships with volu nteers or people who provided career development classes, and  the behaviors of these people and the classes them selves became “social scaffolding” for children because ca reer development, in this study, provided children with opportunities to learn and consider their future, p romoting role modeling of children’s futures and en visioning ways for children to achieve their dreams (see Figu re 3). This study has importance in highlighting th at career development classes present children with triggers for learning how study at school could connect to their futures and imagining more concrete images of these futures.
