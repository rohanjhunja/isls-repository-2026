# Configuring Emotions with Critical Data Literacy and Learning about Japanese American Forced Incarceration

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Kennedy co-designed and facilitated lessons on the forced incarceration of Japanese Americanduring World War II, organizing curriculum materials that encouraged affective, embodied, and justice-focused learning. Employing the emotional configurations (Vea, 2020) and data feminism (D'Ignazio & Klein, 2020) frameworks, our analysis illustrates how one student, Mhina, traversed across interdisciplinary practices and intertwin ed emotional dimensions to craft a data story. Incorporating narrative and storytelling approaches, we depict emotional, moral, and political considerations that were embedded in and mediating student investigations and sense-making with and through data.

The new interdisciplinary field of data science education has begun to detail both opportunities and challenges of incorporating data into education settings (Lee et al., 2021; NASEM, 2023; NCER, 2021; Rubin, 2020). This scholarship underscores the necessi ty of acknowledging students’ perspectives, agency, and practices to “read” and “write” their worlds through data (Bhargava et al., 2015; Philip et al., 2013). Furthermore, the concept of critical data literacy has been leveraged to make visible demands fo r both technical fluency and contextual knowledge of the personal, social, cultural, and political dimensions that data processes are rooted in and contributing to (Lee et al., 2021; Louie, 2022). Thus, this study is situated in the context of an 8th grade Humanities class where a teacher (who is the study’s co-author) and her students learned and expanded each other’s experiences with data during discussions and activities focusing on the wartime incarceration of Japanese Americans. We present a case study  to detail the relationship of emotional configurations (Pierson et al., 2023; Vea, 2020) and data feminism practices (D'Ignazio & Klein, 2020). This line of inquiry, intertwined with storytelling approach, allowed us to begin depicting emotional, moral, a nd political considerations that were embedded in and mediating students’ investigations and sense-making with and through data. This study explores how do emotional configurations affect critical data literacy learning?

## Literature review and conceptual framing

Our approach to studying critical data literacy and practices is situated in an interest to center learners’ emotions and embodiment in learning spaces. While emotions and embodiments are inextricable to learning and development, educational systems often restrict and sterilize the exploration of these expressions (see Boler, 1999). Positioning emotions as individualistic, irrelevant, and disruptive to the pursuit of scientific investigation, mainstream curricula and pedagogies often disregard learner bodie s, subjectivities, and embodiments to perpetuate an overarching perspective that insists learning is apolitical, acultural, and ahistorical (CalabreseBarton & Tan, 2020; dos Santos, 2009). Entangled in this culture, educational research that draws on soci oemotional aspects of learning tends to discuss emotions from vantage points that emphasize the recognition, regulation, and management of emotions as necessary skills aiding educators’ decision-making and relationship building (see Durlak et al., 2011; Osher et al., 2016). Within this context, researchers remain both hesitant about and ill-equipped in fully capturing the diverse characteristics of emotions, as well as their nuanced impacts on classroom learning and the world beyond school (see Boldt et al., 2015; Hochschild, 2013). A growing but significant body of literature vouches for better understandings of emotions in STEMrich contexts (e.g., Takeuchi and Dadkhahfard, 2019; Vossoughi et al., 2020). This work has detected and illuminated emotions in learning by attending to the interplay between emotion, engagement, and cognition and recognizing its intricate and complex nature (see Evans et al., 2018; Lanouette, 2022; Rowe et al., 2023). Pierson et al. (2023) illustrated how a teacher intentionally fostered a scientific learnin g environment welcoming and respectful of emotions. Drawing inspiration from the concept of “braiding” (1) in Braiding Sweetgrass (Kimmerer, 2013), Pierson and colleagues contextualized the organic coordination between emotion and learning—referred to as emotional configurations (see Vea, 2020)—that took place across  individual, intra-personal, and whole-class levels. The authors called for theories examining the presence and dynamics of emotions in learning, arguing for their potentials to architect new, alternative modes of investigation. 350 This study leverages data feminism (D’Ignazio & Klein, 2020) and emotional configurations (Vea, 2020), both rooted in decolonizing education research and feminist theories. D’Ignazio and Klein (2020)’s data feminism framework envisions opportunities for students to engage with seven key aspects of critical data practices. The first two elements delve into examining and challenging power when working with data. This includes investigating how power dynamics shape data systems; employing data to discern power  imbalances, inequity, and injustice; as well as drawing insights from data to engender changes. The next two elements — elevating emotion and lived experience and considering context —highlight the importance of affective connections, personal relevance, and  contextual knowledge in using data accurately and ethically (p. 149). The final three elements —rethinking binaries and hierarchies, making visible labor, and embracing pluralism — address the epistemic dimensions of data practice. They encourage learners to identify bias and social constructs inherent in data, reflect on the labor that goes into the production of data and recognize that certain data practices marginalize and erase labor, as well as engage with diverse perspectives. Thus, D’Ignazio and Klein deconstructed interconnected elements consequential to the power structures shaping and are shaped by data practices. Vea’s (2020) emotional configurations framework guides our examination of both the mediating role of emotions in learning and the interplay between emotion, sensemaking, and practice within students’ engagement with data. First, exploring learning of emotion in and as sociocultural practice, Vea identified the limitation of perceiving emotion merely as a mediator of learning, advocating instead for a reconceptualization of emotion as both an integral component and a central focus of learning practices. The author stated, “like ‘knowledge,’ emotion involves not merely pre-given internal states, but rather in-progress configurations that include meaning making and embodied practice in the social world.” (p. 315) Vea  proposed the concept of emotional configurations, defining it as the co-constitution, co-emergence, and interrelation of emotion, sensemaking, and practice within a specific social context to inform or alter learning trajectories. Second, Vea delved into the politics of emotion and learning by explaining how norms, ideologies, and power control who is allowed to express emotion as well as rules and consequences governing emotional engagement. Finally, Vea connected to the literature on social movements (Goodwin et al., 2001; Jasper, 1998; Snow & Soule, 2010 [all cited in Vea, 2020]) and scale-making for equity (Jurow & Shea, 2015), illustrating political possibilities and new forms of collective action that arise from better understanding and embracing emotional configurations. Next, we turn to briefly describe the historical context of the Japanese American forced incarceration and review our project’s curriculum. This foregrounds both historical and curricular context through which our students engaged with interdisciplinary da ta learning, and within which we investigate notions of emotional configurations and critical data literacy.

The history of Japanese American forced incarceration February 1942, two months after the attack on Pearl Harbor, U.S. President Franklin Delano Roosevelt issued Executive Order 9066 authorizing the relocation of approximately 126,000 individuals of Japanese ancestry into detention camps, deeming them as a threat to national security (National Archives-a, n.d). Within days, the entire Japanese descendant population on the West Coast were forced to leave behind their homes, properties, and belongings to find themselves living in relocation centers (also known as “internment camps") that were isolated, makeshift, and overcrowded until the end of World War II. These included not only people born in Japan and later immigrated to the U.S. (issei) but also two-thirds who was U.S. born (nisei). In 1943, the War Depart ment and the War Relocation Authority (WRA) conducted a loyalty assessment to determine the eligibility of Japanese American adults for military draft and the release of those deemed “loyal” to non-restricted states (Densho Encyclopedia, n.d.a). They required all prisoners aged above 17 to complete the “Application for Leave Clearance” form, commonly known as the loyalty questionnaire. Questions 27 and 28 of this form asked prisoners to renounce any allegiance to the Japanese emperor, swear allegiance to the U.S., and assert willingness to serve in the U.S. military. Those who responded “No” were labeled as disloyal and separated into the Tule Lake, California relocation center (see National Archives-a, n.d.; Densho Encyclopedia, n.d.b). These incidents presented injustices sanction by the U.S. government and marked following decades of uncertainty and hardship for Japanese Americans, who faced ongoing anti-Japanese violence, property and financial losses, as well as physical and mental anguish (Hosokawa, 2005; Takei et al., 2020; Wakatsuki Houston & Houston, 1973). Nevertheless, this history has been absent in most K-12 curriculum (An, 2022; Goodwin, 2010).

## Study design

This study is situated in a larger research practice partnership project (DataPBL) spanning AY2022-23 and AY2023-24. The co-design team consisted of curriculum developers, data education specialists, learning sciences researchers, and educators affiliated with  Concord Consortium, EL Education, and CU Boulder, and two public schools: Marbles Expeditionary and Oak Park Expeditionary (2). We were tasked with developing an addendum 351 focusing on data practices for an existing curriculum on Japanese American internment  (JAI) (3): The first unit covers English Language Art (ELA) and Social Studies materials and background knowledge; the second unit focuses on argumentative  writing connected to themes in  the Farewell to Manzanar  memoir; and the final unit prompts students to apply their learning to current day implications. The DataPBL  addendum was aimed to support student in integrating data inquiry into their projects. This study focuses on Marbles Expeditionary, a public charter school serving preK-8 students in an urban community in the upper Midwest. Its student body is predominantly Black or African American (70%) and from low to middle income backgrounds, with 88% of students qualifying for free or reduced-price lunch (4). Kennedy taught Humanities, which merged Social Studies and ELA in a block schedule. In her classroom, students read Farewell to Manzanar (Wakatsuki Houston & Houston, 1973)—a memoir in which Jeanne Wakatsuki Houston recounts her family’s forced relocation from their home in California’s Ocean Park to the Manzanar incarceration camp in the central California desert, along with experiences during and following their release. Students engaged in activities, such as gallery walks and multimedia analysis of pictures from Manzanar’s yearbook, newspapers clippings, and videos about survivors’ stories. They also had a conversation with a Japanese American guest speaker who survived incarceration.

For the data-infused component, students explored what data is, how it has been used in the world, and how it applies to their everyday lives. Additionally, they were introduced to the Common Online Data Analysis Platform (CODAP; Concord Consortium, 2025).  Under the “data story writing” assignment that overarches the module, they were asked to identify a research question tying to the memoir and examine a dataset that our team had randomized and extracted from the WRA records. Then students developed graphs  to explore and contextualize claims on prisoners’ health, education, and other social status determinants to address such questions. This inquiry process drove them to both critically examine systemic injustices imposed on Japanese Americans and draw parallels to present-day social justice issues.

## Data analysis

We employed ethnographically-informed multiple case study methods. The research question that motivates this article is: How do emotional configurations affect critical data literacy learning?  Data collection included classroom observations and observation notetaking, video and audio recording, learning artifacts, as well as interviews with Kennedy and 16 focal students across the two-year period (5). Data was analyzed to retrospectively identify and contextualize (see Geertz, 1973) the presence and dynamic of emotional configurations (Pierson et al., 2023; Vea, 2020) during students’ engagement with Farewell to Manzanar and the JAI datasets, which spanned five weeks in the Spring semesters. For data analysis, Tran  first reviewed fieldnotes and analytic memos, making annotations on segments relevant to and important for (see LeCompte et al., 1993, p. 236) studying emotional configurations and critical data literacy. Tran and Kennedy met to evaluate the data collected, reflecting on  how they characterized student engagement with data and  contributed to our interpretation of the two conceptual frameworks. This process informed our selection of focal students and guided our construction of three c ase studies. The process of building case studies involved 1) arranging the data associated with three selected cases into three separate packages, 2) coding student interview transcripts  to observe  patterns of significance that informed further analysis and interpretation of emotions, emotional configurations, data literacy practices (see Merriam, 1998; Patton, 1990), 3) triangulating evidence  (e.g., classroom recording logs, teacher materials, and learning artifacts) to corroborate our claims, and 4) writing. The coding scheme encompassed emotion, social and political relations between data learning and power, data literacy practices, and possibilities for collective actions and becoming. Of note, similar to  Pierson and colleagues’ approach to analyzing emotional  configurations, our goal was not to catalog every instance of salient emotions arising from students’ interactions across the three units. Some emotions can be hard to observe, depict, and  explain than others, and contextualizing emotional configurations certainly risks incompleteness.  We recognized this challenging aspect while continuing to assemble details appearing  consequential to the co-emergence and interplay of emotion, sensemaking, and practice. With  this, we sought both analytical tools —such as analysis of classroom dialogues and artifacts to examine different utterances and forms of emotional embodiment and expression—and writing  approaches that allowed a creative, affective, and cohesive engagement with these data (i.e. Flint & Perry, 2024; Toliver, 2021). Taking up a short story writing approach, our case studies blended depictions of how students generally presented themselves in the learning environment with narratives highlighting moments and factors influencing their emotionally laden experiences. During writing, we paid effort to interlace Farewell to Manzanar’s excerpts as well as syntheses and artifacts from the Japanese American incarceration with a hope to call more attention to this largely overlooked injustice. After crafting the case studies, we reviewed and distinguished similarities and 352 variations—a process once again surfacing compelling patterns that clarify our understanding of emotional configurations across cases (Huberman & Miles, 1994). Here, we share one of the three case stories that we wrote.

## Case study-Examining the loyalty questionnaires, people who answered “no

no,” and relocation after incarceration Events in this case study took place in DataPBL first implementation year. For several weeks, the class read Farewell to Manzanar. The memoir depicted tumultuous experiences starting with Jeanne’s father being beaten and imprisoned by the FBI; being accused of disloyalty and espionage leading him to alcohol abuse and domestic violence. It also portrayed the chaotic and humiliating conditions the family faced during three and a half years of confinement and the lasting trauma from public hatred and prejudice that continued to haunt Japanese Americans long after their release. Through read-aloud sessions and discussions in ELA classes, students shared their own sensemaking of text, developing literacy skills. In Social Studies portion of the curriculum, they learned about the social and political contexts of the Japanese American forced incarceration, connecting it to systemic, ongoing discrimination against Asian Americans. This case study explores the emotions of one student, Mhina, across multiple weeks of reading the book, engaging with the JAI dataset, and creating a data story. Mhina is a Black, female student who loves history, science, math, slam poetry, art, and running track.  For DataPBL assignment, Mhina set out to write a data story on a topic that she connected with emotionally from reading the book but found no data to support that investigation. We narrate Mhina’s in-the-moment emotional engagement— encompassing what she sees and feels —and over-time reflection on its relationship with d ata inquiry to contextualize her emotional configuration throughout this process. The classroom Mhina stepped into Kennedy’s classroom. This was not only where she and her peers spent time every school day for Humanities, but it was also her home room. Kennedy’s room exuded an inviting atmosphere. It was full of colors. Even before stepping into the room, one would be captivated by the wall outside, adorned with decorations, featuring photos and images under bold yellow text cutouts on a black background: “Who lives? Who dies? Who tells our stories?” Recently, the class went to watch the Hamilton live  performance, and, they were inspired to borrow this title of Lin-Manuel Miranda’s song to anchor their conversations during this semester. Inside the room, there was an entire wall that was chock-full with books. Students could satisfy their curiosity about the history and literature of the U.S. by choosing from an eclectic mix of literary classics, like To Kill a Mockingbird, Anne Frank: The Diary of a Young Girl, All That She Carried, and contemporary titles for young adults, like Summer of the Mariposas, Hidden Figures, Stamped, The Hate You Give, and The Fault in Our Stars. All the walls were covered with displays of student work, arts, maps, national flags, old and new posters, and group projects. Tucked away at the back of the room was the peace corne r that Kennedy  put together for students to come in and sit down whenever class felt too overwhelming. “Today, we will be looking into the Japanese American incarceration dataset, and we will craft our data story on CODAP. We are going to tell a story about the experience of Japanese Americans before, during, and after the incarceration.” “Japanese American incarceration,” Mhina whispered.

At the beginning of the semester, Mhina did not know what that term meant. During the gallery walk, she and her friends had so many questions.

In our case study, we leveraged narrative and storytelling to access these dimensions in the situated context of data learning. We now turn to further discuss how our findings characterize emotional configurations, synthesizing how emotions emerged, were exchanged, and woven into data practices. Following events that signaled what Mhina saw, felt, and experienced across DataPBL sections, we trace how emotional configurations were shaped and manifested. The case study illuminated that Mhina’s investigation was rooted in her emotional reactions—disbelief, shock, and anger at the Loyalty questionnaire, along with worry and concern about prisoners who refused to comply. We also documented Mhina’s sense of puzzlement when she noticed gaps in the dataset, ones that disrupted her investigation and led her  to shift focus to a topic better supported by the data. We narrated how Mhina thoughtfully shifted her inquiry in a new direction after getting clarity on a path to move pass the unavailability of relevant data. Finally, we uncovered the moment she revisited ideas from her original investigation, speculating “I noticed Not relocated may have been people who said No No in the survey.” Her wondering whether the data labeled as “Not relocated ” might represent individuals who answered No No on the survey signaled a moment of skeptics, as well as emergent desire to decode what the data was not yet telling her.  T hese instances exemplified how emotions accompanied and guided inquiries, even influencing how Mhina created and explored her data questions. We recognize that emotional events—identified and narrated here by researchers—are situated in and interacted with other emotionally-laden anchors that may or may not readily highlighted in our case study. Yet, we hope our careful delineation of a circumsta nce that 356 highlighted how emotional configurations are both sustained and interrupted by various trajectories of emotion, sensemaking, and practice may enrich understandings of emotions and emotionally laden dynamics in learning. We noted salient moments in Mhina’s experiences that  offers clarifying example for  conceptualizing emotion as shaped by norms, ideologies, and power  (Vea, 2020, p. 313). The literature explains the politics of emotion as the intertwining of emotions with power and power relations (Ahmed, 2002; Goodwin et al., 2001; Hochschild, 1979). This case study in part revealed the groundwork that  Kennedy laid prior to and alongside introducing students to the datasets. For example, it suggested that Mhina did not learn data in isolation; instead, she interacted with the data by drawing on elements i n Farewell to Manzanar, along with multimodal artifacts presented during gallery walks, and insights from classroom discussions. This process resembled the notion of guided participation (see Vea, 2020, building on Rogoff, 1995), where Kennedy ’s scaffolding helped students develop familiarity with both the context and emotional resources related to the wartime incarceration of Japanese Americans. In this vein,  Mhina identifying, “ I would have said  No No ” and “I don’t trust you know more” emboldened an emotional state and expression of “broken trust”—one unfolded as part of the guided participation process. Here, trust is linked to the tension between the U.S. ideal of “all people are created equal” and examples where the government breaks that trust: imprisoning all Japanese American people merely because of racist and xenophobic wartime hysteria following Pearl Harbor.  Put it another way, by prompting students to reflect on and discuss the history of power abuse and its moral implications, activities and interactions in Kennedy’s classroom facilitated “normative pressure, for others to participate in a particular way of feeling … by eliciting affects, which may be ambiguous in their meaning, and shaping them into emotional configurations that have strategic value” (Vea, 2020, p. 332).

We described how such an abundance of feelings and emotions influenced a focal student’s interpretation of the data and its significance. This case study also helped us depict how emotions lingered, finding themselves in other and newer forms of discourse, and thus  shedding light on a transformative shift. In the case of Mhina’s data exploration, this shift was evident in her final writing to express remembrance of prisoners who answered No No. We argue that her eventual return to the original inquiry also signaled a critical engagement with the data in front of her and an agentic move to re-emphasize the matter she had previously identified, rather than simply moving on. Our study presents meaningful contexts where emotion was an integral component of learning and a key resource that informed data investigation. Furthermore, we underscore how the student not only grappled authentically with and articulated her perspective, but also confidently claimed their own emotional responses, altogether conditioned new layers of relations and possibilities for knowledge construction (Boldt  et al., 2015).
