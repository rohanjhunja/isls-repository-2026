# Trend Trackers: Building Data Literacy Through Problem-Based Learning

**Conference:** ISLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In this demo, we discuss the co-design of an interactive, problem-based science supplement called Trend Trackers. Trend Trackers aims to engage middle school students with socioscientific issues that impact cities and communities all over the world. Students take up the role of a data scientist to address public health problems within the fictional city of Springfield. In this demo, we will explore a challenge in which students are asked to design a vaccination plan to reduce the rates of flu in Springfield. Students engage in interactive and immersive activities to collect wastewater samples, measure rates of flu in the community, and generate evidence through a series of wrangling, visualization, and analysis activities. Students then share their findings and collaborate with their team to co-construct a vaccination plan for Springfield.

In K-12 education, students are introduced to data science practices such as wrangling, visualizing, and analyzing data by exploring data about themselves or their local communities. Students build on prior knowledge and personal experiences to ask questions about the context in which data were produced and to reason about the social and environmental implications of data (Lee et al. 2021; Wilkerson and Laina 2018). However, data scientists often work with data that are collected from unfamiliar, inaccessible, or dangerous sources (Wilkerson and Polman 2020; Wise 2020). Exploring these types of data sources offers students opportunities to build an understanding of data as situated in complex social and environmental contexts and produced through a series of decisions. However, students need support to think critically about the unfamiliar context in which these data are collected. Building on prior research that highlights modeling (Wolff et al. 2019), filtering, and embodiment as promising strategies to engage students in reasoning about secondary data sets, we co-designed an interactive, immersive science supplement. Trend Trackers is an interdisciplinary tool that aims to engage middle school students in a problem-based learning experience, collecting and analyzing data about socioscientific issues through a series of gamified, immersive, and collaborative activities. In this presentation, we describe and discuss early findings from the co-design of one 45-minute module within Trend Trackers in which students are challenged to co-construct a vaccination plan to prevent a flu outbreak in the fictional city of Springfield. Students take up the role of data scientists, working in teams and collaborating with scientific experts, to measure rates of flu across Springfield using wastewater. During the co-design process, we conducted a total of 12 co-design meetings, seven with a high-school math teacher and a middle-school science teacher and five with an expert virologist. We also conducted cognitive interviews with six individual students in grades 7-10 and with two small groups, before pilot testing Trend Trackers with two different schools. We asked: (1) How do youth draw on their virtual experiences sampling and analyzing wastewater to understand data as produced through a process of scientific inquiry? and (2) What data practices do students engage in as they construct and justify evidence for their vaccination plans? This work contributes to literature in data science education by offering a tool that supports students in building data literacies in the context of scientific research by drawing on a problem-based learning model. In K-12 education, students are introduced to different data science practices in math and science. In science courses, for example, students collect and visualize data through lab activities. In math, students focus on data English language arts and history courses might construct data-based arguments. These practices are data literacies that work together to help students develop “the ability to ask and answer real-world questions from large and small data sets through an inquiry process, with consideration of the ethical use of data” (p. 23, Wolff et al. 2016). Data scientists, however, address real world problems by drawing on these interdisciplinary practices as problemsolving tools, collecting, analyzing, and using data to inform decisions in complex social contexts. To support s tudents in taking up the role of data scientists to address community-level socioscientific issues, we designed a problem-based learning experience, building on three design principles to help students consider the context in which complex, unfamiliar forms of data are produced and used. The principle of personal data collection suggests that personally collecting data prompts students to think critically about what is measured in a data set, the context in which the data were produced, and the potential implications or applications of data-based findings (Wolff et al. 2019; Lee et al. 2022). However, when students work with secondary data sets that are unfamiliar, Wolff et al. (2019) suggests integrating simulations or modeling activities to engage students with a data collection experience they candraw on to make sense of data. Interactive and immersive technologies, which build on principles of embodiment (Dunleavy et al. 2009), have potential to support students in generating lived experiences collecting and measuring data. In Trend Trackers, students are challenged to analyze rates of flu across the fictional city of Springfield by measuring the amount of flu RNA in wastewater samples. We propose that gamifying the collection of wastewater samples and engaging students in a virtual lab to measure the amount of flu RNA in samples can help students make sense of this novel data source. The expansion principle suggests that students build confidence in reasoning about a complex dataset by first looking at a snapshot of the full dataset and then expanding their inquiry to the full data set (Wolff et al., 2019). To support this iterative analysis of data, Trend Trackers engages students in analyzing data in a threestage process. To begin, student teams engage in a data collection game, in which each team member has enough time to individually collect about 3 or 4 samples from a region of Springfield. Students first independently wrangle and visualize their sample of data. Then, students have an opportunity to engage in analysis and modeling activities using data collected by the whole group. Finally, student teams share their evidence and collaboratively construct a vaccination plan to minimize the spread of flu within Springfield. The inquiry principle suggests that students develop data literacies by engaging in a process of asking questions, collecting data, analyzing data, and interpreting results. However, in working with unfamiliar data, Wolff et al. (2019) suggest that students engage in guided inquiry to familiarize themselves with the data and then engage in a process of student-driven inquiry. Building on a problem-based learning framework (Hmelo‐Silver 2004), Trend Trackers scaffolds student inquiry in the following ways. The first module includes inquiry-based activities that introduce students to the main problem facing Springfield, their role as a data scientist, and the different data sources they will use throughout the four following modules. Within each of the following four modules, students then have opportunities to investigate their own statistical question or choose from a selection of questions. Finally, integrating independent and collaborative activities offers students opportunities to reflect on and compare their process of inquiry with their peers. In this demo, we discuss findings from an iterative co-design process in which we aimed to support students in understanding wastewater as an innovative source for measuring and monitoring flu rates in a community; build confidence engaging in data science practices; and develop an understanding of data as situated in personal, cultural, and sociopolitical contexts.

Trend Trackers In Trend Trackers, students encounter interdisciplinary challenges as they work to generate evidence-based solutions to socioscientific problems by engaging in a process of scientific inquiry. To design a vaccination plan for Springfield, students measure flu RNA by collecting and processing wastewater samples from districts across Springfield, generate evidence by visualizing and analyzing data, and co-construct a vaccination plan grounded in their team’s evidence. Students are also encouraged to consider the role of collaboration in producing and using data throughout the process, first with their peers, then with scientific experts, and finally with community leaders. Next, we discuss how three design principles informed initial activities in Trend Trackers and how early pilot testing with students has informed iterations on design features. Collecting and processing wastewater samples Building on the principles of personal data collection and the expansion principle, we designed a data collection experience in which students first play a game to collect wastewater samples (see Figure 1) and then process their samples in a virtual lab (see Figure 2). In the initial design, students were given a limited amount of time to collect wastewater samples from as many districts as possible by driving a car around a map of Springfield. The goal was to simulate the real world challenges that scientists face in collecting data and to reinforce the role of collaboration. After collecting samples of wastewater, students enter a virtual lab in which they have an opportunity to get handson experiences with the scientific tools used to produce a data set measuring rates of flu from wastewater. To produce their data set, students pasteurize their samples, isolate flu RNA, and measure the amount of RNA. Preliminary findings based on small group playtesting and early pilot tests in the classroom showed that students enjoyed taking up the role of a scientist, learning about “what real scientists do in a lab,” and “how water s amples are tested.” However, we also found that students needed more support to collaborate in the data collection game. Building on student recommendations, we added a planning stage and an evaluation stage. Prior to the game, student teams now orient themselves to the map of Springfield and the constraints of the game, including where each team member is located in relation to wastewater plants. They are prompted to work together to collect data from different regions of Springfield and enter the game only when the whole team is ready. After students collect their samples, they are shown a visual summary of their team’s data, which highlights districts they personally collected as well as districts collected by the rest of their team. Visualizing and analyzing data Data exploration in the initial design of Trend Trackers aimed to mirror the iterative practices of data scientists, offering students multiple entry points into data inquiry. Data scientists might alternate between wrangling, visualizing, and analyzing as they make observations and ask questions about their data. In Trend Trackers, students can choose from a selection of wrangling, visualization, and analysis activities in order to generate evidence. In Wrangle Data, students work to improve the quality of their data, addressing issues such as formatting errors and suspicious values. In Visualize Data, students construct different types of graphs to answer their investigative questions. In Analyze Data, students perform unit transformations, learn about measures of center, and use models to make predictions. In each data science practice, Trend Trackers’ activities highlight different data moves (Erickson et al. 2019). For example, in Wrangle Data students are encouraged to sort data; in Visualize Data students are prompted to group their data, and in Analyze Data students engage in calculating and transforming data.

Based on findings from early playtests and interviews with three middle school teachers, we found that students needed more support to wrangle their data. One teacher reflected that “kids trust numbers too much, and they skip that step, and they shouldn't be able to skip that step,” because it impacts the accuracy of students’ visualizations and analysis. We also found that while students constructed multiple pieces of evidence (see Figure 3), they needed more support to ask and answer different kinds of statistical questions within a socioscientific context.

In the initial design, we proposed that the features supporting the expansion principle in data collection might offer students opportunities to share new information about Springfield and co-construct claims as a team. We proposed that opportunities for dialogue and consensus-making would help students select appropriate data to use as evidence, provide sufficient evidence, and document their reasoning (Berland and McNeill 2010). ## Interactive demo and discussion

In our demonstration, we will introduce Trend Trackers, presenting the design conjectures that have informed our work and initial findings from co-design and play-testing with youth and teachers. We will then engage the audience in a guided exploration of three activities in Trend Trackers: the data collection game, the virtual lab, and a data analysis activity. Discussion and feedback will be organized around the following questions: (1) In what ways does Trend Trackers offer youth opportunities to consider the context in which data are produced and used? and (2) In what ways might Trend Trackers support youth in understanding the role of a data scientist?
