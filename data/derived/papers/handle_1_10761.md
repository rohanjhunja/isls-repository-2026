# ISEA: Instructor-in-the-loop Student Engagement Analytics
via FAccT AI

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
AI has become increasingly popular in analyzing student engagement in online environments over the last decade; however, it is still not widely adopted in practice by instructors, partly due to AI’s black box problem. Engagement analytics may fail to analyze the complexity of the engagement construct in theory, and instructors may lack trust and control in techniques. To tackle these issues, we designed and developed the ISEA toolkit. We then conducted interviews with six instructors to understand their concerns with applying AI in engagement analytics and their experience in using ISEA. Participants showed diverse ways to analyze student engagement via ISEA and expressed how it improved their trust in engagement analytics. The results showcased an effective way to integrate instructors’ opinions, learning theories, and techniques together toward acceptance of AI when analytics are more transparent.

## Introduction

Understanding student engagement in large online cou rses that lack face-to-face communication is challenging for instructors (An et al., 2021). Engagement learning analytics (LA)  contributes to improving instructors’ understanding of students’ engagement (Caspari-Sadeghi, 2022). However, the adoption of LA techniques such as AI in real-world settings is still not widely accepted (Swamy et al., 2023). This slow uptake could be explained by several challenges regarding 1) the understanding among stakeholders of AI techniques, 2) data interoperability and 3) ethical issues, including privacy concerns, security and transparency (Celik et al., 2022). Also, engagement is a complex construct in theory, and researchers increasingly acknowledge its multifaceted nature, encompassing behavioral, cognitive, emotional, and social dimens ions (Fredricks & McColskey, 2012). Its meaning varies in educational contexts, while most LA does not allow instructors to personalize the analysis (Roberts et al., 2017). Studies on fairness, accountability, and transparency (FaccT) in socio-technical systems have great potential to address these challenges. Fairness means that one should be able to understand the reasoning behind a system’s decision; transparency means inputs to the system and the system’s use itself must be know n (Speith, 2022). There are several different strategies to ex plain the AI model, such as simplification of the explanation, explanation by  feature relevance, visual  explanation, and local explanation (Arrieta et al., 2020). This stud y mainly used an interactive visual explanation strategy. In this study, we designed and  developed the engagement LA toolkit ISEA which was enhanced by FaccT AI techniques.  ISEA involves instructor-in-the-loop of engagement clustering algorithm design, interactively visualizes student engagement by clus ters and individuals, and enables  instructors to apply fair AI algorithm to adjust analytic results. Then we let s ix instructors use ISEA in a hypothetical scenario of a large online course. We conducted interviews to ask their concerns on adopting engagement analytics enhanced by AI, observed how they use ISEA and asked about their experience after using it. We aimed to fill the gaps that the LA research comm unity lacks empirical studies  that explore: 1) instructors’ attitudes on using AI for LA regarding its fairness and transparency issues, 2) how instructors conduct engagement LA via FaccT AI,  and how they feel during and after the use. Our study focus ed on the following two research questions (RQs): (1) RQ1: What are the instructors’ attitudes toward cur rent engagement LA using AI regarding its transparency and fairness?  (2) RQ2: Does ISEA help instructors perceive transparency and fairness in engagement LA, and if so, how does it achieve this?

## Methods

To create a system that includes instructors in eng agement LA and achieve transparency and fairness, t he toolkit design and development teams went through three rou nds of design, from a low-fidelity sketch to a high-fidelity prototype and finally to a final prototype with sample data. The team was composed of one member, who was the main designer and developer, one was an expert in e ngagement and learning theory, one was an online co urse curriculum expert, and one was an algorithm expert. Sample data for this tool was extracted from a typical online learning community in environmental education from a study the research team previously completed (Zhu et al., 2024a). The tool was developed using R Shiny, an R package to build interactive web apps from R; a multi-fair capacitated (MFC) algorithm (Quy et al., 2022) was used to adjust the cluster for equally distributing demographic factors to achieve fairness.

The layout of ISEA is presented in Figure 1 (a). There are two components to ISEA: a clustering component and a plotting componen t. Each component was divided into three parts: input area, table area, and graph area. Inputs and  information tables are on the left side, while grap hs are on the right. The clustering component involves instructors in the clustering al gorithm design to group students  based on their different patterns of engagement and visualizes the group engagement patterns in general. The instructors can select which variables to be included in the  algorithm; then all students would be visualized as  points in the right-side graph, and their relevant locations would be based on the simi larities between the variable values. The tool will suggest the number of groups the instructors may choose based on the data structure. While the instructors do not need to follow the suggestions and can choose  a different number of groups. Instructors can also visualize demographic variables in the graph, and the tool will calculate fairness metrics based on distribution similarities between each group to suggest if the current engagement algorithm is fair. The instructors may click the “adjust cluster” button, which will use the MFC  algorithm to adjust the grouping a little bit to ma ke the selected variable distributed  in each group similar. The result of the adjustment will also be shown in the right-side graph. The plotting component visualizes an individual’s behavioral change over t ime to demonstrate the details. When users click any point  in the clustering graph, the responding student’s behaviors will be visualized in the plotting graph. At the same time, this student’s data will be listed in the table in this component. Users can also specify which variables they would like to visualize and what groups they would like to compare. In addition, the plotting component will give users a general description of how  a student behaved compared to other students on ave rage to help instructors better understand this student’s behaviors.

Six online course instructors (two males and four females; the average age is 36.7) participated in this study. They all had master's or PhD degrees in sustainability and varying online teachi ng experiences on edX Edge (average years of experience is 3.3).  All instructors consented to participate in the Cornell IRB-approved study.

The interview was to understand instructors’ attitu des toward AI in engagement LA  regarding fairness and transparency and how their use and experience of ISEA. It was conducted through ZOOM meetings. The duration of the interviews ranged from  45 to 60 minutes. Figure 1(b) describes the whole study process. At the beginning, we briefly introduced the study and asked participants about their teaching experience. Based on their answers, we introduced the interviewees to a hypothetical scenario: “You are teaching a similar course for a large group (100+) of students from over 20 countries on edX Edge ……” Next, we asked interviewees about their strategies and challenges in  understanding student engagement in this scenario. We also asked their attitudes toward the features that edX Edge provided that cou ld label “active learners” and “inactive learners” reg arding whether and how it might help them to understand student engagement. Furthermore, we asked whether they had consideration for demographic factors in engagement. After the semi-structured interview, we presented the interviewees with a brief tutorial of ISEA and answered any questions they had about this tool. Then, we reviewed the scenario we introduced at the beginning and ask ed interviewees to use ISEA to design a student eng agement labeling algorithm by determining engagement variables, number of groups, and demographic adjustment choices. We let the interviewees remote control the screen a nd answered questions when they forgot the usage of  any feature of the toolkit. Before they started, we ask ed interviewees to “ think aloud” when they used ISEA (Van Someren et al., 1994). After they completed the task, we asked about their experience of using this tool regarding perceived values and trust and suggested improvements.

Data includes interview transcripts and the user te sting process videos. All interview  sessions were transcribed before the qualitative data analysis. Atlas.Ti, a qualitative  data analysis software, was used to code and synthesize data based on our research questions. Following McDonald et al. (2019)’s metho ds and given the direct alignment between the study protocol and the analysis themes, statements of interest were jointly coded by two researchers according to the pre-set themes of the study protocol: (a) instructors’ needs for understanding student engagement; (b) instructors’ attitudes towa rds engagement labeling analytics on edX; (c) instructo rs’ opinions on ISEA. The resulting coded statements were examined by the authors using the constant comparative method (Zhu et al., 2024 b). Besides analyzing the interview scripts, we also an alyzed the interviewees’ user testing videos by applying user journey mapping (Endmann & Keßner, 2016). User journey mapping (UJM), or customer journey mapping, is used to understand users, behaviors, experiences, and attitudes towards a service or application via visualizing all possible points of contact (touch points) between a customer and any s ervices. ## Results

By analyzing the interview scripts, we summarized i nterviewees’ opinions on student engagement and att itudes toward engagement analytics. First, all interviewees stated that understanding student engagement in large online courses is critical but challenging. Its importance affects both students and instructors. However, all interviewees also agreed that it is challenging to evaluate student e ngagement, especially in large online  classrooms. On the one hand, it is challenging to know every student’s  situation “with one glance.” On the other hand, engagement itself is hard  to define and can be measured from so many different perspectives, and some are not consistent. Second, automatically labeling active and inactive students on edX Edge using  current engagement learning analytics is generally not helpful, mainly because of its opacity. Even though the edX Edge insights feature gives instructors immediate information on who’s active and who’s not active, this information does not provide a clear understanding of student engagement as instructors do not know  1) what engagement means on edX insights. Third, fairness in engagement analytics is not a consideration for most interviewees. Four interviewees expressed that they would not think demographic factors correlated with engagement would be problematic, but they would like to know the reasons behind engagement/non-engagement, so they candevelop some teaching strat egies accordingly. Mentioned reasons included educational backgrounds, language skills, and learning interests. By analyzing user testing videos, we summarized how  interviewees interact with ISEA for LA engagement and their experience using the tool. The user testing process  demonstrated that the ISEA enabled participants to complete the engagement clustering algorithm design and have more trust in engagement analytics. Five interviewees were able to complete the design within 15 minutes.  All interviewees tried different variables and group combinations for algorithm design, and one even tried six different combinations for “exploring student patterns” and made two different algorithms as final decisions: one for “identifying class active students” and the

## other for “identifying discussion forum active students.”

Interviewees interacted with ISEA mainly in 6 touch points, and they perceived LA transparency  and trust during the interaction. Touch points are the places where users interact with the system and key steps for users to complete tasks (Endmann & Keßner, 2016). The six touch points are presented in Figure 1(c). They can be further combined into three groups: selection (variable selection and group num ber selection), visualization (individual visualization and  general visualization), and adjustment (algorithmic  adjustment and manual adjustment). Selection enables interviewees to “per sonalize the engagement algorithm” and “integrate t he teaching approaches” into it. Visualization helps interviewees  to better understand “the meaning of each group” and “identify how students are (or are not) engaged.” Not all participants followed the bidirectional directions that the figure represents. Via interacting with ISEA, interviewees felt they understood how the AI algo rithm works to group students, had “a sense of control” on the alg orithm, and perceived more trust in LA results. Inside each touch point, interviewees could synthesize considerations from algorithm suggestions and their judgment. ISEA further enhanced their understanding of engagement analytics. For example, when deciding group numbers, the algorithm suggested that a participant has four groups, while she decided to have two groups because “having two levels of engagement is easier for maki ng responding teaching strategies.”  Two participants constantly adjusted the variable combinations until they found two distinctive clusters on the graph and followed the algorithm suggestions of the two groups. It inspired participants to regard engagement analytics as a dynamic process that changes over time and context. ISEA also led two participants to consider fairness when they were designing the engagement clustering algorithm.

## Discussions and conclusions

This study tackled the issue of the rare use and lo w acceptance of AI in engagement learning analytics  by online course instructors in real-world settings. It explored instructors’ attitudes towards LA regarding transparency and fairness and demonstrated the use of ISEA to enhance engagement and understanding. The effectiveness of ISEA may inspire other AI applications in learning analytics to adopt similar strategies to address the acceptance issues by including instructor-in-the-loop design and increasing techniques’ FaccT, especially its transparency. AI transparency is a prominent issue in engagement LA, as mentioned by participants in this study. To trust and apply AI in engagement LA, instructors ne ed to know the exact meaning of engagement labeled by AI and how it is calculated. This is aligned with Laufer et al. (2022) that AI explainability comes from data source's explainability and how inputs lead to outputs. The instructors-in-the-loop strategy enhanced their understanding and acceptance of AI in LA (Lewis et al., 2023). However, AI fairness is mentioned less in this paper. For most interviewees in this study, fairness was not initia lly considered in analytics. Some of them realized the fairness issue in user testing when using algorithmic adjustment features. This may indicate that further exploration of AI fairness in LA would be necessary and meaningful.

This study has several limitations. First, we used a fair clustering algorithm that was one of the transparent or explainable models. Opaque models, such as deep learning models, are far more difficult to explain. Second, the study applied the user study in a hypot hetical scenario. The lack of a real-setting field study may decrease its external validity. Future work can apply ISEA to real settings for evaluating its value in practice.
