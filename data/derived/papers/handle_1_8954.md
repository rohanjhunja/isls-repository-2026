# An Exploratory Study on Students’ Digital Curation Competency and Experience in a General Education Course

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

An Exploratory Study on Students’ Digital Curation Competency and Experience in a General Education Course Jeremy Tzi Dong Ng, Yining Sun, Xiao Hu jntd@connect.hku.hk, ynsun@connect.hku.hk, xiaoxhu@hku.hk The University of Hong Kong, Hong Kong S.A.R.

Abstract: Essential in this information landscape, digital c uration is broadly defined as the practices of digitally organizing and re-presenting the cultural record of humankind for creating value and impact, producing visual and textual info rmation for presenting a narrative to audiences. Despite the prevalence of our daily-life curatorial practices and being well-suited to a diversity of disciplines, training in digital cur ation knowledge and skills has not been democratized to be inclusive for students from non-technical backgrounds. This pilot study 1) proposes a maker activity, namely creating digital galleries of cultural heritage, in a general education course, 2) examines to what extent this learning activity helps develop digital curation competency of undergraduate students, and 3) investigates how this digital curation experience benefits their professional and personal pursuits.

## Introduction

In this technological age, information is available  in vast quantity and varying quality (Cherrstrom &  Boden, 2020). In our daily life, we perform the social practice of information curation mostly without awareness, such as compiling relevant information for purchasing masks, organizing personal photo albums, and documenting social events in a community (Cherrstrom & Boden, 2020; Ungerer, 2016). Traditionally, curatorial activities for cultural heritage materials take place in memory institutions (e.g., museums, libraries) (Cherrstrom & Boden, 2020), while nowadays digital curation is broadly defined as the wide-ranging practices of “organizing and re-presenting the cultural record of humankind in order to create val ue, impact, and quality” (Sheffer & Hunker, 2019: p.80). Typically, it results in a series of visual and tex tual information for presenting a particular discipline-dependent narrative to both professional and public audiences (Cherrstrom & Boden, 2020; Sheffer & Hunker, 2019). Due to the commonality of curatorial practices (Che rrstrom & Boden, 2020), digital curation has been increasingly integrated into students’ learning pro cess (e.g., content curation for assignments) (Unge rer, 2016). In the ever-growing information landscape, digital curation has evolved to be a transferrable skill th at prepares university students for the demands of a digitally savvy workforce (Sheffer & Hunker, 2019). Training in digital curation has a strong pragmatic focus on students’ competencies (Kim, 2015): their understanding of users’ needs, ethical use of information, content organization and management, technical skills of using online curation tools, and so on (Cushing & Shankar, 2019; Feng & Richards 2018). This has led to pedagogical practices emphasizing hands-on experience (Cherrstrom & Boden, 2020), where students usually collaborate and work on topic-specific projects with real-world audiences (Sheffer & Hunker, 2019). Despite being well-suited to many disciplines, there is currently a lack of studies in digital curation education for students with diverse backgrounds, especially those with limited technology knowledge or support (Kim, 2015, Sheffer et al., 2019). Towards democratizing digital curation training and  making it more inclusive (Hu et al., 2017), this exploratory study proposes a maker activity namely creating digital galleries of cultural heritage, and examines how it could help develop digital curation competencies of undergraduate students with diverse backgrounds, and how this digital curation experience would benefit them. In the context of an undergraduate general ed ucation course, we pose the following two research questions: RQ1: To what extent candigital gallery creation help develop students’ digital curation competencies? RQ2: What are the benefits of digital curation experience as perceived by students? Findings to the first research question will improve our understanding about the extent to which students candevelop digital curation competency through the  maker activity of creating a digital gallery, wher eas the second research question can offer empirical evidence on the values of democratizing digital curation education.

Our findings will also yield pedagogical implications on how to optimize students’ learning experience.

## Research Context and Methodology

This pilot study was conducted in a university-wide undergraduate general education course in Hong Kong on the topic of digitizing cultural heritage. The course learning outcomes include having students “design user-centered digital products for presenting heritage information” and “apply principles and ethics of information to heritage preservation”. The capstone of this course was a gr oup digital curation project in which each group of  3 to 5 ICLS2022 Proceedings 1517 © ISLS students collaborated to create a digital gallery for a self-chosen cultural heritage. Built towards this assessment, classes comprised both weekly lectures on key topics (e.g., ethics of digital preservation) and tutorials with handson digital curation activities. Omeka.org, an open-source tool commonly used in digital curation educa tion (Sheffer & Hunker, 2019), was used as the platform for creating the digital gallery, owing to its ease  of use, flexibility, and affordances in presenting multimedia materials (Hu et al., 2021). Towards the end of the course, each group submitted the link of their public-facing digital gallery and a report documenting and explaining their work. They were informed that the best-performing galleries would be selected by the teaching team for display at a public showcase of student works. Fig. 1 shows some example galleries. Owing to similar content and pedagogical implement ations, students from the 2018 (n = 97) and 2019 (n = 102) cohorts were grouped into one population. With informed ethical consents which assured students whether they participated in this study would not affect their course grades, a total of 199 students (female = 60%) in 58 groups joined this study voluntarily without remuneration. 36% of them were Science, Technology, Engineering, and Mathematics (STEM) majors, while the rest (64%) were non-STEM (e.g., Arts, Business, Journalism, etc.). This study employs qualitative methods including artefact analysis (RQ1), for the usefulness of artefacts in assessing student competencies (Kim, 2015), and thematic coding (RQ2). To answer RQ1, based on constructs in existing studies on digital curation (e.g., Sheffer et al., 2019), we developed a rudimentary analytic framework with six categories (e.g., user needs assessment) e ach indicating a digital curation competency (under lined in Table 1). Then, a more fine-grained coding framework was established from a bottom-up, data-driven approach, generating individual codes (e.g., awareness of users’ needs). For RQ2, we collected open-ended responses from an optional course-end survey asking about students ’ perceived benefits of their digital curation expe rience. Adopting the grounded theory approach (Strauss & Corbin, 1994), excerpts were extracted and iteratively coded using the coding framework (Table 2). To ensure ano nymity, a research assistant removed the students’ names from the data before analysis. To ensure reliability, 25% of the artefacts (RQ1) (15 groups) and excer pts (RQ2) (n = 40) were randomly selected and independently coded by two researchers knowledgeable in digital curation, attaining excellent levels of agreement with Cohen’s kappa of 0.90 and 0.96 respectively (MuHugh, 2012). ## Preliminary Findings

For RQ1, upon analyzing each group’s digital galler y and the corresponding report, we counted the numb er of groups (total = 58) satisfying each code (Table 1).  First, most of the groups not only identified thei r target user groups (#1.1 in Table 1) (95%), e.g., “elderly residents” (Group 1), “foreign tourists” (Group 58), but also listed these users’ information needs (#1.2, 86%), e.g., “ proper ways to use the [heritage]” (Group 52), “det ailed descriptions of the [heritage]” (Group 47). This shows students’ basic understanding about user-centered design (Nelson et al., 2011). In terms of metadata quality, the majority (91%) followed the standard taught in class (#2.1), which has been widely adopted in the cultural herit age domain (Hu et al., 2021). Most groups completed  their metadata fields (#2.2) (72%), while it was common for the remaining groups to leave some fields empty without justifications. These indicate the need for more time and efforts on teaching metadata as a functional competency in digital curation (Feng & Richards, 2018). Next, while all groups supplied photos or pictures for presenting their heritage items (#3.1) (some examples shown in Fig. 2), only less than half of the groups (40%) presented audiovisuals (#3.2), which were mainly for introducing the heritage’s history (e.g., Group 20) or giving a guided tour (e.g., Group 57). It is thus desirable to encourage students to leverage multimedia materials in the future. As for facilitating users’ information discovery (#4), many groups (69%) showed awareness of how users could leverage the default “basic search” (e.g., Group 25) and “ad vanced search” (e.g., Group 8) functions on Omeka. About two-thirds of them (64%) were also aware of user’ browsing experience (#4.2), but only about half (48%) implemented tags, free short texts annotating heritage items (#5.3), for facilitating browsing. This demonstrates that students had the basic understanding of design ing public-facing products of digital curation from  users’ ICLS2022 Proceedings 1518 © ISLS perspectives but were not sufficiently versatile in  making use of all available functions (Cherrstrom & Boden, 2020). For information ethics (#6), a considerable number of groups (59%) showed awareness of ethical concerns (#6.1), mostly on avoiding copyright infringement, such as using materials with Creative Commons licenses (e.g., Group 8), seeking usage permission from the owner of resources (e.g., Group 22), and creating original materials (e.g., Group 35). Nonetheless, only one-thirds (31%) included copyrights statements in their digital g alleries (#6.2). This suggests that more training on implementing intellectual property rights is needed. ## Conclusion and Future Work

Our preliminary findings reveal that, through the m aker activity of creating a digital gallery, studen ts largely demonstrated 1) a basic understanding about concept s (e.g., user-centered design, information ethics) and 2) functional competency (e.g., inputting metadata, av oiding copyrights infringements) for digital curati on. Acquiring these 21 st century knowledge and skills were perceived by the  students as benefits of their digital curation experience, which they deemed useful for their professional and personal pursuits. As an ongoing study, we are conducting in-depth analysis of the student-inputted metadata and their reflective writing.
