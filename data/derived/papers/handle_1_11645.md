# Towards Institutional Change: Supporting District-level Praxeological Shifts in Integrated Computing Instruction through a Research-Practice Partnership

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper draws on a Research-Practice Partnership in which we introduced organizational routines (ORs) involving co-design and co-reflection cycles for teachers and coaches to integrate computational thinking (CT) into math and science instruction. Using thematic analysis of educator interviews and class observation notes, we characterize coaches as co-designers and brokers for supporting boundary crossings of integrated CT knowledge and practice within and across schools, and advancing shifts away from techno-centric to learner-centric perspectives on design.

## Introduction and theoretical background

There is consensus that integrating computational t hinking (CT) practices into math and science classr ooms is a worthy goal (NGSS Lead States, 2013; NCTM, 2016). H owever, the field of Learning Sciences is still gra ppling with questions around how to build capacity to brin g about this change in a district (McGill & Thigpen, 2024). Moreover, scaling and sustaining this work requires professional learning (PL) opportunities that can enable shifts in the competencies of educators, but also the refining of systemic structures that can maintain the new pedagogies and support educators. In this paper, we share lear nings from a two-year Research-Practice Partnership (RPP) between two educational institutions and an underse rved, urban school district in the state of Maine (“District”). The RPP’s vision is to integrate computational thin king (CT) practices into upper elementary math and science classrooms to teach district-mandated standards. Looking back at the work done t hrough this RPP, we ask: 1. How do the proposed organizational routines enable and sustain the RPP’s goals of integrating CT practices into math and science instruction?; and, 2. How does the  design team learn to integrate computational think ing practices into math and science lesson sequences over the course of the project? We use the construct of an organizational routine (OR) - “repetitive, recognizable patterns of interdependent actions involving multiple actors'' (Feldman & Pentland, 2003, p. 95) - to investigate the impact of and iteratively refine our RPP model. Scholars a rgue that the formation and establishment of ORs pr ovide important mechanisms of change within organizations  (Feldman & Pentland, 2003; Sherer & Spillane, 2011). ORs are critical aspects of any organization, inclu ding school districts. Studying ORs in t hree school districts adopting new curricula, Haverly and colleagues (202 2) argued that they promoted teacher learning by be coming boundary practices. In other words, they served as a mechanism throug h which ideas, resources and tools were shared and exchanged between key stakeholders and o therwise isolated groups in a community. The author s characterized stakeholders that facilitated such ex changes as boundary spanners because of the critical role they played in bringing new ideas into a group and transferring ideas across groups.

The study The RPP’s goals are to build capacity at scale to b roaden access to computing within the District thro ugh establishment of sustained PL and curriculum design structures that supported the co-design of integrated CT-MS lessons and the co-reflection of CT-MS pedagogical practices among RPP participants. The District ranks 216 out of 230 districts in the state. Just over half of the students identify as non-white, far above the Maine state average of 13%. Data comes from 13 semi-structured interviews with 3 co aches and 8 teachers, with some educators interviewed multiple times. Interviews focused on t he purpose of technology and CT in integrated math and science instruction, how successful the integrated lessons were in supporting student learning, and th e perceived value of a PL community. Notes from design team meetings and PL community co-design sessions were used to triangulate and provide further context for interviews. Our analysis began with identifying the norms and practices introduced within the co-design sessions to characterize the ORs initiated and (when relevant) sustained. Using thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2012) and the co nstructs of “boundary crossings” and “boundary span ners”, we analyzed interviews to look for evidence of: 1. Sources and mechanisms of spread of the ideas and materials;

2. Shifts in ideas about and/or attitudes towards CT integration through these new tools and practices.

## Findings

Establishment of coach role as lead designer and broker The RPP surfaced the potential of the coach role as broker (Cobb et al., 2009) of CT-integrated instruction within the district. Although standards-based instructional support was a defined role for the coach position, opportunities to work with other coaches to design cross-curricular instruction was largely unr ealized. The RPP provided a dedicated space for coaches to engage with existing  tools in the district (e.g., Ozobots) to illuminate how they could elevate standards-based practice through integration with CT practices. Moreover, coaches played a critical role as district-wide brokers, distributing lesson materials and pro viding in-classroom support to RPP and non-RPP teachers. As one coach noted, “Once they [non-RPP teachers] see the fun, exciting things that oth er [RPP] classrooms are doing, they’re like, ‘Oh, can you come do that with my room?’.. ‘This isn’t so bad!’” Supporting boundary crossings within and across schools for teacher partners Advancing knowledge through an RPP involves develop ing generalizable knowledge that increases the accessibility of findings (Bevan et al., 2017; Farr ell & Coburn, 2017; Farrell et al., 2019). In our w ork, we noted the “stickiness”, or persistence of organizational routines was dependent on their transition to boundary practices, or routines that supported interactions between dif ferent actors and contexts. Interviews confirm the successful transition of our co-design and co-reflect ORs into boundary practic es. For example, teachers and coaches from different grade levels and schools came together to rehearse and iterate on integrated lessons designed by coaches and reflect on their teaching. Interviews revealed the emergence of lead teachers, whose classrooms served  as models of instruction for other teachers. Moreover, a teacher shared the PLC supported strengthened connections with the school technology coach. He shared that “H aving somebody within my building who I know I coul d be “Hey, what is this?” And he has all these different ideas about stuff. So it's good, like, I feel more confident.”

## Shift from technocentric to learner-centric perspectives

Integrating CT into math and science instruction ca n be transformative for student learning because of  the reflexivity between computational, mathematical and  scientific practices (Guzdial, 1995; Kafai & Harel, 1991). Engaging in co-design and co-reflection cycles with coaches has led partner educators to shift from a technocentric approach to a learner-centric one by responding to the reflexivity betwee n a computational tool, target learning goals, and students’ existing conceptual resources.  “[T]he best thing that’s come out of this is me lo oking at it from another lens of, not what you’re doing as a te acher, but what should the kids be doing. And how are they going to be interacting with each other and with the objects that they’re going to be learning with?”, RPP Coach.

## Discussion

We contribute to the literature on computing integrati on by highlighting the value of adopting constructs  from organizational learning as a lens to identify, eval uate and (as needed) refine the practices being ini tiated and/or maintained in an RPP to meet target goals. We showed how establishing structures which placed t eachers in contact with knowledgeable others (coaches) who bro ught a complementary expertise (e.g., comfort with technologies and subject matter) was critical to su pport teachers in bringing  new tools and practices into their classrooms. One implication is to identify alliances with expertise and skill sets to support teachers with adopting new kinds of instruction in their classrooms. Another implication is adopting an asset-based approach in working with districts. Just as the field of Learning Sciences has adopted this approach for learning at the individual level (e.g., Smith et al., 1993; Warren et al., 2001), we  call for orienting to and building on the assets-individual, material, social or otherwise-that districts come with to lay the foundational groundwork for partnerships.
