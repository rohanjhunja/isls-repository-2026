# From Regulation to Co-Regulation: A Game-Based Simulation for Human and Simulated Learners during Complex Problem Solving

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper presents MetaSim, a game-based simulation designed to foster the development and transfer of metacognitive and self-regulated learning (SRL) skills in human learners. Grounded in contemporary theories of SRL and metacognition, MetaSim targets algebra by integrating cognitive, affective, motivational, metacognitive, and social dimensions of learning and complex problem solving. Central to MetaSim’s design, human learners interact with simulated learners (SLs), who serve as adaptive pedagogical agents (PA), embodying their human learner’s SRL profile. While human learners engage in problem solving, the SLs model, explain, and justify SRL strategies, prompting learners to reflect on and refine their own regulatory behaviors. This paper pres ents a high-level overview of the four phases of teaching and testing the simulated learners’ metacognitive capabilities in MetaSim.

Developing robust SRL skills is crucial for student success in complex STEM fields that require strategic learning, problem-solving, reasoning, and adaptive expertise. SRL involves a cyclic process where learners plan, monitor, control, and reflect on thei r cognition, affect, metacognition, and motivation (CAMM) in response to internal conditions and task demands (Greene et al., 2024; Winne & Azevedo, 2022; Zimmerman & Moylan, 2009). Despite decades of research emphasizing the importance of SRL, many learners, particularly in STEM, lack the metacognitive skills necessary for sustained  and effective regulation during problem-solving tasks (NASEM, 2025). Addressing this gap requires the development of learning environments, such as game-based AI simulations, that support SRL processes and assist learners in developing metacognitive and reflec tive skills, as well as adaptability for a sense of agency over how they learn (Azevedo & Wiedbusch, 2023). We address this issue by proposing the conceptualization of Meta Sim, a new game-based AI simulation environment based on emerging research in the area of simulated learners (SLs; Käser & Alexandron, 2024; VanLehn et al., 1994). This paper presents a high-level overview of the four phases of teaching and testing the simulated learners’ metacognitive capabilities in MetaSim.

Teaching and testing the SLs’ metacognitive capabilities in MetaSim MetaSim focuses on how human learners interact with, teach, observe, question, and learn from  and with SLs using an AI-powered, game-based simulation environment, designed to foster metacognitive and SRL skills in the context of high school algebra problem solving (Azevedo et al., 2026). The overarching aim is to scaffold learners' own metacognitive growth by having them externalize and refine their knowledge of SRL processes through interactions with adaptive, intelligent SLs. In this section, we provide a  brief description of how these interactions unfold across four dynamic, iterative, and cyclical phases. Phase 1: Explicit t eaching of SRL knowledge and strategies to simulated learners during algebra problem solving using multimodal trace data In the first phase, learners take on the role of SRL instructors, teaching SLs specific metacognitive and SRL strategies (e.g., planning, goal-setting, monitoring, and self-evaluation) while solving algebra word problems. We will focus on complex algebra word problems, as they tend to be highly challenging for high school students (Borchers et al., 2025). These problems pose challenges for students as they require translating complex verbal information into mathematical and diagrammatic representations, app lying appropriate cognitive strategies, solving them by engaging in multistep processes, and often inducing impasses or errors. Importantly, students must monitor each step for accuracy, which demands strong problem-solving and metacognitive skills, highlighting the need for their development. In addition, such problems require SRL skills to engage in complex problem solving by (1) understanding the problem, (2) using several effective cognitive strategies, (3) monitoring various aspects of the problem,  (4) determining when to seek help, and (5) regulating one’s emotions (e.g., confusion, frustration) and motivation (e.g., task value, task interest, self-efficacy) when faced with challenges such as impasses, incorrect or incomplete solutions, etc. (Borchers et al., 2025). 424 During this phase, students teach their SL SRL strategies and demonstrate how to solve problems using these strategies. MetaSim  collects and utilizes multimodal trace data (e.g., eye tracking, facial expressions, log files, clickstreams, dialogue interactions) to capture both the learner's teaching behaviors and the SLs’ responses. Learners are prompted to verbalize and demonstrate declarative (what), procedural (how), and conditional (when and why) knowledge about SRL strategies, along with the algebra knowledge and skills needed to solve the problem. However, due to space limitations, this paper focuses only on SRL strategies. The multimodal data helps to create detailed profiles of the learner's instructional behaviors, supporting adaptive feedback loops between the learner and the SL.

This phase promotes the learner's ability to articulate and externalize their SRL knowledge and skills and fosters the SL's ability to acquire and encode these strategies. At the end of this explicit teaching phase, we expect the learner to have taught the SL everything they need to know to solve the algebra problem. Each student would be designing their own SL, and therefore, we envision a group of SLs, each reflecting and embodying the algebra and SRL skills of their corresponding human learners, populati ng MetaSim that will be used throughout the various phases described below.

In Phase 1, learners engage in a structured teaching mode where they instruct their SL on essential algebra problem-solving techniques alongside key SRL strategies. Core game mechanics include drag-and-drop scaffolding tools, content and strategy selection menus, and interactive dialogue trees, enabling learners to model and verbalize planning, monitoring, and evaluation processes. Leaners receive real-time multimodal feedback via gaze heatmaps, facial emotion recognition overlays, and gesture tracking, which visualize how SLs attend to and engage with the teaching process. A dynamic SRL dashboard tracks the use and sequencing of cognitive and metacognitive strategies, allowing learners to adjust their teaching and instruction based on the SL’s evolving understanding and behavior. These mechanics encourage deliberate practice of SRL instruction, reinforcing how teaching others (especially the SLs) can enhance the human learner’s own metacognitive awareness and instructional efficacy.

We acknowledge that learners will not always explicitly teach their SL everything they know and do not know. Additionally, the sequencing of algebra and SRL knowledge, processes, and skills that are taught may not be in an ideal sequence for optimal problem-solving. However, the human learners’ multimodal data may reveal both (i) implicit SRL knowledge and skills that were not explicitly taught to the SL and (ii) a lack of specific SRL knowledge and skills. This  data will be used in other phases to reveal, highlight, model, and scaffold learners’ emerging SRL skills. Elaborations of data used for this and other phases described below are beyond the scope of this paper.

Phase 2: Learners observe SLs enact, model, and e xplain taught SRL strategies during problem solving In the second phase, learners transition from teaching to observing. The SL now engages in algebra problem-solving by enacting the SRL strategies they were previously taught in phase 1 by the learner. These enactments are accompanied by real-time, metacogn itive narration, where SLs explain what they are doing (e.g., " I am identifying knowns and unknowns before solving"), why they are doing it, and how it supports effective learning and problem-solving. Learners observe these behaviors through multimodal cue s, including animated gestures, gaze shifts, and verbalized reflections. This observational learning context is designed to reinforce the effectiveness of strategies, promote metacognitive monitoring in the learner, and provide a comparative model of SRL i n action. MetaSim  may introduce challenges, distractors, or misconceptions that highlight when SRL strategies are applied appropriately or inappropriately; foster ing learners’ metacognitive monitoring and evaluation, and therefore prompt their metacognitive regulation strategies. As each learner observes their SL enacting, modeling, and explaining the SRL strategies previously taught during algebra problem-solving. Through this observational (vicarious) learning phase, learners develop problem-solving skills such as identifying relevant information, selecting appropriate algebraic operations, and applying systematic procedures to solve equations (Dӧrner & Funke, 2017). Simultaneously, they strengthen SRL skills, including monitoring (e.g., detecting accurate SL strategy application, SL errors or strategy misapplication), evaluating (e.g., assessing the appropriateness of strategies used by SLs), and reflecti ng (e.g., comparing their own [i.e., taught] strategy use with that of the SL who may, in certain situations, fail to apply or misapply taught strategies). In addition, observing SLs verbalize their reasoning processes helps learners internalize, assess, and reinforce their declarative knowledge (i.e., a learner’s explicit understanding of their own cognitive abilities, task demands, and available learning strategies), procedural knowledge  (i.e., knowing how to implement them), and conditional knowledge (i.e., knowing when and why to use specific strategies) (Winne & Azevedo, 2022). This process fosters a deeper understanding of strategy transfer, metacognitive modeling, and the iterative nature of self-regulation. It ultimately enhances learners' ability to plan, regulate, and adapt their own learning in future 425 problem-solving tasks (Zimmerman & Moylan, 2009). It also emphasizes Winne’s (2018) COPES (i.e., conditions, operations, products, evaluations, and standards) and SMART (i.e., search, monitor, assemble, rehearse, and translate) schemas for SRL. These models facilitate learners’ development of internal standards and practice of cognitive evaluations for accurate self-regulation. The instructional principles guiding this observational learning phase are grounded in social cognitive theory (Zimmerman & Moylan, 2009), cognitive apprenticeship (Collins & Kapur, 2022), and modeling-based instruction (Seel, 2003), emphasizing how learners acquire skills through guided observation, reflection, and internalization. First, the principle of modeling  ensures that SLs explicitly demonstrate and verbalize SRL strategies such as planning, monitoring, and evaluating during algebra problem solving, allowing learners to witness effective cognitive and metacognitive behaviors in context. Second, scaffolded observation is used, where learners are supported by cues, prompts, and visualizations (e.g., strategy tags, feedback timelines) to focus attention on critical aspects of the SL’s behavior. Third, reflection and explanation prompts encourage learners to engage in metacognitive reasoning by comparing the SL’s actions to their knowledge and experiences. Fourth, the principle of strategy abstraction and generalization guides learners to move beyond specific examples to broader SRL principles they can apply across problems. Lastly, feedback-informed learning is employed, where learners receive targeted system feedback on their interpretations and reflections, reinforcing accurate understanding and promoting self-correction. Together, these principles foster the development of SRL and problem-solving skills by making otherwise internal strategies visible, explainable, and transferable through structured, guided observation.

During this phase, system features and game mechanics are designed to immerse learners in reflective and analytical thinking as they observe their SLs enact, model, and explain taught SRL strategies during algebra problem-solving. Key mechanics include interactive replay tools that enable learners to pause, rewind, and annotate the SL behaviors  and highlighted SRL strategy tagging, which visually identifies when and how strategies such as planning, monitoring, and evaluation are used. An SRL strategy dashboard updates in real time to show which SRL strategies are being used, enabling learners to compare their own approaches with the SL’s. Additionally, adaptive reflection questions guide learners to evaluate the effectiveness of the SL’s decisions and consider how they might improve their own problem-solving and SRL approaches. These mechanics foster metacognitive awareness, strategy internalization, and skill transfer by turning observation into an active, scaffolded learning experience.

We expect this phase to take longer than Phase 1 and for learners to engage in many cycles of it, depending on several personal and educational goals ranging from instructional conditions, goals of teaching, learning and assessment, learners’ SRL skills and expertise, and other individual differences. This cycle may also lead to learners revisiting Phase 1 as they teach their SL similar or more complex algebra word problems, without proceeding to Phases 3 and 4, described below.

Phase 3: Learners interrogate SLs about their SRL b ehaviors and prompt them to justify, refine, or improve strategy use Phase 3 fosters metacognitive discourse between learners and SLs. Learners are encouraged to interrogate their SLs by asking them to justify their use of strategies, explain their rationale, reflect on their outcomes, and consider alternatives. For example, a learner might ask, "Why did you decide to skip the step of checking your answer?" or Can you explain how you determined which strategy was most effective for this type of problem?". Learners can also challenge their SL to try out more effective or nuanced strategies, pushing the SL to iterate and evolve. Multimodal trace data, including speech patterns, emotion detection, and response latency, are used to dynamically assess both the learner's questioning depth and the SL's metacognitive adaptability. This reciprocal teaching loop encourages learners to reflect on their own SRL repertoire while cultivating critical thinking, argumentation, and diagnostic reasoning. SLs will have “access” to detailed process models of SRL skills (e.g., Azevedo & Dever, 2022) that they candraw on to explain, justify, and model SRL skills; otherwise, the only SRL skills they will embody are those taught by their human learners. Due to space limitations, we are unable to describe this architectural feature in this paper.

As learners interrogate their SLs about their SRL behaviors and prompt them to justify, refine, or improve their strategy use, they acquire advanced metacognitive (e.g., monitoring use of strategies) and problem-solving skills that deepen their understanding of effective learning regulation. Through this active coaching role, learners develop strategic monitoring by identifying gaps or missteps in the SL’s reasoning and evaluative judgment as they assess the appropriateness and timing of the SL’s chosen str ategies. This also strengthens the learner’s conditional knowledge by enabling them to reason through when and why specific SRL strategies should be used, and how to adapt them based on task demands or performance outcomes. This  process fosters reflective questioning, cognitive flexibility, and the ability to generate and test alternative strategies, as these are key 426 competencies for adaptive problem-solving (Spiro et al., 2019). By requiring learners to articulate feedback, challenge faulty assumptions, and co-construct improved solutions with their SLs, this phase promotes self-awareness, transfer of SRL knowledge, and executive control over learning processes, ultimately enabling learners to better regulate their own learning in future tasks.

The instructional principles guiding the interrogation phase are rooted in metacognitive scaffolding (Azevedo & Hadwin, 2005; Tabak & Reiser, 2022), Socratic questioning (duBoulay et al., 2023), and learning-by-teaching (Biswas et al., 20 16) frameworks, designed to promote deep reflection, reasoning, and transfer of SRL strategies. Central to this phase is the principle of elaborative interrogation (Dunlosky et al., 2013), where learners are prompted to ask “why” and “how” questions that require the SL to justify and reflect on their strategy use, encouraging the learner to think critically about the underlying rationale of SRL behaviors. The principle of reciprocal teaching (Rosenshine & Maester, 1994) positions learners as cognitive coaches, reinforcing their own understanding by teaching and correcting others (i.e., SLs). Dialogic learning further supports this by fostering back-and-forth exchanges where learners evaluate explanations, identify weaknesses, and collaboratively refine strategies. Instruction  is also guided by the principle of productive struggle (Kapur, 2024), where learners are challenged to analyze flawed reasoning or incomplete SRL strategies in SL responses, promoting the development of strategic thinking and error detection. Finally, guided self-explanation ensures that learners not only critique SL’s choices but also articulate alternative strategies and justifications, strengthening their conditional knowledge and ability to generalize across tasks (Tan et al., 2025). These principles together create a high-engagement environment where learners internalize SRL processes by actively regulating another artificial learner’s (i.e., their SL’s) behavior.

In the interrogation phase of the game-based simulation, learners engage in an interactive dialogue with their SL, using branching question trees, voice or text-based input, and strategy-probing tools to ask SLs to explain, justify, or reconsider their use of SRL strategies during algebra problem solving. Game mechanics include dialogue-based scaffolding, where learners can choose from or generate prompts targeting specific SRL phases (e.g., "Why did you choose that strategy?" or "Was that the best time to evaluate your progress?"), and adaptive SL responses generated from a dynamic SRL reasoning model that reflects the SL’s current understanding and learner profile. System features such as real-time strategy maps, confidence meters, and rationale logs support learners in evaluating the coherence and effectiveness of the SL's reasoning. A feedback engine guides learners to refine their questioning techniques by offering suggestions for deeper inquiry or highlighting missed opportunities to challenge flawed reasoning. This phase transforms learners into metacognitive coaches, reinforcing their own SRL skills by prompting critical thinking, strategy evaluation, and justification processes that deepen their understanding of when, why, and how to regulate learning. Phase 4: SLs prepare human learners for more complex algebra problems In the final phase, the roles shift again. The SLs now act as peers, coaches, or mentors, helping the human learners tackle more complex, multi-step algebra problems. These problems could involve advanced algebraic equations, word problems with multiple variables, or real-world applications of algebra. The SLs scaffold learners' planning, problem decomposition, and metacognitive monitoring in real time, drawing upon the strategies previously modeled and refined. They may prompt the learner to plan, reflect during the task, or self-evaluate their progress. The system uses real-time multimodal trace data to assess the learner's current SRL state and adapt the SL's guidance and feedback accordingly. This phase tests both the learner's internalization of SRL str ategies and the SL's capacity to transfer and apply learned metacognitive behaviors to novel, more demanding contexts. By simulating adaptive peer support, the SLs serve as a metacognitive catalyst, encouraging learners to autonomously transfer and refine their SRL strategies in future learning situations. This phase is significantly cognitively demanding, abstract, and strategic, and therefore focuses on qualitative shifts in learners’ development of SRL skills. The time spent in this phase will differ based on individual student needs, instructional demands, teaching and instructional goals and objectives, and contextual factors. In addition, we envision a mode in this phase whereby learners activate other learners’ SLs and run ‘what if’ simulations, comparing their SL with those of other learners who embody more sophisticated SRL skills to ensure that they develop more task-and domain-specific independent SRL skills. In summary, this four-phase process combines AI, game-based learning, and multimodal analytics to develop a rich, interactive learning environment. It not only improves the metacognitive skills of human learners but also assesses and enhances the SRL competencies of SLs, laying the foundation for a new era of intelligent, teachable agents that co-evolve with their human counterparts. SLs have the potential to revolutionize research into intelligent, AI-driven game-based simulations designed to prepare learners for complex workforce challenges and enhance their problem-solving skills.
