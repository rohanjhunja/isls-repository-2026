# Evaluating and Assessing Learning in AI-Embedded Environments: Perspectives Across Learning Scientists

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This hybrid poster symposium brings together eight studies that examine how evaluation can serve as a generative process for designing, enacting, and refining AI-supported learning environments. Moving beyond questions of technical accuracy or efficiency, these contributions frame evaluation as a relational and ethical practice that shapes how educators, learners, and technologies collaborate. Across diverse contexts —from middle school classrooms and tutoring programs to teacher coaching and high school psychology— researchers employ mixed methods, including discourse analytics, qualitative video analysis, and practical measures of student experience, to understand AI’s impact on collaboration, belonging, and cognition. A shared emphasis on human-centered des ign and co-creation underscores the importance of positioning teachers and students as partners in shaping AI systems that amplify rather than automate human intelligence. Together, the studies highlight how rigorous, equity-oriented evaluation can illumin ate both the affordances and risks of educational AI.

AI-powered technologies are becoming increasingly prevalent in classrooms, promising to partner with teachers and learners in authentic, collaborative learning environments. Yet their adoption also raises pressing questions: How do we evaluate whether thes e tools enhance purposeful collaboration, deepen student belonging, and build AI literacy? How do we assess their ability to reduce teachers’ cognitive load in order to free them for more complex and humanizing pedagogical moves? And, equally important, how do we guard against the threats to equity posed by AI systems that replicate or amplify historical biases? This symposium brings together a set of completed studies that address these questions by examining how evaluation methods can support purposeful partnering with AI. This includes looking at the design, enactment, and evaluation of AI-supported tools and curriculum routines in diverse classroom contexts. Collectively, the contributions highlight how evaluation is not only about measuring outcomes but also about shaping the trajectory of design, guiding adoption, and ensuring that technologies serve expansive educational purposes rather than narrow, efficiency-driven goals.

These evaluation studies bring together evaluation of several different visions and interpretations of AI supporting learning and equity. One example is CoBi, a collaborative AI partner co-designed with teachers and 2305 students. CoBi supports community agreement routines, facilitates meta-collaborative reflection, and provides non-evaluative feedback on student talk. Research across projects demonstrates how teachers integrate and adapt CoBi to foster classroom belonging, support equitable participation, and develop students’ AI literacy by critically engaging them with both the strengths and the limitations of the system. Another AI powered tool is the Talk Trees, a dynamic and AI-powered display of automated classificat ions and transcriptions of both teacher and student talk, often correlating to equitable and discursive pedagogies and interactions. Another tool discussed covers supports for the coaching of teachers that uses collaborative, evidence-based reasoning. This symposium concludes with discussion of a tool that supports cognitive demand and orchestration of collaborative tasks in high school Psychology class. In addition to reflecting on how to evaluate AI tools and agents, some studies discuss the evaluation of  AI curriculum, often with synergy between AI powered tools, to promote AI literacy (Gendreau et al, 2022), critical agency (Basu et al., 2009), and rightful presence (Calabrese Barton & Tan, 2019). These enactments illustrate the potential of AI tools to offload repetitive or low-level instructional tasks, enabling teachers to invest more energy in responsive, high-value pedagogical practices. Evaluation methods across these projects highlight new approaches for studying AI in education. They include qualitative analyses of classroom interactions, measures of student sense of belonging and collaborative engagement, and teacher reflections on wor kload and professional learning. Importantly, the studies also emphasize the need for new assessment frameworks that align with the unique affordances and risks of AI — frameworks that value student-centered knowledge building, ethical awareness, and cultura l responsiveness as much as technical accuracy.

By framing evaluation as central to both design and implementation, this symposium addresses what we call the “triple innovation challenge”: preparing teachers to teach about AI, to foster collaborative knowledge-building, and to use and critically reflect  on AI tools in practice (Ko, 2024). In alignment with the ISLS 2026 theme, “Partnering with Purpose,” the session underscores the importance of collaborative, design-based partnerships among researchers, teachers, and students in shaping futures where AI is leveraged to expand possibilities for equitable, authentic, and humanizing learning. ## Overview of contributions

The eight poster contributions spandiverse research on multiple continents all brought together by their similar foci on evaluating AI-tools in learning sciences research and made distinct by their unique perspectives on what evidence and information should be prioritized to assess the success and affordances of the interventions they study. Contributions include papers from the evaluation of NSF-funded AI institutes designing AI partners and 2306 AI curriculum, papers from tools to support teachers and tutors in improving their pedagogy and a paper evaluating an LLM that supports collaborative orchestration and cognitive load on students and teachers during a high-school Psychology class. Themes and theories from contributions include sociocultural theory, cognitive load theory, orchestration and collaborative learning. Evaluation methods include a range of mixed-methods approaches to video review, qualitative analysis of participant interviews and quantitative analysis of both surveys and assessments.

Reflecting on our shared positionality, for every submission, authors present frameworks for evaluation of tools that their teams and/or colleagues helped design. One of the primary contributions of this symposium is to bring together diverse thinking on these approaches to help limit the potential confirmation bias and groupthink of evaluating tools that we all are invested in designing. While we do not advocate for working towards a unilateral and standardized evaluation criteria, finding commonalities and principles across designs will help researchers choose the best tools for their own research. By understanding and appreciating evaluation methods and metrics from our peers, we all as the learning sciences community can more objectively evaluate AI-powered tools that are becoming more and more common in learning sciences research. Individual contributions Poster 1: Using the MOSAIC and MOSAIC-AI Protocols to Evaluate AI-Partners’ Impact on Support for Small Group Collaborative Work Indrani Dey, Nga Hoang, Jeffrey Bush Tayne Collaboration has strong potential to facilitate learning, but effective collaboration is challenging (Barron, 2003). Students frequently need additional support to sustain meaningful collaboration (Jarvenoja & Järvelä, 2009), yet teachers face the challenge of monitoring multiple groups simultaneously while also attending to individual needs (Dillenbourg et al., 2018). This “orchestrational load” makes it difficult for teachers to provide consistent, timely support. To address this challenge, technological  tools such as dashboards that leverage learning analytics, have been developed to help teachers track progress and intervene when necessary. While some AI-based tools have been developed to support collaboration (Han et al., 2021; Zheng et al., 2023), they often do not consider face-toface classroom collaboration contexts and remain limited in scope. As a result, deploying and evaluating AI tools without considering learners’ and teachers’ diverse needs, perspectives, socio-cultural and historical context s, may reinforce surface-level gains rather than foster deeper collaboration. Context and overview of methods used for evaluation of this AI tool or curriculum Our work aims to address this gap by grounding the design of AI support in classroom practice. Specifically, we focus on understanding what forms of support teachers and peers already provide during collaborative learning, how they emerge, and how students  take them up. By systematically capturing and analyzing these support moments, we aim to inform the development of AI systems that extend, but not replace, existing classroom practices. This human-centered approach is essential for creating technologies that equitably support collaboration and reduce the orchestration challenges teachers face in managing multiple groups.

## Initial findings, pilot results, and next directions

We developed the MOSAIC (Moments of Support Analysis in Collaboration) protocol (Hoang et al., 2024) to understand the nature of support in collaborative classrooms. MOSAIC captures support moments (instances when individuals outside a group (e.g., teachers, peers) intervene), capturing who  initiates and provides support, what type of support is given, and how students take it up. Applied across different contexts, MOSAIC has demonstrated that it is curriculum-and activity-agnostic. It revealed that proced ural support (e.g., guidance on how to proceed with tasks) is far more common than collaboration-focused support, even in classrooms and lessons designed around collaboration. This finding suggests that a teacher’s time that is spent on routine support could potentially be offloaded to an AI partner, allowing teachers to focus on more targeted support. Moreover, MOSAIC analyses highlighted activity design as a key factor influencing support needs, reinforcing the importance of aligning curriculum and AI partner design to balance procedural and collaborative support. Building on this initial work, we adapted the MOSAIC protocol to develop MOSAIC-AI (Dey et al.,

2025) to examine the nature of support during student-AI interactions. MOSAIC-AI introduced codes to capture

the timeliness, appropriateness, and reception of AI interventions. We applied MOSAIC-AI in a Wizard-of-Oz (WoZ) study where an educational expert acted as an AI partner. Our findings mirrored classroom results: most interventions were initiated by the support provider (i.e., the AI partner or Woz) and focused on task-related 2307 support. Although students initiated support about 20% of the time, it was often for getting their ideas validated by the AI. This suggests that while students may readily approach an AI partner for help, this reliance may lead to students offloading the more cognitively demanding tasks to the technology. Our analysis with MOSAIC-AI also highlighted limitations of speech-only AI partners, which can miss nonverbal indicators of disengagement or conflict. In the WoZ study, we observed an instance where WoZ’s encouragement of one student’s idea while another student was trying to pursue a different idea, caused friction between them. This unintended consequence further highlights the importance of designing human-centered interventions and prompts further thinking about evaluating these interventions. Instead of conducting a single evaluation at the end to determine the tool’s success, continuous real-time evaluation should be implemented to assess students’ responses to the intervention and guide adjustments when outcomes differ from expectations.

It is essential to inform the development of AI-based educational tools in classroom practices. Systematic protocols such as MOSAIC and MOSAIC-AI can reliably capture classroom behaviors; using these protocols as a lens can help identify patterns that can be used to inform tools and/or curriculum design. For example, our analyses highlighted that procedural support is essential and should be integral to AI partner design to support students and help teachers orchestrate. At the same time, it also highlighte d potential risks such as over-reliance on AI validation. By using systematic protocols to study classroom interactions as well as emerging tools, we emphasize a human-centered approach to AI partner design and evaluation grounded in classroom practices, while attending to the diverse collaborative contexts and needs of both learners and teachers. Poster 2: Assessing middle school AI literacy through human–AI collaboration Nga Hoang, Collette Heskett, Mon-Lin Monica Ko, Julia Eden, William Penuel Artificial intelligence is shaping how people learn, work, and interact in everyday life. To prepare students for this reality, assessments must move beyond testing what students can recall about AI and instead capture their AI literacy (Casal-Otero et al., 2023). In this work, we define AI literacy as students’ ability to reason about how data, models, human participation, updating processes, and contextual factors interact to shape the behavior of machine learning systems.

In science education, significant progress has been made with assessments anchored in phenomena and real-world problems, which enable students to demonstrate understanding by engaging in sense-making, reasoning, and decision-making (NRC, 2011). With respect to AI literacy, this involves weighing trade-offs and deciding when and how AI should be used. Building on this approach, the purpose of this poster is to show how we adapted problem-anchored assessments to the context of AI literacy. Specifically, our assessment has students evaluate a moderation solution that involves human-AI collaboration, considering competing priorities, recognizing biases, and making choices about human-AI interaction, thereby preparing students for future encounters with AI systems in social, and professional domains. Assessment design We designed a summative assessment for the Moderation unit, which engages students in identifying sources of bias and racism within video gaming communities, while envisioning how AI can be leveraged to support the creation of more inclusive gaming environments. The assessment has two parts: In the first part, students revisit the values and behaviors they desire in gaming communities from earlier in the unit, explore how humans and AI can support moderation, discuss ways to mitigate bias in AI, and examine the harms and benefits that players have experienced in different servers. These reflections help students connect principles such as respect, compassion, and belonging to concrete moderation practices. In the second part, students evaluate two possible moderation designs for gaming servers. Solution 1 enforces rules through automatic punishments based on a fixed list of banned words created and trained by three non-diverse individuals who founded the server. Solution 2 emphasizes community agreements, human-guided dialogue, and an AI system trained with input from a more diverse set of players that continues to learn over time. Students compare the solutions in terms of reducing harm, creating a sense of belonging, and avoiding bias. By the end of the assessment, students decide which solution is better based on criteria they developed. Student responses indicated that 76.5% (N = 115) identified Solution 1 as more biased and preferred Solution 2. This finding suggests that the assessment was successful in eliciting students’ ability to apply their understanding of AI, along with concepts of fairness and human-AI collaboration, to evaluate the two solutions and recognize 2308 bias in AI-mediated systems. However, while many students were able to detect that bias was present in the system, by pointing out that a system “created and trained by three White boys” reflect bias, students often struggled to distinguish between sampling bias in data collection (for example, relying on data from only three individuals) and labeling bias in annotation (for example, all annotators sharing the same characteristics and perspectives), highlighting areas where the curriculum can be revised and strengthened. This work demonstrates how problem-anchored assessments can be used to assess students’ AI literacies, moving beyond surface-level recall. By engaging students in evaluating design solutions involving human-AI collaboration, our assessment design provides a model for assessing students’ AI literacy. Poster 3: Developing and Evaluating Content Assessments and Student Metrics for AI-Embedded Learning Environments Quentin Biddy, Thomas Breideband, Melissa Campanella, Sidney D’Mello, William Penuel, Jason Reitman, Jeffrey Bush Tayne Artificial intelligence (AI) is rapidly shaping how people learn, work, and interact in everyday life. To prepare students for this reality, curriculum and research must be informed by assessments that attend to dimensions of the student experience as well  as AI literacy—defined as their ability to reason about how data, models, human participation, updating processes, and contextual factors interact to shape the behavior of machine learning systems (Casal-Otero et al., 2023). Within two curricula, Sensor Immersion and AI and Self Driving Cars developed by the NSF Institute for Student and AI Teaming (iSAT), we developed two complementary assessment approaches that evaluate both students’ conceptual learning in AI-embedded contexts and their collaborative experiences with an AI-powered collaboration support agent, CoBi (the Community Builder). We used an iterative, co-design process to develop these assessments in partnership with middle school teachers who co-designed and piloted items within the two units. These assessments not only measured students’ AI and computing understanding but also informed the ongoing development of CoBi, an AI partner designed to scaffold collaborative learning and community building during group work (Biddy et al., 2024; Bush et al., 2024; Breideband et al., 2023; Chandler et al., 2024). Together, these assessment s ystems— pre/post content assessments and mid-lesson student experience exit tickets (SEETs)—provide a framework for evaluating the curriculum. Within the context of lessons that focus on learning about AI and that are supported by AI, this framework attends to both a) what students learn and b) how they experience learning. Sensor Immersion (SI) is a three-week middle school unit introducing students to computing and AI concepts and practices needed to use sensors as tools for scientific inquiry and engineering design. It addresses CSTA middle school computing standards, AI4K 12 standards and Next Generation Science Standards (NGSS) science and engineering practices. AI and Self-Driving Cars (SDC) is a five-week middle school unit that builds upon the skills and concepts introduced in SI with a  more explicit machine learning and AI focus. Students explore the decisions a self-driving car must make, learning about data collection, classifier training, neural networks, bias, and human–robot interaction.

Content assessment development The pre/post assessments focus on AI-Literacy to gauge changes in student understandings. The SI pre/post assessment evaluated computing knowledge related to sensors and programming and students’ ability to apply this knowledge to design sensor systems addressing real-world problems such as noise pollution or wildfire mitigation. Assessment items required students to model sensor systems, interpret data, and debug code in blockbased programming environments. Rubrics were refined through pilot testing to capture authentic student reasoning and creativity, such as innovative debugging strategies. Content assessments were also integrated into the mid-unit SEETs. These measured students’ understanding of basic programming, its relationship to physical computing, how different sensors collect data, how to debug programs, and how to connect sensor data to real-world problems. Content item analysis from both the pre/post tasks and the mid-unit SEETs are forthcoming (pending data cleaning) and will be included on the poster if space allows.

## Practical measures for evaluating collaborative learning with CoBi

While content assessments captured what students learned, the collaboration SEET items were designed be practical measures (Yeager et al., 2013) to assess how students experienced collaboration in CoBi-supported lessons. CoBi is an AI partner that helps students and teachers reflect on the relational dimensions of collaboration (Breideband et al., 2023). CoBi scaffolds groups to define “community agreements” such as being equitable, 2309 respectful, and “moving thinking forward.” Using automated analysis of classroom talk, CoBi visualizes examples of these behaviors to support reflection and discussion. To evaluate CoBi’s influence on student experiences, SEETs included Likert-scale items aligned with eight constructs: (1) Community Agreements, (2) Respectful Collaboration, (3) Equitable Collaboration, (4) Commitment to Community, (5) Pushing Thinking Forward, (6) Collaborative Problem Solving (Harrison et al., 2023), (7) Group Climate, and (8) Group Influence. The SEETs were implemented in both treatment and control classrooms across two districts, comprising 15 classes taught by eight teachers. Condition assignment occurred at the class level, with using CoBi or not serving as the only experimental difference between conditions. SEET collaboration items analysis is forthcoming and will be included on the poster if space allows Together, the content and collaboration assessments from the SI and SDC units provide a comprehensive model for evaluating learning in AI-embedded environments. The co-design approach ensured that both assessment systems were grounded in teachers’ practice  and responsive to classroom realities. The results suggest that integrating AI tools like CoBi into authentic STEM curricula can simultaneously advance students’ AI literacy and foster collaborative norms that support equitable and reflective learning. The co-design process was central to achieving these outcomes. Teachers’ involvement in designing assessment tasks and refining AI feedback tools ensured alignment between instructional goals, evaluation assessments can be mutually generative—assessment data informs AI design, while AI feedback supports student learning and teacher reflection.

Future directions include deepening analyses that link students’ self-reported collaboration metrics with CoBi’s algorithmic measures of discourse, investigating longer-term effects of AI-mediated collaboration, and extending these designs to additional curricular contexts. Collectively, this work demonstrates how co-designed assessment and AI tools can enable the learning sciences community to evaluate, support, and reimagine learning in increasingly AI-embedded educational environments.

Poster 4: Catalyzing Collaborative Reflection: Evaluating AI-Supported Instructional Routines with the Community Builder AI-Partner, CoBi Thomas Breideband, Greg Benedis-Grab, Jeffrey Bush Tayne Collaboration is widely recognized as a vital 21st-century skill (Yang et al., 2022), yet it is difficult to practice. Classrooms that adopt collaborative learning approaches (Laal & Ghodsi, 2012; Vygotsky & Cole, 1978) are, in principle, well positioned to foster these skills by helping students actively engage with the social and cognitive dimensions of teamwork. Yet research consistently shows that orchestrating collaboration in classrooms is challenging (Chen, Tan, & Pi, 2021; Van Leeuwen & Janssen, 2019). Teachers often manage overlapping tasks in fast-paced, noisy classroom environments (Roschelle, Dimitriadis, & Hoppe, 2013; Tissenbaum & Slotta, 2015), and may overlook moments to scaffold students’ collaborative practices. Consequently, students often infer collaboration strategies independently, leading to persistent gaps in their learning (Fiore et al., 2018; Graesser et al., 2018). To address these challenges, we designed an AI system called CoBi that provides real-time visual feedback on students’ collaborative discourse, embedded in instructional routines that (a) help students surface what good collaboration looks like and (b) use visual feedback to reflect on the class’s alignment with their perceived ideals. Alongside the visualization, the interface provides prompts and sentence starters to aid reflection. By creating opportunities for reflective practice (Moon, 2006) within authentic, teacher-led routines, the system aims to help students strengthen collaboration skills over time. We evaluated this approach across two year-long classroom enactments.

We used a quasi-experimental design to compare CoBi classrooms against control classrooms. The study took place during the 2024–2025 school year across four districts, involving middle school STEM and science teachers in the Western United States. Particip ating teachers received coaching on how to use CoBi and its instructional routines. Classroom sessions were recorded using a follow-camera on the teacher (with a lapel microphone) and additional cameras capturing consented student groups during collaborative activities. Our evaluation focused on curriculum enactment integrity and the range of adaptations teachers made to the revisiting routine. First, we developed a coding scheme examining teachers’ orchestration strategies, students’ responses to discussion 2310 prompts, and the use of community-related language. Inter-rater reliability was moderate to high between two coders (κ = 0.6-0.82). The data included 80 videos with an average duration for the revisiting routine of 3m19s. We then analyzed teacher adaptations with a separate coding scheme; inter-rater reliability was high for high-integrity (κ =.85, p <.001), diverse adaptation (κ =.74, p <.001), and use of student ideas (κ =.74, p <.001).

Using thematic analysis, we examined a random sample of 265 minutes of teacher orchestration. The analysis of coded classroom observations revealed associations between teacher orchestration and students’ ceasollaborative discourse. Teachers’ use of sentence starters was correlated with students’ use of sentence starters (ρ =.37, p <.001). Import antly, teacher sentence starters were also positively associated with students’ use of community-oriented language of (ρ =.39, p <.001). By contrast, students’ use of sentence starters alone was not significantly associated with their use of community-oriented language (ρ =.17, p =.13). We fitted a mixed-effects logistic regression with a binomial link function followed by an analysis of group contrasts to investigate whether students’ use of community language differed between conditions. The model included a random intercept by classroom. Results from the generalized linear mixed-effects model indicated that students in the control condition had significantly lower odds of using community-oriented language compared during the revisitings to the AI-supported condition (OR = 0.03, 95% CI [0.00, 0.36], p =.006). The analysis of classroom video data revealed two overarching themes in how teachers orchestrated instructional routines—with and without CoBi: high-integrity use and diverse adaptations. High-integrity use was the norm among teachers implementing the system. Of 59 coded moments, 54 (92%) demonstrated high-integrity use compared to only 33% in control classrooms, indicating that CoBi supported more consistent and reflective orchestration. Alongside fidelity of use, teachers frequently introduced diverse, hi gh-integrity adaptations (36% of moments), creatively expanding the system’s use beyond its initial design. These adaptations included peer “shout-out” activities and instant polls assessing the perceived accuracy of AI classifications. Teachers also used CoBi as a learning opportunity for AI literacy. For example, in 75% of instances involving system misclassifications, teachers turned technical mistakes into moments for discussing AI accuracy, reliability, and data interpretation. CoBi helped surface student contributions, with teachers directly or indirectly incorporating student ideas in 70% of moments. However, overreliance on AI outputs sometimes limited authentic student agency, highlighting a need for improved professional development work with teachers prior to implementation.

When CoBi was present, teachers typically integrated it with fidelity to the intended design rather than treating it as an optional supplement. In contrast, in control classrooms without CoBi reflection routines shifted away from data-driven visualization discussions toward more generalized whole-class conversations about community and collaboration. This contrast indicates that CoBi helped scaffold—and, in many cases, stabilize—teachers’ reflective routines, as high-integrity enactment was notably less frequent in its absence. Poster 5: Talk Trees: Supporting growth in agentic student discourse Sandra Sawaya, Thomas Breideband, Chelsea Brown, Sidney D’Mello, Jennifer Jacobs, Ashieda McKoy, Jeffrey Bush Tayne

## Context

Classroom discourse is a central driver for student engagement and learning (Böheim et al., 2021; Howe et al., 2019). Research evidence shows that learning outcomes improve when educators engage students with highleverage discourse moves, such as asking open-ended questions, pressing students to explain their reasoning, and building off their responses (Alexander, 2018; Howe et al., 2019; Michaels & O’Connor, 2015). A growing body of work has studied the development and impact of ed tech AI tools that provide feedback on the discourse that occurs in learning contexts. When we organize the current landscape of such tools (presented in the poster), we notice that most a re available only to educators (i.e., coaches, teachers, or tutors), and/or at the individual-user level, and with no or limited feedback on student discourse. By designing feedback intended only for the individual, professional learning of educators, thes e tools may inadvertently reinforce the educators’ role as the sole agents accountable for the quality of the classroom discourse and  overlook students as agentic and foundational actors for discursive learning environments. These gaps in the literature seed the design of our Talk Tree AI tool, which emphasizes both educator and student agency and mutual accountability as a means to improve classroom discourse. We designed Talk Tree to provide feedback on the well-established framework of academically productive talk (APT) which emphasizes quality and rigorous student and teacher discourse that is associated with enhanced engagement and learning 2311 outcomes (Michaels & O’Connor, 2015). In this work, we present the design of the Talk Tree and its enactment in two real-world contexts of small-group remote math tutoring and in-person classroom teaching. Inspired by the tree visualization from (Breideband et al., 2023) and its metaphor for growth, we adapted CoBi from the context of discourse for collaborative problem solving to that of discourse for learning. As a way to promote student agency, the. Talk Trees is a visualization that celebrates students’ high-quality discourse in the form of a tree with colorful flowers representing evidence of APT moves – red for providing evidence, yellow for relating to another student’s moves, blue for making a claim – while leaves reflect the overall quantity of student talk (Figure presented in the poster). Educators use conversational scaffolds to integrated into the Talk Trees interface to engage students in-class discussions centered around celebration, comparison and ideation relating the AI’s evaluation of their own classroom discourse. This activity promotes student agency by shifting the role of students from passive participants to active agents in shaping the nature of classroom discourse. Educators also had access to a user interface showing multiple trees with the ability to change the dates, and the group of students. On the backend, the Talk Tree is powered by services to process classroom recordings (obtained with necessary permissions and safeguards in place) through automatic speech recognition and APT discourse classification AI models.

After designing the initial Talk Tree interface, we studied its enactment in two real-world contexts of remote group tutoring and in-person classroom teaching in Spring 2025 (three tutors, four teachers) and Fall 2025 (five tutors, four teachers). Through PL sessions, tutors and teachers met with members of our research team to discuss the Talk Tree interface and how to meaningfully use it with students. Then, over the course of a three-tonine-week period, they implemented the tool in their classes and sha red insights into its use in practice and potential value by participating in surveys and interviews.

## Key Findings and Implications

Through self-reports, educators found the Tree, specifically its ability to visualize discourse, useful to help students take ownership over their talk. One educator shared that “[A student] was like, I wonder what our talk trees are going to look like if we start talking more, start participating more.” Some teachers also noted a change in the intentionality of their students’ discourse and noticed become more aware of and attentive to the way they contributed to classroom discourse during instruction. Through enactment studies in two contexts, we observed educators adapting Talk Trees, the tool for their local contexts and using it to implement student-centered activities such as reflection, celebration, ideation, and practice (an adaptation). One educator  stated that Talk Trees “did motivate [students to] stay focused and constantly speak up and not slow down.” Another educator shared that students “felt a lot more compelled to have discussions building on one another.” Our findings suggest promise in designing AI tools for classroom feedback as a means to encourage student discourse. Poster 6: Human-centered evaluation of AI to support instructional coaching James Malamut, Dora Demszky Instructional coaching can lead to improvements in both instruction and student learning (Kraft et al., 2018), especially when focused on reasoning about classroom evidence (Kochmanski & Cobb, 2023). However, evidence-based reasoning might be underutilized in coach-teacher discussions (Horn et al., 2017). Although video can be a helpful tool in promoting reasoning from evidence (Gaudin & Chaliès, 2015) and the curation of video clips can be a learning experience for coaches and teachers (Calandra et al., 20 08), finding the right video can be very time-consuming and expensive. Additionally, individual video clips provide lots of rich information about particular moments in classrooms but do not necessarily support teachers in reasoning about connections between those moments (Coles, 2014).

New technologies have emerged to help support the curation and analysis of classroom evidence by automatically detecting relevant discursive moments. However, their utility as instructional artifacts in coaching needs to be better understood. In order to do this, we partnered with instructional coaches to guide the design, enactment, and evaluation of one such tool: M-Powering Teachers (MPT). We used semi-structured interviews with 20 coaches to better understand how they were approaching coaching and challenges they were facing. During these interviews, we shared a prototype of MPT and asked participants to imagine its potential affordances and limitations. We used their feedback to guide the development of the tool wherever possible. We observed 9 coaches as they enacted coaching conversations with teachers and coded for 2312 specific coaching practices. We tracked which practices co-occurred with and without the use of MPT. After identifying themes in how coaches used MPT to enact coaching practices, we conducted more interviews about their experiences.

Coaches reported valuing MPT’s efficiency, visual representations, and perceived objectivity but raised concerns about accuracy of transcription and identification of talk moments. Coaches were observed using MPT to demonstrate effective instructional practices, probe teacher reasoning, challenge teacher thinking, and compare/generalize across detected moments.

The combination of asking coaches to imagine, observing their enactment, and eliciting their reflection mirrors the triple innovation challenge identified in this symposium. Anticipating the promises and pitfalls of MPT was both a way to prepare coaches to  take advantage of it and guide its design. Observing coaches pilot MPT provided insights into how well (or not) the tool fostered collaborative, evidence-based reasoning. Those insights were further enhanced by final interviews in which coaches critically reflected upon their own usage. Taken together, these methods and findings demonstrate a holistic and human-centered approach to evaluating the performance of one AI tool by better understanding the experiences of its users. Poster 7: Using LLMs to support responsive teaching Hillary Swanson, Ha Nguyen, Idris Solola, Rida Munir, Ravi Sinha Responsive teaching refers to a family of pedagogical strategies that pursue student thinking (Levin et al., 2013). It “begins with watching and listening to” students and then builds on their thinking, guiding them to refine the resources they bring to their learning into more formal understanding (Robertson et al., 2016, xiii). Responsive teaching supports learning (Radoff et al., 2018) and increases motivation (Gray et al., 2022). Despite its many benefits, it remains challenging for teachers to implement. A particularly well-known challenge is the so-called “constructivist’s dilemma,” where teachers have difficulty deciding which of the many student ideas to pursue (Prawat & Floden, 1994).

To address this and other challenges in our work with middle school science teachers, we turn to generative AI (GenAI), in particular large language models (LLMs) and multimodal models (MLLMs). With their huge processing power, access to data, and natural language capabilities, MLLMs like Claude (Anthropic, 2025) make the task of responding to individual student ideas —including their written explanations and multimodal learning artifacts such as drawings and models —not only possible, but generative for learning (Nguyen & Park, 2025). We have developed prompts that guide MLLMs to assess student work and provide individualized feedback to students, teachers, and researchers.

Through a codesign approach (Penuel, 2019) in the context of a research-practice partnership (RPP; Coburn et al., 2013) with two middle school science teachers, we are developing tools for student-,  teacher-, and researcherfacing feedback on student work. Our tools employ Claude to identify ideas in student work that might serve as resources (Hammer, 2000) for the construction of more formal scientific knowledge. In student-facing feedback, Claude asks students to elaborate on these ideas, prompting their  refinement. In teacher-facing feedback, it identifies student resources and suggests what scientific concepts they might undergird. In researcher-facing feedback, it visualizes student resources and compares them with experts’ knowledge models, supporting researchers’ analysis.

We will present initial prototypes for student -, teacher-, and researcher-facing feedback. Our findings show that Claude can identify resources in student thinking and follow up in meaningful ways. For example, in the context of a modeling activity meant to teach conservation of energy, one 8th grade student explained the changing energy of a roller coaster as: “The potential energy is slowly going down and the kinetic energy is going up.” Claude analyzed the student’s thinking, noting: “Strong qualitative pattern, but lacks explicit reference to energy conservation.” This shows that Claude has identified this reasoning as a seed of scientific thinking, it just needs to be refined to connect with the law of conservation of energy. It then prompts the student  to refine the idea: “If PE goes down and KE goes up, what happens to the total?” “Can energy disappear, or is it changing form?” This question validates the student’s thinking and then prompts them to refine it through questions that explicitly reference conservation phenomena and support responsive teaching. 2313

## Poster 8: LLMs integrated into discussions

Sebastian Andreas Simon, Ban Mouid Shiwalia and Guido Makransky Generative Artificial Intelligence, in the form of LLMs, has triggered a wave of new promising results in individual learning setups (Makransky et al., 2025). Recently their versatility has been identified as promising feature for collaborative learning setups (Cress & Kimmerle, 2023).The complexity of collaborative learning processes and outcomes (cognitive, social, collaborative) has birthed the subfield of collaborative analytics within CSCL (Martinez-Maldonado et al., 2021). In this contribution, we present first observations from a pilot investigating how generative AI can assist collaborative learning processes through a threefold motivation of human-centered theory, practice and technology.

From a theoretical perspective, we build on Collaborative Cognitive Load Theory (CCLT; Kirschner et al., 2018), which highlights that while collaboration enables learners to share cognitive effort, it also introduces needs for coordination that increase extraneous load. AI agents capable of listening and interacting during group work could mitigate this by offering timely clarification, focusing attention, and sustaining dialogue when teachers are unavailable. For teachers, who face orchestration load as th ey monitor multiple groups, such systems could act as facilitators, handling minor requests, detecting misunderstandings, and maintaining group dynamics. Recent LLMs exhibit potential to interpret conversational flow, infer intent, and respond contextually, thus paving the way towards dynamic group scripting systems that adapt rather than follow fixed scaffolds (Doherty et al., 2025). These perspectives motivate a scheduled AI study in a high school Psychology course, building on observations from a pilot study. We describe these observations and provide insights into the scheduled study design. Context and overview of methods used for evaluation of this AI tool or curriculum According to CCLT, high cognitive load can arise when groups rely on randomness-as-genesis strategies-cognitively expensive trial-and-error approaches to generate and test possible solutions. Here, an AI mediator can help regulate the process, maintaining cognitive efficiency by prompting structure or recalling relevant theoretical concepts.

In both pilot and scheduled study we expect these situations to occur due to activity design features of complexity, e.g., positive interdependence (Laal, 2013). In the control condition, students rely on their own resources or teacher assistance, while AI-assisted groups candirectly request help from the AI. In the pilot, students indeed prompted the AI either for direct solutions or for hints about solution strategies. For the scheduled study, we plan to analyze conversation data to identify “blocked” situations (e.g., random exploration) and observe whether AI intervention leads to an “unblocked” state (e.g., improved conceptual alignment or theoretical grounding). Coding will build on CSCL frameworks such as MOSAIC (Hoang et al., 2024), comparing AI-supported sequences to similar patterns in control groups. Additionally, drawing on Transactive Memory Systems (TMS) theory, we examine how groups offload, retrieve, or repair shared knowledge through AI. We will code mechanisms such as memory offloading (delegating recall to AI), efficient retrieval (AI provides contextual cues), and repair of forgetting (AI reminds who contributed missing information). We hypothesize that AI assistance will reduce extraneous cognitive load and lead to higher learning outcomes in post-tests.

## Initial findings, pilot results, and/or next directions

In our pilot study, five groups of four students played the collaborative game Keep Talking and Nobody Explodes for 45 minutes, assisted by an AI with real-time transcription and speaker diarization. Students could query the AI about game mechanics or request strategic help.

We observed the AI’s integration into group dialogue, despite a 3– 5 second response delay and occasional bugs, with several instances of group unblocking following AI interaction. Notably, when facing memory-based challenges, groups attempted to incorporat e the AI as part of their transactive memory system, asking it to recall earlier utterances. Although this was hindered by system latency and time pressure, the pattern suggests an emerging collaborative adaptation to AI.

Both the unblocking and memory offloading patterns will be systematically examined in the scheduled study to better understand how generative AI can meaningfully support collaboration and reduce cognitive load in group learning contexts.

Across the eight studies presented in this symposium, a coherent narrative emerges about what it means to evaluate and partner with artificial intelligence in the learning sciences. Rather than treating evaluation as a post-hoc 2314 measure of success, the collective work here positions it as a generative, iterative process—an engine for learning, design refinement, and reflection. Each project demonstrates that evaluating AI systems in education is inseparable from understanding how they reshape relationships among teachers, students, and technologies. A central thread running through these contributions is the commitment to human-centered evaluation. Whether examining AI partners like CoBi and Talk Trees, adaptive orchestration tools, or large language models supporting responsive teaching, the studies share a concern with how AI can augment rather than replace the social fabric of learning. These tools were co-designed with educators and learners, foregrounding the local contexts, values, and goals of their communities. This attention to context reveals  that AI’s value lies not in its autonomy but in its ability to sustain human agency, foster belonging, and scaffold collaboration. A second theme concerns methodological innovation. Across posters, researchers extended and blended mixed-methods approaches—ranging from discourse analytics and practical measures of student experience to qualitative video analysis and interview analysis—to capture the nuanced interplay between learning processes and AI mediation. These approaches underscore that evaluating AI tools requires tracing both micro-level interactions (such as student talk, assessment achievement and metacognitive reflection) and macro-level change (such as shifts in classroom norms or teacher orchestration practices). Together, these methodological advances move the field toward richer and more equitable evaluation frameworks that honor learning as socially situated and dynamically evolving.

Finally, the symposium highlights an emerging ethical and professional imperative: to cultivate researchers’, educators’ and students’ critical AI literacy. Participants in these studies were not passive users of technology but active interpreters—questioning algorithmic feedback, recognizing bias, and repurposing tools for locally meaningful ends. These projects reimagine professional learning and research-practice partnerships as sites where AI itself becomes an object of inquiry, critique, and collaborat ive authorship. Taken together, these contributions help form a paradigm for evaluating AI in education—one that values equity, transparency, and human flourishing as rigorously as it values technical performance. Partnering with AI, in this sense, is not only a technological act but also an epistemic and ethical one: a collective commitment to shaping futures where human intelligence is leveraged to mitigate harms and maximize the potential of artificial intelligence towards the broader purposes of equity, justice, and critical agency.
