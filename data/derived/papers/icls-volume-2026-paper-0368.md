# AI/ML Education in Motion: Embodied Machine Learning with Wearable Technology in Informal Contexts

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Systematic reviews on the application of AI technologies in STEM Education recommend the use of age-appropriate AI/ML tools in primary and secondary school settings, in contexts that appeal to students’ interests. In this pilot we incorporate a new modality (body movement) and tool (BBC micro:bit) into an AI/ML unit for young learners to understand how they engage with machine learning concepts. Students articulated novel uses for ML tools, drew connections to other practices, demonstrated metacognitive awareness, and described their roles as designers. These findings highlight the value of scaffolded, collaborative, embodied learning designs in supporting emerging AI literacy.

## Theoretical and conceptual framework

Embracing a worldview that values epistemological pluralism, we draw upon the tenets of Constructionism (Papert & Harel, 1991)  and Embodied Learning (Abrahamson & Lindgren, 2022)  to conceptualize our design. Key ideas from constructionist theory include that children learn more effectively through the creation of tangible, publicly sharable objects that bring their personal ideas to life (Papert, 2020), while acknowledging diverse ways of knowing (Bang & Medin, 2010). Embodiment is the theoretical perspective that learning is distributed across the brain, body, and environment  (Anderson, 2003). Wearable devices that capture motion provide a unique interface for incubating learning opportunities enabl ing users to engage in physical movement while generating data that can then be used to train ML models. Thus, the act of collecting data is coincidental with and indistinguishable from the movement, in this case playing a sport. ## Research design and methods

In this iteration, we include d a wearable device to detect motion  the BBC:  micro:bit (Figure 1),  a compact yet powerful wearable device that incorporates multiple sensors, including an accelerometer. The motion data is represented via live visualizations of the accelerometer sensor in space on the training page of the connected web platform (Figure 2). These visualizations help learners make the connection between their own movements and the data generated. We implemented this study at a non-profit tennis academy in the Southeast U.S. that serves underprivileged youth. The study was held over three days (9am to 12 noon) in  a playroom at a public parks facility where the participants trained to play competitive tennis.  A total of 12 participants joined the study (11 to 14 years  old; 5 boys and 7 girls). The participants were placed in two groups, and the unit was implemented with each group  on consecutive days  (Timeline in Figure 3). We collected pre-post survey data (2 open-ended, 8 multiple-choice) and student artifacts (ML models). On the third day, we conducted semi-structured interviews (~30 min each) with two participants from each group (pseudonyms: Angie, Sage, Sally, Vicky). The sampling was driven by logistical constraints and no compensation was given. The interviews were recorded, and the transcripts Figure 2 Visualization of the Motion Training Data Figure 1 BBC: Microbit 2530 were uploaded into Atlas (a coding software) and inductive thematic analysis methods were applied (Braun & Clarke, 2021). We sought to discover, describe, and answer the following research questions:

• How do our learners  develop an understanding of ML concepts through embodied interactions  with

physical computing tools to construct ML models?

• What understandings do students demonstrate about the processes of training and learning in ML

systems?

Figure 3 Timeline of The Unit The average score for the 12 learners on ML items (maximum = 7) increased from 4.17 (SD = 1.67, Range: 1-6) to 5.58 (SD = 1.19, Range: 4-7). A Wilcoxon signed-rank test indicated that campers' change in test scores was significant (W = 0, p < 0.05). The modeling targets involved different domains (tennis, basketball, and dance). In response to our prompts, and acknowledging our small sample, several  tentative themes emerged from the inductive analysis of interview transcripts. Physical engagement with the micro:bit, using their own movements to generate training data and evaluating the ML models was key to constructing understanding of the ML modeling process. The embodied data collection process aided with  visualizations of the motion  helped them understand the role of quality training data and appreciate the use of sensors that capture  data from the physical world Embodied interactions and direct manipulation of devices provided access to complex technology, and students viewed ML modeling as something tangible they could build, modify, and test themselves. As Sage described it, “...I feel like exploring, like, oh, we cando this, we cando that ”.  Although their language was sometimes informal, all students described ML as a process of input-modeling-output, aligning with core ideas of supervised learning. Vicky offered  “Maybe do like even more trials to try to get it as accurate as possible ” as a potential solution to improving model accuracy.  Students imagined novel uses for ML tools in their own lives. Sa ge envisioned a singing coach and a  cooking assistant (“to count whisk movements while baking”), the former utilizing a completely different micro:bit sensor (sound) tha n she experienced. Angie  suggested applying the technology to build a jump-rope counter, and as a training tool to improve handwriting and drawing/sketching skills. Sally proposed a Just Dance-style training app that could be used to correct dance steps in learners.  Such examples reflect the creative transfer of ML learning  into personally meaningful contexts  to imagine new uses cases. These find ings underscore the importance of allowing students to move from structured tasks  in familiar contexts (e.g., tennis movement recognition) toward open-ended exploration, which can foster ownership and identity as technology creators.  One learner also drew connections between the ML modeling process and other disciplines with Sally likening the training phase to multiple experiments in a science lab and act of building ML models to what engineers do. While c omparing the pros and cons of an  AI tennis coach vs  a human, although students saw value in AI’s capabilities (accurate, tireless worker), they remained cautious about its limitations. Sage argued, “AI can be good, but humans can also be good… AI and human at the same time,” emphasizing the importance of human emotional intelligence and adaptability, particularly in domains such as coaching where psychological support is critical. This nuanced view  recognizes both the capabilities and the limitations of AI, suggesting that even brief interventions can prompt critical thinking about AI/ML technology. This emerging criticality around AI usage and capabilities can support learners in imagining and  designing new use cases  and tools using this technology.

The findings suggest that informal, interest-driven spaces with tools that enable embodied human-computer interactions may offer fertile ground for introducing complex ideas like ML in accessible, student-centered ways. By designing and building real ML models, collaborating with peers, and contextualizing their tools in everyday life, students demonstrated early but authentic forms of AI literacy. Designing for embodied  learning, creative freedom and autonomy  can result in  meaningful engagement with complex technologies of AI/ML in young learners.
