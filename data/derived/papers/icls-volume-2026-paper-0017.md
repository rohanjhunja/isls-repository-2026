# Theoretical Construction and Case Study of Temporal Learning Analytics Based on Learning Engagement Dynamics Framework

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Temporal learning analytics faces challenges of lacking systematic theoretical guidance and "black-box" feature extraction. This study proposes the learning engagement dynamics (LED) framework, decomposing digital learning behavior into three complementary, cognitively grounded dimensions: cognitive temporal regulation (CTR), task engagement dynamics (TED), and information exploration and integration (IEI), enabling a transparent mapping from clickstream data to cognitive constructs. In a theory-driven case study, we derived 16 temporal features and analyzed 837,144 interactions from 117 middle school students in AI-literacy modules using cross-classified nested random-effects models. Findings: (1) CTR (behavioral regularity) shows significant associations with cognitive engagement; (2) TED signals relate positively to accuracy on structured tasks, suggesting the value of sustained deep engagement; and (3) IEI exhibits task-specific patterns, systematic exploration associates with improved accuracy, while broader exploration relates to potential interference. LED provides a theoretical foundation and preliminary empirical insights for personalizing intelligent learning platforms.

The rapid development of online learning platforms has opened new horizons for educational research, as these digital learning environments continuously record every interaction between learners and content, tools, and tasks, generating massive amounts of time-series log data (Romero & Ventura, 2020; Lyu et al., 2026). In assessment-learning integrated courses, where learning activities and assessment processes are tightly intertwined, learners receive continuous assessment while completing tasks to consolidate knowledge (Hernández, 2012). At the same time, they face challenges associated with self-paced study, knowledge construction, and progressively structured tasks (Tabuenca et al., 2015). This context heightens the need to understand the temporal patterns of students’ digital learning behaviors. Temporal learning analytics, as an important branch of learning analytics, is dedicated to revealing the dynamic changes and inherent patterns of behavioral patterns in the learning process, and attempts to uncover the underlying cognitive mechanisms (Sun et al., 2023).  Research shows that successful learners often demonstrate more regular learning behavioral patterns and better time management abilities (Kizilcec et al., 2017). Recent advances in temporal learning analytics have revealed the complexity of learning rhythms, including multiple dimensions such as sustained engagement capacity, engagement stability, and deep engagement frequency  (Goh, 2025; Li & Xing, 2025;  Sun et al., 2023). Despite the increasingly rich temporal data and the widely recognized analytical value, current temporal learning analytics methods still face significant theoretical and methodological challenges. First, there is a lack of systematic theoretical frameworks to guide the extraction of temporally interpretable features with cognitive explanatory power from raw log sequences (Saqr et al., 2019). Most existing research adopts exploratory data mining approaches (Papamitsiou & Economides, 2014; Gibson & de Freitas, 2016), which, while capable of discovering interesting behavioral patterns, lack tight alignment with mature theories such as self-regulated learning, cognitive load, and knowledge construction, resulting in limited interpretability and transferability of features (Baker & Yacef, 2009). Second, existing methods often treat temporal behavior as a single-dimensional phenomenon, ignoring the complex interactions of learning behavior across multiple dimensions such as cognitive temporal regulation, task engagement dynamics, and information exploration integration (Viberg et al., 2020).

Thus, we propose the learning engagement dynamics (LED) theoretical framework. This framework contains three mutually complementary core dimensions: The c ognitive temporal regulation (CTR) dimension, based on self-regulated learning theory (Zimmerman, 2002) and metacognitive theory (Flavell, 1979), focuses on learners' rhythm control, metacognitive monitoring, and attention management abilities along the time axis (Daumiller & Dresel, 2019); The t ask engagement dynamics (TED) dimension, based on multidimensional engagement theory (Fredricks et al., 2004) and flow theory (Csikszentmihalyi & LeFevre, 1989), describes learners' deep engagement potential, engagement stability, and sustained engagement capacity (Li,..., & Oh, 2025); The i nformation exploration integration (IEI) dimension, based on information processing theory and 91 cognitive load theory, measures learners' exploration strategy efficiency, cognitive load management, and knowledge integration abilities (Curum & Khedo, 2021).

To preliminarily explore and apply the theory proposed, we conducted a case study based on a developed "Artificial Intelligence in Mathematics" online learning course module, collecting student clickstream logs. Based on the LED framework, we developed a complete indicator system containing 16 temporal features, achieving the translation from raw clickstream data to dynamic learning engagement, providing an operational methodological pathway for theory-driven indicator construction. Specifically, this paper addresses the following research questions (RQs):

1. How to construct a theoretical framework for temporal analysis to guide feature extraction based on log

time series?

2. Based on the case study of students learning AI in this paper, what is the relationship between students'

temporal cognitive features and the accuracy of closed-ended question responses?

3. Based on the case study of students learning AI in this paper, what is the relationship between students'

temporal cognitive features and students' self-reported cognitive engagement?

## Related work

Temporal learning analytics has its theoretical foundation rooted in multiple important theories from cognitive science and educational psychology. Self-regulated learning theory provides an important framework for understanding learners' time management and strategy regulation (Zimmerman, 2002), emphasizing learners' active role in goal setting, progress monitoring, and behavioral adjustment. Recent research has further revealed the complexity of self-regulated learning in digital environments, particularly the temporal dynamic characteristics of metacognitive monitoring and strategy regulation (Aksela et al., 2024; Decormeille et al., 2025). Metacognitive theory (Flavell, 2024) reveals learners' monitoring and regulation mechanisms of their own cognitive processes, which manifest as observable features such as revisiting behavior, dwell time changes, and strategy adjustments in digital learning environments.  Cognitive load theory (Sweller, 1988) provides important insights for understanding learners' information processing capabilities in complex tasks, particularly the allocation of cognitive resources during multi-source information exploration and integration processes. Research indicates that cognitive load plays an important role in digital learning env ironments (Skulmowski & Xu, 2022). In terms of methodological development, early temporal learning analytics primarily relied on descriptive statistical methods, focusing on basic temporal indicators such as learning duration and access frequency (Baker & Yacef, 2009). With the development of data mining techniques, researchers began adopting more complex methods, including time series clustering (Boroujeni et al., 2016), sequential pattern mining (Chen et al., 2024), and Markov chain modeling (Moreno-Marcos et al., 2020), attempting to discover hidden behavioral patterns from large-scale learning logs. In recent years, deep learning techniques such as LSTM and recurrent neural networks have been widely applied to learning trajectory modeling and prediction (Piech et al., 2015), while process mining techniques have also been used to extract learning process models from event logs (Bannert et al., 2015; Li,..., & Woodhead, 2025).

Although these methods have achieved good results, there are certain deficiencies in theoretical explanatory power. Existing methods are mostly data-driven exploratory analyses that, while capable of identifying statistically significant behavioral patterns, lack systematic frameworks that align closely with cognitive science theories to guide feature extraction (Winne, 2017). This results in discovered patterns that are sometimes difficult to associate with known learning mechanisms, and the feature extraction process often relies on researchers' experience, lacking systematic theoretical guidance (Gašević et al., 2015). More critically, existing studies' extracted temporal features lacking clear correspondence with specific cognitive processes (such as metacognitive monitoring, attention allocation, strategy regulation, etc.).

## Learning Engagement Dynamics (LED) theoretical framework

To address the systematic challenges of temporal learning analytics in mathematics learning environments, we propose the l earning engagement dynamics (LED) theoretical framework. Built on the characteristics of log-sequence data in digital learning environments, LED offers an initial analytic paradigm that combines theoretical depth with methodological practicality for temporal analyses. ## Case study

AI literacy education AI literacy education, as an emerging branch of STEM education, poses unique and complex challenges for self-regulated learning. In AI learning, learners need to establish connections between abstract mathematical concepts and concrete computational applications, and this cross-domain knowledge integration process demands cognitive regulation strategies. AI learning tasks often have high cognitive load and multi-level complexity, requiring learners to simultaneously engage in conceptual understanding, model  construction, and tool operation, which demands strong multitasking coordination and cognitive resource management capabilities (Li, Xing, & Adhami, 2025).

Our course module serves as a supplement to the U.S. middle  school Algebra 1 curriculum, providing self-paced AI literacy education for American middle school students. The course combines abstract algebraic concepts with AI sentiment analysis model construction through five progressive learning modules (approximately 250 minutes total), embodying the characteristics of STEM integrated learning and self-paced learning educational practices. This study collected fine-grained interaction log data from 117 students in this course (with 108 students in accuracy analysis and 79 students in engagement analysis after excluding incomplete data), totaling 837,144 records, providing an empirical foundation for temporal analysis. Each learning project includes multiple questions (specific statistical descriptions are provided in the results section), with questions primarily being closed-ended, along with some open-ended questions, following a progressive design principle. After completing the questions in each module, students filled out self-reported measures of cognitive engagement. We collected timestamped student clickstream logs, focusing on statistical analysis of self-regulated learning behavioral patterns when students read the current question and read instructional materials from other questions. 93 This approach extends prior work that has validated automated assessment frameworks using click-based engagement metrics (Li,..., & Li, 2025). These behavioral patterns can be described as temporal clickstream behaviors involving multiple browsings of other content sections and returning to the current question, reflecting the complex dynamic process of information exploration and integration. Each assessment-learning integration module typically comprises more than thirty questions, sequenced so that knowledge points and difficulty progress in a logically scaffolded manner. Before submitting an answer to any given question, students may navigate to other questions or to supplement al materials; the platform’s interaction logs record the position and type of each such action (e.g., click or swipe). The latencies between consecutive actions constitute a clickstream time series. For each focal (target) question, we isolate the segment of the clickstream that begins when the learner first starts browsing the target (as indicated by a click or swipe) and ends when the answer to that target is submitted. This segment may include one or more cycles in which the learner departs to other questions or readings (off-target) and then returns to the target before answering. As noted in the Introduction, this pattern is characteristic of assessment-learning contexts and is informative about students’ learning strategies, particularly their self-regulation and information-processing capacities. Based on the LED framework, this research adopts a theory-driven feature engineering approach, we categorize learning behaviors into two core action scopes: target question cognitive engagement (target) and external information exploration (off-target). Th is division reflects the dynamic balance between focused cognitive processing and divergent information seeking in digital learning environments.

The LED framework's nine abstract behavioral indicators are systematically operationalized into 16 concrete features in Table 2. One CTR indicator (micro-adjustments) was not operationalized due to clickstream granularity limitations. Their distribution across the three theoretical dimensions follows the LED design philosophy, in which different dimensions of clickstream time series are transformed into theoretically meaningful statistical values: CTR includes 2 features (metacognitive monitoring and strategy complexity); TED includes 6 94 features (depth, stability, frequency, maximum, and central tendency); IEI includes 8 features (frequency, depth, stability, complexity, and scope). Based on the theoretical guidance from previous sections, for temporal features extracted from student clickstream logs, we apply cross-classified (students × modules) and nested (items within modules) random-effects models to handle complex hierarchical data and evaluate how features predict students' response quality in assessment-learning integrated projects. As outlined in the research questions, we focus on two core dependent variables (extendable to similar projects).  Closed-ended question accuracy (binary).  Measures knowledge mastery and problem-solving efficiency in structured tasks, coded as 0 (incorrect) and 1 (correct). Grouping variables for random effects include student ID (user_id), module ID (module_id), and question ID (question_id), forming a three-level cross-classified nested structure. Cognitive engagement (continuous).  Based on a post-project scale item: "Because of this activity, I started to think a lot about math and artificial intelligence." Labels are mapped to approximately interval values for modeling:  Strongly agree  = 2,  Agree = 1, Neutral = 0,  Disagree = - 1, Strongly disagree  = -2. Independent variables are project-level aggregates (mean behavioral features per student-project). Controls are simplified to user_id and module_id. Given cross-classified and nested structures, traditional fixed-effects models cannot properly account for dependencies. Mixed-effects models separate student, project, and item differences to yield more robust fixed-effects estimates. Key considerations: (1) Cross-classified structure: students engage in multiple projects and vice versa, requiring random effects at student (user_id) and module (module_id) levels. (2) Nested items: questions are nested within modules (question_id within module_id); question-level random effects (e.g., item_in_project) account for shared difficulty/content. All 16 temporal behavioral features from the LED framework are variance-standardized. For  binary outcomes,  we fit cross-classified multilevel logistic regression (GLMM, logit link) to accommodate binomial outcomes while modeling fixed effects (behavioral features) and random  effects (individual/item differences), avoiding underestimated standard errors from ignored dependencies. For  continuous outcomes  (engagement), we fit cross-classified multilevel linear models (LMM) under approximate normality, decomposing fixed vs. random contributions and clarifying direct effects and moderating individual differences. The CTR dimension, based on self-regulated learning theory (Zimmerman, 2002) and metacognitive theory (Flavell, 2024), found that the strong effect of current_question_visit_frequency on cognitive engagement (βstd = 0.6148) validates theoretical expectations from Winne and Hadwin (2008) regarding the central role of metacognitive monitoring in self-regulated learning. Frequently entering and maintaining scroll cycles above individual average levels reflects stable behavioral rhythms and sustained attention capacity, consistent with core viewpoints from time cognition theory (Block & Zakay, 1997) about temporal regulation abilities. Meanwhile, the negative effect of current_focus_durations_entropy (β std = -0.3857) further supports metacognitive theory's viewpoint about the importance of strategy stability, where excessive behavioral complexity may lead to cognitive resource dispersion and reduce the quality of deep thinking. The TED dimension, based on multidimensional engagement theory (Fredricks et al., 2004) and flow theory (Csikszentmihalyi & LeFevre, 1989), found that the positive effect of current_question_max_session_duration in closed-ended questions validates theoretical expectations about the importance of deep engagement for structured cognitive tasks. This aligns with flow theory's core viewpoint that focused states promote high-quality learning. The IEI dimension indicates that, in closed-ended tasks, systematic revisiting (captured by scroll_out_in_frequency) is beneficial, whereas overly broad exploration (higher exploration_breadth) is detrimental to accuracy.

From the perspective of analysis results for AI and STEAM learning platform design, this study provides empirical evidence and operational design strategies for personalized learning systems and intelligent educational interventions. Based on CTR dimension findings, learning platforms should focus on cultivating and monitoring learners' behavioral rhythmicity. The strong predictive value of current_question_visit_frequency  indicates that platforms can predict cognitive engagement by real-time monitoring of learners' behavioral regularity. When detecting overly random or irregular behavioral patterns, systems should provide appropriate rhythm regulation prompts and metacognitive strategy training. Meanwhile, when current_focus_durations_entropy increases abnormally, platforms should trigger interface simplification and cognitive load regulation mechanisms to help learners refocus attention. Based on TED dimension findings, platforms should leverage temporal engagement signals to trigger just-in-time focus supports; for closed-ended learning tasks, the positive effect of current_question_max_session_duration indicates that platforms should create technical environments conducive to deep focus, supporting learners in entering and maintaining deep engagement states through reducing irrelevant distractions, optimizing interface design, and providing focus modes.  Based on IEI dimension task-specific scroll_out_in_frequency is low, systems should encourage learners to engage in systematic information revisiting and knowledge confirmation; when exploration_breadth is too high, systems should provide focused guidance to help learners avoid cognitive resource dispersion.

Several limitations qualify these findings. First, effect size considerations: While statistically significant, behavioral features explain limited practical variance (marginal R²: 0.5% for accuracy, 5% for engagement), with random effects dominating. This suggests modest real-world predictive utility despite theoretical meaningfulness. Second, measurement limitations: Cognitive engagement relies on a single self-report item, limiting construct validity compared to validated multi-item scales. Third, methodological constraints: As an exploratory study, we did not apply multiple-testing corrections, systematic feature selection, or extensive multicollinearity diagnostics, warranting cautious interpretation of individual coefficients. Our operationalization choices (entropy binning, kurtosis thresholds) represent initial specifications requiring sensitivity analysis in future work. Fourth, design limitations: The correlational approach precludes causal inference about whether behavioral patterns drive outcomes or reflect individual differences. Fifth, generalizability concerns: Findings are based on a single AI literacy course with American middle school students; cross-cultural, cross-age, and cross-disciplinary validation is needed. Finally, theoretical scope: The LED framework remains exploratory, requiring deeper development of dimensional interactions and broader validation. We acknowledge the tension between 'measuring what we value' versus 'valuing what we can easily measure', our clickstream focus captures observable patterns but may miss cognitive processes not reflected in navigation behaviors. This study constructed the Learning Engagement Dynamics (LED) theoretical framework, successfully achieving systematic mapping from temporal clickstream data to cognitive constructs, providing theoretical contributions and methodological innovations for te mporal learning analytics. Based on empirical analysis of AI learning projects, findings revealed the strong predictive effect of behavioral regularity on deep cognitive engagement and the task-specific characteristics of information exploration strategies.
