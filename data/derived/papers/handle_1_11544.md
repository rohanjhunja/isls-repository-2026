# Designing a Translanguaging Learning Environment with Multimodal Technologies that Afford Multisensory Meaning Making

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This proposal presents the design of a translanguaging learning environment. It integrates multimodal technologies, including a haptic glove and an interactive computer visualization, to deliver a multisensory experience. It contributes to translanguaging pedagogy by leveraging fluid language practices with multimodal learning technology, enabling English Language Learners (ELLs) to utilize their full linguistic and sensory resources to make sense of the science concept.

## Introduction

With growing immigration, 5.3 million students in U.S. public schools are English Language Learners (E LLs) (NCES). This large population of ELL students highlights an  urgent demand for effective support in science learning, where language barriers can hinder comprehension of complex concepts and limit access to educational opportunities (Fang, 2006).

As a practical theory of language, translanguaging offers both pedagogical and theoretical approaches to address this challenge. Theoretically, translanguaging is understood as the use of one’s entire linguistic repertoire, unconstrained by socially or politicall y defined language labels. It reimagines language a s a multilingual, multisensory, and multimodal resource for meaning making (Li, 2017). Pedagogically, translanguaging practice extends beyond simple oral code-switching; it embraces  the multimodal nature of meaning  making (Lee, 2021). Translanguaging pedagogy involv es teachers actively encouraging students to use their full linguistic a nd multimodal repertoire to make sense of the content-to-belearnt. This approach empowers students as meaning-makers who use a broad array of multimodal tools—visual, auditory, and gestural—to engage with scientific concepts (Poza, 2017). Despite efforts to integrate translanguaging into s cience education, most current  translanguaging literature focuses on K-12 populations in formal classroom settings and favors a linguistic focus  such as code-switching (Hou et al., 2024; Gallagher and Colohan 2014). In addition, these studies primarily used ethnographic approaches (Prilutskaya, 2021). A subset of the translanguaging studies conceptualize multimoda lity as the use of both linguistic and non-linguistic modalities for successful learning and c ommunication (García & Li, 2014; Lee, 2021). For example, Williams (2020) explored how emergent bilingual students draw on their multimodal resources, such as gestures and manipulation of physical models, to co-construct meaning. Despite the merits this type of study holds, physical models  have distracting features such as color or texture (Utta l et al., 2009). Multimodal learning technologies complement this approach by delivering multisensory feedback, supporting the multisensory meaning making of the science content.

Therefore, in this design, we aimed to build upon translanguaging pedagogy by designing an inclusive translanguaging learning environment  that incorporates fluid language practice of instruction  and multimodal learning technologies. The learning technologies include a haptic glove that produces guided haptic feedback and an interactive computer visualization. The combination of linguistic, visual, and haptic resources together affords students’ linguistic and multisensory meaning making.

## Problem context and project objectives

The problem context is Resting Membrane Potential (RMP), which involves the balance of two influences for a cell to reach a state of dynamic equilibrium. Prior research found that students tend to only notice one influence– –chemical driving force ––while neglecting the other ––electrical driving force (Lira, 2020).  We chose this problem because: (a) it offers a context rich in representations, encompassing both the aggregate diffusion of ions and the individual interactions between ions; and (b) it presents a semantic challenge due to complex terminology. This combination of representational and linguistic complexity makes it an ideal context for exploring how ELLs use multimodal resources to make sense of scientific concepts with the aid of multimodal technology. The goals of this design project were twofold: (1) Creating a translanguaging learning environment with multimodal learning technology and fluid language instruction  that allows ELLs to draw on their full linguistic and multisensory resources while learning  advanced scientific concepts; (2) Using multimodal technology, including haptic feedback and computer visualization  to emphasize various aspects of RMP  and help ELLs develop a deeper understanding and more accurate interpretations of RMP. Learner’s population and design principles The United States hosted approximately 290,086 Chine se students in its universities (IIEODR). The increasing number of Chinese college-level ELLs underscores the importance of providing effective support in science education. So, the target learner population for this proposed design is Chinese international college students. We designed a multimodal learning environment that  incorporates an interactive computer simulation that visualizes individual ions and their movements, and a haptic glove that p roduces guided haptic feedback (Figure 1). This learning environment affords multimodal meaning making via multisensory feedback. Visual Sensory: The computer simulation visually represents the interaction between individual ions and their aggregate movement, providing a “glassbox” approach by allowing students to visualize the science concept and focus on both individual and aggregate level of the phenomenon (Sengupta & Wilensky, 2009). Additionally, the simulation has interactive features and allows students to embody a designated ion with their hand. The hand tracking is accomplished with the Leap Motion controller, and the designated ion is being controlled by students moving their hand towards the screen.

Haptic Sensory: Embodied enactment has been viewed as a form of translanguaging to facilitate learning (Tai & Li, 2023). To support students’ embodiment of the sci ence content, we augment the interactive visualization with a haptic glove that produces guided haptic feedback. The glove varies the direction and intensity of the haptic feedback based on the user's hand pos ition within the simulation, allowing students to "feel" the different sensations that represent an ion's embodied experience and prompting them to coordinate this haptic sensory with the visual representation. Additionally, prior research found that when learni ng about RMP, haptic feedback prompts students to recognize the electric al driving force they had previously overlooked and  to represent this influence through gesture (Tang et al., 2023). Thus, haptic feedback  draws students' attention to invisible forces, helping them develop a more comprehensive understanding of the phenomenon. Instruction with fluid language practice: The researcher will act as an instructor to support ELLs in exploring and interpreting the multimodal technology. This will involve scaffolding their interpretation of multisensory feedback and guiding them in exploring the represented scientific phenomena through a semistructured protocol. It has been demonstrated that both teacher and students prefer a more flexible use of language to make sense of the science concept (Kiramba, 2016). Thus, the language practices by the instructor are best described as the fluid use of English and Chinese according to the demand of communicative interaction. ## Implications

Translanguaging theory highlights the multimodality and multisensory nature of meaning making, but few studies leverage translanguaging pedagogy  with multimodal learning technology that affords mu ltisensory feedback. Combining fluid language practice with multimodal l earning technology contributes to the current translanguaging pedagogy by emphasizing the multisensory nature of meaning m aking via external tools that produce haptic and visual  feedback. In addition, this design has the potential to foste r an inclusive environment for students from socially diverse communities (Lin  & He, 2017) and enhance their equitable  access to science education by validating their linguistic backgrounds and affording them to use their full semiotic resources.
