# Developing a Boundary Practice for Collaborative Task Design in a Design-Centric Research–practice Partnership

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Collaborative learning needs tasks suitable for collaboration. As part of a project to study the design of collaborative environments, we describe how a partnership with an undergraduate mathematics professor led to the development of a boundary practice for collaborative task design. Initially there was a tension between the researchers’ preference for ill-structured tasks and the professor’s priority of conceptual over computational problems. We discuss how focus on a single task resolved the tension and gave both researchers and the professor useful design tools for meeting their respective goals in subsequent task revisions.

## Introduction

The design of a collaborative task is critical to the success of collaborative learning. Here we explore the role of the partnership with a professor in the development of task design conjectures (Sandoval, 2013) and the iterations on task design for collaborative tasks in an underg raduate mathematics class. Design-centric research– practice partnerships (RPPs) are characterized by tacit, conflicting motives (Kali et al., 2018). Developing a shared vision by surfacing contradictions is essential to create new practices for the collaborators (i.e., “Boundary Practices,” Akkerman and Bakker, 2011). We describe how the tas k design iterations allowed for engagement within t he boundary space to create an understanding of the learning goals for this course and the collaborative tasks.

## Setting, design, and methods

We developed five collaborative tasks to be used for remote learning in an undergraduate applied linear algebra course.  The team began work 5 months before the se mester began. After 2 months, we began meeting week ly with the professor to discuss task iterations. A si gnificant shift (henceforth “breakthrough”) occurre d in our thinking 1 month before the start of the semester while discussing revisions to the Week 5 task, leading to revisions of the three existing tasks and a streamlined design process for the remaining two that continued into the semester. Data were notes from meetings and our task iteratio ns. We constructed descriptions of our design conjectures for collaborative task design before, during, and after the breakthrough. Then we connected them in a narrative of the significant elements of our process that contributed to the occurrence of the breakthrough.

## Findings

Initial designs Our initial work was based on prior work on engineering tasks (Shehab & Mercier, 2017), in which ill-structured problems played a central role. Accordingly, we ini tially designed activities around an authentic, ill-structured task.  We conjectured that many solution pathways w ould encourage the group to take up members’ propos als, while the need to agree on a solution would incenti vize moves to find agreement (Fernández et al., 200 1). A scenario and visualization were used to create authenticity and offer multiple layers of meaning to be used to come up with solution ideas. Each task had structured su bparts that were intended to prepare students to en gage with the central task. The task concluded with reflectio n questions that we conjectured would reframe the s hared experience of problem solving as an opportunity for discussion, rather than in terms of getting the right answer. The Week 5 task was unusual in that there were no scenarios or visualizations. Students had to prove a symbolic mathematical statement about matrices, with general guidance on how to devise solution ideas. The task was authentic in that engineers may need to abstract proofs to characterize the behavior of a modeled system. RPP negotiation We first identified the purpose of the tasks: to te ach students to solve conceptual problems, as contr asted with computational problems. The researchers initially u nderstood the ill-structured tasks they had designe d as achieving this goal: students would have to constru ct conceptual heuristics to simplify the complex pr oblem enough to communicate about it and make progress. However, the professor maintained that the problems were too computational, other than Week 5, which seemed promising. The researchers realized that when using the phrase “conceptual problem” the professor was referring to  a specific genre of mathematical problems, a disti nction which did not exist in the STEM fields in which the  researchers were trained. Week 5, which asked stud ents to prove a mathematical statement, was a good example; other weeks, which involved constructing a design followed by significant calculational work, were not—except for the structured subparts intended to prepare stu dents for the ill-structured problem. Subsequently, the researchers realized that in pilot tests of the tasks, t he preparatory subparts themselves afforded collaborative work. We  tabled the conjecture that ill-structured problems  could afford collaboration and revised the existing tasks to focus on the structured, conceptual-problem subparts. Without the conjecture about ill-structured tasks, the researchers decided to revise Week 5 to give it more structure: a walkthrough in six parts of how a mathematician might approach thinking about the proof, including explicit quotations of what the mathematician might be thinking at each step. The professor found this structure in line with his pedagogical goals. Whereas previous worksheets that he had written consisted of isolated problems for students to practice solving in groups, he would go on to use the “math ematician’s walkthrough” in future tasks to turn the problems into opportunities for students to learn a mathematician’s perspective. Modified designs Subsequent revisions to the design conjectures were  minor. Without the ill-structured problem, the str uctured problems took priority and could be refined. We rea lized that the multiple layers of meaning offered n ot only a source of ideas for the group, but also a means to communicate those ideas, such as by drawing on or pointing to the visualization. We also had more time to devote to reflection questions, which during pilot tests were effective at prompting conceptual discussion. We placed reflection questions at different points in the worksheet to reflect on smaller key ideas that were critical to understanding and solving the overall task. The final structure for all tasks except Week 5 consisted of an easy introductory problem to help students begin confidently, two problems divided into subpar ts, each followed by a reflection, and a final “cha llenge problem” without any scaffolding to further engage groups who finished the first three parts.

## Discussion

We begandeveloping task iterations five months bef ore the start of the course. The early development of tasks gave us a boundary object to use to align our visio ns and substantively revise our design conjectures.  The professor’s learning goal priorities led the researchers to focus on a relatively neglected aspect of the task design, which improved the design and our theorizing about affordances for collaboration for this context. In turn, the researchers’ new ideas for collaborative task desig n offered the professor an idea for how to design t asks that would better meet his learning objectives. Viewing the task iterations as boundary objects allowed them to become joint representations of the collaboration, and a malleable tool for fostering understanding and knowledge growth within the team, rather than static objects provided by the researchers to the professor to use.
