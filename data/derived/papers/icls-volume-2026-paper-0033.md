# Towards an Empirical Understanding of Epistemic Data Agency for Middle School Students During a Data Storytelling Summer Camp

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Epistemic data agency highlights learners’ capacity to take ownership of shaping their learning process with data, yet little work has examined how it can be operationalized in educational contexts. In this paper, we advance the construct of epistemic data agency by providing a list of indicative actions that demonstrate students’ epistemic stances with data. Using video recordings from a two-week middle school summer camp on data storytelling, we conducted case studies of two focal students with contrasting prior data experiences and demographics. Our analysis builds on existing frameworks of knowledge-related epistemic agency activities, which we used to identify activity-level episodes, and extends them by inductively deriving action-level indicators that specify how students engaged with data i n practice. This empirical grounding offers researchers and educators an analytical framework for examining how students enact data-related agency and offers a foundation for guiding and evaluating pedagogical interventions to strengthen data literacy education.

The concept of epistemic agency was introduced to the field of educational research within the knowledgebuilding perspective approach to learning (Scardamalia, 2002). It refers to students' abilities to take responsibility for their knowledge-building process by making choices about what to ask, explore, and create. Recent work has spent efforts in developing a more thorough understanding of epistemic agency (Bandura, 1999; Schwartz & Okita, 2004; Palonen & Hakkarainen, 2013; Akgun & Sharma, 2023). Among t hese efforts, Damşa et al. specifically discriminated between two different categories of indicators of epistemic agency: knowledge-related activities, which specifically focus on idea production as the outcome, and process-related activities that focus on organizing and coordinating the process of knowledge production (Damşa et al., 2010). While previous studies on epistemic agency are often conducted in broad domains (Stroupe, 2014; Zhan & Louie, 2024; Baze et al., 2025; Chew & LeTourneau, 2025), few have examined how it manifests in contexts where data is the central medium for learning. A s data becomes increasingly important to engage with the world (Wolff et al., 2016), understanding how learners perform and develop epistemic agency through data is both timely and necessary. To address the need, existing work by Kahn and Jiang (2024) has explicitly derived the terminology of epistemic data agency as “the conceptual and material practices that reveal and shape learners’ relationships to the data.” Yet prior work has not offered empirical guidance on how epistemic data agency can be observed or operationalized in practice. In this study, we extend the construct by grounding it in empirical practice. Specifically, we ask: Which indicators of epistemic data agency can be identified when students work with data in the context of a data storytelling project?

We conceptualize epistemic data agency as a domain-specific instantiation of knowledge-related epistemic agency, enacted when the medium of knowledge work is data. We derived the framework of epistemic data agency in the setup of a two-week Data Visualization and Environment (DVE) summer camp, where students learn data collection and data visualization and  develop stories of personal interest about their communities supported by data. We applied Damşa et al.’s (2010) framework of knowledge-related epistemic agency to video recordings of two focal students’ working sessions on their data storytelling project, and then inductively extended it into an empirical framework of epistemic data agency. The two focal cases, each representing students with distinct backgrounds, illustrate how learners candevelop and manifest epistemic data agency in diverse ways throughout the project. The contribution of this study is an empirical framework that identifies and organizes student actions that are indicative of epistemic data agency. This framework offers researchers and educators an analytical tool to recognize, analyze, and support how students exercise epistemic agency in relation to data.

## Conceptual framework: Epistemic data agency

Epistemic agency broadly refers to learners’ capacity to take ownership over knowledge-building processes by making choices that shape what counts as legitimate knowledge, how disciplinary practices are enacted, and whose perspectives are valued (Miller et al., 2018; Keifert et al., 2018). It situates learners as knowledge producers 243 (Damşa et al., 2014) rather than participants in the productions or inquiries of others. Within data-intensive learning environments, this notion extends into epistemic data agency, which Kahn and Jiang (2024) conceptualize as the practices through which learners reveal and shape their relationships to data. Enacting epistemic data agency involves not only performing “data moves” such as selecting variables, modeling, or encoding (Kahn & Jiang, 2021), but also interpreting, critiquing, and reimagining data stories (Radinsky, 2020). Prior studies have highlighted the importance of fostering such data-related agency and designed learning environments that enable students to act as decision-makers in their data work. For example, Walker et al. (2025) show how youth in data science workshops exercised ownership over data collection and analysis through openended projects, while Ha rdy et al. (2020) reframed students as data producers whose emergent goals, material resistances, and tool use shaped their sense of agency in making and inter preting data. Related efforts, such as storytelling with community datasets (Wilkerson & Polman, 2020), embodied exploration of census data (Roberts & Lyons, 2020), and collaborative inquiry community-relevant projects (Deahl, 2014), similarly highlight ho w students’ roles as active knowledge producers can guide data work. However, despite these advances, few studies have explicitly measured epistemic data agency, leaving open the question of how to systematically identify and analyze it in practice. Addressing the gap, we operationalize this construct at the level of observable actions that learners take in the flow of data work. In contrast to the primarily conceptual account, our contribution is to provide an empirical framework that specifies how epistemic data agency can be identified in practice. Much prior work frames epistemic agency as a collaborative achievement of groups engaged in collective knowledge building (e.g., Scardamalia, 2002; Damşa et al., 2010). While we acknowledge the social and distributed nature of data work, our analysis foregrounds the individual –data relation as the primary unit of their concrete actions reveal and shape their epistemic stances toward data within the collaborative project context. We are specifically interested in how students’ knowledge-related activities with data, such as searching, sharing, structuring, and producing data-based ideas, reveal their epistemic stances. In contrast, process-related activities (e.g., planning, monitoring, coordinating) are treated as important collaborative practices but are beyond the scope of this study, as they pertain more to group regulation than to individuals’ agentic relations with data. Therefore, in this study, we only focus on the knowledge-related epistemic agency (Damşa et al., 2010) and investigate how individual students conduct actions that shape their relationship to data. By foregrounding epistemic data agency at the individual knowledge-related level, our analysis examines how students’ engagements with data reflect and shape their positions as active knowledge producers in data-rich learning environments.

## Method

This IRB-approved study was conducted during a two-week fully-funded summer camp, with the goal to empower students to use real-life data to communicate ideas  and prepare them for future engagement  with authentic data. During the camp, students learned data visualization design, used digital tools such as Tableau to create charts, developed a project that addresses a self-selected community-related question through data visualizations, and presented their project to parents and community stakeholders. A case study approach (Yin, 2009) was chosen as the data analysis method, given the need for an in-depth understanding of the construct analyzed.

## Participants

Sixteen middle school students enrolled in the summer camp, with fifteen providing assent and parental consent to participate (Age Ave = 12.3 years; 6 identified as female and 1 as non-binary). Although 70% reported some prior experience using data,  most had not participated in formal data-related programs. For this case study, we first selected two project groups with well-developed final projects. Both groups examined crime-related data but pursued different questions and produced distinct outcomes, enabling a comparison of how epistemic data agency developed. From these groups, we selected one focal student from each to reflect variation in demographic  and prior experience with data.  Morgan (pseudonym), an incoming 7th-grade female who self-identified as “Black, African, Caribbean, or African American” and “White, Caucasian, or European American,” reported no prior experience with data. Omar, an incoming 9th-grade male who self-identified as “Arab, Middle Eastern, or North African,” reported experience in f our data-related practices, including using spreadsheets, creating graphs, working with data in science projects, and using chart creation tools, placing him on the higher end of the cohort.

## Educational context

Summer camp setup The study was conducted during a DVE summer camp in 2025 on an R1 university campus in the US. The summer camp was designed as a collaborative, project-based learning experience and was offered to middle school 244 students from a local district. Activities were led by a science teacher from the students' school with assistance from the research team, including helping with troubleshooting and questions answering. During the first week, students formed into groups of 2-3 students and identified their project topics as they practiced data visualization skills through structured problems. For the second week, students primarily worked with their groups to develop their projects, with facilitators offering group and individualized scaffolding as needed. On the last day of the camp, students presented their work to parents and community stakeholders in posters or tri-boards at a showcase. Data storytelling project The final project of the summer camp was divided into four design phases: 1) identify the topic and data, 2) plan out the project, 3) create the visualization, and 4) compose the story,  where students decide topics and make decisions collaboratively while the camp facilitators provide guidance. Morgan, together with another incoming 7th-grader, chose the topic of the most commonly committed crimes in their home state. They constructed their dataset based on the state government’s annual crime data report, including the number of different crimes reported each year. After creating the visualizations using Tableau, they used a tri-board and put individual charts and support materials on the board (see Figure 1.A). Omar, together with two other incoming 9th-graders, conducted a project on which stores are robbed the most in the students’ home city.  They constructed their dataset from the open data portal of the city’s police department, which provides detailed reports of crimes occurring in the city, including the location and time of each incident. This group organized their materials on a poster (see Figure 1.B). Figure 1 Students’ Final Projects from Morgan’s Group (A) and Omar’s Group (B). Data and analysis All camp sessions, including the instructions and students’ working sessions, were video recorded and transcribed. In addition, students answered a pre-survey about their prior experience with data and data visualization, and a post-survey self-reporting their experience and growth of skills throughout the summer camp. Finally, students participated in focus groups with 5-6 students each time, which were audio-recorded and transcribed. Students’ worksheets created during the camp and the final projects were also collected. In this work, we focus on the video data. Video analysis provides rich and fine-grained access to students’ interactions with data, enabling us to capture both verbal reasoning and embodied moves (Atkins et al., 2009; Derry et al., 2010). We examined the working sessions of two selected students across project phases (Morgan: 403 minutes; Omar: 407 minutes of video recordings). Our analysis looks for both students’ activities and actions. We use Jordan & Henderson (1995)’s definition of activities as goal-oriented stretches of interactions that are typically marked by a recognizable purpose or set of tasks, such as students trying to understand what the two axes mean in a graph. Actions, in contrast, are observable and discrete components of activity that the students conduct that help them move towards the goal of their activities (Damşa et al., 2010), such as searching “y-axis” in Google to find the definition, or drawing a sketch of the x-axis and the y-axis and confirming with teammates. We conducted a two-stage qualitative analysis. In the first stage, we deductively identified activities by applying Damşa et al.’s (2010) framework of knowledge-related activities: searching information, sharing ideas, structuring ideas, and producing ideas. For each project phase, we identified video episodes in which the focal student engaged in these four categories of activity, accompanied by detailed descriptions of what the student said and did in sequence. The first author initially segmented a subse t of the video data into episodes and discussed the segmentation and descriptions with the research team weekly. After reaching an agreement, the same criteria were applied to the full dataset and reviewed with the team, resulting in 82 episodes for Morgan and 92 for Omar, with the length from 30 seconds to 6.5 minutes).  In the second stage, for each category of activity in each project 245 phase, we inductively coded the descriptions of the identified activities into finer-grained actions that represent the discrete and observable moves the student made (e.g., entering a new data point and clarifying a terminology), with codes first derived by the first author and then iteratively refined through team discussion before being applied to the full dataset. These identified actions serve as empirical indicators of epistemic data agency, specifying how students enacted the broader knowledge-related activities in the context of working with data. In this way, our contribution builds on an existing framework of activities while extending it with empirically grounded indicators of action.

This study advances an empirical framework that identifies actions through which students enact epistemic data agency from searching and organizing data to structuring and producing data-driven ideas. Mapping these actions across the four project phases, we demonstrate that epistemic data agency involves not only interpreting and visualizing data but alsonegotiating meaning and design decisions. This framework contributes to data literacy education by offering a lens to analyze how individuals work with data within collaborative contexts. Future work would apply the framework on other students to examine the degree to which learners activate epistemic data agency and to identify how to design curriculum and instructional interventions to effectively support  it. This approach can help the field of data literacy education move toward a richer understanding of how epistemic data agency can foster more empowered and authentic participation in data literacy learning. In our case study, the two focal students have various prior experiences and demographic backgrounds. Analyzing how they work with data following the proposed framework revealed not only how one student’s agency developed throughout the project, but also how epistemic data agency can manifest differently across learners. For example, Morgan, who had less prior experience with data, initially expressed and negotiated her understanding through questions, clarifications, and coordination with peers. Omar, in contrast, frequently proposed and justified data and design decisions early in the project, assuming a directive stance in team suggest that episte mic data agency manifests dynamically, shaped by learners’ prior experiences, self-efficacy, and ongoing roles within collaboration. The framework thus serves as a useful analytic lens to identify not only whether students enact epistemic data agency, but also to what extent and in what ways it emerges, offering a foundation for designing future interventions that support diverse learners. While the current work did not prioritize explaining these factors in-depth to explain the reasons for these difference s, future research could explore the influencing factors, such as students’ backgrounds, self-efficacy, and collaboration dynamics, and investigate personalized strategies to activate epistemic data agency across diverse learners (Damşa et al., 2010). While prior work on epistemic agency often emphasizes its collaborative nature (e.g., Scardamalia, 2002; Damşa et al., 2010), this study centered on epistemic data agency as features of individual students: how a learner takes ownership of interpreting, representing, and communicating data in the context of a group context. Focusing on the individual level does not conflict with collaborative perspectives; rather, our findings reveal how individual actions intersect with shared epistemic processes. For example, Morgan’s questions elicited teammates’ clarifications, while Omar’s design justifications prompted group reasoning about communicative intent. Prior research in collaborative learning similarly suggests that epistemic work often emerges through the i nterplay between individual contributions and shared meaning-making processes (e.g., Stahl, 2006; Suthers, 2006). These interactions demonstrate that individual epistemic acts can serve as entry points for collective sense-making. Future work could examine how individual and collective agency co-evolve in data-rich learning settings and how instructional design can foster both individual and collaborative epistemic engagement. This study’s summer camp context and small number of focal cases might limit the generalizability of the findings. Firstly, the phases in which we identified epistemic data agency from the two focal students are not the only phases where such agency may occur. For other students, the same epistemic action might emerge in different phases or recur across multiple stages of the project. Future research should examine how these indicators manifest across larger and more diverse populations. Additionally, our focus on knowledge-related activities excluded process-related activities, such as goal setting and time management (Scardamalia, 2002). Future studies could integrate process-related indicators to provide a fuller picture of how learners manage and negotiate both individual and shared epistemic goals when working with data.
