# Designing for Resilience as Preparation for Challenging STEM Tasks

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
We designed a novel instructional package targeting distinct facets of mental and emotional resilience for thirty-nine primary school students as preparation for a collaborative design challenge in a Singaporean science classroom. Our mental resilience package nudged sensemaking around growth mindset by having students make predictions on myths, and offered vicarious learning opportunities for everyone to evaluate peer quotations of reframing failure. Our emotional resilience package used contrasting cases complemented with expressive writing to help students discern the usefulness of positive and negative emotions in learning. Several data sources – including students’ reflections, their descriptions of enacted resilience behaviors, and observable measures of science learning – showed evidence that this work may have already begun planting seeds for normalizing failure and persevering emotionally through setbacks.

Resilience, a core twenty-first century skill (Center for Curriculum Redesign, 2024), is increasingly vital as educational systems worldwide emphasize active, student‑centered learning. We examine how mental and emotional resilience training can be designed to complement disciplinary curricula by provoking sensemaking that makes abstract ideas of resilience more concrete. Our study investigates the reflective learning processes triggered by this design (RQ1), whether students can transfer core ideas of resilience into practice during a follow‑up STEM task (RQ2), and whether their conceptual understanding of the science topic improves (RQ3). Drawing on Yeager and Dweck (2012), we conceptualize mental resilience as constructive responses to academic challenges – such as strategy exploration, increased effort, and adaptive interpretations of failure – and target two facets: cultivating a growth mindset  (Dweck, 2006) and deepening the utility value of failure  (Harackiewicz & Priniski, 2018; Sinha, 2025a). Prior growth mindset designs often rely on direct instruction (Burnette et al., 2023), which may harm students with low expectancy for success (Qin et al., 2021). Building on Sinha (2026), we instead use prediction‑and‑explanation cycles to surface misconceptions and introduce productive surprise in the learning process. Likewise, whereas past utility-value interventions have focused on relevance of academic tasks (Hulleman & Harackiewicz, 2021), we address a gap by repeatedly engaging students in evaluating relatable peer quotations that highlight the constructive role of failure across learning contexts (Sinha, 2025a).  Complementing this, we conceptualize emotional resilience as students’ ability to recognize, manage, and recover from emotional challenges during learning (Johnson et al., 2017; Polizzi & Lynn, 2021), with emotion regulation (Gross, 2015) as a central mechanism for sustaining engagement. Reflective journaling can scaffold students’ monitoring and evaluation of emotions (Lynn et al., 2024); here, we extend expressive-writing approaches (Pennebaker, 2004) through novel reflection prompts that link emotional awareness to metacognition about the usefulness of emotions for learning, regardless of valence. Because emotions serve both hedonic and instrumental goals (Tamir, 2009), emerging work in the learning sciences (Sinha, 2022; Sinha et al., 2025) has begun emphasizing improving beliefs about the utility of emotions themselves (Sinha, 2025b). Accordingly, our design integrates sensemaking‑focused activities such as evaluation through emotional contrasting cases, showing how different appraisals of the same emotion (e.g., pride, interest, shame, anger) may lead to divergent learning outcomes in school settings.

## Method and study design

We recruited N = 39 primary students (9-11 years) from a mixed ability classroom in Singapore (n = 17 females).

The study was approved by the ethics commission of  Nanyang Technological University (IRB-2023-1040). A within-subjects design was used (see Figure 1), where all students first individually went through the emotional resilience and mental resilience packages on two consecutive days in counterbalanced order. Subsequently, in preparation for a follow-up science task, the school teacher divided the class into n  = 8 groups based on their knowledge of classroom dynamics. Over the next four days (days 3 to 6), all groups went through the design challenge of creating a shadow puppet show targeting the physical science topic of light, where they manipulated light and objects to create a variety of shadow effects (e.g., different shades of gray). Finally, on day 7, all students individually went through a teacher-led reflection where they verbalized challenges and emotions for each day of 2620 the science task. Individual-level pretest (administered before exposure to the resilience packages) and posttest (administered after the teacher-led reflection) were carried out to evaluate improvements in science understanding. For RQ1 (using thematic analysis on M 3, M4, M5, and M6), we found students  reflections on mental resilience training to reveal three developmental distinctions: (i) cognitive understanding of failure – recognizing mistakes as learning opportunities (“It is good to learn from your mistakes…”, “Learning is a ladder…”); (ii) behavioral responses to failure  – retrying or adjusting strategies (“… take a break and retry”, “I kept falling…I still try”); and (iii) sharing/abstracting failure  – advising others and universalizing growth mindset principles (“you don’t need to be embarrassed…”, “With effort you can improve”, “A cheerful spirit is good medicine…”). For emotional resilience training, students made meaning through: (i) recognition and acceptance of emotions as valid and informative (“It is okay to be angry…”, “It is okay to feel embarrassed because it helps you realize your mistake”); (ii) self‑regulation and coping (“take a deep breath”, “I will tell teacher I am feeling sad”, “hear calm music”); and (iii) personal emotional growth – learning from emotions for future improvement (“I felt upset…I started studying and improved”, “I will handle my emotions by learning more and not cheating”). For RQ2 (using thematic analysis on M1 and M2), we found that students verbalized applying resilience in the STEM task differentially. Mental resilience manifested as (i) social persistence – overcoming group friction and assuming responsibility (“I had to do the script at the start, one member did not help”, “I told my <group> if they make any mistakes it is ok… ”, “…asked my group to keep quiet and listen to each other’s ideas”); and (ii) task persistence – sustained effort and strategic adjustment (“…w e worked together and  never gave up”, “the script was not good… did not give up and made changes … used our different strengths”). Emotional resilience appeared as (i) recognition/management of task‑evoked feelings (“stress and anger as our puppet did not turn out”, “anxious because the design was hard”); (ii) shifts from avoidance to engagement (“felt stressed when there was a time limit…we did it fast and right”, “did not have script…we improvised … and managed”); and (iii) reflection on social dynamics (“felt sad because I was left out”, “… group finally helped…I felt happy too”). For RQ3, paired t-tests showed that at a group level (i) understanding of screen/light/object positions was stable (M7-t1: 1.25±0.25; M7-t2: 1.29±0.18, p = 1.00), (ii) conditions for shadow formation were similar (M9t1: 1.81±0.13; M9-t2: 1.67±0.21, p = 0.36), and (iii) materials knowledge for puppet design improved descriptively (M8-t1: 0.62±0.26;  M8-t2: 1.60±0.20; M8-t3:1.83±0.17), with one of three pairwise differences between the initial and intermediary timepoint being significant (p = 0.01, d = 1.27). At an individual level, students’ scores (max

11) significantly improved from 7.77±0.39 at the pretest to 8.50±0.38 at the posttest (p = 0.04, d = 0.34).

Taken together, m ental and emotional resilience reflections in our data revealed clear developmental progressions – from understanding to strategy use to outward social generalization – aligning with work on errorpositive climates (Clifford, 1984), emotion regulation (Wright et al., 2025), and metacognitive growth (Yeager & Dweck, 2020). These reflective processes further transferred into the follow‑up STEM task, where students demonstrated both task persistence and socially oriented resilience (Vaughn & DeJonckheere, 2021), as well as collective regulation of group emotions.
