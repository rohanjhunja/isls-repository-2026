# A three-prong framework for designing theory-based math games

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
A common critique of ineffective learning games is their lack of theory-based design and integration of instructional features. While a few game-design frameworks have been proposed, they tend to take a limited approach. Some do not consider emotional aspects of games; others do not consider links between gameplay and learning. An approach that brings together the multiple aspects of educational game design is needed. This is important to build domain-specific thinking, build connections between gameplay and learning, and tap into the emotional and motivational aspects of games. This article describes an iterative approach to math game design by bringing together decades of previous research. It outlines how this approach is applied to create a game for secondary mathematics, specifically geometry. Progression of puzzles based on mathematical complexity, connections between learning objectives and gameplay, and inclusion of emotional elements are described. The challenges, opportunities, and outcomes of the approach are discussed. Gaps in math game design Interactive digital experiences like games can be useful tools for mathematics education as they can transform abstract mathematical concepts into concrete experiences (Potega vel Żabik et al., 2024). For example, games can simulate the abstract nature of mathematical concepts and provide low-stake and real-time interactive environments for mathematical problem solving (Hui & Mahmud, 2023; Klopfer & Thompson, 2020; Sharma et al., 2022). However, it is difficult to design games that incorporate such elements in a meaningful way to create an enjoyable and effective learning experience for adolescents (Vandercruysse et al., 2016). Despite their potential as engaging learning tools, research has shown that math games and interactive experiences have lacked theoretical foundations (Sharma & Dubé, 2024). More generally, researchers have regularly identified the need for theory-based development of educational games to make them more effective in achieving their intended learning goals (Dubé & Dubé, 2021; Vandercruysse et al., 2016). However, the challenge is to integrate learning into games while not taking away the “fun” (Garris et al., 2002). From the learning perspective, ineffective educational games are criticized for lack of guiding theories and instructional features (Plass et al., 2015). From the fun perspective, ineffective games are criticized for their bad, unintuitive design, and a mismatch between learning goals and gameplay (Plass et al., 2015). It is important to bridge these gaps by grounding the design of math games in discipline and gameplay related theoretical frameworks. In their systematic review, Ke (2016) found that game-based learning (GBL) research has predominantly focused on evaluating the effectiveness of digital games. However, researchers should also elaborate and provide descriptions of theoretical foundations, design decisions, and rationales for their choice of game-interactions (Kiili & Lainema, 2008). In this article, we describe three dimensions to educational game design based on relevant theoretical frameworks. We present a three-prong approach to math game design. It builds on previous frameworks and suggests an iterative process of math game design that considers 1) mathematics instruction and thinking, 2) gameplay interactions tied with learning, and 3) the emotional aspects of games tied with learning. We describe this approach with an illustrative example of our process of creating GeometryBOX, a geometry game for adolescents. Three aspects of educational game design Discipline-specific theory Many researchers have called for educational games to be informed by learning theories guiding their designs (Plass et al., 2020). This can refer to a discipline-agnostic theory of learning (e.g., Constructivism). While such a theory can inform the game type and mode of learning, it does not address disciplinary needs. I t does not inform how the progression and order of in-game tasks align with the development of mathematical thinking. This requires a discipline-specific theory. A discipline-specific theory should be chosen to serve two purposes. First, it guides the in-game instruction and progression based on the requirements and the “ways of thinking” associated with the discipline (Honey & Hilton, 2011; Turkay et al., 2014). This can ensure that the game enables thinking processes in the player that are aligned with the requirements of the discipline (Mayer, 2020). Second, it informs all the subject matter-related aspects of the game, like operationalizing learning objectives for the in-game 61 problems. A discipline-specific theory informs how the in-game problems and gameplay intend to achieve the target learning outcomes (Plass et al., 2020). Gameplay-specific theory As opposed to the discipline specific theory that focusses on progression of in-game problems in line with the thinking process of the discipline, a gameplay specific theory informs the overall design of the game environment and specific player actions tied with the learning objectives. In their systematic review, Ke (2016) identified five themes and ways for integrating learning and playing in digital games. These provide a starting point for how learning goals could be mapped to the interactions offered by the game environment. The five themes are listed in Table 3 later in this article. However, it must be noted that these themes are not mutually exclusive, and one game can integrate learning opportunities in more than one way. The themes identified by Ke (2016) inform broad areas and approaches for integrating learning in games. In addition, it is also important to identify how learning content can be intrinsically embedded into the gameplay and mapped to player’s actions. This is needed to inform how all types of in-game player actions are associated with learning processes and to avoid unnecessary gameplay that can cause distractions and enhance extraneous load which can be detrimental to learning (Mayer & Moreno, 2003). Here, the Learning Mechanic – Game Mechanic (LM-GM) Framework (Arnab et al., 2015) can be a useful tool. It can be used to build a map of learning elements in a serious game with its mechanical/gameplay elements. Here, learning mechanics refers to the objectives, activities, and thinking processes focused on the context of learning. Game mechanics refers to the type of gameplay, game elements, and objects available to the player. This framework provides a starting point for designers to consider these mechanics and identify how gameplay and pedagogy are interrelated in their game. Educational game designers should carefully consider how each interaction a player makes in the game (game mechanic) is associated with the intended learning objective and processes (learning mechanic). This is useful to describe how the actions taken by the players in the games activate the thinking processes required for learning. Emotional design principles Games have the capability to induce achievement emotions that can support learning. Loderer and colleagues (2020) identified five emotional design principles to promote experiences and emotions that aid learning. Carefully considering emotional design principles to inform educational game design can help balance the “fun” and learning in games. Additionally, they can induce emotions conducive to learning like enjoyment and alleviation of boredom. Finally, emotional design principles in games can alleviate t he association of negative emotions, negative math attitudes, and math anxiety in children (Wen & Dubé, 2022; Young et al., 2012). The five principles are visual aesthetic design, musical score, game mechanics, narrative, and incentive system. Visual aesthetic design refers to the in-game visual elements that create engaging and immersive experiences. Musical scores in educational games refer to background tunes, sound effects, and audio feedback to enhance players’ experiences and learning. Game mechanics refer to the interactions and rules that drive the gameplay. Narrative refers to storylines and contextual problem solving that enhance engagement. Finally, the incentive system refers to the rewards and penalties to induce motivation during gameplay. Educational game designers should consider these five aspects at all stages of game development. When thoughtfully applied to its design, emotional design principles can make the game “fun” and “appealing” to the players while keeping intact the intended learning objectives (Mayer & Estrella, 2014). We propose an approach based on the three theoretical aspects of educational game design. Critically, all three should consistently inform design decisions across all aspects of the game (Figure 1). They are applied iteratively and inform the design process at all stages, which can include level design, characters, and in-game problems. Discipline-specific theory in GeometryBOX As described previously, we aimed to identify a discipline-specific theoretical framework aligned with the intended learning objectives (geometry learning) and the target audience (adolescents). Additionally, it should inform the progression of in-game pro blems and enable the thinking processes needed to achieve learning objectives. To address these aspects, we used the Van Hiele Model (VHM) of geometric thinking (Hiele, 1999) as the guiding discipline specific theory for our game. It is a hierarchical framework establishing the existence of five different levels of geometric thinking of increasing complexity (Arnal-Bailera & Manero, 2024). The increasing complexity of levels informed the increasing complexity of puzzles and specifically informed the “level design” of the game. Second, Hiele (1999) outlined the focus of the VHM on visual and non-verbal thinking and exploration which make it appropriate to inform the design of play-and game-based instruction. Finally, VHM is a topic-agnostic theoretical model. GeometryBOX targeted “classification of quadrilaterals”, a sub-topic in plane-geometry, generally included in middle and secondary geometry curriculum (Vízek et al., 2025). The five levels of VHM are (a) visualization – referring to visual perception and recognition of mathematical objects; (b) analysis – referring to the ability to recognize and name properties of geometrical figures, (c) abstraction – referring to identification of properties and relationships between geometrical figures, (d) formal ded uction – referring to deduction of geometric proofs and understanding hierarchical nature of mathematical properties, and (e) rigor – referring to identification, comparison, and interrelation of different geometrical systems. VHM has previously been used to inform the design of several curricular and instructional materials for geometry (Senk et al., 2022). Such operationalization is necessary to map how a theoretical model is applied to define the context of gameplay in association with the intended learn ing objectives. Emotional design principles in GeometryBOX 65 Visual aesthetic design We used bright, warm, and contrasting colors in the visual design of the game. All the geometrical figures, that were the primary objects of gameplay, were bright purple in color to create a visually appealing interface (Wen & Dubé, 2023). The overall theme of “outer space” was consistently depicted through all the game objects. For example, the cursor was styled as a “crosshair”. Players solved geometrical puzzles to fix batteries and repair their ship. On the “progress map” screen of the game, the batteries were represented as “lightbulbs” that changed color from orange to green after successful completion of a puzzle. This aimed to create a sense of curiosity and break-down the overall game into smaller goals inducing a sense of achievement (Garg & Baroody, 2024). Musical score Audible feedback from a learning environment has the potential to enhance the perceived pleasantness of gameplay by extending the sensory experience (Nacke et al., 2010). A consistent, low, buzzing sound was used to convey the sound as their ship navigated outer space. Different types of audio feedback were used when players made correct/incorrect moves in the game, successfully completed a level, depleted their energy or shields, collected stars while navigating, and shot enemy objects. Dynamic sounds along with encouraging phrases like “You’re good” (with a bright audio cue) after a correct move and “Try again” (with a dull audio cue) after an incorrect move were used to keep the players motivated during gameplay (Cayari, 2022). This way, the audio was also aligned with the game situation to induce immersion and emotional engagement (Andersen et al., 2021). Such acoustic characteristics were used in the game to draw attention to important content associated with learning (Collins, 2009; Plass et al., 2020). Game mechanics The central puzzle solving mechanic of the game was to “drag and drop” geometrical shapes into the correct bins to fix the batteries (Figure 2a, 2b). This built energy for the players’ ship to navigate and collect as many stars as possible. Sorting geometrical objects can engage students to build new knowledge based on their existing conceptions (Kobiela et al., 2018). It can help them identify and un derstand the properties of geometrical objects by noticing similarities and differences (Kobiela et al., 2018; Roth & Thom, 2009). Hence, sorting via “dragging and dropping” geometrical shapes into bins was built as the central game mechanic. Other game mechanics in the puzzles were primarily informed by the learning objectives at each level. New mechanics are introduced as players progress to puzzles at higher levels of the Van Hiele Model. They aim to sustain curiosity and enjoyment while preventing boredom and predictive gameplay (Loderer et al., 2020). They also create a sense of excitement as players progress in the game. For example, at puzzles from VHM level four, players are introduced to the “slicing” mechanic. They can break quadrilaterals into smaller diagonals by “slicing” them along a diagonal of their choice. Table 2 shows new mechanics introduced in puzzles at each new VHM level. In addition to inducing positive emotions that aid learning, the game mechanics are introduced in a structured order that is informed by the discipline-specific theory, the VHM model. This avoids a mismatch between progression of gameplay and learning which can enhance self-efficacy and reduce negative emotions such as frustration (Plass et al., 2012). This is associated with the recommendations of (Masek et al., 2017) where “fun” and “excitement” in the game are fundamentally grounded in the problem solving associated with learning. Narrative Narrative in educational games can provide a framework for structural representation of problems (Dickey, 2011). There can be many ways of including narrative in learning games. This is based on the genre of game and core game-mechanics (Ke, 2016). Our game had a focus on mathematical learning outcomes. The core mechanic involved was “logic and thought during puzzle solving”. Additionally, it was categorized as a “casual puzzle” game as players solve geometrical puzzles to progress. In GeometryBOX, narrative was embedded via environmental storytelling and backdrop missions. Elements of the game environment focused on conveying the narrative theme of the game. Nomenclature for points, rewards, and penalties referred to the narrative theme. Additional gameplay puzzles for the game incentive system were created and were also situated in the overall narrative theme of the game (Figure 2c, 2d). In our game, narrative was integrated as a broadly defined goal with a background plot and adventure missions (Jenkins, 2002). An overarching narrative can create expectations about upcoming events and build suspense that sustains attention for learning (McNamara et al., 2010). The narrative in GeometryBOX of “fixing batteries” to “repair the ship” and “navigate the outer space” was designed to contextualize learning and provide an overarching story connecting in-game progression, gameplay loop, rules, events, rewards, and penalties (Loderer et al., 2020). Through all these aspects, narrative was tightly and meaningfully associated with gameplay and learning. It was not included as an afterthought where the story and narrative are only depicted through one aspect (e.g., cut scene and videos) and not tied to gameplay (Naul & Liu, 66 2020). On the contrary, the primary narrative related objective of “fixing batteries” was tied to the central learning objective of solving geometrical puzzles. Figure 2 Examples level screenshots from GeometryBOX Incentive system Rewards and penalties at the center of GBL can help keep the players motivated (Loderer et al., 2020). The incentive system in GeometryBOX is applied in three layers. First, every “game turn” of “dragging and dropping” an object costs a certain number of resources. In this way, players are nudged towards solving the puzzles in minimum possible moves to pres erve the available resources. Hence, they are incentivized for adopting mathematical strategies and discourage “trial and error” based strategy. Second, when players deplete all their resources by making too many incorrect moves, they need to replenish it by completing another game level where they “shoot the debris to clear the boosters” (Figure 2d). This resets their available resources to 100%. This incentive system of managing resources and performance-based progress is dependent on individual learning and gameplay strategy. It aims to increase perceived autonomy and learner control and induce positive achievement emotions (McNamara et al., 2010). The third layer of incentive system is based on collecting “stars” to increase overall points. This layer in tends to enhance the value of learning through “internal compensation”, i.e. achievements in the game (Loderer et al., 2020). After players solve enough puzzles to bring their available resources above a certain threshold, new gameplay is unlocked in which the player controls their spaceship and steers it through an “asteroid field in outer space”. This three-layered, cyclic incentive system in GeometryBOX is intended to create positive achievement emotion of anticipation through feedback, novelty, and repe tition (Pekrun et al., 2014). It also aims to promote optimized gameplay linked with learning as thoughtful, strategized, and mathematical gameplay is rewarded. In sum, GeometryBOX is designed for adolescents to understand the properties and relationships between special quadrilaterals. It is tightly aligned with the learning objectives associated with this topic. This aspect was primarily informed by the VHM, the discipline-specific theory guiding its design. The gameplay and learning were integrated based on the five themes identified by Ke (2016) and are described via its LM-GM map. Emotional design principles were applied to all five features of the game to create a positive and enjoyable learning experience for the players. Discussion This article suggests a three-prong approach to educational game design with an elaborate example of a math game. Educational game designers should follow an iterative process of applying this approach to their design. They should bring these three theoretical approaches together by going beyond previous approaches to educational 67 game design that only consider these aspects independently. In their review of existing educational game design frameworks, Sasupilli and colleagues (2019) identified six prevalent game design models. However, none of them focused on domain specific, play specific, and emotion specific theoretical foundations simultaneously. They were limited to either one or only two of these aspects. For example, the experiential gaming model (Kiili, 2005) does not consider blending learning through game mechanics. In the context of mathematics education, the application of a theoretical framework of math instruction is critical to promote students’ domain-specific mental models for learning (Pirnay-Dummer et al., 2012). This factor becomes much more critical in the context of math as it relies on students’ development of mental models of mathematical objects, their properties, and their relationships (G arg et al., 2025; Prayekti et al., 2020). Additionally, the use of Ke’s (2016) framework is needed to address the “what, how, where, and when learning is embedded” in games. This framework helps game designers answer broader questions about leaning in games. It informs design decisions like choice of game genre (simulation versus puzzle), absence or presence of narrative, narrative design (environmental backdrop versus roleplay), representation of learning content (content as game objects versus simulation of concepts), frequency of learning moments in games (exploration and discovery versus immediate feedback), and presence or absence of in-game learning supports. Further, at a finer level, the LM-GM framework (Arnab et al., 2015) helps designers address how all game features (game mechanics) are linked to learning mechanics. Creating an LM-GM map of the game during its design process is crucial to establish granular relationships between pedagogy and gameplay. Finally, to leverage the potential of games to induce emotional experiences for learning, the application of emotional design principles is essential. This can ensure a balance between fun and learning by fostering positive emotions (Byun & Joung, 2018). Specifically for mathematics, enhancing the overall learning experience can be transformative to address concerns with the negative perception of math as being too challenging and uninspiring (Wen & Dubé, 2023). Careful integration of emotional design principles that meaningfully and systematically induce enjoyment, anticipation, and excitement (Putwain et al., 2018) can counter negative impressions of math like fear and anxiety (Budhathoki et al., 2022). Navigating challenges While there are theoretical benefits of applying the three-prong framework to educational games, it also comes with its own challenges and opportunities. It is possible that there is overlap, friction, and conflict when certain game elements are informed by two approaches. The design process for GeometryBOX serves as an example to illustrate how such challenges can be overcome. This is mainly exhibited by two elements: game mechanics and narrative. We describe how all game mechanics were linked to learning via the LM-GM map of the game. The LM-GM map describes the way in which mechanics were implemented and their link with learning processes. In addition, a range of game mechanics were built to create anticipation and excitement as an outcome of the emotional design principles. Further, the addition of new mechanics to create anticipation and excitement was based on the increasing complexity of the discipline-specific VHM model. This way, the design of game mechanics was grounded in all three frameworks that each supported learning through different dimensions. Similarly, learning was integrated through an implicit narrative in the game via environmental storytelling, backdrop, textual and visual references to narrative theme. This was based on Ke’s (2016) framework. Narrative can be beneficial for math learning by providing learners with a framework for representation of problems (Irmayanti et al., 2024; Junkin, 2019). Here, narrative was not included as explicit framing but, rather, the action of solving geometry puzzles to achieve learning outcomes was implicitly linked to the game narrative (solving puzzles = fixing “batteries” = repairing “ship”). Moreover, narrative served as a tool to create an immersive and enjoyable learning experience based on the emotional design principles. This demonstrates how the inclusion of narrative was also informed by two distinct frameworks. Yet, it aimed to avoid contradictory and conflicting experiences. In this case, grounding the narrative in multiple theoretical argument s is likely to strengthen its effects on learning rather than weakening it. Conclusion The GeometryBOX game described in this article is an example of applying distinct but relevant theoretical frameworks of educational game design to create a geometry game. It bridges the gaps between cognitive and domain-specific, gameplay-specific, and emotional foundations of game-based learning. Thoughtful and implicit design of game features tightly tied to learning objectives have the potential to result in positive learning outcomes and make learning an enjoyable experience. We aim to conduct evidence-based, experimental studies on the effectiveness of GeometryBOX on geometrical understanding of adolescents. This article describes an iterative approach to math game design by bringing  together decades of previous research. It outlines how this approach is applied to create a game for secondary mathematics, specifically geometry. Progression of puzzles based on mathematical complexity, connections between learning objectives and gameplay, and inclusion of emotional elements are described. The challenges, opportunities, and outcomes of the approach are discussed. Gaps in math game design Interactive digital experiences like games can be useful tools for mathematics education as they can transform abstract mathematical concepts into concrete experiences (Potega vel Żabik et al., 2024). For example, games can simulate the abstract nature of mathematical concepts and provide low-stake and real-time interactive environments for mathematical problem solving (Hui & Mahmud, 2023; Klopfer & Thompson, 2020; Sharma et al., 2022). However, it is difficult to design games that incorporate such elements  in a meaningful way to create an enjoyable and effective learning experience for adolescents (Vandercruysse et al., 2016). Despite their potential as engaging learning tools, research has shown that math games and interactive experiences have lacked theoretical foundations (Sharma & Dubé, 2024). More generally, researchers have regularly identified the need for theory-based development of educational games to make them more effective in achieving their intended learning goals (Dubé & Dubé, 2021; Vandercruysse et al., 2016). However, the challenge is to integrate learning into games while not taking away the “fun” (Garris et al., 2002). From the learning perspective, ineffective educational games are criticized for lack of guiding theories and instructional features (Plass et al., 2015). From the fun perspective, ineffective games are criticized for their bad, unintuitive design,  and a mismatch between learning goals and gameplay (Plass et al., 2015). It is important to bridge these gaps by grounding the design of math games in discipline and gameplay related theoretical frameworks. In their systematic review, Ke (2016) found that game-based learning (GBL) research has predominantly focused on evaluating the effectiveness of digital games. However, researchers should also elaborate and provide descriptions of theoretical foundations, design decisions, and rationales for their choice of game-interactions (Kiili & Lainema, 2008). In this article, we describe three dimensions to educational game design based on relevant theoretical frameworks. We present a three-prong approach to math game design. It builds on previous frameworks and suggests an iterative process of math game design that considers 1) mathematics instruction and thinking, 2) gameplay interactions tied with learning, and 3) the emotional aspects of games tied with learning. We describe this approach with an illustrative example of our process of creating GeometryBOX, a geometry game for adolescents. Three aspects of educational game design Discipline-specific theory Many researchers have called for educational games to be informed by learning theories guiding their designs (Plass et al., 2020). This can refer to a discipline-agnostic theory of learning (e.g., Constructivism). While such a theory can inform the game type and mode of learning, it does not address disciplinary needs. I t does not inform how the progression and order of in-game tasks align with the development of mathematical thinking. This requires a discipline-specific theory.  A discipline-specific theory should be chosen to serve two purposes. First, it guides the in-game instruction and progression based on the requirements and the “ways of thinking” associated with the discipline (Honey & Hilton, 2011; Turkay et al., 2014). This can ensure that the game enables thinking processes in the player that are aligned with the requirements of the discipline (Mayer, 2020). Second, it informs all the subject matter-related aspects of the game, like operationalizing learning objectives for the in-game 61 problems. A discipline-specific theory informs how the in-game problems and gameplay intend to achieve the target learning outcomes (Plass et al., 2020).

Gameplay-specific theory As opposed to the discipline specific theory that focusses on progression of in-game problems in line with the thinking process of the discipline, a gameplay specific theory informs the overall design of the game environment and specific player actions tied with the learning objectives. In their systematic review, Ke (2016) identified five themes and ways for integrating learning and playing in digital games. These provide a starting point for how learning goals could be mapped to the interactions offered by the game environment. The five themes are listed in Table 3 later in this article. However, it must be noted that these themes are not mutually exclusive, and one game can integrate learning opportunities in more than one way. The themes identified by Ke (2016) inform broad areas and approaches for integrating learning in games. In addition, it is also important to identify how learning content can be intrinsically embedded into the gameplay and mapped to player’s actions. This is needed to inform how all types of in-game player actions are associated with learning processes and to avoid unnecessary gameplay that can cause distractions and enhance extraneous load which can be detrimental to learning (Mayer & Moreno, 2003). Here, the Learning Mechanic – Game Mechanic (LM-GM) Framework (Arnab et al., 2015) can be a useful tool. It can be used to build a map of learning elements in a serious game with its mechanical/gameplay elements. Here, learning mechanics refers to the objectives, activities, and thinking processes focused on the context of learning. Game mechanics refers to the type of gameplay, game elements, and objects available to the player. This framework provides a starting point for designers to consider these mechanics and identify how gameplay and pedagogy are interrelated in their game. Educational game designers should carefully consider how each interaction a player makes in the game (game mechanic) is associated with the intended learning objective and processes (learning mechanic). This is useful to describe how the actions taken by the players in the games activate the thinking processes required for learning.

Emotional design principles Games have the capability to induce achievement emotions that can support learning. Loderer and colleagues

(2020) identified five emotional design principles to promote experiences and emotions that aid learning.

Carefully considering emotional design principles to inform educational game design can help balance the “fun” and learning in games. Additionally, they can induce emotions conducive to learning like enjoyment and alleviation of boredom. Finally, emotional design principles in games can alleviate t he association of negative emotions, negative math attitudes, and math anxiety in children (Wen & Dubé, 2022; Young et al., 2012). The five principles are visual aesthetic design, musical score, game mechanics, narrative, and incentive system. Visual aesthetic design refers to the in-game visual elements that create engaging and immersive experiences. Musical scores in educational games refer to background tunes, sound effects, and audio feedback to enhance players’ experiences and learning. Game mechanics refer to the interactions and rules that drive the gameplay. Narrative refers to storylines and contextual problem solving that enhance engagement. Finally, the incentive system refers to the rewards and penalties to induce motivation during gameplay. Educational game designers should consider these five aspects at all stages of game development. When thoughtfully applied to its design, emotional design principles can make the game “fun” and “appealing” to the players while keeping intact the intended learning objectives (Mayer & Estrella, 2014). We propose an approach based on the three theoretical aspects of educational game design. Critically, all three should consistently inform design decisions across all aspects of the game (Figure 1). They are applied iteratively and inform the design process at all stages, which can include level design, characters, and in-game problems.

Discipline-specific theory in GeometryBOX As described previously, we aimed to identify a discipline-specific theoretical framework aligned with the intended learning objectives (geometry learning) and the target audience (adolescents). Additionally, it should inform the progression of in-game pro blems and enable the thinking processes needed to achieve learning objectives. To address these aspects, we used the Van Hiele Model (VHM) of geometric thinking (Hiele, 1999) as the guiding discipline specific theory for our game. It is a hierarchical framework establishing the existence of five different levels of geometric thinking of increasing complexity (Arnal-Bailera & Manero, 2024). The increasing complexity of levels informed the increasing complexity of puzzles and specifically informed the “level design” of the game. Second, Hiele (1999) outlined the focus of the VHM on visual and non-verbal thinking and exploration which make it appropriate to inform the design of play-and game-based instruction. Finally, VHM is a topic-agnostic theoretical model. GeometryBOX targeted “classification of quadrilaterals”, a sub-topic in planegeometry, generally included in middle and secondary geometry curriculum (Vízek et al., 2025). Emotional design principles in GeometryBOX 65 Visual aesthetic design We used bright, warm, and contrasting colors in the visual design of the game. All the geometrical figures, that were the primary objects of gameplay, were bright purple in color to create a visually appealing interface (Wen & Dubé, 2023). The overall theme of “outer space” was consistently depicted through all the game objects. For example, the cursor was styled as a “crosshair”. Players solved geometrical puzzles to fix batteries and repair their ship. On the “progress map” screen of the game, the batteries were represented as “lightbulbs” that changed color from orange to green after successful completion of a puzzle. This aimed to create a sense of curiosity and breakdown the overall game into smaller goals inducing a sense of achievement  (Garg & Baroody, 2024). Musical score Audible feedback from a learning environment has the potential to enhance the perceived pleasantness of gameplay by extending the sensory experience (Nacke et al., 2010). A consistent, low, buzzing sound was used to convey the sound as their ship navigated outer space. Different types of audio feedback were used when players made correct/incorrect moves in the game, successfully completed a level, depleted their energy or shields, collected stars while navigating, and shot enemy objects. Dynamic sounds along with encouraging phrases like “You’re good” (with a bright audio cue) after a correct move and “Try again” (with a dull audio cue) after an incorrect move were used to keep the players motivated during gameplay (Cayari, 2022). This way, the audio was also aligned with the game situation to induce immersion and emotional engagement (Andersen et al., 2021). Such acoustic characteristics were used in the game to draw attention to important content associated with learning (Collins, 2009; Plass et al., 2020).

Game mechanics The central puzzle solving mechanic of the game was to “drag and drop” geometrical shapes into the correct bins to fix the batteries (Figure 2a, 2b). This built energy for the players’ ship to navigate and collect as many stars as possible. Sorting geometrical objects can engage students to build new knowledge based on their existing conceptions (Kobiela et al., 2018). It can help them identify and un derstand the properties of geometrical objects by noticing similarities and differences (Kobiela et al., 2018; Roth & Thom, 2009). Hence, sorting via “dragging and dropping” geometrical shapes into bins was built as the central game mechanic. Other game mechanics in the puzzles were primarily informed by the learning objectives at each level. New mechanics are introduced as players progress to puzzles at higher levels of the Van Hiele Model. They aim to sustain curiosity and enjoyment while preventing boredom and predictive gameplay (Loderer et al., 2020). They also create a sense of excitement as players progress in the game. For example, at puzzles from VHM level four, players are introduced to the “slicing” mechanic. They can break quadrilaterals into smaller diagonals by “slicing” them along a diagonal of their choice. Table 2 shows new mechanics introduced in puzzles at each new VHM level.  In addition to inducing positive emotions that aid learning, the game mechanics are introduced in a structured order that is informed by the discipline-specific theory, the VHM model. This avoids a mismatch between progression of gameplay and learning which can enhance self-efficacy and reduce negative emotions such as frustration (Plass et al., 2012). This is associated with the recommendations of (Masek et al., 2017) where “fun” and “excitement” in the game are fundamentally grounded in the problem solving associated with learning. Narrative Narrative in educational games can provide a framework for structural representation of problems (Dickey, 2011). There can be many ways of including narrative in learning games. This is based on the genre of game and core game-mechanics (Ke, 2016). Our game had a focus on mathematical learning outcomes. The core mechanic involved was “logic and thought during puzzle solving”. Additionally, it was categorized as a “casual puzzle” game as players solve geometrical puzzles to progress. In GeometryBOX, narrative was embedded via environmental storytelling and backdrop missions. Elements of the game environment focused on conveying the narrative theme of the game. Nomenclature for points, rewards, and penalties referred to the narrative theme. Additional gameplay puzzles for the game incentive system were created and were also situated in the overall narrative theme of the game (Figure 2c, 2d). In our game, narrative was integrated as a broadly defined goal with a background plot and adventure missions (Jenkins, 2002). An overarching narrative can create expectations about upcoming events and build suspense that sustains attention for learning  (McNamara et al., 2010). The narrative in GeometryBOX of “fixing batteries” to “repair the ship” and “navigate the outer space” was designed to contextualize learning and provide an overarching story connecting in-game progression, gameplay loop, rules, events, rewards, and penalties (Loderer et al., 2020). Through all these aspects, narrative was tightly and meaningfully associated with gameplay and learning. It was not included as an afterthought where the story and narrative are only depicted through one aspect (e.g., cut scene and videos) and not tied to gameplay (Naul & Liu, 66 2020). On the contrary, the primary narrative related objective of “fixing batteries” was tied to the central learning objective of solving geometrical puzzles.

Figure 2 Examples level screenshots from GeometryBOX Incentive system Rewards and penalties at the center of GBL can help keep the players motivated (Loderer et al., 2020). The incentive system in GeometryBOX is applied in three layers. First, every “game turn” of “dragging and dropping” an object costs a certain number of resources. In this way, players are nudged towards solving the puzzles in minimum possible moves to pres erve the available resources. Hence, they are incentivized for adopting mathematical strategies and discourage “trial and error” based strategy. Second, when  players deplete all their resources by making too many incorrect moves, they need to replenish it by completing another game level where they “shoot the debris to clear the boosters” (Figure 2d). This resets their available resources to 100%. This incentive system of managing resources and performance-based progress is dependent on individual learning and gameplay strategy. It aims to increase perceived autonomy and learner control and induce positive achievement emotions (McNamara et al., 2010). The third layer of incentive system is based on collecting “stars” to increase overall points. This layer in tends to enhance the value of learning through “internal compensation”, i.e. achievements in the game (Loderer et al., 2020). After players solve enough puzzles to bring their available resources above a certain threshold, new gameplay is unlocked in which the player controls their spaceship and steers it through an “asteroid field in outer space”. This three-layered, cyclic incentive system in GeometryBOX is intended to create positive achievement emotion of anticipation through feedback, novelty, and repe tition (Pekrun et al., 2014). It also aims to promote optimized gameplay linked with learning as thoughtful, strategized, and mathematical gameplay is rewarded.

In sum, GeometryBOX is designed for adolescents to understand the properties and relationships between special quadrilaterals. It is tightly aligned with the learning objectives associated with this topic. This aspect was primarily informed by the VHM, the  discipline-specific theory guiding its design. The gameplay and learning were integrated based on the five themes identified by Ke (2016) and are described via its LM-GM map. Emotional design principles were applied to all five features of the game to create a positive and enjoyable learning experience for the players.

This article suggests a three-prong approach to educational game design with an elaborate example of a math game. Educational game designers should follow an iterative process of applying this approach to their design. They should bring these three theoretical approaches together by going beyond previous approaches to educational 67 game design that only consider these aspects independently. In their review of existing educational game design frameworks, Sasupilli and colleagues (2019) identified six prevalent game design models. However, none of them focused on domain specific, play specific, and emotion specific theoretical foundations simultaneously. They were limited to either one or only two of these aspects. For example, the experiential gaming model (Kiili, 2005) does not consider blending learning through game mechanics. In the context of mathematics education, the application of a theoretical framework of math instruction is critical to promote students’ domain-specific mental models for learning (Pirnay-Dummer et al., 2012). This factor becomes much more critical in the context of math as it relies on students’ development of mental models of mathematical objects, their properties, and their relationships (G arg et al., 2025; Prayekti et al., 2020). Additionally, the use of Ke’s (2016) framework is needed to address the “what, how, where, and when learning is embedded” in games. This framework helps game designers answer broader questions about leaning in games. It informs design decisions like choice of game genre (simulation versus puzzle), absence or presence of narrative, narrative design (environmental backdrop versus roleplay), representation of learning content (content as game objects versus simulation of concepts), frequency of learning moments in games (exploration and discovery versus immediate feedback), and presence or absence of in-game learning supports. Further, at a finer level, the LM-GM framework (Arnab et al., 2015) helps designers address how all game features (game mechanics) are linked to learning mechanics. Creating an LM-GM map of the game during its design process is crucial to establish granular relationships between pedagogy and gameplay. Finally, to leverage the potential of games to induce emotional experiences for learning, the application of emotional design principles is essential. This can ensure a balance between fun and learning by fostering positive emotions (Byun & Joung, 2018). Specifically for mathematics, enhancing the overall learning experience can be transformative to address concerns with the negative perception of math as being too challenging and uninspiring (Wen & Dubé, 2023). Careful integration of emotional design principles that meaningfully and systematically induce enjoyment, anticipation, and excitement (Putwain et al.,

2018) can counter negative impressions of math like fear and anxiety (Budhathoki et al., 2022).

Navigating challenges While there are theoretical benefits of applying the three-prong framework to educational games, it also comes with its own challenges and opportunities. It is possible that there is overlap, friction, and conflict when certain game elements are informed by two approaches. The design process for GeometryBOX serves as an example to illustrate how such challenges can be overcome. This is mainly exhibited by two elements: game mechanics and narrative. We describe how all game mechanics were linked to learning via the LM-GM map of the game. The LM-GM map describes the way in which mechanics were implemented and their link with learning processes. In addition, a range of game mechanics were built to create anticipation and excitement as an outcome of the emotional design principles. Further, the addition of new mechanics to create anticipation and excitement was based on the increasing complexity of the discipline-specific VHM model. This way, the design of game mechanics was grounded in all three frameworks that each supported learning through different dimensions. Similarly, learning was integrated through an implicit narrative in the game via environmental storytelling, backdrop, textual and visual references to narrative theme. This was based on Ke’s (2016) framework. Narrative can be beneficial for math learning by providing learners with a framework for representation of problems (Irmayanti et al., 2024; Junkin, 2019). Here, narrative was not included as explicit framing but, rather, the action of solving geometry puzzles to achieve learning outcomes was implicitly linked to the game narrative (solving puzzles = fixing “batteries” = repairing “ship”). Moreover, narrative served as a tool to create an immersive and enjoyable learning experience based on the emotional design principles. This demonstrates how the inclusion of narrative was also informed by two distinct frameworks. Yet, it aimed to avoid contradictory and conflicting experiences. In this case, grounding the narrative in multiple theoretical argument s is likely to strengthen its effects on learning rather than weakening it.

The GeometryBOX game described in this article is an example of applying distinct but relevant theoretical frameworks of educational game design to create a geometry game. It bridges the gaps between cognitive and domain-specific, gameplay-specific, and emotional foundations of game-based learning. Thoughtful and implicit design of game features tightly tied to learning objectives have the potential to result in positive learning outcomes and make learning an enjoyable experience. We aim to  conduct evidence-based, experimental  studies on the effectiveness of GeometryBOX on geometrical understanding of adolescents. Game designers should consider this approach to create educational games that are not only grounded in theories of learning and instruction but also harness the power of play in a holistic way.
