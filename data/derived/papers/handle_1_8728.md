# The Effects of Affective Feedback on Learners’ Emotions in Asynchronous Video-Based Learning

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study examined the effects of affective feedback on learners’ boredom, confusion, and frustration in asynchronous video-based learning. Affective feedback messages and writing activity were used to deliver the utility value of learning topics. Facial expression data from 30 adult learners were collected and analyzed to identify changes in affective states. The findings suggested that the utility value message had a significant on reducing learners’ boredom.

## Introduction

During the COVID-19 pandemic, asynchronous video-ba sed learning was widely adopted in various learning situations to support emergency remote learning. De spite its flexibility, asynchronous video-based learning has been criticized due to the lack of interaction and delivery-centered content presentation. Learners with fewer selfregulation skills tend to experience various emotional states, including confusions, frustration and boredom that negatively influence learning processes and outcome s. In general, feedback approaches to influence onl ine learners’ emotions have been cognitively-oriented, such as the provision of question items. While cogn itive feedback is essential to help learners better under stand learning content, it is equally important for  learners to understand why they learn, such as the utility value of learning tasks, to sustain their attention and interest during online learning. Pekrun (2006) argues that achievement emotions are mainly influenced by two appraisals: control and value. Control refers to the perceived causal i nfluence over the learning process and outcomes suc h as the belief that learning will be completed through one' s efforts. Value refers to the perceived valence of  action and outcomes, such as the meaning that learners attribute to performing certain learning tasks. With this backdrop, this study focuses on the role of values on academic emotions, especially utility values’ effects on online learners’ affective state s. Utility value refers to how a task fits one’s fu ture plans (Wigfield & Eccles, 2000). This study used two appr oaches to increase the utility values perceived by learners who took a video lecture about big data: affective feedback message and writing activity. First, we de signed affective feedback messages to deliver the utility value of learning content and tasks. Second, we designed writing about the utility value of the lecture topic (i.e.,  big data) as a pre-learning activity. Writing or r eading about the utility value of a learning topic is effective to h elp learners establish relevance to learning. Among  various emotions, this study particularly focuses on boredom, confusion, and frustration that have been reported to have effects on engagement and disengagement in learning situations (D’Mello & Graesser, 2012). In sum, the goal of this study was to investigate the effect of utility  value feedback and writing activity on learners’ b oredom, confusion, and frustration in the situation of asynchronous video-based learning.

## Method

The participants were 30 Korean adult learners (20-35 years old) recruited via convenience sampling. T he participants were randomly assigned to one of three  groups: control group, feedback only group, feedba ck, and writing group. The feedback only group received feedback messages during watching the video lecture. Based on previous research, we designed four types of utility value messages: (a) utility for daily life (e.g., Data analysis is closely related to our lives. Let's try a little ha rder!), (b) utility for jobs (e.g., Data analytics is a competency required by various jobs. Let's study hard until th e end!), (c) utility for membership groups (e.g., D ata analysis can be useful at school or work. Shall we do our best to study?), and (d) social utility (e.g., Data analysis is a good topic for conversations with people from different disciplines. You are doing very well!).  In contrast, the feedback and writing group performed an additional task to w rite about the utility value of big data analysis w ithin 250 words before watching the video lecture. We conduct ed the Wizard of Oz (WoZ) experiment in a quiet roo m where the participants interacted with the online learning system controlled by the researchers. The participants were asked to verbally express their emotions (emot e aloud protocol). In a separate room, we listened to their verbalizations and send out online feedback message s when verbalizations included words related to bor edom, confusion, and frustration (see Figure 1). Corpus grouping respective to each emotion was done before the WoZ experiment (i.e., boredom: I want to quit). For dat a collection and analysis, we recorded learners’ fa cial expressions and analyzed facial muscle movements called Action Units (AUs) based on the Facial Action Coding System (FACS) to identify learners’ emotional states.

## Results

We extracted 1,671 AUs from the recordings and then  AUs less than three seconds were excluded as trans itory states. This left 1,123 AUs for final data analysis. Boredom in the control group was the highest (M = 31.00, SD = 14.40), followed by the feedback only group (M = 16.50, SD = 12.78) and the feedback and writing group (M = 11.50, SD = 6.17) (Table 1). As there was a significant corr elation between the frequency of frustration and confusion (r =.38, p <.05), MANOVA was conducted to investigate the differences in the frequencies of emotions. There was a significant difference in the frequency of boredom among the groups. Scheffé’s test result suggested that the frequencies of boredom between the experiment groups and the control group were statistically significant (p <.05). No significant differences across the groups were found in confusion and frustration. ## Conclusion and implications

Among three types of emotions, affective feedback had significant effects on learners’ boredom while the writing activity had no effect on all three emotional states. As boredom is known to be closely related to disengagement, the feedback assisted learners to remain more engaged. While the existing research suggests that confusion and frustration turn into boredom (D’Mello & Graesser, 2012), the participants tended to experience boredom from the beginning. Follow-up interviews indicated that the participants needed more control over the learning process such as choosing the relevant topic. This finding i mplies that coupling utility value and control is e ssential to prevent learners from easily entering the boredom s tatus from the early stage of online learning. We a lso discovered that the utility messages motivated the participants to remain focused as they emphasized t he usefulness of the video. We plan to conduct a follo w-up study to further investigate the coupling of control and values and the effects of utility messages.
