# “I Guess It Makes Me Feel Better About Myself”: High-Achievement of Adolescent Girls in Middle School

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This research explores how high-achieving middle school girls see themselves as learners and how academic performance shapes their sense of self. Aiming to provide a nuanced understanding of the stresses, pressures, and coping strategies these girls experience, the study uses the Listening Guide method, a feminist, qualitative, voice-centered approach to discovery through listening. Data include multiple interviews with 19 high-achieving middle school girls from a large city in the northeastern United States, highlighting four key narratives. The findings contribute to the larger academic discourse on the extreme levels of daily stresses these girls experience and adds novel insights to how it affects their sense of self as learners, the coping mechanisms they adopt, and the impact of rigidly adhering to a high-achieving identity.

## Introduction

Adolescent girls in the United States, especially h igh-achieving middle school girls, are experiencing sig nificant challenges to their psychological well-being. Research shows a sharp decline in self-esteem during adolescence, with girls’ self-confidence dropping from 68% of the girls feeling confident in who they are at the start of middle school to 50% of girls by the end (Hinkelman, 2023). Amongst fifth grade girls, 76% report feeling the y might “explode” from pressures (e.g. academic, familial, social) and 57%  feel that they are not smart enough for their career aspirations. Additionally, 67% of middle school girls report not speaking their minds in order to be liked by others (Hinkelman, 2023), a behavior that aligns with traditional gendered ex pectations and can signal psychological distress (Brown & Gilligan, 1992). These issues persist among high-achieving girls, as 67% of girls with a 4.0 or higher GPA average refrain from speaking their mind to others, 40% feel they are not smart enough for their career aspirations, 79% report that they feel they might “explode” from the  pressures, and 84% do everything in their power to avoid failing or messing up (Hinkelman, 2023). These alarming statistics emphasize the need for educators and researchers to engage in  the study of middle school girls and their confide nce development, especially high-achieving girls, rather than overlooking them. Alth ough high-achieving girls often perform well both socially and academically in school, this success can lead to the assumption that they  do not require the same support typically given to student s who face more visible challenges. That’s why it i s essential to examine the complex factors that shape their sense of self and confidence to  better understand how to support a psychologically healthy development as learners.

This study builds on prior work  (d’Anjou et al., 2024)  in examining the complex relationship high-achieving middle school girls have with themselves as learners, exploring how their academic performance shapes their sense of self and whether the pressures of su ccess contribute to a form of dissociation from th eir own knowledge about themselves (e.g. thoughts, feelings  rooted in their senses). Using the Listening Guide  (LG) method (Gilligan & Eddy, 2021), a feminist, qualitative approach to psychological i nquiry, this research deconstructs shared narratives to uncover the psych ological logic underlying the paradox of achievemen t and its impact on girls’ sense of self as learners. This work contributes to the larger academic discourse highlighting the extreme stress experienced by high-achieving girls (e.g. Stentiford et al., 2023)  and adds novel insights to  how this stress manifests in their self-perceptions, the coping mechanisms they employ, and  the psychological effects of adhering to the often rigid, self-imposed label of high achievement on their academic journeys. This work urges schools, practitioners, and parents to support high-achieving middle school girls by continuing to actively listen to their experiences, fostering educational environments that prioritize curiosity and resilience, and showcasing diverse pathways to success and personal fulfillment.

## Background

Since the 1950’s, girls and women have dramatically surpassed their male counterparts in educational attainment and school performance (Buchmann et al., 2008), contributing to an overrepresented group of high-achieving girls compared to boys and predominantly participating in Advanced Placement (AP) and college preparatory courses in high school (Buchmann et al., 2008; DiPrete & Buchmann, 2013). High-achieving girls’ experiences, often due to their social and academic success, have (histori cally) been overlooked in research (Pepperell & Rubel, 2009), while some researchers (e.g. Bain & Bell, 2004) have urged for research on this group. Recently, more researchers has shifted their focus to include high-achieving girls and their mental and emotional well-being, illustrating how they experience extreme levels of normalized stress (Spencer et al., 2018; Stentiford et al., 2023). Stentiford et al.

(2023) in a qualitative literature review on girls’ mental health and academic achievement show that the pressures

on girls include: fears for the future; parental/fa mily-related pressures; competitive school cultures; gen dered expectations; imbalances in the extreme of experienced pressures. Additionally, the intersectional experiences of high-achieving girls of color have been studied in depth in recent years (e.g. Mayes et al., 2024). Prior related work indicated that high-achieving girls often have paradoxically low levels of self-concept (Preckel & Brüll, 2008; Rudasill et al., 2009) a striking contrast to their academic success and to the self-concept levels of other student groups. While several theor ies have been proposed to explain this phenomenon, such as the imposter phenomenon (Muradoglu et al., 2022)  and perfectionism (Flett & Hewitt, 2002), the underlying causes remain unclear. To untangle this paradox, mo re research is needed that centers on girls' subjec tive understanding of their self-concept, using an interdisciplinary lens that inclu des psychology, sociology, and education. This interdisciplinary approach is cruci al for examining how social and cultural narratives  around gender and achievement influence individual psychol ogical experiences in educational settings. Girls o f color in particular face the compounding effects to their intersecting identities from dominant cultural narratives, which can impact the support they receive for their development in schools (Nasir, 2011; Smith-Purviance, 2021). The theory on girls' psychological development guiding this research originates from the work of Brown and Gilligan (1992). They explain that as girls approach adolescence, they have a relational acuity rooted in their sensory experiences  (e.g. seeing, hearing, feeling, and thinking) which informs their understanding of relationships. However, as they transition through adolescence, external voices, those of society and culture that promote notions of the "ideal" girl and "ideal" rel ationships, begin to permeate their inner lives, often clashing with their own sensory knowledge. As adolescence pr ogresses, many girls struggle to reconcile these conflicting perspectives, leading their own inner voice to retr eat, as it might disrupt the social and relational norms they encounter. With time, this internal voice, grounded in sensory experiences, may become difficult to access or even forgotten; in other words, dissociated from. Investigating such internal psych ological processes, influenced by individuals situated within social and cultural narratives, is at the heart of this research.

## Methods

This work examines the following research questions: How high-achieving middle school girls see themselves as learners? What can we learn from psychological processes observed during their reflection? Through examining their psychological processes, we aim to understand  the influences on what the girls know, struggle to know or forget about themselves as learners. These question s require a methodological approach that allows us to glean both psychological processes and dominant social and cultural narratives the girls are embedded in. Em ploying the Listening Guide (LG) methodology (Gilligan & Ed dy, 2021) —a feminist, relational, and voice-centered approach to discovery research —the research aims to uncover how individuals naviga te conflicts, emotionally charged topics, and societal power  dynamics (Cruz, 2021; Tolman & Head, 2021)  by analyzing their psychological logic. The underlying assumption for this is that people’s sense of self is intricately connected to the relationships and culture we reside in (Cruz, 2021). Within the LG methodology, one’s voice is seen as the bridge between the individual, the relational and the sociocultural and refers to the physical and em bodied communication of the psyche (Tolman & Head, 2021) to others. This means that through physical voice, an individual can communicate their inner feelings and thoughts to others (Sorsoli & Tolman, 2008). As this physical voice can provide a pathway to someone’s psyche, it enables the researcher to analyze someone’s psychological l ogic, as they  convey both intentional information (such as choosing their words, tonality and pitch), and unintentional (or even unconscious) information, e.g. pausing, word slips, stumbles (Tolman & Head, 2021). The LG understands the multidimensionality of one’s  communication and cultural discourse practices as a process of ne gotiation (e.g. Bakhtin, 1981; Billig et al., 1988)  within each conversation that occurs both consciously and unconsciously (Tolman & Head, 2021). The LG's unique strengths lie in conceptualizing narration as not only what, but how people voice their narratives and how deeper la yers of the expression of the psyche can be uncovered through attuning ourselves to the multidimensionality of voices.

## Data collection and analysis

The participants that informed this study were 19 h igh-achieving middle school girls (ages 11-14), in a large, socioeconomically, and culturally diverse city in the northeast of the United States. However, this paper highlights the narratives of four key informants, namely Millie (age 12, 6th grade), Nora (age 14, 8th grade), Aelia (age 13, 8th grade), Elise (age 11, 5th grade) as they most strongly informed us in our process of coming-to-know to the answer to our research questions. Each participant has a different socioeconomic background, and most went to a variety of schools (e.g. private, charter, public, specialized public). Each informant participated in  two semi-structured, individual and in-depth interviews (45-60 min per interview) and an individual discussion meeting, in public places of their choosing, resulting in 38 au dio-recorded interviews. Each informant chose their own pseudonym and is referred to by that pseudonym. Our co-author Sophie transitioned from pilot-study participant to co-researcher, providing a unique layer of self-reflection and interpretation during the data analysis process. The LG method includes several steps of analysis th at are called ‘listenings’ (in contrast to traditio nal coding), each of which focuses attention on a different aspect of the psychological, social, cultural narratives and movements of the psyche of the informant during the  interviews. In the first ‘listening’, we listened to the interview narratives, what the narrative characters  and plots are, what was absent or not addressed and any emotional hotspots or any telling metaphors used. I n this listening, we  heard girls’ narratives on how they see learning, themselves as learners, high achievement  in school, uncertainties or certainties they know a bout themselves and any influences on how they see thems elves. In the second ‘listening’, we listened to the firstperson voice, tracking the movements of the psyche throughout t he interview by extracting only ‘I-statements’ from the interviews (extracting “I” + attached verb) to compose a poem. The way the I moves throughout  the interview, the rhythm, and disruptions of the I, wi thout contextual information, helps us to follow one’s psychological processes that lay bare one’s psychol ogical logic, that might be hard to discern otherwise. In the third ‘listening’, we listened to the multiplicity of voices within the girls’ narratives that speak to the research question and their interactions (e.g. are they harmonious or  opposing) to reveal additional nuances to the psychological logic of how the girls navigate their sense of self as learners embedded i n social and cultural narratives. Each listening step is layered with per sonal researcher responses and self-reflection notes, as the relationship each researcher has with the data is o f integral importance to the sense-making process. Lastly, we have assembled the evidence collected from each of the prior steps and composed an analysis of the psychological logic of our participants in relation to the topics of learning, seeing yourself as a learner and achievement.

## Results and discussion

What sprung out at us during our first listening to  the girls’ narratives, was the severity of stress and anxiety experienced during their high academic performances  in school, which we will lay out in the following section. Nora, eighth grade, has always achieved at the high est level of her competitive specialized public sch ool while simultaneously dealing with severe anxiety, OCD and insomnia since she was little. Recently, Nora self-disclosed experiencing a new type of stress related to her ac hievement: as she started taking anxiety medication, her drive for academic achievements has wavered, bringing her  a different type of stress from her lowered perfor mance. The severity of this new type of stress was express ed through an extreme potential sacrifice sh e was willing to make in order to maintain her high academic performance as she shared “and honestly, I feel like the next 8 or so years, pretty much from like now until I’m like 22,  defines the path of my life [..] when considering like a job or even in like college applications, it’s very depend ent on like grades. So I would honestly rather have  not great mental health, but have a motivator to do well, at least for the next 8 years..then have really not gr eat grades and feel better.” It showed us the high  stakes of stress experienced by girls like Nora, and the importance of their current academic performance on the impact of their  future life goals (“getting into a good college” a nd “getting a good career”). Nora also notes that she is “deathly afraid of like..not being able to even support myself or feeling like..comparing myself to other family members and feeling like I failed in life, based on either acad emic or financial achievements.. [pause] yeah it’s scary.” The impact of uncertainty around their future life goals, needing to be able to create a “good life” for herself, is felt with a level of emotional intensity that seems disproportionate with day-to-day achievements in middle school.

For Aelia during her eighth grade class in a compet itive specialized public school, the severity of st ress felt was related to the importance of high grades f or her college applications and how aware she is of  the expectations of what is needed for a successful college application: “they [college admission offices] look a lot at how much you’ve struggled and how much you’ve come through things and how you overcame struggle and how you’ve shown leadership [..] In a lot of high schoo ls they joke that you need  to cure cancer, to get into college, because they expect you to do like..incredible things by the time you are like 16. And..they expect you to be wellrounded.” Secondly, she shares feeling this type of stress and pressure  based on her father’s life’s achievements. Her father had risen from a low socioeconomic backg round coming to the USA as an immigrant to a high socioeconomic status for him and his family, statin g “[..] if I’m not successful, then I put all my dad’s work  to waste.”. Aelia also struggles with self-disclosed mental health struggles, such as OCD, anx iety attacks and two eating disorders. She noted that it’s hard for her to maintain a healthy balance in juggling her schoo l work, extracurriculars and mental health struggles, leadi ng to a sense of profound exhaustion, mentioned by her as “I think another thing is definitely like the exhausti on of being a student. Like it's very hard to like,  maintain and balance like everything that we have to do as stude nts. So, I'd say like stress, sleep, like social life and li ke a lot of things like, it's hard to be able to do everything including like, extracurriculars and stuff.” Elise talks about the stress she feels during her f ifth grade in a competitive chart school as having a snowballing effect on her learning process: when sh e studies more, she gets less sleep, leading her to  feel more anxious, which again leads her to study more etc. She also mentions the impact of anxious thoughts when she get a bad grade and the day-to-day perceived repercussions on her future, seemingl y out of place for an 11-year old, as she states “I'll be like, if I don't, um, get a good grade, then I can't do like this activity beca use some schools you have to get like really good grades on a test or something for like high school. And then I think like if I don't get into a good high school, I can't go to a good l ike college and then I'll be like stuck at some col lege that's not really good.” Interestingly, when asked if she hears any of the (future-oriented) pressures to achieve academically from others (e.g. her teachers, parents, friends), she comments that it’s mostly herself t hat enacts the pressu re: “I'm feeling nervous about something, it can be my own self that kind of brings me down. And since I wanna get such good grades, I'll be like, if I don't then like bad stuff happens I guess.” The experience of perceiving pressure to achieve high academically as coming from within has been echoed by every girl, except for Aelia. For Millie in a sixth-grade class in a public school, the importance of achievi ng highly in school is connected to how she feels about herself in the moment. For example, when she doesn’t achieve as highly as usual or if a classmate scored higher than her, she’ll ha ve thoughts like “I am not like..I guess not good e nough.” She also elaborates that these thoughts and/or feelings  are not directly vocalized to her by others, but t hat it seems to be felt from within, for example “I've never really had anyone tell me that, you know, I didn't do som ething well enough. But I just think that myself when I like, h ear what other people get or something.” Her stress  for high achievement in school seems to be related to those internal feelings about herself and wanting to do well to maintain that feeling: “it just proves to me that I am good at stuff or like I am smart. Um, so I just guess it makes me feel better about myself when I do. And like, yo u know, I don't want to feel worse about myself whe n I don't get good grades.” Overall, all girls discussed high levels of stress and anxiety, as well as pressure to achieve highly for their future career aspirations or for validati on of their own self-confidence with their own specific nuances. Sense of self as learners For each of the girls, how they see themselves as l earners is unique: being a “good learner” for Aelia, a “ high achiever” learner for Elise, a “not super hardworking” learner for Millie and a “procrastinating” learner for Nora. Aelia defines herself as a “good learner,” which sh e describes as her knowing “what I need to do to su cceed [..] and what is expected of people to do to succeed,” i n other words knowing how to ‘play the education ga me’ as a learner. For Elise, being a “ high achiever” learner means ha ving the desire and pressure present for academic success, as she mentions she “really tries to get a  really high grade.” In contrast, Millie defines he rself as a “not super hardworking” learner and appears to be in con flict in wanting to achieve her goals  of academic high achievement, while not wanting to work hard towards them. Lastly, Nora  struggles to define herself as a learner, most likely due to her recent effects of anxiety me dication on how she navigates school work and achie vement. She mentions that her struggles with procrastination seems to be her most prominent way of defining herself as a learner, describing it as “in an academic environment I think I put a lot of stuff off and I tend to drift off a lot.” When analyzing the I-poems of the girls’ responses to the question on ho w they see themselves as learners (see Figure 1 below) to understand how the  psyche moves when thinking about itself  in this way, we noticed the following. For Aelia, as we observe in the I-poem, the presence of the second person you-voice and third person they-voice shows us how interwoven the cultural and social narratives of what a learner should be is with how she sees herself. For example, “they want”, “they expect you to”, “you need to” is connected to her own knowing “I know” and her own needs “I need to”. The  terminology used in describing being a “good learn er” is inherently connected to the social/cultural narrati ves of others of what is deemed to be “good.”  There is also a notable pattern present in her I-poem, namely the repetition of “I think I am”, whic h sounds to us that there is a level of uncertainty or insecurity in how she can express who she is. She ends her I-poem with “I want to be”, noting her desire for her future goals, seeing herself being in relationship with her envisioned and aspiring self. In contrast to Aelia’s I-poem, Elise’s I-poem illustrates how the stress/pressure to achieve  comes from within. Elise’s I-poem has a clear forward motion with “I really try”, “I am pressuring”, “I am trying,” indicating a dominant desire of her psyche to keep pushing thr ough whatever hurdles she is encountering, sometimes at the cost of her physical well-being as she mentions “I didn’t sleep”. We also noted the occurrence of future tenses in the final stanza of her poem, e.g. “I will be,” ind icating that she has a strong connection to her pro jections of her future self in how she currently sees herself. For Millie and Nora, a stark contrast in the I-poems emerged. Both of their I-poems are littered with negations, e.g. “I want to”  followed by “I don’t want t o” for Millie and “I am” followed by “I am not” for Nora, indicating an inte rnal struggle. This internal process of negotiation seems to be happening for both girls, navigating their desires and what they are or are not. For Nora, the amount of not knowing and not thinking shows us her dominant unce rtainty and/or hesitancy in her current self-perception due to her major life changes. The mention of not wanting suggests a type of certainty provided by knowing or resisting something she does not want to do. For Millie’s I-poem, the d ominant motion of the poem is a back-and-forth negotiation between what she wants and the external  needs and expectations she is aware of (“I know”) and is resisting, e.g. “I have to” repetition and “I don’t want to”. When her own desire “I want to” comes up, it is either negated immediately by “I don’t want to” or it is h esitant sounding due to the “I think”, “I mean” lea ding up to its expression. Our answers to these questions w ere discovered when listening to the contrapuntal voices in Millie’s interviews. Millie wasn’t displaying contrapuntal voices in th e same way the other girls had; there was a strong consistency throughout both interviews in tone, pitch, and cadence of her voice. In many interviews, girl s often express rehearsed-sounding voices echoing societal and school values (e.g., Nora’s comment: “people who come from the elementary school are seen as..not as inte lligent”). These voices are frequent ly interrupted by more authentic ones, revealing hidden, contradictory exp eriences. However, in Millie’s interview, there is a striking absence of a rehearsed voice; instead, she offers a radical ly honest perspective, sharing ideas, feelings, and experiences about learning and school that remain true to herself. The voice sounded monotone, seemingly bored, strained, exhausted, resigned, uninspired. And I think I just like the feeling that I did good. Like, I like the idea that I guess I'm good at it. That I am smart or something. Like, I think I just like that feeling rather than the actual lik e, learning part. ” As the interview progressed, Millie began to reveal that essentially her sense of confidence is dependent on her performance in school and reputati on as a “smart person.” She viewed anything below her standard as a threat to what she believes about herself to be true: that she is naturally intelligent, for example on an assig nment she wasn’t interested in doing and she got a lower grade than usual she commented “I guess in social s tudies we had to write like a paragraph about a per son and like, I think for everything except for like grammar, I got like a three on. So like I knew that was my fault, but I still kind of disappointed myself for not being able to like, be naturally good at it I guess” and “I also think that I'm good at not having to wo rk. Like, I wouldn't say gifted, but like, naturall y like able to do something, you  know, academically.” Millie’s attitude towards natu ral competence and her work ethic is contrary to most other girls’ perspectives, except for Nora. Nora echoes the sentiments about natural ability, as she repeats statements like “I wish that I was as n aturally good at math as I am on random European monarchies from the 15th century” and “I think I’m pretty smart naturally but if I apply myself I could definitely do better.” The radical honesty voice of Millie, which speaks o f valuing the reaffirmation of this “high achieving ” label or element of her identity as a learner over the actual learning process, led us to see the way the girls see themselves as learners in a new light. If the girls  experience rigidity in the label of “ high achieving” as part of their identity, it would explain how destabilizing of an experience any evidence that would jeopardize that label is. In personal reflection upon the voices of the girls, co-author Sophie, who has been part of the study as a current high-achieving high school girl, discovered throughout t his analysis process how the rigidity of the label “highachieving” has been present for her for years. She wrote in a personal reflection: “The truth is that the vast majority of my academic  stress is generated from myself and has persisted for years due to my own obsession with my  self-perception. I have been labeled as “smart” since I was in kindergarten and rarely struggled academically since. As someone who, like many of these girls, searches for consistency in my life, I q uickly became attached to this intangible idea of smartness. It has consumed my mind for over ten years now. Every bad grade, no matter how trivial, is completely destabilizing, and the idea of having to work hard to achieve a high mark is a sign of my deteriorating intellige nce, as effort of course implies natural incompetence. I vividly remember receiving a bad gr ade in the beginning of eighth grade and being unable to recognize that it was not disastrou s. For the next week, I got nauseated every time I walked past a mirror because I didn’t even recognize or feel like myself. [...] Each of the girls has created a strict criterion for themselves of what constitutes success and landing outside of that range, even slightly, is obsessed over for days. Once this identity has been established, there is no going back. It clings to you until you can learn to detach” (Sophie, co-author) An important part of how this label of “high achieving” is created seems to be found in past experiences, that reinforce the need to hold on to this label, possibly due to the humandesire for consistency and certainty. For example, Aelia mentions that “I think as I’ve always known that I wanted to keep having good  grades in school and I’ve always known that I’ve been a very like, h igh-goals type of person. I think that ever since I was  little, especially coming from [redacted], all my family is  like ‘you’re going to be successful, you’re going to do this, you’re going to the next..’ I’ve always been like ‘Okay I’m going to do that’.” Once these labels have been (self)prescribed, there is little room for deviation as it would threaten the sense of certainty and self-understanding. How each girl deals with any threats to the label o f high-achievement and/or intelligence as part of their id entity as learners is different: how steep the sacrifice is that Nora is willing to make with her mental health to hold on to the label of being high-achieving; how Millie is seen to protect herself th rough not wanting to put much effort into school work; how Elise prefers to avoid topics or challenges such as math by rejecting them as assets to their future goals; how Aelia is unwilling to give any activity up that leads to her label of high-achieving to maintain a healthier mental and physical balance. Consequently, it could inhibit personal curiosity, a goal schoo ls strive to promote, as exploring unfamiliar and challenging concepts may possibly result in outcomes inconsistent with their achievement criteria. The desire to know who we are  and what defines us is a natural human process, bu t girls’ experiences involving such intensely emotional and mental struggles while achieving high ly academically need to be paid more attention to both from an academic and practitioner perspective.

## Implications

In providing a detailed and psychologically layered  account of girls’ narratives in how they navigate high levels of stress in their daily lives, this study adds dep th to related work (Spencer et al., 2018; Stentifor d et al., 2023), which emphasized the extreme levels of normalized s tress experienced by high-achieving middle school girls. The dangers of continued exposure to chronic high l evels of stress are well known, such as affecting b rain structures (e.g. Mariotti, 2015), increased risk of chronic illness (Selye, 1950)  and burn-out (e.g. Salmela-Aro et al., 2009). Therefore, it is imperative to not only study these  stressors further but also develop practical interventions to support these girls in navigating their pressures effectively. A key finding of this study is the interwoven natur e of stress, the girls’ conception of self and the destabilizing effect of any evidence that questions  their current sense of self. This research highlig hts how these girls respond to perceived threats to their sense of self as a high-achiever in various ways: through a more fixed mindset towards intellectual ability  (Dweck, 2006) for Nora and Millie, internalization of external and systemic expectations for Aelia, or avoidance of future inte llectual challenges for Elise. The rigidity of this  identification with high achievement may contribute to a cycle of stress and anxiety that discourages risk-taking, which is known to foster resilience and self-confidence (Fannin et al., 2024; Sandseter & Kennair, 2011). B y failing to address these issues, we risk reinforcing a system where hi gh-achieving girls are caught in a cycle of playing it  safe, inadvertently impacting their self-confidence development. Given these findings, there is a pressing need to t ranslate insights from this research into concrete strategies for educators, policymakers, and parents. The girls in this study noted that while schools and teachers already provide seminars on perfectionism and imposter syndrome, such interventions do not seem to adequately address the core issues they face. The American hig her education admissions process often operates on a system that seeks reasons, however minor, to reject students. These high-achieving girls are highly attuned to this reality, making localized interventions that encourage failure and intellectual risk-taking seem irrelevant in a system that actively discourages such behaviors. Ideally, the higher education system itself needs to shift away from a model that punishes mistakes and reinforces perfection, i nstead fostering an environment where intellectual curiosity and resilience are valued over flawlessness.  In the meantime, effective strategies should not on ly focus on changing the girls’ mindsets but also address their  relationship to the broader systemic pressures the y navigate. This means working to challenge cultural narratives  that equate success with academic achievement and instead fostering alternative definitions of success: ones rooted in curiosity, intellectual exploration,  and personal fulfillment. Rather than asking these girls to change within an unchanging system, interventions should help them develop a sense of self-worth beyond external validation, allowing them to engage in learning for its own sake. While this work is not intended to be generalizable, it is the first step in untangling and understand ing the impact of high levels of daily stress, academic  achievements and its impact on a developing identi ty as a learner. Our recommendation for future work is to e xpand the scope of this study to examine the longit udinal effects of stress on high-achieving girls’ sense of themselves as learners th roughout middle and high school, providing a more generalizable understanding of the se developmental trajector ies. Given the positive impact of involving high-achieving girls in the research and sense-making process  (e.g. through a participatory action research approach), researchers, educators, and policymakers are strongly encouraged to do so as well.

## Conclusion and contribution

This research examines the complex relationship hig h-achieving middle school girls have with themselves as learners, exploring how their academic performance shapes their sense o f self. This work adds to prior work alerting the field to the extreme high levels of stress experienced daily by high-achieving middle school girls and adds shows how  stress manifests itself in thoughts and feelings th e girls have about themselves as learners. Through illuminating the psychological layers to how high-achieving girls see themselves as learners and their coping mechanisms, this study suggests the idea of rigidity of an often-self-prescribed identifying label of high achievement and its destabilizing effects to how the girls navigate their academic careers. As a first step, we urge schools, educators, and parents to actively listen to high-achieving girls’ experiences of stress and the copi ng mechanisms they employ in relation to their learnin g and self-identity. Providing the appropriate emotional support and addressing significant stressors is cru cial in helping them navigate these challenges. Whi le we work toward necessary systemic changes, it is essential to create educational spaces for middle school girl s where curiosity, exploration, and intellectual risk-taking are prioritized, and where making mistakes is recognized as an important step in developing resilience and self-awareness as learners. Furthermore, we emphasize the importance of showcasing the diverse pathways to success, whic h can help counterbalance the pressure of a singula r, achievement-based model of success, thereby supporting the girls’ evolving identities as learners.
