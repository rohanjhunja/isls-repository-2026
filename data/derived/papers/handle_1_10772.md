# “Listen to Each Other Through the Whole Self”: Silence, Stillness, and Collective Tuning in Theatre Learning

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In a black box theater on a university campus, ten actors stand in a line, shoulder to shoulder. Together, they learn how to do something in perfect unison– without ever visibly communicating. This article centers the methodological tensions that arose in using dominant forms of analysis to understand this ensemble event and investigates the possibility of alternate, relational interpretations that may make the actors’ collective learning visible.

Act I, Scene I: Crossing the Threshold The following activity was observed  and recorded  as part of an ongoing ethnographic study of an adva nced university theatre movement class: In a black box theater on a university campus, I am standing in a corner beside a camera. Today, as part of their Viewpoints training, the instructor has asked the ten actors in his class to form a line, shoulder to shoulder. He tells them to imagine a line in front of them– a boundary– that they must cross one at a time. Once they have replicated thei r line on the opposite side of the boundary, they must step forward together and begin to move through the space. The task fails if anyone is left behind.

“Off you go,” he says, and moves to the corner of the room. A deep silence settles over the group. The air grows thick and expectant. I cannot move. My eyes scan the room back and forth.

Seven long seconds pass in stillness. I give in to the unknown. Then, one actor steps forward. A pause.

Then, another. Suddenly, nine actors stand in a line past the threshold. As the tenth actor takes her step towards the boundary, however, the line moves without her. I relax behind the camera. “Go back,” the instructor says, “Close, close.” He pauses to let the actors settle into their line again. “Listen to each other through the whole self,” he says, “It’s about listening.” Silence once again falls on the group. They soften their gazes. I freeze. There is only stillness.

And then– one actor presses across the boundary. Then, another. Then another. The final actor steps forward, and they are with. They’ve done it, and they know they have. Together, they begin to move through the space.

## Background

For those who aren’t familiar with the practice, Viewpoints is a name given to a theatre movement technique that gives actors an embodied language for their body in time and space (Bogart & Landau, 2004). Within Viewpoints exercises, actors practice becoming  aware with their entire bodies, rather than just their dominant senses, surrendering to letting something occur (rather than “making it occur,” p. 19), and sharing responsibility as cocreators of the work they engage in. Given the nonhierarchical and collaborative nature of Viewpoints, it is also a well-known toolin theatre for creating ensembles: a way of bringing together a group of individual actors and making them into a whole. Interactions within Viewpoints exercis es are unconventional, expressive, and abstracted, with almost every exercise happening in silence and, frequently, without ever making eye contact. Instead, actors maintain a heightened sense of awareness of each other through “soft focus”– a state in which they soften the eyes so that they may use the whole body to gather information (Bogart & Landau, 2004, p. 31-33). This heightened awareness allows them to feel for various more-than-human entities (e.g., architecture or shape) and energies (e.g., spatial relationship or kinesthetic responses) within the room in order toinform their actions and respond as an ensemble.

In the vignette presented above, the ten actors are learning how to enact one of these ensemble tasks. On the surface level, they are learning how to cross a boundary. However, because the task cannot be completed unless the group is in perfect synchrony, there is a layer of  invisible complexity. In order to move together, the group must develop an emergently learned and shared sense of space– who will move– and time– when they will move– all in silence. How do we begin to make sense of this kind of learning? We know from existing literature that learning is social and embodied. This embody-hood also includes the myriad of interactions, gestures, and feelings that accompany learning with a lived body (Cole & Engeström, 1995; Erickson, 2004; Merleau-Ponty, 1965). In the learning sciences, we have well-worn grooves that make sense of embodi ed learning through interactionist lenses, particularly focused on the ways in whi ch embodied actions signal learning (Goodwin, 2017). These approaches also, in thei r structuralist way, make sense of collectives by breaking them down into bite-size pieces where individual actions interact and indicate something about the whole. Additionally, while some scholars have begun to note that feeling is an important part of learning (Jaber & Hammer, 2016), we are only beginning to theorize what  feeling as learning might look like, a question that affect theorists wrestle with under the various umbrellas of attunement (Stewart, 2011), resonance (Meyer et al., 2017), and vitality (Stern, 2010).

However, togetherness itself has been historically under-analyzed in the learning sciences beyond “group” analysis– which I distinguish from “ensemble” activity as the difference between considering a collection of single individuals (group) and a collective as one ensemble engaging in activity (Ćosić, 2023). Ma & Hall

(2016) have made strides in the direction of ensemble learning, outlining a framework for understanding learning

that is necessarily done together and in the act of doing together. However, little else exists to make sense of learning to be an ensemble through feeling together.

## Methods and analysis

In the spirit of existing ensemble learning analysis (Ma & Hall, 2016), I initially approached the activity depicted in Act I, Scene I, through the lens of interaction analysis (Jor dan & Henderson, 1995). In order to make the infrastructure of this ensemble visible– that is, how these actors are negotiating a shared sense of space and time– I looked for the moment when their infrastructure broke down. This “tear”  (Ma & Hall, 2016), a rupture in the ensemble, is evident at the millisecond level of footage playback: In the first round of their exercise, one actor steps too soon and breaks apart the collective (Figure 2). However, what happens after this moment– the “repair”– is far less clear. The actors return to their line, and the ins tructor reminds them to “Listen to each other with the whole self.” In the video record following this, suddenly, the actors move in perfect synchrony. Figure 2 A “Tear” in the Ensemble’s Togetherness Note. The actor on the right steps before the actress to his left crosses the threshold. Further focusing on the instructor’s guidance to the actors helped me make sense of how they corrected their course: Rather than steering the focus onto  any single, individual action (“Slow down, Charlie,” perhaps, would have been the instruction  to address the actor on the far right’s eagerness) or an interacti on between individuals (“Pay attention to the person standing next to you,” perhaps, which would  have divvied the relationship of the whole into easier-to-manage groupings), the instructor urges the actors to listen to each other– a collective act. In giving this direction, rather than any one interaction leading the fold, the instructor extends the responsibility of the task to the whole. In beginning to listen, the actors engage in a collective doing. If the actors were all collectively listening, wasn’t I a part of that too? Tensions Throughout my analysis, I noticed the ways in which  an interactionist approach generated walls, rather than connections, for my understanding of listening with the whole self. For one, the video data I captured as part of my ethnographic study somehow took precedence over my felt record (my field notes, visible in Act 1, Scene 1). This was perhaps initially towards an idea of maintaining an objective record– an idea I interrogated, given that I knew well that my camera was hardly an objective agent, acting alone (Hall, 2000; Guzman & Hong, 2022). Upon finding the way  that the group “ tore,” I clung once more to the worn methodological groove. I painstakingly scrubbed one frame at a time, watching for bones settling in feet or chests inching forward to indicate that someone might move. I was unable to find anything, save for the movements of the actors’ chests, ambiently rising and falling as they breathed in their stillness. When “they are with,” the collective moves as one, even on video. Upon noticing that not even the instructor attended to singularity, I felt, as Vannini (2015) urges, the need to step away from forcing the study of structures and symbolic meanings and lean instead into the ways I, as researcher, was also entangled in the relations of this ensemble. In understanding this collective listening as something that emerged as a more-than-human relational happening, I came to know it by tuning myself to the happening– by listening through my witnessing and being open to being affected by the actors in time and space (Vannini, 2015).

Compared to the video recording, which depicted ten actors not moving and then suddenly moving  in perfect synchrony, my field notes noted the weight and reverence of the silence that the actors invok ed (“the air thickens; I cannot move”). This reference allowed me to see the stillness and silence as a complex and important element of their activity. By allowing myself to recognize the feeling of their silence, the actors’ listening became an active and important happening, the opposite of how it appears– sterile, passive– on camera. In the black box, as the actors listened to each other, I listened with them. Unable to anticipate who would move and when, I too softened my gaze in order to take in the whole line. I, too, fell silent and found stillness. In my secondary analysis, I lingered on the feeling that “I [couldn’t] move”– that in order for their event to take place, I had to freeze. I was similarly bound to them, a part of their emergent wholeness. In that silence and stillness, what I discovered was a great feeling for time and space– for the resonance of the room, for the resonance of each actor, and like a sudden boil, a  call to be still, in order for them to  move. With every step forward across the threshold, the actors release d a small burst of energy, responding to what, in their felt resonance, moved them. In their silence and stillness, nothing happen ed, but something happen ed (de Vaujany & Aroles, 2019): rather than the absence of something, the actors’ enacted inaction allowed them a spatial and temporal orientation for their collective work. They create d their stillness in order to tune to each other, something that opened up the possibility for them to find their ensemble. This engaged tuning is a felt, relational, and entangled intra-action (Barad, 2007) similar to the way an orchestra tunes before a performance: Though instruments arrive pre-tuned to some degree, the orchestra tunes to itself and its new space before they perform; a single note is played, and each musician adjusts their instrument to the frequency. Much like the actors find each other in their stillness, as musicians listen to each other tune, they continuously adjust their frequency to what they hear, continuously feeling for the way that the instruments resonate with each other until they are entangled in the act of being tuned to each other. In the case of these ten actors, and the support of their  two silent witnesses – myself and the instructor – the actors use stillness as their orchestral starting note. From there, they engage in their rela tional negotiation of space and time; t heir tacit ensemble learning carved into an invisible, felt experience.

## Discussion and implications

This Viewpoints vignette invites us to open up  the ways we think about learning together and encourages us to wear new methodological grooves into the landscape of the learning sc iences. In considering listening with the whole self  as an entangled tuning, rather than a series of individual actions that comprise a whole, I seek to challenge established notions of learning with others by centering the with-ness, rather than the others. This shift aligns with Ma & Hall’s (2016) call for a more holistic understanding of learning that transcends individual actions and knowing that transcends individual bodies.

Theoretically, I offer tuning as an approach to understanding the tacit, felt work that ensembles engage in. In the case of the ten actors I observed, in order to find synchrony, they needed to feel for each other in space and time. By tuning towards the resonant frequency of the room, relational ly listening for when and who would move, they became an ensemble that was able to accomplish their task. Methodologically, I also highlight the need for re-presencing the lived body  (Merleau-Ponty, 1965) in the collection and interpretation of learning data. Without my felt record of this experience, I would not have been able to, quite literally, make sense of this phenomenon or the ways in which I also tuned to the actors in time and space. In portraying how quickly this relational entanglement is erased by traditional data collection methods and analysis, I hope to demonstrate the need for analysis that entangles us rather than severs us toward an “aesthetic of objectivity” (Denzin, 2003, p. 73).

At its core, studying ensemble learning allows us to better understand the ways we both relate to each other and come to know ourselves relationally. In embracing entangled methodologies and designing for learning that orients people toward relations of collective responsibility, we can move our practices towards the vital work of coming, and feeling,  together– something Bogart & Landau (2004) would argue is “not an end, but a beginning,” (p. xi).
