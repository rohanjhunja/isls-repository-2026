# BlockTalk: GenAI Simulations for Conversational Debugging Practice

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Conversing with students about challenges is central to the work of teaching computing through opportunities to create self-directed and open-ended programming projects. We introduce BlockTalk, a generative AI tool for teachers to practice conversational debugging through simulations. Our exploratory analysis examines teachers’ interactions with the AI and offers insights for debugging pedagogy.

## Introduction and background

Opportunities to create self-directed and open-ended programming projects, where learners can bring their interests and identities  into their computational creations, can broaden participation in computing (Tsan et a l., 2023). Creating self-directed programming projects inevitably involves e ncountering and resolving bugs, and there is a need to understand how teachers support students in learning debugging practices. W e designed a generative AI (GenAI) platform, BlockTalk, to support computing teachers’ professional learning. On BlockTalk, teachers engage with conversational simulations to debug AI students’ projects. We ask: How did computing teachers with varying experience interact with BlockTalk? The development of BlockTalk  is informed by literature in three areas: (1) dialo gic approaches to supporting student learning; (2) computing teachers’ strategies for scaffolding student debugging; and (3) GenAI simulations for professional learning. Talking with  students to scaffold their productive struggle can  be part of a dialogic teaching repertoire (Alexander, 2018; Kim & Wilkinson, 2019). In computing education, teacher s’ approaches to scaffolding productive struggle have been found to vary along two dimensions: teacher ve rsus student understanding and focus on the specific prod uct versus student learning (Elliott et al., 2024).  One way to support computing teachers’ dialogic approaches can be through GenAI simulations, which can support teachers’ need for more low-stakes and high-frequency practice spaces (Reich, 2022). ## Context

BlockTalk is a web-based platform for teachers to engage in interactiv e conversational simulations to practice debugging common problems in student projects. Ther e are ten chat-based simulations. In each simulation, the teacher can chat with an AI student experiencing a bug with their Scratch project. Each simulation is associated with a live and currently buggy Scratch project, wh ich the teacher can interact with as a reference. Drawing on a typology of common errors in Scratch programming pr ojects (Frädrich et al., 2020), each simulation fea tures a different conceptual challenge. Project types are v aried to represent some of the different ways stude nts create personally meaningful projects. The simulations are powered  by OpenAI’s GPT-4o model. When a teacher submits a chat message, BlockTalk sends the chat history to GPT-4o, along with a system prompt, to generate the AI student response. BlockTalk’s system prompt inst ructs the large language model to respond as a fift h-grade student might, including guidelines for student beh avior. The prompt provides the model with information about the student’s project, the blocks they’re currently  using, and a range of possible sol utions. Each simulation is designed around a single conceptual challenge but h as at least three different solutions, to represent  possible learning pathways. When a simulation is completed, the conversation ends and teachers are pointed to r esources about different solutions and related curriculum. We have  conducted individual beta tests and run virtual and inperson workshops for groups. BlockTalk is free, online, and available to all teachers. Teachers and analysis Although anyone can sign up, our sign-up process asks for specific demographic data relevant to teaching Scratch (e.g., years of general/computing/Scratch-specific teaching experience). From July to November 2024, 82 teachers engaged with BlockTalk and engaged in at least one conversation, with a total of 277 conversations across all ten simulations. 44 teachers were from the United State s, 14 from outside the United States, and 24 did no t specify location. Among the PK–12 teachers, 22 taught elementary school (pre-kindergarten to grade 5), 15 taught middle school, and 12 taught high school.

All conversations which teachers have with BlockTal k are stored. In this exploratory analysis, w e focused on less-experienced teachers (less than five years of teach ing, n=41), and more-experienced teachers (with five or more years, n=37), looking at three d imensions of their simulations: completion status, how openended or closed the conversations were, and the specificity of teachers’ language. Across all ten simulations, lessexperienced teachers engaged with 117 simulations (76 completed, 41 incomplete), and more-experienced teachers engaged with 81 simulations (68 completed,  13 incomplete). Conversations were coded on the me ssage level by authors three and four, who met with autho rs one and two weekly. Each message was coded “open-ended” (e.g., prompted student exploration) or “closed” (e.g., was a directive). Less-experienced teachers engaged in a total of 25 open-ended conversations and 92 closed conversations, while more-experienced teachers engaged in a total of 34 open-ended and 47 closed conversations. For each teacher, we developed a brief case study, examining teachers’ language specificity in relatio n to computer programming and problem-solving. We met regularly as a team to discuss differences between less-experienced and more-experienced teachers.

## Initial findings

Across experience levels, teachers engaged in more closed than open-ended conversations, though both types of conversations could lead to successfully completed simulations. While teachers of varying experience levels were able to successfully complete simulations, their st rategies differed in terms of specificity in relati on to computer programming and problem-solving. For example, one K –12 teacher reported having less than on e year  of experience teaching but successfully leveraged a teaching approach characterized by openness and collaboration, even though her approach was less specific to computer programming. For each simulation, the AI student started by saying “My project is not working,” and the teacher responded with “Can you show me your code?” and “Let’s try it.” Even though the teacher’s questions and comments were less specific, their focus on helping the AI student show their work and test ideas was effective in enabling the AI student to resolve their issue. In contrast, the expertise of more-experienced teachers was often reflected in Scratch-specific language, as well as scaffolded questions. One high school IB  computer science teacher with more than 11 years o f experience completed two simulations. He start ed with open-ended questions (“What technique are you using in Scratch to make Grace respond?”), then offered more leading questions to the student (“When should the costume change?”) and explicit invitations to explore certain blocks. His  conversational moves, while still phrased as questions, were often scaffolded towards specific concepts. He closed with a directive: “Test it out! Be a computer scientist!” While the less-experienced teac hers asked questions which were less  specific, which could lead to multiple possibilities, more-experienced teachers more readily identified the AI students’ challenge, and asked questions which led the AI students quickly towards a workable solution.

## Discussion and conclusion

We designed BlockTalk  as an experiment in supporting teacher practice thr ough conversations with GenAI students. BlockTalk, even in this initial stage, of fers two contributions. First, BlockTalk is a funct ional professional learning platform that can support tea cher development of capacity for debugging student programming projects. BlockTalk is modular in desig n, and it is possible to design additional simulati ons which foreground different computational concepts, highli ght different types of projects, and require the AI  student to act in different ways. Building GenAI-supported tools which support teacher thinking and reflection can inform the design of future professional learning experiences, where teachers can share conversation records and examine their instructional moves  together. And although GenAI chatbots are often used as a s ource of expertise and information, we see interesting possibilities for learning designers, which locate the expertise and agency with the teacher or user. Second, BlockTalk data offers a record of teachers’ current instructional moves, which can inform future research on teachers’ debugging pedagogy.

Supporting students in debugging self-directed and open-ended programming projects can open or close different possible debugging pathways. When student s are stuck, they may want a more directed approach  to resolving the present bug, and when teachers have quickly identified the issue, teachers may also want to help the student by resolving the issue for them. Even as we aim to design for students’ self-direction, there may be times when it is more, or less, appropriate to foreground  self-direction and open-endedness in the debugging process. We hope that BlockTalk inspires future work in service of helping teachers design scaffolded spaces for learners’ productive struggle.
