# (Re)constructing What Counts: The Politics and Possibilities of Teacher Noticing

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Teacher noticing, defined as teachers’ ability to attend to and interpret classroom events, constitutes a dominant construct supporting teacher preparation programs and professional development. This conceptual paper argues that noticing frameworks do not simply describe professional knowledge, but actively shape what counts as knowledge in teaching. Dominant cognitive and sociocultural approaches privilege analytical, observable, and individually located forms of knowing while overlooking the tacit, embodied, and r elational dimensions that teachers draw on within their practice, carrying political consequences for how teachers are supported. Building on emerging ecological and relational approaches, we argue that reimagining the construct opens possibilities for recognizing the full range of knowledge teachers draw on in their practice. Through an illustrative example with an experienced secondary mathematics teacher, Clark, we explore what becomes possible when research begins from teachers’ lived experience.

Teachers like Clark and I are paying attention to something ––atmosphere, relationships, kids’ moods, energy––all the ineffable things that shape the complex interplay between mathematical thinking and human connection –– and responding to it from places within ourselves that exist beyond thought or language. To explore these ideas further, I turned to the teacher noticing literature, a dominant construct in mathematics education that delineates the nature of teachers’ attention and interpretations of classroom life toward the goal of fostering meaningful mathematics learning. Positioned as integral for developing high-quality teaching (Jacobs & Spangler, 2017), noticing frameworks are used in teacher preparation, professional development, and 754 increasingly in evaluation systems. Yet in reading this scholarship, I found no answers for the "ineffable magic," no frameworks that would describe the "fairy dust and halo sounds" we imagined and experienced firsthand  in Clark's classroom.

In this conceptual paper, we argue that current noticing frameworks systematically narrow what counts as professional knowledge. While cognitive approaches construct noticing as an individual mental process and sociocultural approaches foreground context and ideology, both paradigms locate noticing within individual minds and privilege observable, technical aspects of practice. The focus on how teachers attend to student mathematical thinking, interpret classroom events, and make instructional decisions captures crucial dimensions of teaching. The problem emerges when these frameworks are treated as comprehensive rather than partial, when what can be measured becomes conflated with what counts as professional knowledge. By privileging technical, observable phenomena, current frameworks background the embodied, tacit, and affective resources teachers draw upon: the "feeling" of a classroom, the "vibes" in the atmosphere, the emotions that resist articulation, the magic we were describing with Clark.

One might reasonably ask why we remain with teacher noticing if these frameworks cannot account for the dimensions of practice we were compelled by. Noticing frameworks are deeply embedded and used as tools for teacher preparation and professional development, shaping what aspiring and practicing teachers are expected to attend to, interpret and potentially act upon. Noticing, as a construct, is meant to explore how  teachers attend to and make sense of the complex world of classroom life. Yet the relational, embodied and affective dimensions that Clark is navigating are also part of that world and are deeply intertwined with mathematical learning. Importantly, frameworks for knowledge have social power. When they valorize some forms of knowledge over others, they privilege some knowers while marginalizing others (Philip & Sengupta, 2021). In this paper, by examining where noticing frameworks draw their boundaries, we can trace what those boundaries render (in)visible and ask what other possibilities might exist to support teachers in navigating the complex world of teaching.

Teacher noticing: an overview of the field Cognitive-psychological perspectives are the dominant theoretical paradigm within teacher noticing research (König et al. 2022; Santagata et al. 2021). König et al. (2022) define this orientation as "noticing […] what teachers attend to and make sense of, focusing on the according mental processes" (p. 2). This definition reveals the paradigm's core assumption: that noticing resides within individual minds. In their influential work, van Es and Sherin (2002) defined noticing as having three components: (a) identifying what is noteworthy about a classroom situation; (b) making connections between classroom interactions and broader principles of teaching and learning; and (c) using contextual knowledge to reason about classroom events (p. 573). Noticing is a systematic, analytical process that can be decomposed into discrete facets. This logic of decomposition becomes even more explicit when researchers isolate individual facets for study, such as Star and Strickland's (2008) focus on "attending" as a foundational skill that must precede interpretation. Skills-based frameworks further support this construction of teachers as individual analytical processors. Jacobs et al.'s (2010) "Professional Noticing of Children's Thinking" (PNCT) framework exemplifies this approach, conceptualizing noticing "as a set of three interrelated skills: attending  to children's strategies, interpreting children's understandings, and deciding how to respond on the basis of children's understandings" (p. 172). Notably, this framework places children's mathematical thinking as the primary focus of noticing. While children's mathematical thinking is essential, it is only one dimension among many others (participation, inclusion, energy, relationships). Similarly, van Es's (2011) "Learning to Notice" framework maps a progression where sophistication is marked by shifts from evaluative to interpretive discourse and from a broad to a narrow focus on students' mathematical thinking. Across these frameworks, a consistent logic emerges: complex practice can be decomposed into measurable c omponents, and teachers can be trained to progress along developmental trajectories.

Sociocultural researchers expanded noticing frameworks to incorporate cultural, contextual, and ideological dimensions. Hand (2012) argued that teachers' dispositions, shaped by their participation in contexts saturated with dominant discourses about mathe matics, influence what teachers notice and how they interpret classroom events. Building on this insight, van Es et al. (2022) theorized multidimensional noticing as requiring both "stretch" (temporal awareness linking past histories to potential futures) and "expanse" (simultaneous attention to multiple phenomena), arguing that "the histories and cultures of teachers, learners and mathematics, and the broader historical, cultural and political contexts in which they exist" shape moment-to-moment classroom interactions (p. 115). Shah and Coles (2020) extended this logic to the social process of racialization, developing "racial noticing" as a framework for attending to, interpreting, and responding to racial phenomena in learning settings. These theoretical moves represent genuine attempts to broaden the field's vision beyond a narrow focus 755 on mathematical content, making space for attention to equity, participation, and the cultural contexts that shape teaching and learning.

Still, a fundamental limitation persists across paradigms. The individual teacher’s mind remains the primary unit of analysis and the primary site of intervention. Consider van Es and Sherin's (2021) recent addition of "shaping" to their framework, intende d to recognize how teachers actively construct interactions to make student thinking visible. While this refinement acknowledges the interactive nature of noticing, it still positions teachers as strategic agents who "gain access to" pre-existing information rather than recognizing how meaning emerges through mutually constitutive social relations. Similarly, van Es et al.'s (2022) multidimensional equity framework theorizes how historical and cultural contexts shape noticing, but ultimately analyzes indivi dual teachers' "systems of noticing" and locates the capacity for disrupting inequitable practices in individuals' expanded noticing abilities. Whether cognitive or sociocultural in orientation, noticing frameworks privilege what individuals observe and articulate. Understanding how these frameworks construct particular forms of knowledge as legitimate requires examining their methodological foundations. From holism to atomization: how measurement shapes what counts Pressure for validity and reliability drives researchers to atomize holistic phenomena into measurable parts, even when their own theoretical framework acknowledges these parts are inseparable. Through this atomization, technical aspects of practice become the legitimate form of professional knowledge while rendering contextual and relational knowledge invisible, because it’s hard to capture in methodological formats aimed at transferability and generalizability. This methodological reduction perpetuates a vision of teaching as discrete, measurable components rather than holistic, integrated practice requiring complex judgment. On a micro level, breaking up aspects of classroom practice into distinct categories is not clear-cut as evidenced by challenges researchers have faced in replicating studies. For example, Star et al. (2011) attempted to replicate the results from the Star and Strickland (2008) study, which found that pre-service teachers were better able to observe classroom interactions at the end of their methods course. Star and colleagues used the same framework with a new group of pre-service teachers to assess what teachers attended to. While Star et al. (2011) claimed to have successfully replicated the results from the 2008 study, the actual findings tell a more complex story.

Both studies showed increases in ability to observe aspects of a classroom environment and communication, but the remaining categories showed divergent results. In the 2011 study, pre-service teachers became worse at observing features of tasks and mathematical content. The authors attribute the decline in observations of mathematical content to their having made the assessment “more pure and difficult” by removing items that conflated mathematical content with communication. Yet this explanation raises a methodological question: when refining an instrument leads to opposite results, and when that refinement is then used to explain the divergence, it’s unclear whether the framework measures stable aspects of teacher observation or simply reflects researcher s' evolving decisions about category boundaries. These substantive differences in key pedagogical categories suggest the framework's categories may be more fluid than initially presented.  These methodological choices or “advances” reveal how measurement frameworks shape and constrain what counts as legitimate professional knowledge.

To be clear, decomposition is not inherently a problem. Practice-based teacher education scholarship has shown that breaking complex practice into components can be a useful pedagogical tool for supporting teachers in learning to teach (Grossman et al., 2009). However, decomposition as a pedagogical tool to support teachers is different than viewing teaching itself as a job of decomposition. In the noticing literature, the decomposed components become the construct itself, and what is measured becomes what counts as professional knowledge. The scaffold gets mistaken for the building.

On a macro level, this tension between theoretical holism and methodological atomization becomes problematized in Kaiser et al.'s (2015) perceiving, interpreting, and decision making (PID) framework was developed to provide situated and performance-related ways to assess teacher competence in order to look at situation-specific skills that teachers employ in their classrooms as captured by the teacher actions of perceiving, interpreting and decision making. Note that Kaiser et al. do not operationally distinguish interpretation from decision-making (grouping them as “ID”), yet they maintain these are three distinct components within their PID framework. They claim their “items require noticing as [a] whole process, comprising perception, interpretation and decision making,” but then separate perception from interpretation/decision-making based solely on item format, using Likert scales for perception and open responses for interpretation/decision making (p. 378). A Likert item asking teachers to rate whether “the task includes some features of an open-ended task” requires interpretive understanding of what constitutes an open-ended task yet is classified as perception. Moreover, Kaiser et al. undermine their own framework by providing their own interpretations for supposedly interpretation-based items 756 (e.g., “obviously she aims at an exchange of results… ” (p. 379). This removes the interpretation component entirely, reducing these items to constrained decision-making tasks while claiming to measure teachers' interpretive abilities.

Stahnke et al.'s (2016) systematic review of teachers’ situation specific skills reveals that Kaiser et al.'s methodological choices are not an anomaly,but rather are representative of a field-wide tension between theoretical commitments to holistic noticing and methodological imperatives for measurable components. What the review doesn't address is how these methodological choices carry political weight that construct particular versions of teaching competence while obscuring others. Their approach asks teachers to demonstrate these skills in decontextualized situations that aren’t their own, where key contextual knowledge is given, not the driver for how teachers come to perceive, interpret and respond. Knowledge about a context differs from knowledge within a context. This decomposition into measurable parts makes sense given the noticing literature’s focus on pre-service teachers (König et al., 2022), a population that, not having classroom experience, cannot draw upon the contextual and relational knowledge that would reveal the limitations of such atomization. The politics of knowledge in teacher noticing These methodological choices carry political consequences that extend beyond measurement validity. Scheiner

(2021) calls attention to "the common divide between mind, body, and matter, as well as the dualism between the

individual and the environment, prevalent in many approaches to teacher noticing" (p. 86). Teacher noticing research continues to look at the individual mind, not at how the mind connects with the body in intuitive and affective ways, nor does it take into account the embodied connections between mind, body and matter within classrooms. As noted in the introduction, frameworks for knowledge have social power. When these frameworks privilege certain types of knowledge, they simultaneously privilege some knowers while devaluing others (Philip & Sengupta, 2021), privileging certain ways of seeing while marginalizing teachers' embodied, relational ways of knowing. In what follows, I trace what kinds of knowledge are marginalized as well as the consequential impact on the marginalized: teachers.

Methodological choices shape what researchers notice and also determine what can count as knowledge in the first place. Pugh (2024) reminds us that “measurement—what David Beer called ‘metric power’ —does not just calculate a standard; it also establishes one. Not being counted can render something invisible and unvalued, while sometimes being counted can be hazardous,” (p. 181). For example, Lefstein and Snell (2011) expose how researchers render their own perspectives invisible when researching teachers’ p rofessional vision. By allowing “the researcher perspective, the position from which teacher vision is judged and found wanting” to disappear from discussion, the field obscures “the perspectival nature of professional vision” itself (p. 507). This normative construction has political consequences: if teachers are judged against researchers’ visions, “one correct way of seeing is authorized as professional; deviations from it are positioned as lacking in expertise” (Lefstein & Snell, 2011, p. 507). Noticing becomes an ability to develop rather than a negotiation of the plurality of visions. These political implications of how normative frameworks advance certain visions of teaching while marginalizing others remain notably absent from current discussions (see Lefstein & Snell, 2011; Louie 2018). As I will show, the problems I have foreshadowed remain, particularly in how these frameworks exclude intuitive, embodied, and relational forms of teacher knowing.

Embodied and tacit knowing: what current frameworks cannot capture Limitations of teacher noticing frameworks matter because they exclude crucial dimensions of how teachers actually come to know and make sense of practice. For example, Berliner (1994) argues that: if the novice is deliberate, the advanced beginner insightful, the competent performer rational, and the proficient performer intuitive, we might categorize the expert as often being arational. Experts have both an intuitive grasp of the situation and seem to sense in non-analytic and nondeliberative ways the appropriate response to be made (p. 16, italics added for emphasis). Berliner thus acknowledges with us that expertise in teaching is often tacit and enacted rather than articulated and deliberate. This tacit knowledge is precisely what analytical frameworks cannot capture or assess. Berliner (1994) also acknowledges that because knowledge is contextualized, "tests and simulations to judge teachers will usually yield many false negatives" (p. 62). Even with his work involving expert teachers, he noted that many experts in his studies were not articulate: "they could not describe what they did well. Nevertheless, they did those things in their classroom quite well" (p. 62). These expert teachers who were "not articulate" may not have lacked knowledge, but because articulation was beside the point. Their knowing was enacted, not described; felt, not analyzed; shared with students, not written for an outside audience. 757 Berliner and others have noted the value of tacit, intuitive knowledge, but they have cast it as a later stage development. Yet novices also draw upon tacit, embodied ways of knowing, whether or not they can yet articulate them. When intuition is treated only as a late-stage achievement, we risk dismissing the intuitive, non-analytic knowledge that new teachers already mobilize in interpreting classroom events, even though this knowledge resists the kind of codification that frameworks require. This developmental stage model makes phenomenological assumptions about the work of noticing that warrant analysis. One assumption is that intuitive knowledge is an accomplishment of expert teachers, while tacit and relational ways of knowing are not accounted for as a part of early-stage teacher development. While novice teachers may indeed develop more sophisticated intuitive judgment over time, like the noticing frameworks I reviewed in the previous sections, Berliner’s framework presumes that technical mastery must precede rather thandevelop alongside  other forms of professional knowledge. By excluding intuition from pre-service teachers’ knowledge resources, such formulations do not recognize nor build upon the embodied and relational knowledge that new teachers already bring to practice.

These methodological exclusions become explicit in studies like Kaiser et al. (2015) who openly acknowledge that "we lose some of the proximity to real-life teaching, but we gain—in a first step —generalizable insight into the teachers' competence" (p. 385). Kaiser et al.’s (2015) willingness to "lose some of the proximity to real-life teaching" in favor of generalizability suggests that measurability takes precedence over ecological validity.

There are several consequences to this observation. First, I argue that intuition is not a late-stage add-on to technical skill; it is a modality of professional knowledge that exists across the career and should be cultivated, not merely certified, by fra meworks for teacher knowledge. Relatedly, researchers and teacher educators should develop approaches that honor the tacit knowledge already present and drawn upon in everyday teaching practice. This also speaks to the onto-epistemic issues raised earlier. Because our frameworks for knowledge have social power, they should make spaces for the kinds of knowledge novices bring to the work of teaching instead of only valorizing that which can be codified through general frameworks. While scholars theorize noticing as a situated, complex, in-the-moment phenomenon, the dominant methodological approaches treat noticing as a decontextualized, individualistic skill that candevelop outside of classroom ecologies. The recursive problem: deficit discourses about teachers Beyond excluding particular forms of knowledge, these methodological choices produce problematic discourses about teachers themselves. Berliner (1994) argued that it "appears that all assessment of expert teachers outside of their classrooms is subject to validity problems that may not be possible to overcome" (p. 62). He describes expert teachers as "more like the race car driver or fighter pilot who talk of becoming one with their machine… acting effortlessly, fluidly… go with the flow" (p. 16). Given thi s validity problem, how does one assess numerically through writing how well someone “go[es] with the flow?” Is there a class to take on how to become “one” with your classroom? This type of knowing is deeply embodied, contextual, relational, immeasurable, and resistant to being captured in decontextualized formats. Yet what happens when we exclude these dimensions? The field has created a self-reinforcing, recursive problem: while calling attention to teachers' propensity for deficit noticing of students, appropriately so, researchers simultaneously engage in deficit noticing of teachers, often inappropriately so. Deficit discourses manifest through "deficit noticing" (Louie et al., 2021) whereby teachers fail to notice students' assets and attend to the contributions of students of color and their families in deficit ways. Researchers often engage in the very practice they critique when they assess teachers. While researchers may simply intend to describe their findings when they characterize teachers as "not particularly observant" (Star & Strickland, 2008, p. 121) or report "minimal engagement with children's thinking" (Jacobs et al., 2010, p. 191), or have “significantly lower skills in all three facets of teacher noticing” (Bastian et al., 2024, p. 261), this language communicates teacher deficits. When teachers do not “notice” in ways that are expected or valued, instead of questioning the utility of the frameworks themselves, the onus is placed on the teachers to get better. We position teachers as "lacking skills" or "underdeveloped," which "contributes to the general malaise of teaching and learning mathematics as inadequate and ineffective" (Mason, 2016, p. 298). Even frameworks that attempt to account for this complexity still often place teachers on developmental trajectories (Turner et al., 2012), whether through looking at how noticing becomes more discursively sophisticated (van Es, 2011) or how noticing differs between novice and experienced teachers (Jacobs et al., 2010; Bastian et al., 2024). These attempts risk what Mason (2016) calls a "linear trail of development" that reduces teaching to measurable endpoints rather than ongoing evolution (p. 298). Who decides what matters? The problem of predetermined categories 758 Even frameworks that have attempted to address these limitations by expanding what teachers attend to still face constraints.  For example, sociocultural research has broadened conceptions of what teachers attend to— expanding beyond mathematical content and student thinking to include participation, equity issues, and students’ cultural assets (Turner et al. 2012; Wager, 2014). However, by directing teachers' attention to predetermined categories, researchers still construct what counts as legitimate profes sional knowledge. The very act of training teachers what to notice assumes researchers know in advance what matters in any classroom moment, dismissing the possibility that teachers' own ways of seeing might reveal dimensions of practice that exceed our frameworks. For instance, teachers working in culturally and linguistically diverse contexts might develop sophisticated ways of noticing multilingual students' mathematical reasoning that don't map onto existing categories of  “mathematical thinking” versus “cultural  knowledge.” Teachers might intuitively feel how power circulates through classroom interactions ––who gets to speak, whose ideas get taken up, how authority is negotiated––that reveal dimensions of equity not captured in written student work. Moreover, teachers' embodied knowledge of when to interrupt or extend a mathematical moment, their intuitive sense of how relationships unfold over time, or their tacit understanding of affective shifts in the classroom represent forms of professional wisdom that resist codification.

When we consider noticing from a sociocultural perspective, the gap between what teachers actually do and what research reveals becomes stark. Teachers’ lived noticing involves simultaneous attention to multiple relational dynamics, embodied responses to classroom energy, intuitive reading of interactional patterns alongside understanding of students and their mathematical contributions. Teachers don’t simply notice “student thinking” and add understandings of “equity issues" alongside “mathematical content,” but experience these as interconnected aspects of a complex, dynamic ecology. These aren't just objects of noticing that skilled teachers learn to include in their cognitive processing. They are the very medium through which noticing occurs, the relational ground from which interpretation emerges. When frameworks privilege observable, analytical knowledge over this holistic reality, they construct hierarchies of legitimate professional knowledge. Toward relational noticing Scholars increasingly recognize that teacher noticing emerges through collaborative rather than individual processes. Through her case study of high school mathematics teacher Amanda, Louie (2018) showed that although Amanda was committed to noticing students' mathematical strengths, dominant ideologies around mathematical smartness persisted. Colleagues who positioned Amanda's approaches as "not real math" constrained her equity-oriented noticing, while a collective culture among like-minded colleagues enabled it, demonstrating that Amanda's noticing resulted from sustained collaboration rather than individual effort. Working in collaboration opens up opportunities for thinking with others, but also feeling  in relation to others. Munson (2020) explored a side-by-side coaching approach supporting teachers during live instruction. In the moment, teachers experienced feelings of struggle, wonder and confidence. Teachers’ noticing drew upon emotional experiences central to how teachers attended to, interpreted and decided how to respond to children’s mathematical thinking. Teachers’ interpretations emerged not just from cognitive processing, but from the embodied, emotional engagement with the “blooming, buzzing confusion of classroom life” (Sherin & Star, 2011, p. 69).

This emotional engagement constitutes relationships between  individuals. Indeed, Dominguez (2019) argues that “neither the cognitive frameworks nor the methodologies developed for teacher noticing fit into the highly interactive and relational experiences of teachers and students in these classrooms” (p. 76). Domi nguez

(2019) calls for theorizing noticing as reciprocal noticing. He  redefines the relationship between teachers and

students to demand looking beyond what is in a teacher’s head. Instead of viewing noticing as something that a teacher does to their students, he theorizes noticing as something that emerges between  students and teachers. Teachers simultaneously are shaped by phenomena around them and reciprocally shape possibilities for students and mathematics within their classrooms (Dominguez 2019; Dominguez 2021). Yet as Scheiner (2021) notes, studies that look beyond the individual teacher’s mind are rare. Scheiner

(2021) invites scholars to take a more ecological view, positioning the teacher as an “active explorer of the world

of classroom events” (p. 86). He calls for teacher noticing to be seen as “an entanglement of the cultural-historical, embodied-ecological and social-material” (p. 91). Scheiner’s ecological model resonates with Mason’s phenomenological approach to noticing. Mason (2002) conceptualizes noticing as “being awake to situations, being mindful rather than mindless” (p. 38). Mason (2002) emphasizes that professional development occurs through sharing accounts with colleagues and being “disturbed” by others' practices.  Although Mason emphasizes collaborative sharing of accounts, his disciplined noticing ultimately locates change within individual awareness, a phenomenological stance that resists the predetermined categories favored in empirical research. 759 Scheiner and Amador (2025) bridge phenomenological and ecological perspectives in their Noticing as Design Framework. Rather than viewing teachers merely as skilled observers, Scheiner and Amador center the teacher as architects of their own professional noticing. Scheiner and Amador (2025) argue that teachers design classroom environments that support "noticing-rich ecologies.” Accordingly, teachers actively shape what becomes available for noticing through their intentional choices about environment, interactions, and experiences. The progression from Louie's collaboratively-constituted noticing, to Dominguez’s view of noticing being coconstructed to Scheiner's view as ecological entanglement illustrates how far the field has traveled theoretically from its cognitive origins. Yet these theoretical advances have not transformed empirical practice. Echoing Mason (2016), we call for researchers to “confine to the filing cabinet studies of what people can and cannot do […] let us focus attention on the web of influences on specific classroom moments, being content for the moment to chart their multiplicity, and to reach out for techniques for studying complex systems” (p. 300). Conceptualizing noticing as an interactional, relational process invites methodological innovation that honors this complexity rather than reducing it.

These theoretical alternatives share a fundamental recognition that teacher noticing is contextual, relational, and collaborative rather than solely individual and technical. By foregrounding how teachers actively construct the conditions for which noticin g matters in their practice, these approaches challenge skills-based frameworks that position teachers as needing to acquire what they lack, rather than as already possessing resources for making sense of their work.

Collaborative inquiry: Exploring joy with Clark Clark's classroom reflects one line of this paper's central argument: joy, relationships, and energy are central to forms of knowledge teachers candraw on and cultivate. The "rainbows" and "fairy dust" Author 2 imagined, the "good vibes" Clark deliberately creates are not decorative additions to mathematics instruction. They constitute a set of relational conditions that make profound mathematical thinking possible. Over two years, our research team worked with Clark to explore questions emerging from his own practice. Clark’s initial question was: what are the sources of joy in the room? Rather than assessing Clark's noticing against predetermined frameworks, we collaborated with him to represent how joy emerged through the ecology of his classroom. In our recent interview, Clark began by reflecting on the time we’ve spent together: Clark: … also a million other things I can think of that I'm doing now, that I may have been doing before, but I just didn't see them as such. The clarity of it is helping me think forward… I just leveled up in my thinking by having more conversations with y'all or just more serious focused conversations about teaching… it’s like concave up (1).. the better I get at noticing things and thinking about things, the more productive all of it feels.

Clark's reflection illustrates how collaborative inquiry supported his ongoing learning, not through transformation into a different kind of teacher, but through developing language and frameworks to make sense of what was already present in his practice. When we showed Clark this representation again, two years later, he became emotional.

Clark: I'm just emotional because like… it's so hard to prove yourself in this profession. I have no power… No one even knows if my class is even good… especially in that there is some data on me that's not going to paint me as a great teacher… that's the thing is I used to play that game

some numbers behind me. And then now it's like I don't, so what do I have? But Clark does have something. He has the desire to create a joyful classroom. He has two decades of experience, deep curiosity, an embodied awareness to want people to walk by his classroom and notice “ it”, National Board Certification––the list of signifiers of his professional commitments goes on. The question, What do I have, becomes a question not of capability but of recognition. This is not a call to abandon systematic inquiry or return to romanticized notions of teaching as ineffable art. This is an argument for research that treats teachers as collaborators in defining what matters in their own practice, and that develops shared tools for engaging with the full complexity of teaching. Different teachers bring entirely different questions and knowledge to such collaborative work. The goal is not to produce more teachers exactly like Clark, but to work with teachers around what they care about, supporting them in recognizing and working with the complex, relational conditions that make learning possible. When teaching requires responding to the students in the room, there are always new opportunities for learning.

## Implications

Methodological and theoretical choices in teacher noticing research shape what counts as legitimate professional knowledge. This is not merely a technical question about research design, but a political question about whose knowledge counts, about whether noticing becomes "an ability to develop rather than a negotiation of the plurality of visions" (Lefstein & Snell, 2011, p. 507). The limitations we have described are not gaps to be addressed by adding new categories or broadening existing frameworks. They suggest that the construct itself may need to be reimagined to account for the relational, embodied and affective dimensions of how teachers make sense of their practice.  For researchers, this calls for methodological innovation that can "chart their mul tiplicity, and to reach out for techniques for studying complex systems" rather than reducing teaching to measurable components (Mason, 2016, p. 300), approaches that begin from what teachers already know and do, that treat teachers’ own questions as starting points for inquiry, and develop tools for engaging with the dimensions of practice that current frameworks overlook, as a shared basis for inquiry. For teacher education, this means recognizing and building upon the embodied, tacit, and relational knowledge that teachers at all career stages already draw upon. The work ahead is developing frameworks with the conceptual and methodological sophistication to recognize, explore, and support these dimensions of practice.
