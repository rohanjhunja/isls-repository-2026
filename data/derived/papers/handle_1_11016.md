## Theoretical framework

The scholarly research on culturally sustaining pedagogy has increased in popularity as issues of equity and visibility of learners’ local cultures, backgrounds, and identities have also increased (Ladson-Billings, 1995; Gay, 2002; Paris, 2012). Culturally sustaining pedagogy aims to uphold and encourage linguistic, literacy, and cultural diversity as a crucial component of the educational endeavor (Paris, 2012). It requires  educators to bring up and engage with learners’ lived experiences, life skills, and cultural resources within formal education. For this pilot research, three participants were interviewed who have diverse ethnic backgrounds (Indian, Chinese, and American) and have taught a foreign/second language to different language learner populations. I conducted online semi-structured interviews on Zoom, with an  average length of 45 minutes  for each. Then, I conducted a qualitative thematic data analysis  (Braun & Clarke, 2006) of interview audio transcripts; codifying data sets to identify patterns and form themes in a second round of  analysis guided by a framework of culturally sustaining pedagogical practice.

Two main themes emerged from the data. The first theme concerns to what extent language educators consider it important to connect their language learners’ local culture, identity, background, and home practices with the curricular content of their language lessons. The second theme deals with the implementation of learning experiences, the resources, and materials used in their foreign/second language lessons to integrate language learners’ local culture and household practices with curriculum in formal schooling environments. Theme 1: Cultural integration in language education-why it matters? Jenny, Sara, and Jessica (pseudonyms) share the same passion for teaching a foreign/second language. Jenny and Jessica have taught English as a foreign/second language to children; Sara has taught Chinese as a foreign/second language to young adults. The three participants agreed that knowing their language learners’ local culture s, cultural backgrounds,  and life practices beforehand is pivotal for their language lessons. For Jenny, making curricular connections to learners’ lived experiences  and household practices “closes a cultural gap or misconception based on like cultural difference,” and “it’s incredibly necessary to make students aware that what they bring into a classroom is valuable.” Jessica also shared a similar thought about the i mportance of knowing learners’ cultural backgrounds: “For me to understand who they are, where they are, make the connection with them, and also for them understand who I am, where I’m coming from is a valuable experience to bring in class.” Both participants asserted that knowing their language learners’ cultural context s and connecting those contexts to foreign/second language content is a powerful way to gain cultural understanding as educators and to welcome learners’ perspectives and experiences from their community as learning resources. However, although Sara 2359 agree d that learning about her language learners’ local culture and identity is important, she also mentioned that it is a challenging task. Sara t aught Chinese as a foreign language to Brazilian language learners, so communication between them was sometimes difficult: “I don’t understand them, and they don’t understand Chinese, so we have to use a mediating language to communicate at that moment.” Therefore, Sara sometimes found it difficult to get to know her learners’ home practices and identities on the first day of class due to language limitations on both sides.

Theme 2: Incorporating local culture in language instruction-What it looks like? The second theme examines activities and resources that these language learners implemented to bridge their local cultures and household practices with the curriculum. The three participants revealed that they attempted to create instructional activities that provided an opportunity to connect with their  learners’ cultural context s, family practices, and identities. For instance, Sara and Jessica mentioned the use of real-life roleplays in real scenarios. Sara remarked, “We did some things together during the class, for example, we went grocery shopping together during class time.” Jessica said, “I relied a lot on what’s available in the locality, like store over there, whatever they were selling. In fact, I would go with my students, we design activities together and then we’d go and we’d buy the thing together.” It was evident that community places aided Sara and Jessica in teaching the foreign language with realia and allowing language learners to understand and practice the foreign language as well. On the other hand, Jenny approached the connection of their learners’ local culture and background with the curriculum in a different way. She mentioned, “I taught Romeo and Juliet…, so I took samples of different versions of Romeo and Juliet, we read a Latinx version of it.” Clearly, looking for diverse alternatives to traditional pedagogical models (e.g. using diverse and reimagined literature  to support language development) provide d a space to make local connections with language learners’ backgrounds and id entities. In addition, participants revealed that they used multi-modal and multilingual materials and resources to link language learners’ local contexts and backgrounds with language content. For example, Jessica commented, “Sometimes, I would ask learners to get things from their homes, whatever they have depending on the activity.” This was a creative use of everyday, culturally significant objects and materials as learning resources to connect language content with home practices. Jenny created materials to map language learning onto existing environments in a different way: “I tried to make everything linguistically accessible and have lots of visuals, color coding things.” In Sara’s case, she relied more on videos as an audiovisual strategy to connect learners ’ household practices with the language content: “I look for some videos that kind of show Brazilian culture but in Chinese.” It is clear that resources and materials to connect language learners’ cultural context s and backgrounds with foreign language content play a crucial role in supporting educators to create culturally sustaining formal educational environments. and backgrounds with foreign language content. Results also reveal  how learning activities and resources can have a positive impact by bridging language learners’ household practices and identities with the curriculum. This research contributes to the scholarship on culturally sustaining pedagogy by offering some exemplary descriptive cases of how educators can connect learners’ cultural contexts and identities with language learning curriculum. Insights will be discussed towards determining a set of best practices to advance an asset-based approach to second language education.
