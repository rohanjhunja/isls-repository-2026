# Examining the Instructional Norms That Support Student Participation and Engagement in Whole Class Science Discussions

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
The latest reform efforts in science education emphasize the centrality of productive classroom discourse to learning science with an emphasis on students’ learning to explain and argue. These instructional demands require that teachers master instructional norms and routines that support these discursive practices. Novice teachers have difficulty learning to notice and respond to student thinking, a crucial element to promoting productive classroom talk. This paper examines a whole class discussion in one novice science teacher’s classroom to document the teacher moves that promote and manage productive discourse and the instructional norms that support them. Structuring learning experiences around these instructional routines and norms in university teacher education programs can help teachers learn to facilitate these types of productive whole class discussions.

## Introduction

Science instruction that meets the demands of the Next Generation Science Standards (NGSS Lead States, 2013) emphasizes the idea that students must learn to explain and argue around everyday science phenomena. Designing this type of instruction asks teachers to create op portunities for students to interact regularly with  each other’s ideas and explanations (Windschitl et al., 2012). F or these student-to-student interactions to be mean ingful, students need to be positioned as constructors and critiques of scientific claims (Ford, 2008) taking ownership and responsibility for the aims, processes, and sta ndards of scientific knowledge production (Elgin, 2 013). Positioning students in this way empowers them as e pistemic agents (Stroupe, 2014) where students are participants in the knowledge production process. This epistemic agency is fundamentally dialogic and demands that teachers re-orient their teaching around promo ting and managing whole class discussions among stu dents (Kelly, 2014; Mercer, 2009).

Promoting and managing productive classroom discour se require teachers learn to notice, elicit, and respond to student thinking (Barnhart & van Es, 2015). Noticing and responding to student thinking is a complex task that requires that teachers having a clear sen se of specific responses and ideas they are looking  for in the context of a particular science lesson or idea that  is being discussed and the practices that will mak e students’ ideas visible (Luna, 2018). Given this complexity, teachers need to implement instructional norms and routines that support these discursive interactions. Exempla ry teachers establish discursive norms using effect ive questioning techniques (McNeil & Knight, 2013), tal k moves (see Michaels & O’Conner, 2012), appropriat e responses to student questions and ideas to promote  elaboration and student to student discourse (Coll ey & Windschitl, 2016; Kazemi & Cunard, 2016), and noticing moments of interaction that can be leveraged for student learning (van Es, 2011). Yet, novice teachers have difficulty effectively using these practices and es tablishing consistent norms for student-to-student talk (Kang & Anderson, 2015; van Es & Sherin, 2008). In this s tudy, I examine a whole class discussion facilitated by a n ovice teacher where students were productively enga ged in science discourse with both the teacher and each other. In this whole class discussion, I identify the instructional norms that are evidenced by student responses to teacher questions and probes. I draw upon the work of Michaels, O’Conner, and Res nick (2008) who describe a set of practices and norms for academically productive talk in the class room (i.e., Accountable Talk). Accountable Talk was conceived to promote equitable student participatio n in rigorous academic settings and encompasses thr ee dimensions of practice for promoting productive stu dent talk (i.e., accountability to the community, a cceptable standards for reason, and knowledge). I used the Accountable Talk framework to document the ways that students responded to one another and the teacher during who le class science discussions. I used these interact ions as evidence for the instructional norms in place. Alth ough there are many participation structures where students interact with each other, I have chosen to focus on  whole class discussions because the facilitation r ole that the teacher plays, provides insight into the ways that the teacher notices and responds to student thinking during the interaction and it reveals the extent to which instructional norms are adopted by students during their interactions with the teacher and each other.

## Methods and study context

This study asks, how do pre-service teachers enact instructional norms intended to promote productive student discourse? In answering this question, I aimed to d ocument the instructional norms that created a lear ning ICLS2022 Proceedings 425 © ISLS environment conducive to whole class discussions. D ocumenting these instructional norms can provide te acher educators and pre-service teachers concrete example s to support teacher learning around the practices that encourage productive student participation and engagement in whole class discussions. This study takes place in a teacher preparation program in a large university in metropolitan southwest United States. The program has an explicit focus on preparing social justice educators to work in schools serving working class communities of color. The program’s c onception of social justice and equity centers on t he idea that students’ histories, experiences, knowledge, and dispositions are assets that support them to engage and learn in the classroom (i.e., humanizing pedagogy). To de fine asset-based teaching, Yosso (2005) uses Critic al Race Theory to conceptualize cultural wealth of marginalized groups that may go unnoticed by teachers. It is vital for social justice teachers to use the knowledge of students, families, and their communities to inform their classroom teaching (Yosso, 2005). Humanizing pedagogy calls t eachers to debunk notions of deficit thinking aroun d the academic achievement of students who have been hist orically marginalized (Bartolome, 1994). In the program, teachers are prepared to design science units and lessons using a humanizing approach. Teachers are also asked to dive deeply into what it means to be a social justice educator by reflecting on their multiple identities in relation to privilege and power. By understanding their posi tionality, teachers form a deeper understanding around why practices that value student voice are necessary for enacting humanizing pedagogy (Takacs, 2003). Teachers come to realize that to meet the needs of their students, critical theories and pedagogies must shape the w ay they approach science teaching.

The faculty in the program have developed and pilot ed a classroom observation framework that operationalizes science teaching from a justice and equity perspective (Nava et al., 2019). The second dimension in this framework focuses on promoting content discourse in the science classroom. The framework guides all the courses that teachers take in the program, and thus promoting content discourse was a major emphasis throughout pre-service teachers’ preparation program. The coho rt understudy included 24 secondary pre-service sci ence teachers participating in a yearlong science method s course. In the methods course, teachers have mult iple opportunities to video record their teaching during  their clinical experiences as part of a series of feedback and reflection cycles to prepare them for their performance task at the end of the program (i.e., EdTPA). In total, I collected 31 video recorded lessons (~1 0 min each) for a little over 5 hours of video. I u sed interaction analysis (Jordan & Henderson, 1995) to examine the opportunities teachers provided for stu dents to interact with each other. Interaction analysis invo lves identifying episodes of analytic interest and the iterative viewing and analysis of such episodes to identify p atterns in interaction. When useful, particular eve nts or sequences of events within episodes were transcribed to explore in fine-grained detail how talk is organized in the moment. My analysis involved viewing episodes of interest, generating potential descriptions and explanations of events, and testing those against the rest of the c orpus, through progressive and recursive analytic i nduction (Erickson, 1998). During my analysis, I focused on student responses in relation to teacher talk as we ll as any student-to-student discourse. For this paper, I focused on videos that contained whole class discussions because I was interested in how instructional norms were evidenced in this setting. Of the 31 video recordings, 16 included a whole class discussion. Of those 16, most of the whole class discussions resembled elicitation discu ssions (Michaels & O’Conner, 2012) where the teacher’s questions to students intended to draw out the “correct” answer, with little attention to student thinking and rare effort to juxtapose students’ ideas. Elicitation di scussions are important for making student thinking visible but are not the types of discussions that foster product ive science learning. One video was an exception. The whole cla ss discussion in Margret’s (a pseudonym) class stoo d out because of several moves she made that fostered the  types of students’ sensemaking that take place dur ing productive discussions in science classrooms (e.g.,  juxtaposing students’ ideas, promoting student to student discourse). Given this one video, I sought out to d etermine the conditions that allowed for this whole  class discussion to take place. I used interaction analys is to create a detailed account of teacher and stud ent moves during the whole class discussion and used this account to identify the instructional norms that were present during Margaret’s whole class discussion.

## Findings

To illustrate my findings, I describe Margaret’s 10-minute whole class discussion that followed a week-long experiment. In the experiment, students identified two locations within their school to collect and document insect biodiversity. Each group of students selected two l ocations for their data collection, a large space c overed with dirt, and another covered by grass, flowers, and other shrubs. The goal of the lab was to document the number of different insect species that collected in a sticky cup with a sugar solution throughout the week. The disciplinary core idea understudy is Interdependent Relationships in Ecosystems (LS2:A). I share excerpts from a transcript of the discussion to depict how Margaret promoted and managed the conversation as it unfolded over the 10 minutes and call attention to the different instructional norms present in the discussion. The instructional norm that students are competen t contributors aligns with the accountable to knowledge dimension in the Accountable Talk framework. Students shared their ideas and knowledge and that knowledge had to hold up against the critique and expertise of the other students in the classroom.

## Discussion and implications

This study sought to understand the instructional n orms that supported student participation and engagement in whole class discussions. To do this, I analyzed the interactions between a teacher and her students during a whole class discussion around a student-centered science experiment where they investigated insect biodiversity at their school site. My analysis revealed that students responded to the teacher and each other in ways that demonstrated that there were specific instructional norms in place that allowed for meaningful and productive participation in the whole class discussion. For example, Margaret asked students on three separate occasions the question, and why is that important? Each time, students responded with some reasoning that connected the evidence that they were presenting to the claim they were making. Margaret’s move of asking for reasoning in a way that made sense to students (i.e., why is that important?) supporte d student participation and engaged them in the sci entific discursive practice of using reasoning to connect a claim to its supporting evidence (c.f. McNeil & Krajcik, 2011). Anecdotally, it seems that practitioners use of the claim-evidence-reasoning framework often strays far away from the discursive practice from which it originated and is largely used as a summative assessment that students submit at the end of a science unit. Using the idea of providing reasoning to connect evidence to a claim as a discursive norm engages students in a knowledge building practice of the scientific community (Manz, 2015). While the instructional norm of using reasoning to  connect evidence to a claim is specific to the discipline of science, the other instructional norms evidenced in Margaret’s class were more generally aimed at building a classroom learning environment conducive to classroom discourse. The teacher education program that prepared Margaret uses a science teaching and learning framework that includes dimensions that are science specific and those that are more general (e.g., classroom ecolog y/learning environment dimension) (Nava et al., 201 9). As mentioned previously, this framework also has a dimension around promoting content discourse. Margaret’s effort to engage her students in rigorous science tasks (i.e., designing and conducting their own experiment on biodiversity), lead students through whole class discussions, and build a positive classroom ecology and learning environment are all evidence of her adoption of the se practices from her teacher preparation program. Yet, my review of the video recorded lessons from her cohort revealed that this type of learning was more of an anomaly rather than the norm suggesting that these instruct ional norms are difficult for novice teachers to ad opt and incorporate into their classrooms. Documenting the instructional norms present in Margaret’s class highlights the ways that the Accountable Talk framework manifests itself in the classroom, yet additional work is nee ded in teacher preparation to ensure that more teachers ca n incorporate these practices and norms into their science classrooms, so that students have opportunities to engage in productive conversations around science ideas. I draw two conclusions from these findings with imp lications for teacher educators and teacher preparation programs. First, is that teacher preparation programs need to find ways to support teacher development around teaching practices that helps them to incorporate instructional norms for managing and promoting student discourse into their classrooms. Encouraging produc tive classroom discourse is a pedagogical indicator  of classrooms that promote greater equity and access to sciences instruction (Lee, Miller, & Januszyk, 2014). Yet, productively promoting and managing student discourse, especially whole class discussions, is difficult for novice teachers. Research suggests that specific practices  in teacher preparation can support teachers’ learn ing of productive instructional norms and routines, includ ing rehearsals and field-based methods courses (Kaz emi & Waege, 2015; Lampert et al., 2013). In addition, having pre-service teachers analyze and reflect on video recorded teaching episodes, both exemplars and recordings of their own teaching, can support teachers to recognize areas for improvement around making student thinking visible and promoting student talk (van Es et al., 2017). Giving pre-service teachers multiple opportunities in thei r teacher preparation program to observe these inst ructional norms in classroom teaching as well as opportunitie s for them to incorporate these into their student teaching placements is needed so that they can include classroom discourse and whole class discussions into their unit and lesson plans. Documenting the instructional norms in Margaret’s class during this whole class discussion provides some concrete examples of what these norms look lik e in practice and evidence for their productiveness  in supporting student participation and engagement in classroom discourse. ICLS2022 Proceedings 430 © ISLS Second, I recognize that Margaret’s discursive segm ent followed traditional notions of scientific discourse and epistemology. Margaret was prepared in a justice and equity focused teacher preparation program, yet I did not necessarily see her engage with her students with issues around power and race that were emphasized in the program. Nor do I see students’ histories and identities affirmed within this discussion. It is not to say that Margaret or any of the other science teachers in her cohort do not take up these types of issues, just that they were not evidenced in the video recorded lessons that were analyzed for this paper. Agarwal and Sengupta-Irving (2019) take up this issue to broaden how and in what ways minoritized students of color meaningfully particip ate in classroom discussions drawing on their identities a nd histories as students of color and providing epi stemic diversity in the perspectives that are valued and s hared about whose and what knowledge counts in a sc ience classroom. Supporting teachers to develop the instructional norms and practices that support student participation and engagement in whole class discussions can both support students to learn science and provide opportunities for students to share and affirm their ideas, histories, knowledge, and identities in ways that contribute to learning within the classroom community.
