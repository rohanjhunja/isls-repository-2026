# (Mis)aligning dialogic practices and beliefs: Teacher change during video- based professional development

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This case study investigates how teacher beliefs, practices, and perceptions of student ability jointly shape the trajectories of teacher change within a video-based professional development (PD) programme on dialogic teaching. Through a comparative analysis of two English language teachers who participated in five monthly PD cycles, we triangulated data from student surveys, teacher reflections, and post-PD interviews. Findings reveal that while both teachers attempted to use Academically Productive Talk moves, their underlying beliefs critically mediated the outcomes. The teacher with constructivist and evaluatist beliefs fostered a co-constructive classroom and achieved significant gains in student perceived discursive engagement. The teacher with transmissive and absolutist beliefs appropriated talk moves for recitation, with no significant student perceived impact. The study concludes that effective PD must move beyond transmitting tools to explicitly target and support the evolution of teacher beliefs, which act as the primary filter for pedagogical change.

Dialogic teaching, characteri sed by teaching ‘through, for, and as dialogue’ (Kim & Wilkinson, 2019, p. 70), is widely promoted through professional development (PD) programmes that introduce teachers to its underlying principles and practical tools. While such PD has proven effective in altering teaching practices (Alexander, 2018; Hennessy et al., 2021), the process of teacher change is complex. Research indicates that this process is mediated by a dynamic interplay between teachers’ classroom practices, their underlying beliefs, and perceived changes in student learning (Desimone, 2009; Guskey, 2002). However, few studies have provided a rich, empirical account of how these three factors jointly shape a teacher’s trajectory of change within a PD programme. To address this gap, this case study investigates the journeys of two English teachers, Erica and Carrie, who participated in monthly video-based PD  for five cycles. By understanding their changes in classroom talk, their students' perceptions, and their teacher beliefs, this study aims to illuminate the nuanced and interconnected pathways of teacher change in dialogic teaching.

Dialogic teaching and talk moves Among many other approaches, teachers can use academically productive talk (APT) moves as a tool to orchestrate classroom talk. Talk moves  are ‘strategic teacher moves designed to open up the conversation and support student participation, explication, and reasoning’ (Michaels & O’Connor, 2012, p. 7). There are four goals underpinning APT moves: (a) helping students share their own thoughts, (b) helping students listen carefully to one another, (c) helping students deepen their reasoning and (d) helping students think with others (Michaels & O’Connor, 2015, see Table 1).

Classroom talk in video-based professional development Among the literature of video-based PD, PD cycles are commonly used for teacher change in classroom talk. In PD cycles, teachers can make plans and set inquiry goals for the next phase, leading to further observations and reflection on the results, which can form a cyclical reflective inquiry process (Vrikki et al., 2019).  Cyclical 1597 reflection plays an essential role in questioning existing practices teach er could lead to changes in teaching and enhanced self-efficacy (Gröschner et al., 2018).

Role of teacher belief for teacher change Teachers’ pedagogical beliefs, or conceptions of teaching, could be categori sed as ‘traditional teaching ’ and ‘constructivist teaching ’ (Chan & Elliott, 2004).  Traditional teaching usually values transmissive (teacher-centered) approach in teaching, while constructivist teaching holds constructive (student-centred) pedagogical beliefs (Cheng et al., 2009). When teachers carry out dialogic teaching, the underl ying beliefs would be that teachers are taking an evaluatist stance that knowledge  should be acquired through reasoning (Wilkinson  et al.,

2017) while holding constructive beliefs that students should participate extensively (Gröschner et al, 2019). In

terms of beliefs in student abilities, teachers may  hold the belief that students’ abilities are fixed and/or context independent, while dialogic teaching may suggest that teacher s view that students  have capacities to learn  and contribute to the lesson (Snell & Lefstein, 2018; Svaricek, 2024). As few studies unpack the factors underlying teacher change in classroom talk, this study aims to explore the following research questions through the case of Carrie and Erica:

1) How did their students perceive changes in classroom talk?

2) What were teachers’ beliefs towards classroom talk after PD?

The PD design and selection of the case The case, Carrie and Erica, was selected from more than 20 English teachers from 7 schools in Hong Kong participating in a PD  programme. The two teachers were selected  with two criteria: 1) they attempted to use a high number of APT moves across the cycles; 2) they showed different  levels of dialogic teaching after the PD sessions. To capture teachers’ understanding of dialogic teaching, the interview prompt “What is dialogic teaching to you” was used to interview all participants after completion of PD. The PD programme consists of five videoand reflection-based monthly cycles to promote dialogic teaching among English teachers, and the participating teacher needs to record their lesson video before taking the monthly workshop. During the workshop, teachers wrote a reflection based on their lesson video recorded and planned for the next lesson recording with reference tonew concepts introduced.

Carrie attempted to use APT moves a total of 54 times in her five video recordings (Clip 0 to Clip 4, C0C4). Carrie has three years of teaching experience with teaching qualifications, who was teaching Primary 6 students with a moderate level of English proficiency. All students in her class (n = 22) use English as a second language. Erica attempted to use APT moves a total of 69 times in her five video clips recorded (Clip 0 to Clip 4, C0-C4). Erica is a qualified English teacher with four years of teaching experience, and she was teaching Primary 3 students who have a moderate to high level of English proficiency. All students (n  = 22) are using English as a second language; except for one boy whose main language at home is English. We triangulated the data collected from the PD programme, including 1) student questionnaires before and after the entire PD; 2) teachers’ written reflections after each PD cycle; and 3) post-PD interviews. The student questionnaire assessing student-perceived discursive engagement with others in class  was developed by the Chen and colleagues (2020) with 5 items in total, using a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (rarely) to 5 (frequently). This scale aims to assess students’ discursive engagement behaviour with their fellow classmates (for example, ‘In English lessons, I listen to my classmates when they speak ’/ ‘In English lessons, I discuss with my classmates to stimulate thinking ’). For teachers’ reflection, participating teachers were required to write a brief reflection online, which could be seen by the facilitator and other teachers from  the same school. For post-PD interviews, teachers were presented with the analysis for all the clips discussed in the PD workshop, to stimulate discussion in the form of a semi-structured stimulated-recall interview. The questionnaires are analysed using paired-samples t-tests to identify changes in students’ perceptions for each teacher, respectively. For both teachers ’ written reflections and post-PD interviews, they were coded qualitatively through thematic analysis to identify the major themes. The data of the two teachers were presented in a comparative way, with the goal of illustrating the trajectories of teacher changes and their underlying beliefs, instead of generalising the conclusion to who taught better. 1598 How did their students perceive their changes in the classroom? Classroom talk: Efficiency vs. Exploration In interviews, two teachers articulated the role of classroom talk from two perspectives. Carrie believed the goal of classroom talk was to get the ‘essence’ of a problem efficiently by ‘instilling’ students and the ‘essence’ was pre-determined by teachers. This may suggest Carrie took a cognitivist perspective on students’ learning: Erica saw students ’ contribution as ‘meaning co-creation’ instead of individually giving the ‘ correct answers’, which might suggest her constructivist view towards classroom talk. Video analysis provided evidence for Erica to be aware of the features of her students’ contributions that teachers need to use to go beyond recitation: I quickly noticed from the data (video) that my students ’ speaking was coming from reading aloud, and that was just like a parrot mechanism… I would like more discussions, so that we could hear what they were saying and how they were formulating ideas. Talk moves: Testing vs. open tree branch Although both teachers attempted to use APT moves as shown in their video clips, t he different beliefs to classroom talk were revealed when asked why they used them. In the interview, Carrie said that APT moves could be effectively used to ‘test’ students’ thinking. As Carrie used APT moves as a way to ‘ test’ the students, she 1599 believed that classroom talk mainly served a way to ‘ read’ or ‘recall’ knowledge. Sometimes the APT moves could not help with student silence.

Instead of ‘testing’ the knowledge, Erica described the use of talk moves in the  metaphor of ‘an open tree branch ’ when describing the use of talk moves that built on students ’ strengths. In this way, students ’ contributions made Erica see that the talk moves work in shaping student learning. Making classroom interactive: Simpler vs. safer Both teachers reported that they value student-teacher interaction in the classroom. However, they reported different emphasis when asked how to make a classroom ‘ dialogic’. Carrie highlighted that the questions could be made simpler to build student confidence, which may suggest her deficit view of students. Erica highlighted the importance of building  a safe environment for not embarrass ing students. She avoided using negative evaluations of student responses. Although she allowed students to speak in a low voice, she held the expectation that the shy student could eventually speak up.

This case study highlights the role of teacher beliefs in the process of teacher change. In this case, Carrie and Erica held beliefs that aligned and misaligned with the principles of dialogic teaching, even if they both attempted to apply the tools for classroom orchestration, i.e., APT moves, in teaching practice. Carrie showed an absolutist epistemological stance and a transmissive pedagogical belief in her interview. She valued interaction in the classroom but aimed to maximise ‘efficiency’. She interpreted the talk moves as the way to ‘test’ student knowledge. A lthough diagnosing students’ problems is highly valued in classroom teaching, its frequent use instead of building on their thinking may deviate from the goal of APT moves, i.e., to help students elaborate their reasoning and think with other students  (Resnick et al., 2010; Michaels & O’Connor, 2015). This is consistent with research showing that teachers adapt new pedagogical tools to align with their pre-existing skills, knowledge, and personal preferences, rather than adopting them without change  (Grossman et al., 1999; Sedova, 2021). From the interviews, teachers ’ beliefs in student ability also affect their classroom practice. When encountering student silence, Carrie voiced that she would use a simpler question or ask the student to recall what had been taught, while Erica said she would stand closer to the students  to offer encouragement. These reflect their contradicting  perceptions of their students ’ ability, and when teachers hold a deficit view of students, applying dialogic teaching seems unrealistic. T eachers experience cognitive dissonance when their values in dialogic teaching conflict with their deficit beliefs about students’ abilities, leading them to lower expectations rather than provide support (Svaricek, 2024). In addition, dialogic teaching presents a complex duality for student identity: they aim to create an inclusive environment while simultaneously emphasi sing cognitive challenge and accountable student contributions, a combination that can be particularly threatening for pupils perceived as low ability (Snell & Lefstein, 2018).

PD designers need to consider teacher beliefs as an important aspect supporting teacher learning of dialogic teaching. Surveys on pre-service and in-service teachers reveal that teachers generally hold  relativist epistemological beliefs (Chan & Elliott, 2004; Wilkinson  et al., 2017), and thus it requires efforts for teachers to embrace dialogic teaching  as both belief s and practices. Changing teacher beliefs is challenging, as shown in Wilkinson and colleagues’ (2017) PD programme, where they found that teachers’ epistemological beliefs showed no development beyond a relativist stage. One opportunity could be when teachers encounter an ‘impasse’, which is the ‘conflicts, contradictions, uncertainties emerged’ during cyclical reflection that disrupts teachers’ ‘cognitive equilibrium’ or encroaches upon their ‘zone of proximal development’ (Svaricek, 2024, p. 312). Svaricek (2024) proposed a cyclical model positing that teacher belief alteration requires navigating an emotional impasse, a process in which the researcher (facilitator) is essential for providing support and co-constructing teaching With the importance of teacher beliefs, it is worth noting that the nexus between teacher beliefs and instructional practices is multifaceted and remains incompletely elucidated.  A vital direction for future inquiry is to determine why teachers’ epistemological stances often fail to translate consistently into practice, particularly by examining the barriers that impede even principled advocates of dialogic teaching (Reznitskaya & Gregory, 2013). This calls for further investigation on shaping teacher beliefs and aligning them with teachers’ attempts in dialogic practices.
