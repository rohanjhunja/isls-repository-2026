# “A Tangled Moment”: Unpacking Conflicting Frames by Examining Pedagogical Judgment

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Ambitious and equitable pedagogies have been widely accepted as a way to address systemic inequities. Current research and professional development efforts overemphasize practice and fall short in unpacking the complexities of supporting teachers’ uptake of reform-oriented instruction. These practice-based efforts often fail to adequately address dominant discourses in mathematics classrooms – of particular interest to this analysis is a culture of exclusion. This paper describes how exclusionary and inclusionary frames were evidenced in instructional practice and how those frames surfaced and shifted during sustained reflection on instruction. Attention to pedagogical judgment in the analysis lays bare taken-for-granted conceptions of ambitious mathematics instruction. This case is conceptualized as extreme, demonstrating the ease of invoking exclusionary frames, despite the teacher’s commitment to broadening access to mathematics and supportive professional network. Findings illustrate how attending to the components of pedagogical judgment can support teacher sensemaking around navigating the intricacies of ambitious and equitable instruction.

This study explores a common and important contradiction in mathematics teaching by looking at one teacher, who I call Julie Woodman (all teacher and student names are pseudonyms). Julie was an experienced teacher, committed to her professional growth and to building positive relationships and mathematical experiences for her students. Even though she was deeply committed to ambitious and equitable math teaching and to broadening access to rich mathematical engagement, there were still ways that exclusion came out in classroom activity. To understand why that might be, I studied two different sites of teacher sensemaking –– during a lesson and a video-based coaching debrief afterwards. My analysis of her sensemaking was guided by the three components of pedagogical judgment – action, responsibility, and reasoning – to describe (1) how inclusionary and exclusionary frames of mathematical activity and ability were evidenced in instructional practice and (2) how Julie was supported to surface and shift these frames. I see this as an extreme case (Yin, 2009) of a common phenomenon that occurs (namely, exclusionary experiences in mathematics classrooms), even with an experienced and committed teacher. Ambitious and equitable mathematics pedagogies have been widely accepted as a way to address systemic inequities (Boaler, 2002; Sztajn et al., 2017). Ambitious teaching works to support students’ conceptual and procedural understanding through meaningful mathematics engagement (Cobb et al., 2020) while equitable teaching serves to support all students’ learning. These pedagogies aim to position students with mathematical authority and agency by redefining competence in more expansive ways, opening up participation structures to allow for and foster more diverse ways of being, belonging, and knowing (Cohen et al., 1999; Esmonde, 2012; Gresalfi et al., 2009). However, classrooms do not exist in a vacuum – they are situated within broader discourses and power dynamics that are (re)produced through classroom activity and interaction. A great deal of professional development efforts have been made to support such reform-oriented instructional practices, and despite the ubiquity of these ideas, there is dearth of research regarding the necessary resources to support teachers and their learning in enacting reform-oriented teaching (Horn & Garner, 2022; Louie, 2017a).

## Theoretical Framework

To understand the complexity of inclusionary instructional practice and teacher sensemaking, I draw on three theories: the culture of exclusion (Louie, 2017b), frame analysis (Goffman, 1974), and pedagogical judgment (Horn, 2019). Broader power dynamics play out in classroom interactions in the stories or broad narratives that serve as resources for participants to interpret and organize activity (Hand et al., 2012). Of particular interest to this analysis is how exclusion is fostered in the mathematics classroom. Louie (2017b) has described a culture of exclusion. Traditionally narrow conceptions around mathematical activity and ability occur in tandem with the (re)production of marginalization (Chen & Horn, 2020) resulting in a dominant one-size-fits-few discourse that is persistent and invasive in mathematics classrooms (Louie, 2017b). Exclusion manifests in two ways: “(a) exclusion from meaningful engagement with disciplinary ideas and practices through narrow definitions of mathematical activity ICLS2022 Proceedings 671 © ISLS and (b) exclusion from positive identities as learners and doers of mathematics through narrow and hierarchical definitions of mathematical ability” (Louie, 2017b, p. 490). One way that exclusion is made salient is in how mathematical activity and ability are framed (summarized in Table 1). Frame analysis (Goffman, 1974) therefore lends itself as a framework in order to describe and make sense of classroom interactions and the lesson debrief. Work in teacher education is dominated by practicebased approaches, focused on the practical applications of teaching (pedagogical action). This has led to a predominance of “ideal types” (Horn, 2019), presented as tidy, one-size-fits-all solutions that do not account for the inevitable challenges that arise for teachers as they enact these practices in their individual contexts. Horn’s pedagogical judgment provides a framework to describe the interplay between a teacher’s pedagogical action, responsibility, and reasoning. I draw on this framework as a way to ensure that my analysis addresses the complex and interconnectedness of Julie’s classroom practice that is rooted in her commitments, obligations, and sensemaking. Analyzing one of Julie’s lessons provides evidence of her pedagogical action, while the subsequent debrief provides an opportunity to explore her reasoning and responsibility Teacher conversations centered on problems of practice make possible opportunities for rich interactional professional learning and reflection (Horn & Little, 2010), and sustained conversations and reflections focused on problems of practice reveal teachers’ pedagogical judgment (Horn, 2019). This makes the lesson debrief an appropriate place to explore her reasoning and responsibility. Furthermore, the pedagogical judgment framework makes it possible to bridge the classroom and teacher learning contexts in my analysis.

## Methods and Data

This analysis comes from a larger ethnographic study, connecting research and practice to understand and support urban mathematics teachers’ development and enactment of ambitious instruction (Horn & Garner, 2022). The research team developed a Video-based Formative Feedback (VFF) cycle to support and create opportunities for coinquiry into practice. The VFF cycle consisted of five steps: (1) the focal teacher identified a question or goal of interest; (2) on the day of the lesson, the teacher selected four focal student groups (blue, purple, green, and orange groups) to audio record; (3) the class was documented using a whole-class tablet camera mounted on a Swivl tripod, audio recorders for the four student groups, and a GoPro point-of-view camera attached to the teacher; (4) the research team reviews and analyzes the video and audio and identifies clips that support co-inquiry; (5) the research team meets with the teacher and their school-based colleagues within two days of filming to view and discuss the clips in light of the teacher’s question. The VFF debrief (step 5) is filmed with a wide angle view of the participants. I have analyzed two data sources in this analysis: the classroom and the debrief. The classroom video data includes a whole class video and audio recordings of four focal groups. The primary participants of the debrief were the focal ICLS2022 Proceedings 672 © ISLS teacher, and two researchers who facilitated the session (Brette and Lani). There was another researcher present, taking fieldnotes and a partner teacher at the school, however their interactions during the debrief were relatively minimal. The focal teacher, Julie Woodman, was a high school teacher with over five years of experience. She was supported by a selective and well-resourced PD organization, and Julie was deeply committed to broadening access to mathematics. In interviews, Julie expressed a desire to foster students’ understanding through mathematical discourse and prioritizing building relationships in her classroom. Focal lesson Julie’s AP Statistics lesson introduced hypothesis testing through a card game. Julie called on student volunteers to draw a card: if they drew a black, they got an extra credit point, if they drew a red, they had to sweep the classroom. Six students drew cards with replacement, and all six drew the same color card. The underlying question at play was If the deck is fair, how likely is it to get 6 red cards in a row? Following the game, students worked in groups during three episodes of groupwork, each followed by a whole class discussion.

## Data analysis

For the post-lesson debrief data, I applied methods of flexible coding (Deterding & Waters, 2021) – initially indexing the entire debrief and utilizing analytic memos to derive the “main stories” (p. 725). My unit of analysis was episodes of pedagogical reasoning (Horn, 2005). I partitioned the conversation into EPRs based on the central topic of the conversation, and using open, concept-driven coding, I coded for aspects of pedagogical judgment and inclusive and exclusionary framing. To code for pedagogical judgment, I attended to Julie’s pedagogical actions (when she talked specifically about what she did during the lesson), pedagogical reasoning (when she described her thinking, e.g., “I was intending…” or shared other contextual details to describe her thinking), and responsibility (when she expressed a sense of commitment, e.g., “I go really freaking fast because I have to”). When coding for inclusionary and exclusionary frames, I attended to what or who Julie and the facilitators discussed and used the framework described by Louie (2017, p. 496). I also looked for challenges or resistance to a particular framing. I confirmed interpretations with fieldnotes, interviews from the broader study, and with the researcher facilitators. For the classroom data, I created a side-by-side transcript for each focal group within each episode of groupwork and the following whole class episodes. The groupwork episodes served as my unit of analysis, coded according to the culture of exclusion framework to describe students’ conversations and mathematical activity that followed from Julie’s initial framings. Specifically, I attended to how much students were talking, what kind of mathematical activity came up, evidence for sensemaking, and how connected the conversations were (i.e., did students engage with one another's' ideas, taking them up and building off each others’ thinking?).

## Findings

I will describe how frames of mathematical activity and ability were co-constructed in the classroom video by describing Julie’s verbal directives and the resulting student conversations. Turning to the debrief, I will explore how Julie made sense of the classroom activity and describe how the exclusion that came out in the classroom activity contradicted with her inclusionary commitments to teaching. Conflicting Frames in Pedagogical Action Groupwork episode 1: What could be going on here?

Julie launched the first episode by telling groups “I need you to talk to each other about two possible explanations for how [drawing six red cards in a row] happened.” Julie’s direction to students was open-ended and nonroutine. Furthermore, her launch elicited focused math talk (Manz, 2012) and rich sensemaking in students’ conversations. The groups generated the following possible explanations: 1) all the cards are red; 2) students drew cards from the same location in the deck; 3) the cards weren’t shuffled; and 4) six students drew red cards by chance or bad luck. Although the groups’ resulting conversations were driven by answering Julie’s question, students freely contributed multiple ideas.

For example, the purple group began their discussion declaring that all the cards must be red, eliciting laughter from the group. Chris then posed a question about the probability: “They were all red? Or there's a 50-50 chance though right?” The group took up Chris’s question and engaged for several turns of talk. Alyssa confirmed ICLS2022 Proceedings 673 © ISLS his probability: “It should be if it's a standard deck of cards and she didn't rig them” and elaborated that cards were drawn with replacement, making the chances the same. She then proposed another explanation that could affect the probability – students were picking from the same place in the deck. Bethany disagreed until Chris suggested that Julie could have been making students pick the same card. He expressed doubt with this suggestion, but Bethany added on “Oh like if it was on the top or something?” The group’s dynamic was overall affirming of one another’s ideas, both in utterances to show agreeance as well as in the way they took up one another’s ideas and questions, giving them value and merit in the conversation. Chris’s question was especially compelling – despite no explicit mention of probability during the game, it came up in all of the focal groups’ conversations, intuitively and organically describing the probabilistic nature of the activity without any focus on calculations or procedures. Further evidencing this pattern, Danny, a member of another group, made a connection to statistical significance: Of course they're all red, uhh, so in regards to how we got it, it could have just been that she took all the black cards out of it, or…she reduced the amount significantly to the point where it would be statistically…it's uh statistically impossible for us to actually get a black card. The openness of Julie’s launch invited varied participation, and students were not limited by procedures or formulas, making it possible for students to make connections to other mathematical ideas. Students’ discussions were indeed mathematical in nature as students made sense of Julie’s question and their symmetric experience in the card game. Taken together, her question and the resulting conversations invoked the sensemaking frame of mathematical activity. Furthermore, the episode described here points to a discourse of inclusion, in terms of who had access to meaningful mathematical activity and ways in which students could be perceived as competent. Groupwork episode 2: What is the probability?

After settling on the two explanations, students are unlucky or the deck is not standard, Julie told students, “Assume I'm telling the truth. If I'm telling the truth, I need you to calculate a probability of actually drawing six red cards.” This was perhaps a shift away from sensemaking, evidenced by directing students to find “the probability.” Indeed, students’ talk centered on recalling the procedure to determine the probability. Unlike Julie’s previous question, students did not contribute as freely, nor did they take up one another's ideas. To illustrate, the blue group was only concerned with trying to calculate the probability. Although multiple students offered possible solutions, no one’s idea took root in their conversation: Isabel:  Six divided by… George:   No it’s one out of 52...to the sixth powe r.

Jacob:   One times [...] It’s a one in 64 chance I think. One in 64. George:   How you know?

Jacob:   It’s a one in 64 … George made a bid to engage with Jacob’s solution, however Jacob only repeated his answer instead of discussing it with his groupmates. Further constraining students’ sensemaking, many groups did not have enough time to reach a consensus, leaving students’ contributions and ideas disconnected and unresolved – valorizing speed and correctness. For example, the green group did not arrive at calculating a probability because they were trying to determine the number of cards in a deck: Farah:   How many are red and how many are black?

Danny:   So we already know it's independent you kn ow what I mean? Elijah:   How many cards are in a deck? 32?

Danny:   50? 72? So like, fifty two cards? Four tim es 13 is what, 52? And two jokers. 54. Farah:   But there’s still red and black.

The majority of turns of talk turns were trying to make sense of the composition of a deck of cards. The group surfaced everything they needed to reach an answer – the number of cards, the color composition of the cards, and identifying that the card draws were independent. The group was moving towards finding a probability; however their individual ideas did not come together. Overall, the focal groups’ conversations were answer-focused with little ICLS2022 Proceedings 674 © ISLS to no sensemaking, explanation, or co-constructing of ideas. Considering the nature of students’ conversations that Julie’s prompt elicited coupled with the time constraint suggested that this episode invoked the rote practice activity frame and the hierarchical ability frame. Importantly, Julie’s framing on the activity alone is not necessarily enough to be considered rote practice. This move will be further unpacked in a later section of this paper, but it could be conceptualized as sensemaking if students’ conversations suggested this kind of mathematical activity. However, her framing of the question and the time constraint she imposed led to a shift in students’ participation and the nature of their mathematical engagement towards routine, rote practice motivated by a singular correct answer. Students did not participate as inclusively as they had in the first episode, as some students were excluded from mathematical sensemaking and opportunities for competence if they did not draw on an algorithm or work quickly enough. Groupwork episode 3: Is Ms. Woodman lying?

In the subsequent whole class discussion, a student shared how to calculate the probability: “It’s like one half probability of getting a red. That times the sixth power. [...] One over…64” or 1.65%. Julie then prompted groups to discuss, “I want someone to use that 1.5% in a sentence to answer the question of whether you think Ms. Woodman is lying to you or not.” Along the same lines as the second episode, this question alone doesn’t necessarily point to a particular frame. However, students took up her question as a cue of using ‘statistical significance’ as students worked to recall a particular scripted answer: “Um:: we have like do you remember the 5% thing?” again suggesting basic recall rather than sensemaking.” Students in the focal groups were focused on coming up with a specific explanation or conclusion in a noticeably different way than in the first episode. In this episode, students’ conversations centered on trying to come up with a correct answer that filtered their sensemaking: Myles: I feel like she is lying because isn't it like a very small chanceLiam:  -I thought statistically significant-it's less than 2.5% Nina:  No it's less than 5%.

Myles made a bid that could have prompted the group towards sensemaking, however the following turns of talk immediately went to “the 5% thing.” Illustrated by these examples, students engaged in closed and routine mathematical activity that centered correctness – pointing to the rote practice activity frame. Considering all three episodes together, students took up Julie’s directives in ways that undermined inclusionary discourse brought out in the initial episode. As the nature of students’ conversations changed, the mathematical activity with which they engaged shifted from rich sensemaking to narrow answer-getting as students worked to recall algorithms and scripted answers. Importantly, in the second two episodes, students were excluded from mathematical activity and did not have a chance to show mathematical competence – what students needed to participate (and to feel successful in doing so) was speed and correctness. Julie and her students co-constructed frames of mathematical activity and ability that brought exclusion to the surface of classroom activity. It is important to note that I would not say that this exclusion cancels out or undoes the inclusionary discourse in the initial episode, nor does it necessarily work against the inclusionary culture that might exist in the classroom outside of this singular activity. What this suggests, however, is that the culture of exclusion makes these frames sensible choices in interaction, making exclusionary experiences a common phenomenon in mathematics classrooms. What is compelling about this lesson is that Julie’s pedagogical judgment is rooted in inclusionary commitments, as I will unpack in the next sections of this paper.

Then I also have a large group of students that are coming from regular Algebra II and this is their first time feeling an honors math class. They've gone through this process of failing a lot of times, but improving, improving, improving, but those other kids have been acing every test. I'm just wondering how that dynamic looks like in a group. ICLS2022 Proceedings 675 © ISLS Throughout the debrief, Julie elucidated two primary responsibilities that motivated her lesson design and instructional choices – one of them being to attend to issues of status. Julie described how she designed lessons with status in mind, saying “I try to take a lot of the theoretical math out of it, like the formula-based stuff, and I try to make it something graspable.” Then, connecting to the card game task, “there was no math involved there. Maybe a little bit at the end, but every one of them could have contributed to that conversation.” Julie’s reflections made clear that her intentions were rooted in her pedagogical responsibility to status. The task was open-ended, something that “every one of them could have contributed to” and, indeed, students did not need to rely on routines or algorithms but their intuition and experience instead. Her planning and design of the task, grounded in this responsibility, invoked the multidimensional frames of mathematical ability and activity. Julie also spoke of a second responsibility – one to the AP curriculum. She described an obligation to support students to make connections between important mathematical ideas. For example, she interpreted her second prompt as a connecting to another mathematical idea: “I thought it was kind of cool that you're finding a pvalue using prior knowledge, which I thought was kind of a cool way to introduce it.” As she narrated her rationale, grounded in this responsibility, Julie drew on the sensemaking frame of mathematical activity. Rooting out exclusionary frames in pedagogical reasoning As Julie reflected on the lesson and responded to the video record of the focal groups, she elaborated on her pedagogical reasoning, surfacing assumptions about what she thought students should be able to do. The first two clips highlighted two groups answering Julie’s first prompt (groupwork episode 1). Clip 1 featured Jacob’s correct probability with no reasoning or explanation, Julie diagnosed as an issue of conceptual understanding: “This concept, the multiplication rule for independent events, which we've already taught in regular stats, we go over that so many times. It's one of the hardest things for kids to just remember.” Brette presented a second clip of the green group who didn’t have time to come to a consensus about the probability. Julie was surprised that the group spent time discussing the number of cards, and she expressed that she expected that calculation to be very quick. Brette used this clip to challenge Julie’s diagnosis and presented two potential barriers to students: a pacing issue and the contextual underpinning of the card game impacted students’ sensemaking. In this conversation, Julie connected her pedagogical reasoning to her actions. Because Julie had expected students to answer her question quickly, she did not provide enough time for students to reach an answer. Brette explained, “It seemed to create a pattern where the higher status kids are getting the right answers and the lower status kids didn't have a chance to get there.” Although Julie’s intentions were grounded in her inclusionary reasoning and responsibility, as the lesson played out, her subsequent actions (the wording of her directive to the group and the lesson pacing) undermined her intentions and invited ICLS2022 Proceedings 676 © ISLS exclusionary frames of mathematical activity and ability as the students took up and responded to her moves. Contemplating the facilitators’ claims, Julie shared with the team: I think I wasn't expecting to use the 1.5 before the lesson so I think I was just kind of going past where I wanted to go with that. [...] That moment was so unplanned because what I said, the goal wasn't even for them to get, like going into the lesson, that 1.5% was not something I was going to go for at all. [...] Then I was like in the moment like this will look good. They know how to calculate this probability already. They can just do it real quick. It's less than five percent. They don't even need the whole normal curve thing. Julie provided important context behind her move to have students calculate the probability – it was an unplanned, in-the-moment decision. In fact, Julie explained that she noticed a student calculating the probability and saw this as an opportunity for students to move towards hypothesis testing by connecting to their understanding of probability. In this narrativization, Julie interpreted her move to be inclusionary, drawing on the sensemaking activity frame. Julie was supported to consider a different interpretation of her pedagogical actions – that the activity was actually exclusionary, drawing on the rote practiced ability frame and rote practice activity frame – by confronting the video record and the facilitators’ moves to press against her assumptions. Lani described these conflicting frames as a “tangled moment,” sympathizing with the tension Julie had uncovered in the debrief. Julie went on to explain how she now understood the conflicting frames in the activity: The deck of cards was a great way for them to develop those two competing hypotheses without realizing it, but then when I went to finish it off and do the math and stuff like that, that was completely unplanned. I wasn't mentally prepared for how to wrap that up and transition. As Julie engaged in sustained reflection, she laid bare her pedagogical reasoning, surfacing assumptions she had made during the lesson. The researchers challenged these assumptions, and using the video record, illustrated how students’ conversations during the second two questions exacerbated status issues. In response, Julie articulated a conflict between her pedagogical actions and her pedagogical responsibilities: the actions she took that were rooted in her responsibility to the AP curriculum undermined and conflicted with her responsibility to status and equity.

## Discussion

The theoretical contribution of this paper stems from drawing connections across the classroom and teacher conversation settings. It has been pointed out that current literature on teacher learning rarely draws empirical connections between multiple contexts (Lefstein et al., 2020). By attending to Julie’s sensemaking in multiple contexts, I was able to describe not only how exclusion came out during classroom activity, but also how she understood her actions, both in the moment during the lesson and later in reflection during the debrief. To only consider what played out during the lesson would be to tell a story of how mathematical ability and activity frames can contribute to inclusionary and exclusionary practices. In fact, current research and related professional development efforts do not adequately address the complexities of classroom activity and teachers’ sensemaking of ambitious and equitable mathematics instruction (Horn, 2019; Horn & Garner, 2022). An overemphasis on Julie’s pedagogical actions furthers a dichotomous view of teaching (that is, a good versus bad binary). Turning to the debrief as a context to study teacher learning, the debrief was designed to support her inquiry and to avoid evaluation and “best practices.” In preparation, Brette and Lani wanted to draw Julie’s attention to how status issues were exacerbated and students were excluded from meaningful mathematics engagement. However, the debrief was not facilitated to provide Julie with simplistic fixes like “ask more open questions” and “give students more time.” Rather, the facilitators unearthed Julie’s assumptions and used the video record to support Julie in connecting her pedagogical actions and reasoning to students’ mathematical activity and engagement. Furthermore, the debrief provided Julie an opportunity for sustained reflection and a chance to revisit her lesson which illuminated a conflict between her pedagogical responsibilities. Evidenced in the literature, reformed oriented pedagogies and similar frameworks are presented as tidy solutions (Horn & Kane, 2019), however, this analysis suggests that this is not the case. Classroom activity is deeply complex that is not dictated solely by teachers’ intentions and actions and requires time and careful attention to understand. For teachers to grow in their practice, it is necessary to support them with feedback that centers on their pedagogical judgment and space to explore and reflect on their teaching.
