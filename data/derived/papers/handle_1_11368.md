# In Screams, Shouts, and Symbols: Noticing Young Children’s “Knowing” in a STEM + Arts Unit

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper examines how a class of Pre-Kindergarten children exhibited-through play-the physical properties of sound-art production. More specifically, it focuses on how, through language, embodiment, and the material infrastructure of the learning environment, children enacted and recognized one another’s knowledge states to interactionally accomplish varying iterations of ‘sounding out’ their learning to peers. In so doing, it traces the embodied and multimodal resources of exhibiting how sound moved – using couplings of talk, gesture, and visual renderings – to render children’s visible and embodied knowledges across time. Drawing on three larger “tracks” across the integrative STEM+ Arts unit, this paper illustrates how a trajectory of “knowing-in-interaction” came to be rendered public and how, through these more micro-analytic moves of children’s microgenetic learning, brilliance and heterogeneity in early STEM + Arts was showcased.

## Introduction

“I don’t know if they could name it,” Miss Allison (all names are pseudonyms) – an early childhood educator and cooperating teacher – asserted, “but they felt it.” Reflecting on the video of shared instruction, she continued. “I mean, they’re young. You know this. You were a teacher. They do not have the exact language or the, you know, the science words, but they got it. In screams and shouts, and signs, and symbols.” Classroom science in the early years – as the brief exchange illustrates – has continued to provide young children the opportu nity to exercise curiosity and build theory through phenomena-anchored investigations (Chaille & Britain, 2003). From examining local life science in the early childhood classroom  (e.g., detailing homes and habitats of area animals) to asking larger questions of earth and space science (e.g., observing and recording weather), research in early  science and the learning sciences more specifically signals how these experiences are only bolstered through an affective and integrative approach (Harlan & Rivkin, 2012) that feat ures purposeful play showcasing new ways to represe nt scientific thought and social interaction that bolsters knowledge development (Danish & Phelps, 2011; Keifert et al., 2021).

Taking an “interactionist perspective” (Goodwin, 20 13; Hutchins, 1995) to learning, this paper follows Miss Allison’s invitation to examine the so-called “screams and shouts, and signs and symbols,”  of early STEM learning to explore how a class of Pre-Kindergarten children exhibited-through play-the physical properties of sound-art production. More specifically, I focus on how, through language, embodiment, and the material infrastructure of the learning environment, childre n enacted and recognized one another’s knowledge st ates to interactionally accomplish varying iterations of ‘sounding out’ learning to their peers. Tracing the e mbodied and multimodal resources of exhibiting how sound move s – using couplings of talk and gesture – I trace children’s visible and embodied knowledge across time.

Examining Young Children’s STEM + Arts Learning through Co-Operative Action Like others interested in children’s socio-scientific understandings of sound (see, for exampl e, Andrus & Scott, 2022; Chang et al., 2024; Shu et al., 2023; Suárez & Otero, 2023), this paper starts with the presuppo sition that learning is a process informed by individuals’ prio r histories of participation and that meaning-making is deeply connected to repertoires of personal practices that  inform our ways of knowing and being in the world (Lave & Wenger, 1991). As a result, this paper takes as its starting point that learning is not merely influenced by cul ture but that it is a cultural process (Nasir et al., 20 14). Through this perspective, children – and young children more particularly – make sense of the natural world and phenomena in complex, situational, and consequential ways. Embodying knowing and unknowing in interaction: Co-operative action as an analytic and theory of learning Recognizing that STEM + Arts learning is a “multi-party interactive field” (Goodwin, 2006, p. 12) wit hin which “multiple participants are building in concert with  each other the actions that define and shape their  lifeworld” (Goodwin, 2000, p. 75), this pa per traces the structures of early childhood inquir y through both the detail of embodied actions of activity as well as the talk and texts that emerge from it. My goal, in taking an interactional perspective to learning, is to highlight how young children’s participation in integrative STEM + Arts exhibited sophisticated “forms of involvement performed by pa rties within evolving structures of talk” (Goodwin & Goodwin, 2004, p. 222). In particular, I turn to Goodwin’s (2013) embodied participation  framework. “Building action,” according to Goodwin (2018), “by accumulat ively incorporating resources provided by others cr eates a distinctive form of sociality: […] is one of the ways in which we inhabit each other’s  actions, including those of no longer present predecessors” (p. 31). Through th is process, what he calls co-operative action, participants “build action by laminating different kinds of meaning making resources together” (Goodwin, 2013, p. 16). These actions include: “the mutual orientation of the par ticipants’ bodies toward each other,” oral language  and speech (i.e., screams and shouts), “hands making environme ntally coupled gestures,” and other phenomena “bein g intensely scrutinized by the participants as part o f the work they are doing together” (Goodwin, 2006,  p. 38). Through what he named as “epistemic ecologies” (Goo dwin, 2013, p. 8), these embodied frameworks of participating help position participants with respe ct to one another as knowe rs and doers. Goodwin (2010) later argued that these forms of embodied participation c an provide a “public substrate,” or “a place where diverse semiotic resources can be brought together and accu mulated through time into a public configuration” (pp. 1920). In other words, s ubstrates are sedimented outcomes of earlier actions. They ar e given their shape “through the transformative sequences of action that culmina te, at this moment, in the current action” (Goodwin, 2018, p. 39). Substrates, however, can be repurposed and app ropriated. As Keifert and Marin (2018) summarize, “ people in interaction create different worlds through the accumulative reuse and transformation of substrates, and the resulting need to support newcomers to become competent members in the cultural practices that are accumulated and valued in a given community” (p. 177).

Examining young children’s STEM+ Arts learning as co-operative action allows us to understand three things simultaneously. First, operations work on other semiotic aggregates and the material infrastructure they are developed by. Second, these operations and actions produce cumulative effects that then, lastly, become processes of learning that are both stabilized in the moment but also move beyond the present situation. Below, I explore how embodied interaction allowed children to create public substrates of shared attention (e.g., noticing knowing), which in turn transformed into resources for later embodied action (i.e., showing knowing) and explanation.

## Methods and modes of inquiry

Drawing on data  from an IRB-approved qualitative study examining how eight 3-year-old Pre-Kindergarten children experimented with sound during an integrat ive four-week STEM+ Arts inquiry, this paper focuses on three learning sessions: an introductory session wherein children exhibited knowledge of tempo through fabric dancing and two sessions  at the “soundboard” station. An arts-science installation wherein children worked independently and collaboratively with the researcher and their peers, the soundboard transduced sound through paint, metal tacs, pigment, vibrating speakers, and  canvas. Working across whole group instruction and later transitioning into small group center time, the sou ndboard became a staple of the inquiry. Acting as b oth an anchoring phenomenon that started the inquiry and a form of summative assessment, the soundboard bookended the month-long physical sciences investigation.

Generated data included observational notes, 1:1 in terviews with children, video recordings of coordinated social action at various areas of instruction (e.g., the rug, the soundboard station), and visual artifacts (e.g., child-produced drawings). Data analysis was an iterative process of examini ng children’s embodied interaction during the sound art exercise and tracing sound (i.e., tempo, volume) on its own accord. To understand the relationships among modes, bodies, environments, and materials, I followed Erickson’s (2006) whole-to-part analytical procedure. First, I watched the videos (n=157 minutes) all the way thro ugh, using field notes to mark time and note verbal/nonverbal phenomena. Then, we noted significant episodes, what Jordan & Henderson

(1995) refer to as “hot spots” or “shapes of events.” Working from an interactionist perspective, thes e episodes

signaled transformations of knowing and noticing knowing in the peer group. Taking these  select episodes forward, I transcribed them using common conventions in conver sation analysis, noting turn design and sequence (Jefferson, 1984), and including figure drawings representing a relevant aspect of the interaction. Examining the video of these focal events more closely, I traced how children used an array of semiotic resources to explain, exhibit, and qualify how sound operate d. Focusing on the linguistic and paralinguistic, which served to describe an observation (i.e., substrate) or present a cause-effect explanation (i.e., lamination), I present t wo episodes from the larger class inquiry These episodes, what I call tracks,  serve as illustrative examples of children’s knowing-in-interaction trajector ies. Doing so, I showcase the temporal unfolding of activity and how the introduction of n ew fields (e.g., the shift from children making the ir marker dance to transducing sound through their voice) were tied to shifting exhibits of knowing and unknowing in the STEM + Arts inquiry.

## Findings

In presenting findings as trajector ies of children’s knowing-in-interaction, I highlight moments of STEM+ Arts learning as dynamic, intertwined, and always already emergen t rather than as static aggregations of individual acts that were sequentially performed. Like more fo rmalized approaches to conversation and interaction al analysis, sequential analyses of the “dynamic, co mplex, and temporal unfolding organization” or lear ning and action (Goodwin, 2018, p. 33) underscore children’s individual and collaborative learning progressions. I invoke the metaphor of musical “tracks” across findings to invite consideration of how each transformation, independent from and entangled with the others, resonate s with the others, striking a chord  to understand how the children utilized embodied resources tonegotiate disparate perspectives as they co-constructed explanatory meaning. Track 1. Showing knowing through the material interface Early sessions in the STEM + Arts inquiry were grou nded in music and movement, a central curricular location wherein we facilitated learning about sound's physical and mechanistic properties through the visual and performing arts. From showcasing choreographed move ments of “making markers dance” to “match” music to collectively dancing to songs with varying volumes and tempos, children rehearsed “showing” their individual and collective noticings about sound. In this track, an exemplar of how children took resources from one field (e.g., the English language development block) to another, I zero in on an activity where the children were asked to match the tempo of music with the movement of a fabric. Here, I showcase how Dillon – the focal child (Child) featured here – not only signaled recognition of academic language (e.g., tempo) but recalibrated the resource rendered viable in the public substrate of playing with his swatch of cloth. Track 2. Recalibrating roles in laminated actions In later sessions, the soundboard station – a sound art installation where children used a microphone a nd an amp to transduce visual markings on stretched canvas – became a centered source in the whole-class learning environment. Indeed, this installation introduced a new semiotic  field for eliciting attention and participation. In this focal session, we highlight how children recalibrated roles, such as phonons (sound particles), by observing a peer shouting through the microphone. With the sound table displayed on the classroom SMART board, students re-presented what they said by choreographing movements to match the transduced sound and materials. Through a close analysis of the video, this track highlights how another child, Lucy  (Child), watched the Teaching Artist as a barometer of being recognized as a knower. Usi ng language from the inquiry, the lead teacher (Tea cher) asked the children if they are “playing like particles.” Quickly taking up the invitation, a peer of Lucy’s (Child 2) began to repeat the word “particle” before acknowledging that she “loves” the activity. Through examining gaze and gesture  on the video, I trace in this track  how Lucy ’s (Child)  epistemic state is recalibrated through the Teaching Artist ’s hand, smile, and nod  (i.e., laminated action s) as well as another colleague’s directive (for example, Child 3 suggesting to ‘move back’). Replaying the Disney song “Let It Go,” the Teaching Artist uses the child’s frame of their bodies mirroring the soundtable to question if the song's volume was “big or small.” She then quickly shifts her talk to reframe her query a nd asks, “Or is it quiet?” Through a fine-grained analysis of children choreographing their collaborative movemen t to mirror how the tacs were transduced through th e soundtable, I  showcase how knowing and unknowing  (e.g., Child 2 stating the sound is loud)(is not a static position but rather moved along a continuum across time, place, and material infrastructure.

## Summary and discussion

The goal of this study was to examine how young children’s trajectories of knowing and noticing knowing shifted across an integrative STEM + Arts unit focused on s ound-art. To address this goal, I examined the productio n of talk, text, and peer-to-peer interaction over time to interrogate how learning was co-operative, that is, how it was built on (re)calibrated action from children learni ng from one another, their teachers, and the learni ng environment.

As both tracks  illustrated, children’s growing understandings of concepts lik e volume or  their explanatory capacity to describe a phenomenon like sound propagation were mediated through both the ma terial realia of the inquiry (e.g., fabric) and interaction with one another (e.g., playing like particles while watching the soundboard showcase sonic movement and force). Alth ough their work was primarily focused on studying how pilots used their bodies to “imagine or pre-enact the actions they expect to take” in a simulator, I follow Hutchins and Nomura (2011) here who suggest that “while the space implied by the pr evious gestures and talk was completely invisible and imaginary, it endured as a  resource that could be exploited by subsequent mea ning making activities” (p. 24). In other words, what individual children showcased through movement (e.g., Dillon’s increased speed and gesture with the fabric) transf ormed over time as a shared resource for co-presence and relational understanding. Although minor, I believe these moments are significant and can be understood as what Vossoughi and colleagues (2020) refer to as “embodi ed pathways,” or “courses of possible action involv ing participants’ bodies and voices that model particul ar social, intellection and ethical relations” (p. 185). Although varied in size, shape, and form, these pathways cre ated multiple trajectories of knowing-in-interaction for the children.

This work contributes to a more nuanced understanding of the diverse ways young children use language, their bodies, and the material environment to accom plish the doing of STEM + Arts learning in the early years. These traces of knowing-in-interaction, as we saw,  introduced nuance into the organization of activity  that not only shaped how we  – as adults and teachers – interpreted young children’s work and representatio n of science concepts but also the forms of pedagogical moments and movements we adopted and took up in noticing knowing. Indeed, like others (see, for instance, Farris et a l., 2019; Pierson et al., 2022), I believe that with a focus on the body, early years educators can better underscore what is worth representing and how to re present it  in early science learning.
