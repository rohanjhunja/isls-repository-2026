# Teacher Sensemaking of Potential Educative Features in Science Curricular Materials

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Educative curricular materials have been suggested as a primary lever for shifting instruction in science education, however, little is known about what NGSS-based curriculum materials communicate to teachers and how. This study explored how 9 in-service middle school science teachers made sense of and used educative features in two NGSS-aligned science curriculum materials. We interviewed the teachers about how they interacted with 12 potential educative features when planning and enacting a lesson. In this paper, we focused on one feature, “Lesson-level Performance Expectations.” Our analysis identified three ways that the feature contributed to teachers’ enactment and growth: the written performance expectations 1) shaped teacher-student interactions, 2) directed teachers’ attention to the dimensions of NGSS in teaching and learning, and 3) were used to gauge progression of learning. These findings suggest multiple ways that educative features could open up opportunities for teacher learning.

## Introduction and theoretical background

The adoption of the NGSS (NGSS Lead States, 2013) i n the U.S. has led to significant nation-wide effor ts to develop high-quality science curriculum materials that meet the high demands of these standards. The curriculum materials that emanated from the new standards shar e a set of design principles intending to shift sci ence instruction from having students “learn about” cont ent to having them “figure out” key ideas through t heir increasingly sophisticated participation in science and engineering practices (Edelson et al., 2021). These design principles include: 1) a phenomenon-based approach that students actively make sense of puzzling scien tific phenomena throughout the unit, 2) engaging students  in scientific practices (e.g., modeling, argumenta tion, explanation) to collaboratively build knowledge, and 3) valuing students’ ideas and questions and making learning experiences coherent from students’ perspectives (e.g., Krajcik et al., 2014; Windschitl et al., 2018). Designing and enacting curriculum materials has bee n a recurring vehicle for driving instructional change in science education. However, in order for curriculum materials to be taken up as resources fo r transforming science instruction, we need to carefu lly consider the dynamic interactions between the t eacher, students, and the new curricula. Specifically, Remi llard (1999) argued that in order for curriculum ma terials to effectively support instructional transformation, features of the materials must speak to the teacher, rather than only speaking through the teacher (i.e., by directing teacher actions or providing materials for students). Features that speak to the teacher have been called educative features; they may provide specific content support, communicate curriculum designers’ rationale for ped agogical choices, or help teachers envision and mob ilize materials within their specific classroom context (Davis & Krajcik, 2005; Remillard & Kim, 2020). This study builds on existing scholarship on teache r’s sensemaking and use of educative features by investigating how in-service teachers interact with educative features in NGSS-aligned curriculum materials.  The need for this work is twofold. First, the emerging scholarship around teachers’ use of educative features has been predominantly focused on pre-service teachers. This work suggests that there is much to be understood is about how pre-service teachers attend to features that ar e intended to be educative (Arias et al., 2016) or use them in ways that lead to adaptive enactments across divers e teaching contexts (Drake et al., 2014; Land et al., 2015). These questions are even more critical to consider for in-service teachers, as open-education resource s that are NGSS-aligned have become increasingly available (e.g. Edelson et al., 2021) and the use of curriculum materials are a key lever for shifting how in-service teachers engage students in science. Second, to date, there has not been a systematic investigation of what and how NGSS-based curriculum materials communicate to teachers about the instructional shifts that the NGSS require. At the heart of the vision of NGSS is the assumption that teachers, the curriculum materials, and students work together to  create and then take up opportunities to co-constr uct explanatory accounts for relevant phenomena (Edelson et al., 2021). Thus, if NGSS-aligned curriculum materials are designed to be taken up in various ways by teac hers, depending on their understanding of their students, the ICLS2022 Proceedings 1489 © ISLS schools, and communities in which they teach, then a wide array of enactments are to be expected, and the various educative features that are embedded within these curricula should be designed to support meaningful adaptation. Here, we build on the rich scholarship in mathemati cs education on teacher’s interactions with curricu lum materials (see Remillard & Kim, 2020) and present a n exploratory study that examines how in-service te achers make sense of educative features in a set of NGSS-a ligned curriculum materials. In this short paper, we present findings from our analyses of one feature that reve aled both variation and complexity in terms of teac hers’ sensemaking and reported use for planning.

## Methods

We interviewed nine middle school teachers about how they interacted with two NGSS-aligned science curricular materials developed as part of a curriculum & professional development project. These two middle school units use a phenomenon-based approach to coherently support students’ sensemaking and knowledge-building through collaborative scientific practices over time. One unit was focused on cell development (biology, grades 6-8), and the other was focused on ecosystem interactions (biology, grades 5-8). Based on a review of existing literature about educative curriculum materials in science and mathematics (e.g., Davis & Krajcik, 2005; Remillard & Kim, 2020) and a review of 4 sets of NGSS-based curriculum materials from two developer organizations including OpenSciEd, we found 12 features that were consistently identified as potentially educative. Examples of these features w ere lesson-level performance expectations (Krajcik et al., 2014), assessment guidance, pedagogical rationale and additional guidance for specific activity structures such as Scientists’ Circle discussion, and examples of teacher questions and student answers for class discussions. Two members of the research team conducted teacher interviews over Zoom. 9 middle school teachers participated in the interviews, which lasted for approximately 1.5 hours and were recorded. Teachers’ experience ranged from 4 to 18 years of teaching. The teachers  had enacted either one or both of the focal units using the curriculum materials, except two teachers who were planning on teaching the units. All but one of the teachers who taught the units had attended a professional development session about the units before implementing them. The goal of the interviews was to learn how teachers used the curriculum materials of a specific lesson to plan and teach. After several general questions about their teaching experience and overall approach to material use, the interviewers specifically pointed out each of the 12 potential educative features and asked: Did you use this feature? Was it helpful, neutral, or did it detract from your ability to plan/teach in the way that you wanted to? How and why? Teachers were also asked if they had any suggestions to improve the specific feature. After transcribing the interviews, the six-member research team used an inductive comparative coding approach to analyze how teachers interacted with th e 12 potential educative features. Each week, at le ast two members open-coded a portion of the interview data and brought it to the full group meeting. The team discussed patterns and variations, occasionally conducting an additional round of open-coding to clarify patterns or resolve discrepancies and uncertainties. From the analysis, we identified major ways that teachers interacted with each of the features. In this process, we regularly compare d patterns across participants, and compared our em erging categories to  findings from similar studies in the literature (e.g., Drake et al., 2014; Land et al., 2015). In this short paper, we focus on teachers’ use of one feature, “Lesson-level Performance Expectations” (Lesson-level PEs; Krajcik et al., 2014) because eight of the nine teachers described it as one they regularly used when planning for and/or enacting instruction. In a ddition, teachers’ sensemaking about this feature r eflected adaptive use of the materials. Thus, the variations in teachers’ responses offer depth to the existing reports in the literature for how teachers read and learn from educative curriculum materials.

## Findings: Teachers’ sensemaking of lesson-level performance expectations

The Lesson-level PEs were presented in side-by-side sections titled “What students will do” and “What students will figure out” on the first page of each lesson i n the curriculum (see Figure 1). The “What students will do” section color-coded the PEs along the three dimensions of the NGSS—science and engineering practices (colorcoded in blue), disciplinary core ideas (orange), and crosscutting concepts (green). The “What students will figure out” section summarized the substance of the ideas that were to be expected as students would meet the PEs. Eight of the nine teachers reported that they actively used the Lesson-level PEs during lesson planning and/or teaching. Only one teacher stated that they usually skipped this section of the curriculum beca use the content was repeated later. Our analysis identified three ways that the PEs contributed to teachers’ s elf-reported enactment and growth: (1) shaping teacher-student interaction, (2) directing attention to the three dimensions of the NGSS; and (3) providing high-level information for gauging the progression of student learning. So, you can see where students are about that, and you can see how they grow.” Like this, the teachers used the color-coded PEs that matched the three dimensions of NGSS to assess and promote students’ and their own growth about the dimensions and to better connect the three dimensions in their instruction. Sometimes the written PEs worked as a reminder for them to address important ideas and concepts. For example, DV said, “Like, if we’re on the right track. And as you get later in the unit, I would start to look at this more like a checklist.” RP said that she often checked on the PEs while teaching to make sure that she was using time “correctly and usefully for students.” Teachers also used the PEs to report their objectives and progress to their administrators, especially about which NGSS components they were addressing in each lesson. In sum, the PEs worked as a reference and guidance for the teachers to “move forward.”

## Discussion

The eight teachers who reported using the Lesson-level PEs in their planning did so in a variety of educative and adaptive ways. The feature informed their understanding of content and pedagogy in the context of the lesson and informed how they might customize enactment to their own specific classroom context. Unit materials typically include learning goals aligned to standards, and therefore, this feature is familiar for teachers. Using this feature to, “Gauge progres sion of learning,” aligns with the general role tha t learning ICLS2022 Proceedings 1491 © ISLS objectives serve in teaching: to help prioritize selection of content and use of time, develop classroom activities, and evaluate student progress in light of the objec tives. This use also reflected teachers’ need to me et school policies of writing learning objectives on the board.

The other two ways that teachers used the Lesson-level PEs (“Shaping teacher-student interaction” and “Directing attention to the dimensions of the NGSS”) offer insights into how this information plays an “educative” role. In particular, RP’s use of the right-hand lis t of “What students will figure out” to structure q uestions and talking points for discussion reflects its utility in shaping her practice of providing opportunities for students to reason through science ideas using talk, a central principle of instruction that meaningfully supports the NGSS’s intent (Windschitl et al., 2018). Similarly, JK’s use of the feature to help develop the project tracker highlights its utility in supporting another core instructional pr inciple, students building ideas incrementally and through critique (ibid). In this way, the Lesson-level PEs provided educative support in shaping teachers’ ins tructional practices. Similarly, regarding, ”Directing attention to dimensions of the NGSS,” both RP and AH refle cted on how the color-coding of the crosscutting concepts i n the Lesson-level PEs helped them to structure the ir own learning about how to incorporate crosscutting conc epts in their instruction as well as helping them e valuate students’ growth in using crosscutting concepts. Thus, this feature helped leverage their own pedagogical growth, an important prerequisite for teacher learning from curriculum materials (Arias et al., 2016). We posit that the combination of the two lists—”What students will do” and “What we will figure out”— was important in making this feature educative and in driving the observed variations in reading and u se. The familiar structure of a PE and color-coding offered connections to formal standards, while the bullet points on the right-hand side offered a snapshot of what enacting  those standards in a classroom context could look like. Teachers attended differentially to one side or the  other, depending on their needs. At the same time,  the combination reflects a novel way of thinking about learning goals in that it emphasizes the “means” as well as the “outcomes” that the NGSS PEs are meant to achieve. Embodying the pedagogical practices that support th ese “means” is a critical challenge in the shift to NGSS (Windschitl et al., 2018); this study suggests that the Lessonlevel PEs may be one feature that can provide educative supports for in-service teachers in shifting their instruction in meaningful ways.
