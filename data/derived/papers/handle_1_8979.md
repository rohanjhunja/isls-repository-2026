# At the Heart of Learning: Affect, Identity and Care

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Over the last few years, learning scientists have become increasingly interested in research around student’s motivational and emotional processes, guided by a commitment to affirm learners' individual identities and support justice-oriented design and pedagogies. With ample evidence to show that the Covid-19 pandemic has exaggerated learner’s struggles with their thoughts, feelings, and identity, the importance of such research has never been higher. This symposium brings together an international group of scholars with varying research contexts (online, in-person, hybrid, blended), working with students of different age groups (K12, Undergraduates, pre-service teachers) to present their work around affective learning and teaching. The symposium’s focus on affective learning designs that acknowledge learner emotions, affirm their identity, foster designer empathy and build a pedagogy of care will springboard deeper discussions in the ISLS community to further explore “global perspectives on equity and justice that promote the empowerment of educational innovation for all” (ISLS, 2022).

## Introduction

This symposium responds to repeated calls by UNESCO (2020) and education scholars (Darling-Hammond et al., 2020; Ladson-Billings, 2021) to “redesign, reorient, and realign” and “build back better” beyond the crisis phase of the COVID-19 pandemic by exploring how learning and teaching can be situated in affective contexts that care about the students’ being.  Such care is deeply roo ted in and intrinsically related to emotions, cogni tions, and identities (Frijda, Manstead, & Bem, 2000). Understanding this relationship between emotion, cognition  and identity would help researchers to reveal complex key issues in the learning sciences research that concern justiceoriented pedagogies. Yet, although research on emotions and learning goes back many years, there has been scant connection with emotions, identities, and care. Further, research and discourse on the nature of nurturing affective empathy in students is lacking. This symposium aims to reflect on the relationship between empathy, emotions, and identities by looking at these co-constructed, overlapping concepts to advance our understanding of learning and teaching in varied contexts. Identities influen ce the kinds of emotions and beliefs of care and em pathy that individuals attribute to themselves and to others. Emotions, in turn, can influence identities and how we construct them. The learning sciences work could leverage such dimensions in novel assessments, learning analytics, and equitable justice-oriented pedagogies.

Overall Focus of the Symposium Organized as a blended event, the symposium will focus on increased awareness and discourse around humanistic learning designs and pedagogies. In order to “redes ign, reorient, and realign”, we plan to have all re gistered attendees of the Conference participate in an optional online identity-awareness activity that will engage them in empathetic scenarios in a simulated environment. These scenarios will be informed by our research and collective understandings of affect. The symposium will be enacted in two parts at the Conference. First, each presenter will introduce their research and present findings and i mplications of the same, engaging the audience affe ctively. Identity Feedback in Humanistic Emotional Configurations:  An Analysis of a Humanistic Knowledge Building Community In the learning sciences, humandevelopment is often viewed as a process of peoples’ transforming participation in the sociocultural activities of their communities (Rogoff, 2003). From this perspective, learning is a matter of identity, whereby people pick up the valued discourses, practices, and values of the cultures where they participate as they gradually become more central members (Lave & Wenger, 1991). This enculturation process is laden with emotions, too, as participants negotiate the unique emotional configurations as they learn how to emote in culturally-valued ways (Mesquita & Leu, 2007; Vea, 2020). Identity and emotions are thus inextricably linked.

With an eye on elucidating some of the ways that emotions and identities are interrelated in the context of schools and classrooms, our research has focused on designing and researching humanistic knowledge building communities (HKBCs: Hod & Ben-Zvi, 2018). This model of classroom communities not only addresses advancing collective knowledge (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2014), but also aims to support the transformation and growth of its members’ identities. We view these humanistic activities as essential to helping learners hold more tentative views of knowledge, be more open to considering and working with different viewpoints, see the connections between the ideas participants advance with their identities, and be willing to give and take feedback both about their ideas as well as their identities. The current study as part of this symposium focuses on identity feedback, which we define as the stories that participants in a community tell about a particular member about their ways of knowing, doing, or being (Herrenkhol & Mertl, 2010; Sfard & Prusak, 2005). Identity feedback can relate to what a person tells about themselves outside of the community, but the most potent type of this feedback also connects to the way any one person experiences another in the here-and-now of the group (Yalom & Leszcz, 2020). This type of feedback is often overlooked and at times culturally taboo, out of fear of embarrassing a peer. But, under the emotional configurations – combinations of feeling, sense-making, and practice (Vea, 2020) – that are unique to humanistic discourse, participants can be more direct and open with one another, ultimately giving them invaluable feedback that can help them make intentional changes to their identities. The humanistic emotional configurations include active and empathic listening, unconditional positive regard, and being congruent or real (Rogers, 1969).

ICLS2022 Proceedings 1811 © ISLS For this study, we collected data focused on the identity feedback that several students within a semester-long HKBC received. We used a grounded approach (Charmaz, 2008) to examine every instance of identity feedback that we could find that related to these students. We categorized the hundreds of instances that we found into nine types of identity feedback, each of which could be expressed at three different levels (nonelaborated nor specific, elaborated or specific, elaborated and specific). In this presentation, we will illustrate some of these ways that identity feedback was given in the context of the unique humanistic emotional configurations with the aim of showing how this feedback was consequential to the identity transformations of these students.

Evolution of the Academic Emotions of Academically Low-achieving Students in Knowledge Building Knowledge Building is a pedagogical approach in which students take collective cognitive, metacognitive, and emotional responsibility to continuously advance the frontiers of their community knowledge (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2014). Improving community knowledge, which involves continuous cognitive disequilibrium and equilibrium experience, is exciting but challenging, especially for low-achieving students. Low-achieving students usually enter schools with relatively weak collaborative, cognitive, and metacognitive competencies, and limited interest in learning (Becker & Luthar, 2002). Supporting low-achieving students’ successful Knowledge Building can greatly benefit them (Yang et al., 2020). The Knowledge Building process can trigger various emotions (e.g., curiosity, confusion) that may promote or hinder idea improvement. Emotions can critically influence students’ regulative actions, motivation, and learning processes and outcomes (Di Leo et al., 2019; Muis et al., 2018; Zhu et al., 2019). Generally, positive emotions (e.g., curiosity, joy) can facilitate learning, while negative emotions (e.g., frustration, anxiety) can hinder learning (Di Leo et al., 2019; Muis et al., 2018). Low-achieving students may be more likely to experience negative emotions because of their relatively weak learning interest and preparation. These negative emotions may lead to unproductive collaboration, and inquiry during Knowledge Building. This study aimed to investigate what types of emotions low-achieving students experience and how their emotions evolve in the Knowledge Building process. The participants were 120 students from two Grade 9 classes and two Grade 11 classes in Hong Kong. In total, 1,565 Knowledge Forum notes on Visual Arts were collected. Concerning data analysis, first, we classified the notes of each class into inquiry threads. An inquiry thread is a sequence of notes addressing the same problem or topic (Zhang et al., 2007). Second, we conducted content analysis to understand the academic emotions reflected in each thread (each note within a thread as a unit) using an academic emotion coding framework. The framework includes seven emotions: joy, surprise, curiosity, confusion, anxiety, frustration, and boredom; and none to indicate no obvious cues of emotions. The development of the framework drew on the model of impasse-driven emotions (D’Mello & Graesser, 2012) the framework for measuring epistemic-related emotions (Pekrun et al., 2017) and the definitions of emotions proposed by Craig et al. (2008). Finally, we employed sequential pattern analysis to analyze the evolution patterns of emotions in inquiry threads. ## Developing empathy through a learning progression: Perspectives of senior-

year engineering undergraduate students Fostering empathy within engineering education may have many benefits, such as promoting the individual engineer’s ability to self-regulate interpersonal difficulties, to understand the aspirations of society (Rasoal et al., 2012), to become intrinsically motivated to act selflessly (Batson et al., 2009), or to augment ethical decision-making skills (Oxley, 2011). Nonetheless, within engineering education literature, explicit attention on empathy has been minimal, as shown in a literature review research study by Strobel (2013). Engineering design courses provide space in the curriculum where students are required to empathize with the design context and potential users of their designs. Within design courses, empathy may involve an awareness of the broader context and how elements or multiple stakeholders within that context fit together. Often empathy is instigated by the instructor's encouragement and left to students’ willingness and intentions to truly understand their stakeholders. However, in many instances, design requirements include both technical and human-centered constraints. Specifically, due to the vast range of potential target users of products that engineers design for, it is imperative to incorporate the needs, emotions, challenges, restrictions of such clients into the design process (Gray et al., 2015).

Empathy is both a cognitive and an affective process. It involves a person's perceptions, thoughts, and feelings and how those concepts become manifested into a deeper understanding of others. Broadly defined, empathy refers to ‘the reactions of one individual to the observed experiences of another’ (Davis, 1983). As these distinctions show, even in fields where empathy is frequently used, it is a complex and nuanced construct. Kolko (2014) suggests that empathy, which he believes can be both taught and developed, is imperative for the creation of human-centered products, and that successful design requires the integration of human factors and an empathy with the users. In a developmental sense, there are several stages and schematic models where one moves from less to more advanced cognitive, social, or moral stages/schema through milestones. For example, we used conceptual models that integrate all aspects of development into a single unifying staged theory, be it their cognitive/ethical development (Lochrie, 1989) or their reflective judgment (Zeidler et al., 2009). In the context of this study, design courses are required for all students across their four-year education and offered through a learning progression called the design spine. We conducted a workshop and designed a reflective assignment for students to reflect on their various design experiences and how empathy played a role in each of the years. Through a thematic analysis, we found that students emphasized that the nature of the firstyear design course involved more on framing design problems and allowed them to explore their identities as a designer. Building empathy in the design process starts with making space for self-empathy (Krol & Bartz, 2021)). In the following course, students had to design for a local community. Students pointed out that perspective-taking was essential to understand the problem space that they were designing for at this stage (Hess ICLS2022 Proceedings 1814 © ISLS et al., 2015). As students advanced into their specialization years, they reflected on the specific tools that they used to gain empathy and how that connected to the norms of the professional world. Lastly, students articulated complex understandings of empathy in their profession such as through team interactions, course objectives, and industry expectations. The implications of empathy in engineering entails a deep understanding of the engineering problem, including the values and perspectives of different stakeholders (e.g. the end users, design partners who may represent different sub-disciplines or geographic or cultural aspects of the project) which will be discussed in the symposium presentation.

“Punks”, “Troublemakers” and “Inventors” – A Tension between Aspired and Embodied Values in STEM Education Particularly in science, technology, engineering, and mathematics (STEM) education, we value innovation, creativity, “thinking outside the box”, “pushing boundaries”, “challenging paradigms” and “coming up with new solutions” - (Hart Research Associates, 2015). But instead of allowing an unrestricted application of knowledge in order to come up with new solutions, many school systems and learning environments value compliant originality and conforming behavior over independent thinking (Beghetto, 2016). Thus, noncompliant personalities and non-conforming behaviors in young learners are often interpreted in a negative way, teachers try to shut it down (Ripley, 2016) and call the students misbehaving or “troublemakers”. As a consequence, classroom management styles like the imposition of sanctions are a first step toward penalizing unwanted behavior. Even suspension is often used due to what the teacher interprets as misbehavior (c.f., Lewis et al., 2008).

There seems to be a clash between aspired STEM attributes and values and what is considered a good student and how this notion is embodied in schooling systems. Defiance, disturbance, and misbehavior in general are serious issues in every-day schooling: It stresses students and teachers (Aldrup, Klusmann, Lüdtke, Göllner, & Trautwein, 2018; Kulinna, 2007) and distressed teachers are more likely to interpret behavior as misbehavior (Herman, Hickmon-Rosa, & Reinke, 2018). Most disciplinary classroom management styles lead tonegative emotions, impaired wellbeing, impeded learning or negative student-teacher relationships and do not lead to behavior change (Goodboy, Bolkan, & Baker, 2018). Unfortunately, a large number of students, who are defiant and do not have the tools to adapt, do disengage, lose interest and may even drop out of school. Furthermore, negative disciplinary actions in an US-context are immoderately applied to non-white and male children, especially African Americans and Hispanics (Blomberg, 2004; Losen et al., 2015; Townsend, 2000) and though male students and students of color and underrepresented minorities (URMs) are disproportionately more likely to be suspended and labeled “troublemakers” by their teachers, and thus suffer negative outcomes.

This contribution to the symposium brings together results on an emergent research agenda on troublemaking sharing (1) theoretical frameworks and conceptualizations to unpack the phenomenon of troublemaking and deviance in school, (2) and preliminary results on teachers’ conceptualizations of troublemakers (Strobel & Koch, 2019). The long-term goals of this line of research are to differentiate between individual actions and their intentions of so-called troublemakers; reframing what it means to be a troublemaker and how to respond and support behaviors of troublemaking.
