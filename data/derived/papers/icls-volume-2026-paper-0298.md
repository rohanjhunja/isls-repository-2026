# Exploring Connections between Teachers’ “Co-Learning” Interactions and their Opportunities to Learn Equity-Oriented Mathematics Instruction

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
There is a pressing need to support pre-and in-service teachers in learning about equity-oriented instruction in mathematics classrooms. In response, our project team designed tools to support mentor teachers (MTs) and teacher candidates (TCs) in “co-learning” (i.e., learning from and with each other) about asset-focused noticing in clinical settings. In this paper, we use conceptions of “togetherness” and “independence” to characterize interactional differences we noted while observing elementary teachers’ co-learning tool use. This study adds nuance to the field’s understanding of teachers’ collaborative learning experiences by pushing beyond the assumption that collaborative learning needs to be harmonious. We offer an illustration of how two teachers, operating in mostly independent and sometimes contentious ways, learned about equity-oriented mathematics instruction. We juxtapose these interactions with another dyad that fits the quintessential mold of an “ideal” partnership, who exuded consistent balance and unity as they, too, co-learned about equity.

Our project team designed tools to support mentor teachers (MTs) and teacher candidates (TCs) in “co-learning” (i.e., learning from and with each other) about equity-oriented instruction in mathematics classrooms (Kulow et al., 2024), specifically focused on asset-focused noticing (Stafford et al., 2026). The co-learning tools were designed with the assumption that having teachers collaborate around a shared equity-focus supports meaningful learning opportunities for both teachers (Thompson et al., 2015). We found this was indeed true; in interviews, teachers consistently reported the tools supported productive learning experiences (Stafford et al., 2026). However, our firsthand observations indicated the nature of teachers’ tool use interactions varied, which we believe had important implications for what and how teachers learned about equity. We explore tool uses for two qualitatively different MT-TC dya ds to understand how interactional differences shaped teachers’ opportunities to learn. We chose one dyad that struck us as unquestionably collaborative; they smiled, laughed, and finished each other’s sentences as they engaged in seemingly balanced and supportive conversations. In contrast, the second dyad appeared tense; they did not smile or laugh and occasionally interrupted each other as they tried to reach consensus. Our intent is not to determine which dyad was better since we believe productive learning happened in both cases. Rather, we aim to understand how interactional dynamics shaped teachers’ opportunities to learn in the two contrasting cases. We introduce conceptions of “togetherness” and “independence” to characterize the dyads’ interactional differences and explore the following questions: 1) How did dyads exhibit togetherness and independence during tool use? 2) How did togetherness and independence shape opportunities to learn about equity-oriented instruction? Analyses suggest the first dyad’s togetherness  supported them to investigate genuine curiosities about students’ mathematical engagement through shared inquiry and complementary observations. On the other hand, independence opened up opportunities for the TC in the second dyad to shi ft focus to students’ mathematical strengths (as opposed to what students did not know), allowing them to practice asset-focused noticing together.

## Theoretical framework

We draw on sociocultural and situated theories that claim learning happens through participation in cultural activities (Lave & Wenger, 1991; Wenger, 1998), with discursive practices as central to developmental processes (Lerman, 2001). We look for teachers’ “learning” in what we see teachers actively practicing as they engage with one another (e.g., what do teachers say and do?) (Lave & Wenger, 1991; Putnam & Borko, 2000). We consider equity-oriented mathematics instruction to be teaching that disrupts patterns of exclusion and celebrates the brilliance of students who are often marginalized (e.g., Louie, 2017). Our tools focus on supporting teachers to learn about one slice of equity-oriented teaching, that of asset-focused noticing (Louie et al., 2021; van Es et al., 2022). Enacting equitable teaching is complex, responsive work that requires teachers to enga ge in ongoing learning, ideally in collaboration with others (Horn & Garner, 2022; Gibbons et al., 2021). 1981 Our project team’s “humble” theory (Cobb et al., 2003) of co-learning equity-oriented instruction (i.e., how teachers learn with and from each other about equity-oriented practices) continues to evolve. Differences in how teachers engage with each other have implications for how and what teachers might learn. Teachers can share ownership of tasks with their partner, engage in tasks through collective and connected contributions, and exhibit solidarity as they explore shared curiosities and work toward shared goals, which we conceptualize as exhibiting “togetherness” in co-learning (Baldinger & Louie, 2025; Roth & Tobin, 2010). They can also divide ownership of tasks, engage in tasks through individual and separate contributions, and exhibit discord as they privilege their own perspectives and goals, which we conceptualize as exhibiting “independence” in co-learning. Both togetherness and independence have affordances (and constraints) for learning. This paper aims to clarify what these affordances and constraints might be and how they play out in the context of our co-learning tools. Data comes from a larger corpus collected for The Co-Learning Math Teaching Project, funded by NSF. The same TC (Christine) participated in both focal dyads. Christine switched MTs mid-year as was customary in the teacher education program. She was paired with Rachel (MT) Sept-Dec, then Dana (MT) Jan-June. Christine was an undergraduate elementary teacher candidate. Rachel was a first-grade teacher (16 yrs teaching) who had prior experience reflecting through an equity lens. Dana was a third-grade teacher (22 yrs teaching) who had far less experience learning about equity. Participants identified as white women. Names are pseudonyms. Christine used the co-learning tool seven times over the school year across both partnerships. For analysis, we selected her first tool use with Rachel to compare with her last tool use with Dana. The co-learning tool prompted teachers to select an equity-oriented focus question, plan for and enact observations and check-ins during instruction, and reflect after instruction on learning and next steps. Participants self-recorded tool uses with iPads. Tool use videos were transcribed for speech and gestures; audio recordings of check-ins were transcribed for speech. Transcripts were split into Conversation Topics, defined as talk pertaining to one topic. Interaction Codes were developed through open coding (Corbin & Strauss, 2014) and assigned to excerpts of Conversation Topics, identifying interactional aspects relevant to teachers’ learning. Interaction Codes included: Contributions (e.g., expressed curiosity, proposed next step), Pronoun Use  (i.e., collective (we), singular (I)), and Discourse Events (e.g., interruption, repeated partner’s words).  Next, Conversation Topics were coded as exhibiting Togetherness and/or Independence. Togetherness was operationalized as interactions with shared curiosities, collective pronouns, or discourse events implying solidarity (e.g., finished partner's sentence, overlapping talk-same ideas). Independence was operationalized as interactions wi th individual curiosities, singular pronouns, or discourse events implying discord (e.g., interruptions, overlapping talk-different ideas). Finally, equity-related Conversation Topics (e.g., supporting access, identifying students’ assets) were flagged. Case 1 (MT Rachel & TC Christine): Learning through Togetherness Rachel and Christine exhibited togetherness consistently throughout their tool use as they pursued their agreed upon co-noticing focus (understanding students’ varied strategies). They expressed shared curiosities, including wondering together about students’ mathematical strategies and how manipulatives might support students’ access to content. Collective pronouns dominated conversations, with both using the word “our” numerous times, indicating shared ownership (e.g., “our focal question,” “our students”). There were a few instances of singular pronoun use, but they were connected to shared outcomes (e.g., co-teaching the lesson, developing shared understandings of students’ strengths). Rachel and Christine also displayed numerous signs of solidarity through their discourse, with no signs of discord. They said the same words at the same time (9 times), repeated what their partner said (9 times), and finished their partner’s sentence (7 times), indicating they agreed on lesson goals, plans for execution, and assessments of students’ strengths and needs. They were discursively in sync. For example, at the end of their debrief, Rachel said, “All the strategies that we discussed were able to be shared.” Christine replied, “Yep.” Both nodded, and after a 2-second pause they said in unison, “which was excellent.” Christine responded, “Jinx!” Rachel followed, “Owe me a coke. Just kidding!” They both laughed. Togetherness enhanced opportunities for both Rachel and Christine to learn about equity-oriented mathematics instruction by facilitating opportunities for them to practice asset-focused noticing together. The following example illustrates how this dyad’s togetherness supported them to share ideas and observations freely, engage in collective sense-making, and develop next steps, in support of students’ deep engagement with mathematics. Case 2 (MT Dana & TC Christine): Learning through Independence In contrast to Case 1, Dana and Christine’s tool use was dominated by independence, as they pursued their agreed upon co-noticing focus (building on students’ partial understandings). The only spoken curiosity was an individual curiosity expressed by Christine. Christine wondered about students’ understandings of fractions, noting they seemed to forget at first but were quick to remember once they started talking about them. Instead of validating the curiosity, Dana dismissed it by pointing out what students did not yet know how to do. This dyad also used more singular pronouns than collective, most of which were used to share individual assessments of students (e.g., “I think [student] has a really great grasp of…”) and propose next steps (e.g., “I feel good moving on”). Finally, this dyad displayed discord consistently through their discourse in the form of interruptions and talking over one another, indicating they disagreed about what they should talk about, noticed different aspects of students’ engagement, and had different assessments of students’ understanding. For example, Dana started the check-in by saying the lesson was taking too long. Christine interrupted mid-sentence, changing the topic, to share observations of students “productively struggling.” In addition to displays of independence, there were a few notable signs of limited togetherness, indicating this dyad agreed on the goal of “pulling out what [students] are understanding” and were committed to using the co-learning tool as designed. Collective pronouns were used occasionally (“We’re trying to get everybody to have a voice”), and there was an attempt at solidarity displayed through discourse (6 total acts of solidarity, compared to the other dyad’s 25), with Christine finishing Dana’s sentences twice, along with four instances of repeated or overlapping same talk. Like the other dyad,  Dana and Christine had opportunities to learn about equity through their tool use. However, in contrast, it was independence-with a bit of togetherness-that supported their learning of asset-focused noticing practices. Similar to the previous example, independence created space for Christine to share asset-oriented noticing, but this time independence was exhibited through less contentious means. Dana began this exchange by using a singular pronoun. She asked, “Anything else you want to talk about?” instead of asking, “Anything else we want to talk about?” This prompt elicited Christine’s individual thoughts as she held the physical co-learning tool in her hand, referencing the tool’s debrief section (B8). Christine took this opportunity to summarize some of their asset-focused noticing activities, including praising Dana’s decision to include [Student D]’s ideas in the whole class lesson (B10). Even though Dana was not the one articulating students’ mathematical strengths, this passage shows how she leveraged one of the strengths Christine highlighted during their check-in. We presume Dana’s willingness to (perhaps tentatively) accept and implement asset-focused practices was facilitated by the dyad’s shared goal to uncover students’ understandings. In contrast to traditional mentor-mentee relationships, it was the teacher candidate in this case who took the lead in modeling the targeted instructional practices. Our analysis suggests facilitating productive learning opportunities is not simply about how well two teachers get along or how well their personalities or beliefs align. In fact, we can imagine dyads exhibiting high degrees of togetherness (or independence), whose learning opportunities would not have been nearly as robust as the ones presented here. In the case of Rachel and Christine, they both came to the partnership with well-established assetorientations (Rachel from years of professional development and Christine from her teacher education program), so their togetherness served to reinforce their equity commitments and give them opportunities to practice and reflect on asset-focused noticing as like-minded colleagues (Horn & Garner, 2022). But if the ir shared orientations, beliefs and commitments had been more aligned with traditional hierarchical and exclusionary views of mathematical competence (Louie, 2017), it is certainly possible that togetherness would have severely constrained the dyad’s opportunities to learn about equity and instead served to uphold the status quo. In the case of Dana and Christine, their orientations and previous experiences were not well aligned, but they did agree to undercover students’ understandings and both seemed committed to using the co-learning tool with fidelity. This minimal amount of togetherness was enough to support Christine in using acts of independence to challenge the status quo and further an equity-oriented learning agenda for her and her partner.
