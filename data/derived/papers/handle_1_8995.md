# Exploring Identity Resources in Co-Designing Chatbots

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Co-design with students honors their lived experiences and invites participants to create novel solutions. We present insights from designing with youth ages 14-16 to create a collaborative chatbot in science. We explore the identity resources (material, relational, and ideational) embedded in the design activities, with implications for how designers and educators can provide access to resources and expand how youths participate in design practices.

## Introduction

Co-design invite stakeholders such as students to r eimagine learning environments, bring in lived expe riences from other contexts, and creatively develop novel a pproaches (Bang & Vossoughi, 2016). Co-design cente rs young people’s voices and challenges the power stru ctures that underlie learning and participation. Ce ntral to recognizing youth’s voices is an understanding of t he different identity resources—material, relationa l, and ideational—that they draw from to relate to shared design practices (Nasir & Cooks, 2009; Roque, 2020). Material resources describe the physical artifacts and setti ngs for the practice. Relational resources capture the social relationships that enable youth to strengthen their  connections to the practice. Finally, ideational r esources describe how youths position themselves and their v alues within the practice. Individuals use these id entity resources to develop their positions within “figure d worlds” of socially constructed norms and expecta tions (Holland et al., 1998). Figured worlds may overlap.  For instance, the figured world of a design sessio n might intersect with the figured world of interactions between co-designers and facilitators, and the larger figured world of designing. We explore (1) the features of identi ty resources that may foster participation in desig n practices, and (2) how these resources support the figured wor lds of design and youths’ lived experiences to over lap. Overlapping figured worlds allows individuals to adapt their positions and participation in design practices.

## Methodology

The project took place in an environmental science curriculum for students in grades 8-11 in the south western U.S. Students learned about a local park’s efforts to restrict overfishing, created models of the marine ecosystems, collected and analyzed data on the park’s biodiversity, and made recommendations about conservation efforts to park scientists. The chatbot was part of a small-group modeling activity to provide conceptual and participation prompts to students. The research team conducted co-design meetings to align the chatbot’s designs and talks with students’ values. Participants included one male and four female students (hereon addressed as co-designers). The co-designers, who self-reported interests in scienc e and design, were recruited from classes implement ing the curriculum. The design team participated in four 60-minute online sessions over 2 months, where they e ngaged in activities facilitated by the researchers (a graduate and undergraduate student in Education). Data sources included design artifacts (e.g., part icipants’ chats, co-designed digital artifacts), field notes written by the facilitators during and immediately after each design session, and participants’ verbal reflections before and after the design process. Examples of the reflection questions were “What are you hoping to gain from this experience” and “What are your takeaways from the design process?” The author and a research assi stant analyzed data through the lens of identity resource s (Nasir & Cooks, 2009), with a focus on how participants interacted with the physical materials, relationships, and self-positioning within design practices.

## Connect material resources to participants’ lived experiences

Material resources involve the physical space in which design meetings occurred and the activities and artifacts within that space. The activities intentionally involved artifacts familiar to the participants. The first activity asked participants to find an object in their home and describe how their desired chatbot may take up the object’s features (texture, shape, characteristics, etc.). Alice (11 th-grader, Asian American female) immediately grabbed  a blue stuffed animal on her desk, and stated that the bot should be “round, soft, and cute”. Meanwhile, Alex (11th-grader, ICLS2022 Proceedings 1918 © ISLS Asian American male) showed the group his home assistant and mentioned “smart and responsive” as desirable chatbot characteristics. Involving familiar objects might have allowed participants to connect the fig ured world of design and agents and relationships in their home experiences. For example, in designing how the chatbot could provide hints to students in the modeling activities, Manila (10th-grader, Latinx female) stated: “You know, like my Google Home or Alexa, when you ask it questions it replies with information. Our bot cando the sam e: Oh you’re stuck. You can start by thinking about fish, sand, planktons, whales,...” Furthermore, the activities spanned beyond the phys ical space of virtual meetings. Co-designers completed weekly cultural probes (e.g., “take a pho to that connects your daily activities such as comm uting to schools and the marine ecosystems”). Annie (10th-grader, Latinx female) reflected on how her conversations with friends had revolved around a recent oil spill close to school. This observation led to a discussion in the co-design group around how prompts focused on memorable events might enrich students’ responses to the bot. Promote dialogues Relational resources involve opportunities for connection with other co-designers and facilitators. The facilitators were intentional about promoting dialogues among participants. Opportunities for connections with others, such as through design role-playing, might enable participants to empathize with the users they were design ing for. Consider the overlap of figured worlds of designing, schools, and personal lives in the following vignette. In a design session, co-designers joined an empath y mapping activity to imagine the profiles of futur e users with the chatbot, the challenges these fictio nal profiles may face, and the design opportunities  that arose. Co-designers engaged in role-playing to create and act out learner-chatbot dialogues, based on the learner profiles they identified. Distracted George was a profile that co-designers came up with, as someone with average science knowledge and can be easily distracted. Co-designers came up with solutions to remind the fictional George that he was going off-task or to alert the teachers. When asked about the rationale for these solutions, Alex stated that “it’s a classroom norm that kids react to”. This co mment illustrates how youth grounded designs in experiences as learners, often with teachers’ monitoring. Later  in the design session, following role-playing betw een the imagined bot and “George”, co-designers identified a new strategy for the bots to share experiences. Manila suggested: “The bots can say: I will tell you my ex perience maybe it will help you remember what´s yours, and then tell a little story. This will make it more hu man and personal.” Manila’s comments illustrate a s hift in her design lens to weave experiences and multiturn, relational conversations with users into the chatbot’s dialogues. She positioned the chatbot as a guide for narrative construction, acting as a “human and personal” facilitator.

## Foster participants’ agency as ideational resources

Ideational resources refer to what is valued within a practice and how youths evaluate their success as designers. Prior to the design sessions, some participants lik e Manila and Annie had reservations that they were “not computer scientists” because they “haven’t built technologies”. They often turned to the facilitators to evaluate the design solutions with questions such as “does t hat sound right?” We saw this as an opportunity to remind students of practices that designers leveraged, such as user testing. Between the third and fourth design sessions, the co-design team administered a written survey to an external audience—38 eighth-graders who were also taking the marine science curriculum. The survey asked the eighth graders to choose between the co-designed dialogues (e.g., off-task nudge versus storytelling). Co-desi gners then went over the survey results together. T his process allowed youth to position themselves as designers evaluating their work with a broader audience. Manila reflected, “I’m excited because there are only a few of us here, so it’s nice to hear how others like what we design”. In this work, we outline the identity resources that allow young people to build from experiences at home, in school, and as designers. We highlight the utility of materials and artifacts that bridge across physical spaces, the role of dialogues among participants, and the focus on youth’s agency to develop ideational resources.
