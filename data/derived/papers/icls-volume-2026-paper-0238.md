# More Than Meets the Eye: Transforming Black Boys’ STEM Identities through Liberatory Co-Design and Computational Making

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper examines how liberatory co-design within computational making fosters agency and technical skill development among Black boys. Drawing on a pilot program in the Black Male Animation Lab, we show how connecting students’ cultural interests like Transformers and anime to stop-motion animation created co-designed learning. Findings highlight three themes: storytelling functioned as identity work through community narratives; collaboration and co-design shaped the lab as students negotiated problem-solving, positioning themselves as leaders; and students bridged cultural and technical literacies by connecting fabrication to culturally relevant themes. These findings demonstrate how liberatory co-design transformed the lab into a space where Black boys resisted deficit framings, affirmed identities, and pursued STEM on their own terms, offering a methodological contribution to equity-centered learning.

## Literature review: Participant-centered solutions

Stop-motion animation (SMA) exemplifies computational making as a technically rigorous and culturally resonant practice that combines digital and physical processes (Berland & Duncan, 2016). It provides tactile engagement (Farrokhnia et al., 2020) while linking narrative and identity to computational processes (Wishart, 2017). For example, newcomer youth used SMA to represent stories of forced migration, allowing marginalized youth tonegotiate lived experiences through technical practice (Dutta et al., 2024). For Black boys, connecting SMA to interests in comics, anime, or Transformers transforms computational making into a culturally situated practice that affirms identity while building fluency in STEM. Fabrication extends this connection, moving ideas from digital models into tangible products through iterative design (Bang & Vossoughi, 2016; Blikstein, 2013). 1672 When tied to culturally meaningful narratives like customizing toys, it becomes a computational making practice that affirms agency and identity. For Black boys, fabricating parts for animation projects situates technical learning within their cultural worlds, transforming making into a liberatory act. These practices align with culturally responsive computing, where computational skills are best developed in identity-affirming contexts (Worsley, 2021). Designing affirming computational environments is crit ical, as research on ambient belonging shows how stereotypical cues in STEM environments candiscourage participation (Cheryan et al., 2009). Programs such as YOUmedia Chicago and Digital Youth Divas demonstrate how embedding computational making in community and cultural contexts strengthens students’ self-perceptions as creators (Larson et al., 2013; Pinkard et al., 2019). Taken together, this work underscores the transformative potential of computational making when embedded in cultural meaning-making.

Yet for Black boys, systemic harms persist including over-policing, misinterpretation of creativity as disruption, and deficit-based assumptions of ability (Edouard & Stewart, 2024). While participatory design and Youth Participatory Action Research (YPAR) (Ozer, 2017) create important openings for youth voice, they often stop short of naming and addressing these systemic inequities directly. Our framework of liberatory co-design builds on this literature by explicitly targeting systemic harms as objects of design and positioning Black boys as co-theorists of their learning environments.

## Theoretical framework: Liberatory co-design towards design agency

Black boys’ experiences in STEM are shaped by systemic harms that mark them as outsiders: over-policing in classrooms, misinterpretation of creativity as disruption, and persistent doubts about their intelligence and worthiness (Edouard & Stewart, 2024). These conditions narrow who is seen as a legitimate STEM participant, silencing the cultural practices that animate Black boys’ everyday lives. Liberatory co-design offers a response by positioning Black boys not as participants in someone else’s program, but as co-theorists of their learning environments. Unlike participatory design or YPAR (Ozer, 2017), which emphasize inclusion and voice, liberatory co-design explicitly targets systemic harms as objects of design. Its goal is not just to invite participation but to transform the conditions of participation, reframing STEM environments so that Black boys’ cultural practices, ways of knowing, and creative expressions are central. This framework draws on design justice (Costanza-Chock, 2018), systems thinking  (Cababa, 2023), and liberatory design possibility (Handley, 2024) as a layered architecture: each informs how the others are enacted rather than operating in parallel. Within this study, design justice names who design typically excludes; systems thinking informs how the lab environment traced connections between design choices and broader social conditions; and moments of liberatory possibility name the interactional openings in which students exercised genuine authorship. Agency, in this framework, is understood not as individual self-expression alone but as the capacity to act on and within systems of meaning, cultivated when students are positioned as co-theorists. Our framework is built upon computational making, enabling students to explore their interests by designing and fabricating characters and toy parts, integrating cultural expression with computational problem-solving. This turns making into a liberatory act: one where personal narratives and technical skills intertwine, and where Black boys’ c reativity becomes a source of recognition and agency in STEM.

This pilot study evaluated the impact of computational making on three Black male students, two high school students and one undergraduate mentor, over a structured six-week animation lab program during the summer of 2023.

The summer program, housed within the Black Male Animation Lab, was a culturally grounded STEM enrichment experience for Black male youth. Participants were recruited through community outreach with local schools and youth organizations, with selection centered on students with limited prior computational exposure but demonstrated creative interest in storytelling and design. The two high school participants, referred to as Boy A and Boy B, attended five days per week consistently across the six-week program. Boy A’s creative investment was rooted in anime and character design, shaping his approach to 3D modeling and animation sequencing. Boy B’s projects were driven by superhero narratives from his neighborhood, reflecting a persistent drive to embed community stories into computational form. An undergraduate mentor served as a near-peer guide, facilitating technical problem-solving and modeling iterative design practices. As a pilot study, the small sample size is acknowledged as a methodological limitation; the goal was theory-building and the development of a replicable design model rather than generalizability.

The lab provided a hands-on learning environment equipped with animation software, 3D printers, and digital design platforms. Students used 3D design software and printers to fabricate parts for Transformers projects, combining cultural interests with computational tools to turn digital designs into physical objects. 1673 Data were drawn from artifacts (3D-printed models, stop-motion animations, digital files), reflective writing, screencasts, and session recordings. We used artifact analysis to examine the technical and creative qualities of the produced work; content anal ysis of reflections and logs to trace computational practices; and thematic analysis to identify recurring patterns around identity, skill development, and emotional engagement. Prompt logs from design software provided further insights into how students used computational tools to create culturally relevant projects. Stop-Motion Animation (SMA) connected computation with students’ personal and community stories, embedding cultural contexts directly into the design process. As one participant explained, “When I worked on stop-motion animation, it felt like I could tell my own story while also learning how to use all the tools to make it come to life.” The three themes that follow illustrate how liberatory co-design created conditions for this kind of meaning-making and technical development to occur simultaneously. Agential design The first theme, Agential Design, highlights how students used tools such as 3D printers, animation software, and design platforms to transform digital concepts into physical artifacts. Boy A, whose creative investment was rooted in anime and character design, iterated on character geometry until physical prints matched the figures he carried in his head. One student noted, “Learning to use the 3D printer was cool. I never thought I could design something on the computer and hold it in real life,”  reflecting both the excitement and empowerment of creating tangible products. The act of holding a self-designed object made real the connection between computational skill and material agency. By designing characters and artifacts that reflected their own cultural  aesthetics, students exercised design agency in a space rarely structured around Black boys’ creative visions. Computational problem-solving 1674 The second theme, Computational Problem-Solving, shows how students broke down complex tasks into smaller, manageable steps through coding and animation. Collaborative troubleshooting, such as refining printer settings or adjusting animation sequences, positioned students as capable problem-solvers and leaders. Boy B’s approach was notably iterative: when his animation sequences failed to render, he returned to the code with the disposition of someone who expected the work to require revision. As one student described, “Coding the movements for my stop-motion character taught me how to think step by step — it’s like solving a puzzle, but fun.” The framing of coding as puzzle-solving, undertaken in the service of a story he cared about, illustrates how identity and technical reasoning were fused rather than sequential.

Cultural connection The third theme, Cultural Connection, underscores how students incorporated community narratives and personal experiences into their projects, using storytelling as a form of identity work. Where Boy A drew on anime and Japanese animation aesthetics to shape the style and movement of his characters, Boy B’s animations were rooted in the geographic and social specificity of his neighborhood. Both were engaged in identity work, but through distinct cultural registers that reflected their individual histories. One participant explained, “I made my animation about a superhero from my neighborhood because that’s the kind of story I wish I saw growing up,” demonstrating how liberatory co-design affirmed lived experiences and countered erasure in STEM spaces. The specificity of “my neighborhood” names a place, a belonging, and an audience that mainstream STEM narratives rarely acknowledge. In the Black Male Animation Lab, Black boys transformed 3D design, fabrication, and stop-motion animation into acts of cultural expression and authorship, consistent with research demonstrating that culturally responsive computing fosters a link between technical skills and personal experiences (Pinkard, 2001; Bang & Vossoughi, 2016). Methodologically, this paper frames liberatory co-design as distinct from participatory design and YPAR (Ozer, 2017): while those approaches invite youth voice, liberatory co-design names systemic harms and centers agency. Computational making, when intertwined with cultural narratives, operated as a liberatory practice that deepened both technical learning and identity development. Within the Black Male Animation Lab, students acted as co-theorists of their learning environment, exercising governance by choosing which stories to tell, deciding on project directions, and shaping how tools and processes were used. One participant captured this agency when he said, “I can use this  to tell my story,” setting the tone for what the work meant and how the group moved forward. Such moments demonstrate how ownership and cultural authorship functioned as key design mechanisms, positioning storytelling as both a technical and political act. By foregrounding student governance, liberatory co-design distinguishes itself from participatory approaches, shifting attention from inclusion to transformation, and centering youth control over the conditions of learning. Success, therefore, was not measured solely by outputs but by the degree to which design choices affirmed belonging, identity, and voice. Looking forward, this work positions liberatory co-design as a scalable model for computational making. Expanding the approach to a larger cohort of Black boys in an advanced animation lab integrating 2D/3D design and motion capture will test how cultural relevance and systemic critique can be sustained across contexts.
