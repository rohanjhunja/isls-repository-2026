# Gamified Expectation Management to Foster Collaborative Learning

**Conference:** ISLS 2023

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Experiences with collaborative assignments in education influence both students’ development of collaboration skills and the cognitive affordances of this instructional approach. Expectation management can have a positive effect on students’ collaboration experiences. But students are not always aware of specific expectations they have and need support to help them make (implicit) expectations explicit. Gamification might strengthen the effectiveness of this support since it can help overcome communication and engagement barriers. In this regard, the use of game fiction (i.e., avatars) seems especially promising. In this study, three conditions are compared: no support, expectation management support, and gamified expectation management support. Results indicate higher collaborative performance and individual learning for students of the gamified expectation management condition over the other conditions, but no differences were found when looking at students’ collaboration quality and their attitudes towards collaboration.

## Problem statement and context

There’s increased attention for collaborative assignments in education. To harness the sought after positive effects, the related collaboration should be of good quality (Saab et al., 2007). One of the many c hallenges that impact collaboration quality finds its origin in the psychology of expectations; students anticipate others to be similar to them while in reality they might be different (Bird & Osland, 2005). Such mis taken expectations can lead to misunderstandings resulting in sub-optimal collaboration (Bird & Osland, 2005; Lawrence, 2013). In contrast, if students know what to expect from each other they can gain more from the colla boration (Cagiltay et al., 2015). Thus, managing expectations can be beneficial for collaboration (Hartley & Dawson, 2010). Expectation management involves exploration, determination, and evaluation of the team’s assets before the team engages in a task. Hence, prompting students to engage in expectation m anagement could benefit their collaboration. However, people are often unaware of expectations they have (Bird &  Osland, 2005). Thus, students might need support that helps them to make implicit expectations explicit (also see ter Vrugte & de Jong, 2017). We carefully conjecture that, in order to fully benefit from such suppor t, it is essential that students share and discuss the received information regarding their expectations and that gamification can help facilitate this. Gamification of expectation management Gamification is the application of game elements in settings other than games (Deterding et al., 2011). The game elements (like points, badges, and leaderboards) are used to trigger psychologic al processes that evoke a high level of engagement, like often seen in players of games. A promising element when it comes to behavioral learning outcomes, but less commonly studied, is the element of game-fiction (Sa iler et al., 2017; Sailer & Homner, 2020). Game fiction, like narrative or avatars, introduces elements of fantasy to the educational context (Bedwell, 2012). An example of the successful implementation of game fiction can be found in studies addressing the use of avatars. An avatar is a visual computer-generated form refle cting the user (Antonaci et al., 2019). Avatars can provide crucial information and help other users form expectations. This makes avatars promising tools for expectation management.

Current study The current study explored whether students benefit from support that helps them  make (implicit) expectations explicit and whether the implementation of gamification (i.e., game-fiction in th e form of avatars) contributes to the effectiveness of this support. It was expected that the expectation ma nagement support and gamification positively impacts collaboration quality as should be evident from evaluation of stude nt’s collaboration, their group performance and individual learning, and their attitude towards collaboration. ## Method

Design The study was conducted with first year psychology students and followed an experiment al randomized control group design with three conditions: 1) a control condition where students were prom pted to share their expectations but received no support to help make their expectations explicit, 2) a  support condition where students received support to help them make their expectations explicit before they were prompted to discuss them, and 3) a gamified support condition where students received the same support to help them make their expectations explicit after which they were prompted to create avatars as a means to share these expectations. All students in all conditions worked collaboratively, in the same group of four to  six students, on two consecutive assignments. At the start, each group received an expectation management prompt to discuss factors that could affect their collaborative process, and at the end of each assignme nt students delivered their group product (which was graded), completed an individual test (which was graded), and were asked to assess their collaboration experience. Expectation management support Students in both support conditions received a survey to make implicit expectations explicit. The survey assessed seven bipolar dimensions that conceptualized potential conflict points in collaboration at the individual-level and aligned with skills for effective collaboration identified in literature (e.g. Meyer, 2014; Mickan & Rodger, 2000; Saab et al., 2007). After students answered all questions, they were informed how they ranked on each dimension. They could then discuss this information following a ‘standard’ prompt that was provided to all students at the start of the collaborative assignment: ‘Please discuss with your team how you envision your collaboration on this project. Pay attention to what you expect from each other, your qualities, and challenges.’ Gamification Students in the gamified condition completed the expectation management survey and were prompted to build an avatar passport using the survey results (see Figure 2). To create a continuous meani ngful gamified experience, students were challenged to ‘evolve’ their avatar over the two collaborative assignments. This ‘evolving of their avatar’ aligned with the evaluation that was standard after each assignment in all conditions. (also see Figure 1).

## Participants and results

The preliminary analyses reported here, pertain to 238 first year Psychology stu dents from a Dutch University spread over three conditions: non-gamified support condition (66.0% females, 34.0% males; Mage = 20.49 years, SDage = 2.00), gamified support condition (64.2% females, 35.8% males; M age = 20.37 years, SD age = 2.93), and control condition (68.5% females, 26.6% males, 0.8% others; Mage = 20.31 years, SDage = 1.72). ## Conclusion

Students’ attitude regarding their collaboration and collaborative skills was not affected by expectation management support. Still, students in the gamified support condition outperformed student s in the other two conditions when looking at the quality of their group performance and how much they learned individually. This aligns with research findings that suggest that students can gain more from collaborating if they know what they can expect from each other (Bird & Osland, 2005; Cagiltay et al., 2015; P opov, 2013). Yet, there was no evidence for this; as the difference in group per formance and individual learning was not reflected in how students evaluated their collaborative behavior. This stands  out, since there is a general consensus that quality of collaboration is a predictor of collaboration effectiveness (also see Eshuis et al., 2019). Future research is needed to help explain why gamification had the effect on performance and whether this was due to promoted engagement and expectation management (as theorized by Falloon, 2010; Pickard et al., 2016).
