# Artworks as Designed Prompts: How Contemporary Art Organizes Gallery Talk

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study investigates how contemporary artworks serve as designed prompts that scaffold collaborative knowledge building in naturalistic gallery talk. Grounded in social constructivism, we report a two-case qualitative study from FAYM2024 in Tokyo: interactive gallery talks (seven adults, 20s–50s) paired with two interviews with the corresponding artists. Viewer transcripts were segmented into meaning units and coded for knowledge-building moves (proposal, visual evidence, challenge/revision, integration, holding ambiguity). Artist interviews were coded for designed prompts and evaluative remarks about audience responses. A joint alignment per artwork then contrasted artists’ stated design intentions with discourse trajectories, focusing on relations of alignment and productive divergence and evidencing each with thick excerpts. The study traces how artwork properties—as conceived by their creators — organize sense-making sequences in situ, and how divergences between intention and interpretation can open generative paths for discussion. These findings offer implications for gallery facilitation and the design of art-as-learning experiences.

From a social constructivist perspective, learning in museums and galleries occurs as visitors co-construct understanding through culturally available mediational means —including artworks, labels, and spatial arrangements (Hein, 1998; Falk & Dierking, 2016). Hein’s conception of the “constructivist museum” makes this explicit, arguing that exhibitions should be designed to support visitors’ own sense-making rather than unidirectional transmission. On this view, an artwork functions as a semiotic resource around which people propose, justify, refine, and sometimes stabilize shared interpretations. Empirical research in informal learning treats talk as the trace of learning. Learning in museums and galleries has been conceptualized as “conversational elaboration”: over the course of a visit, group discourse becomes more analytic and better organized as participants connect claims to exhibit features and to one another’s ideas (Leinhardt and Crowley, 1998; Leinhardt, Crowley & Knutson, 2002). Complementing this, Pierroux (2010)’s guided-tour study shows how educators’ narrative framings and multimodal pointing to artworks mediate such meaning-making in situ, shaping when and how visitors propose, justify, and revise interpretations. For research on art viewing—particularly in na turalistic settings—this suggests that progress in understanding should be observable as shifts in the structure of group discourse. Stage models of art appreciation culminate in cognitive mastering—effortful sense-making of “what the artwork is about” (Leder, Belke, Oeberst, & Augustin, 2004). Resolving ambiguity can be intrinsically rewarding—the aesthetic aha—and reorganizes engagement (Muth & Carbon, 2013). Recent evidence also nuances this mechanism: depending on type and degree, ambiguity candampen liking or perceived meaningfulness (Hitsuwari et al., 2024), suggesting that not all ambiguity is equally generative for interpretation. The VIMAP model similarly suggests that encounters with contemporary art may involve moments of disruption followed by reflection, as viewers reorganize their understanding in response to works that resist immediate interpretation (Pelowski et al., 2017). Recent work also shows how cue placement and depictive structure steer interpretation in educational contexts, and how light-touch scaffolds—such as brief historical framing or prompts to compare oppositions within a work—can boost interest and perceived mastery for modern/contemporary art (Bianchi et al., 2025). Psycho-historical accounts emphasize that artworks are designed prompts embedded in intentions that audiences often reconstruct (Bullot & Reber, 2013), a view increasingly connected to learning in the arts (Starr, 2023).

Crucially for the present study, artist-side cognition/design is beginning to be articulated: contemporary artists report process modification and an embrace of uncontrollability to open problem-finding pathways (Okada & Yokochi, 2025). In parallel, Wijntjes & van Middelkoop (2024) propose a contemporary framework connecting vision and depiction, mapping how texture, color, light, space, and material (and their cue placement) structure perception and interpretation in educational contexts.  Together, these literatures suggest that some features intentionally built into contemporary artworks may function as designed prompts for sense-making and, in turn, as resources for collaborative knowledge building (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2006). 1357 Taken together, these studies suggest that (a) learning in museums and galleries is visible as changes in group discourse; (b) contemporary artworks often invite effortful sense-making rather than immediate closure; (c) minimal scaffolds can modulate engag ement; and (d) features intentionally built into artworks may shape the course of collaborative interpretation when those works are viewed. What remains under-specified is the mechanism linking artists’ designed prompts to the micro-processes of collaborative knowledge building during real-time, adult group discussions of contemporary art. This gap matters because, without examining both artist-stated design intentions and viewers’ unfolding discourse, it is difficult to explain why some artworks sustain interpretive divergence rather than treat it simply as misunderstanding. In this study, designed prompts refer to features intentionally built into an artwork that are expected to invite, channel, or complicate interpretation during observing how audiences subsequently reason with the finished artworks.

## Research questions and methods

Grounded in social constructivism and the empirical/aesthetic literature above, we pursue the following questions:

• RQ1. How did specific features of each artwork function as designed prompts in the unfolding of

viewers’ interpretations during group discussion?

• RQ2. How did viewers’ interpretive trajectories relate to the artists’ stated design intentions in each case?

By pairing artists’ accounts of design intentions with analyses of viewer talk, the study examines how artworks function as resources for collaborative interpretation.

## Context: FAYM2024

As our empirical field, we drew on FAYM2024, an exhibition curated by the third author and held in Nishi-Azabu, Tokyo, from 7–19 October 2024 (WALL_alternative, 2024). Under the theme “Turning Nishi-Azabu Inside Out,” the program sought to bridge everyday life  and artistic exp ression through exhibitions by four artists, complemented by public interviews and workshops designed to surface alternative readings of the neighborhood. The gallery talk was conducted on October 11. A graduate student—not briefed on the study’s research aims—served as facilitator under the supervision of the first author. Two artworks were the focus: Kiyoshi Kusumi’s “Say Nothing Board” series and Toshiya Higashitani’s « Diary» (live painting). The « Say Nothing Board» are photographic landscapes of urban signboards whose lettering has been erased by weather and time; the specific art work discussed in the gallery talk  was entitled «2021: A Roadside Ody ssey» (Figure 1). Higashitani’s «Diary» (livedrawing) visualized utterances in situ during the dialogue, continuously updating throughout the session (Figure  2). Both can be characterized as experimental contemporary art that sublimates quotidian experience into artistic form and thus provide fertile ground for examining how artifact properties organize collaborative sense-making.

## Analytic approach

Coding proceeded along two coordinated schemes and was conducted jointly by the first and second authors. We used a collaborative qualitative coding process guided by the research questions; initial labels were developed on a shared subset, refined through brief discussions/analytic memos, and applied together, resolving disagreements by consensus. Viewer talk was coded for knowledge-building moves and their sequencing. Artist interviews were coded for designed prompts and evaluative remarks about audience responses. For each artwork, we conducted a joint alignment: artists’ design prompts were juxtaposed with discourse trajectories observed in the corresponding viewer session. In reporting the cases, we characterize these relations in terms of alignment and productive  divergence, documented with excerpts that preserve the linkage between claims, visual evidence, and integrative outcomes. This analysis examines how artworks, as conceived by their creators, shaped collaborative interpretation in situ, and where viewers’ interpretations departed productively from those intentions.

Through cross-analysis of dialogue transcripts and artist interviews, we report the findings by artwork. For each case, we describe viewer interpretation, the artist’s stated design intention, and the relation between them. Case 1. Kusumi’s «Say Nothing Board» Kinship to viewers’ cultural imagery Participants in the «Say Nothing Board» discussion perceived the black object as “black” and “creepy”, viewing it as something that defied familiar categorization. However, precisely because it existed in an ordinary residential neighborhood, it was recognized as “alien and conspicuous.” This contrast with the everyday heightened its strangeness. The artist was consciously considering how to capture the scene aesthetically by clearly emphasizing the contrast between light and shadow and the angles, and the participants received these expressions. Furthermore, imaginative associations expanded through cultural and narrative references like ‘a door to another world,’ ‘the anywhere door from Doraemon’ and ‘the monolith.’ Notably, the term ‘monolith’ originates from the film «2001: A Space Odyssey», strongly resonating with the artist's intent behind the subtitle « 2021: A Roadside Odyssey». Here, an unintentional  resonance was established between the artist and the participants through the sharing of cultural imagery.

Participant: To me, it just looks like Evangelion's SEELE or the monolith from «2001: A Space Odyssey». And stuff like that usually shows up in cool scenes, right? So  it feels off that this monolith suddenly appears in the middle of this ordinary, everyday scene.  (Unit 32) Artist’s intent to connect with art history Artist Kusumi positioned the «Say Nothing Board» series as “a practice that deliberately re-presents ‘meaningless things’ observed on the street as artworks,” clarifying his intent to “re-establish signs and landscapes buried in everyday life as art by giving them titles.” He stated that the artwork title «Say Nothing Board» series, much like Marcel Duchamp's «Fountain» transforming an everyday object into art by naming a urinal «Fountain», serves to connect fragments of signs existing in daily life to an art historical context. The artist's intent was partially  conveyed, and some aspects  remained uncommunicated. No comments linking the topic to art history were made by the participants.  However, although the title of the artwork was not mentioned in the dialogue, participants experienced the object through visual expressions and cultural metaphors, strongly resonating with the artist's intent, particularly through the word ‘monolith’. In this case, the artwork sustained exploratory interpretation through ambiguity and everyday/alien contrast, while the relation to the artist’s intention was one of partial resonance rather thandirect transmission. Case 2. Higashitani’s «Diary» Focusing on production process 1359 In «Diary» (livedrawing), the artist visually incorporated participants’ spoken words into the work in real time, strongly emphasizing its dynamic nature—the sense of being “written moment by moment”. In this artwork, many comments focused more on the method of expression than on the content being expressed. The art work was experienced as a device for visualizing thought through recording metaphors like ‘notebook’ or “memories in my brain”. Additionally, dissonance from elements was layered on, creating a sense of infinity from the cycle between words and the artwork—described as “my brain glitches,” “circular reference” or “can't keep up.” Participants complemented this with cultural references like “ Excel references” or “Achilles and the Tortoise,” while enjoying exchanges, sensing a strong interactivity. As a result, emotional reactions were shared, and the mutual updating between the artwork and dialogue itself became a new form of viewing experience. Concentration on “calligraphy” Artist Higashitani defined his ultimate goal of creation as “establishing images or landscapes,” and positioned the live painting as “an extension of painting using words as material, rather than minutes or records.” However, the actual practice on the day  was strongly influenced by physical conditions such as the large screen and acrylic gouache/markers, leading the artist himself to self-assess that it leaned toward a form closer to “calligraphic (Shodo-like) live painting.” Here, “calligraphic” refers to the artistic expressiveness of written lines and forms beyond their communicative function, evoking Shodo, the East Asian art of brush writing as visual expression rather than simply writing for communication.

Higashitani's practice possessed aspects that evoked this characteristic of “artistic expression where characters take on individuality.” This resulted from constraints of speed and materials coming to the fore, countering the ideal of generating images instantly from words, and shifted the emphasis towards presenting the act of creation itself as a performative element. Furthermore, regarding the relationship with participants, he intentionally refrained from speaking, maintaining a “radio-like distance” where he unidirectionally received participants' voices. This stemmed from the artist's intention to preserve a communication circuit mediated solely by the artwork, rather than intervening in the dialogue space. In this case, participants’ interpretations centered on the unfolding process of inscription, while the relation to the artist’s intention is best understood as productive divergence. RQ1 examined the relationship between artwork-level characteristics and collaborative knowledge building among viewers. The findings in these two cases suggest that artwork ambiguity was associated with more sustained exploratory discussion and metaphorica l/cultural associations, whereas more salient visual cues drew participants into immediate inspection.  For instance, the black object in the «Say Nothing Board» was intentionally ambiguous, leading viewers to propose and revise interpretations iteratively. Viewers spontaneously proposed and challenged diverse interpretations, revising their ideas through shared cultural references and visual impressions. In «Diary» (live painting), the structure where visual clues were added in real time induced confusion among participants while simultaneously prompting immediate visual examination, leading to a more process-oriented interactive viewing experience. In other words, these two cases suggest that the degree of ambiguity and the placement of visual cues were related to differences in the collaborative proposal –challenge–revision cycle. RQ2 examined the relationship between the artist's intent and participants' interpretations. The results showed that the artist's intent was not necessarily shared directly in these two cases, but appeared instead as partial resonance mediated through cultural references or as productive divergence on different dimensions.  In «Say Nothing Board», the artist's emphasis on “transforming everyday objects into artworks through titles” and “the art-historical context since Duchamp” was not shared by participants. However, through the cultural reference of “the monolith,” the subtitle the artist added resonated with participants' imaginations, creating partial resonance. This demonstrates that alignment and divergence can coexist within the same interpretive episode. In « Diary», while the artist focused on creating it as “an extension of painting using words as material,” participants became engrossed in the live painting process itself. Although their perceptions existed on different dimensions, this very difference can be evaluated as a productive divergence that generated a new viewing experience. The interpretive gap between artist and viewer revealed by this study suggests a characteristic of communication mediated by artworks. Specifically, the artist's intent is not necessarily conveyed directly; instead, the viewer generates interpretations through visual experiences and cultural references mediated by the artwork. Such interpretations may partially resonate with artist inte ntion, creating new meaning through a back-and-forth between visual form and subjective interpretation. Because this study examined only two artworks in which multiple design features were bundled together, it cannot isolate the independent effect of any single feature, and its claims remain limited to these cases.
