# Classroom Intervention to Support Secondary School Students’ Motivation and Regulated Learning

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
The study design aims to identify motivational classroom interaction practices that support secondary school students' motivation and regulated learning and examine their effects on students' motivational beliefs in short and long term. During the intervention, teachers apply practices developed as part of the study, while situational and general self-report data are collected from intervention and control schools over four semesters. The results will reveal practices that support motivation both in the moment and over time.

## Background

Motivation can be defined as an internal state of m ind that directs, maintains, hinders, or inhibits b ehavior (Graham & Weiner, 2012). Therefore, motivation can be considered the driving force that initiates and maintains the will to learn and engage in goal-directed behavior (Urdan & Kaplan, 2020). Prior research has evidenced that motivated students are more likely to  perform better in academic tasks than unmotivated students (Steinmayr et al., 2019). Poor motivation for learning, in turn, can be detrimental for learning and achievement. Unfortunately, adolescents decreased motivation has been recognized as an increasing challenge in education (Koenka, 2020). Luckily, motivation is not just a characteristic that a student either has or doesn’t  have. Instead, self-regulated learning  (SRL) provides students with strategic mean s to actively influence their motivation  during learning through various motivation regulation strategies (W olters, 2003). Motivation regulation is defined as learners’ actions aiming to manage their own level of motivation or to purposefully sustain or improve their effort or persistence for academic tasks (Wolters, 2003). Prior research has shown that students with stronger motivation regulation skills are more likely to have better ac ademic achievements than students with weaker motivation regulation skills (e.g., Wolters, 1998; 1999). In addition to self-regulation of motivation, prior research provides evidence that various social aspects, such as peer and teacher interactions in the classroom, can impa ct students’ motivation (Bakhtiar & Hadwin, 202 2; Hemi et al., 2021; Shao et al., 2024). In particular, learners can support one another in managing motivational issues through co-regulation or build a favorable motivational atmosphere together through socially shared regulation  of learning  (Järvenoja et al., 2023). These forms of motivation regulation are particularly present in collaborativ e learning (Bakhtiar et al., 2018; Järvenoja et al., 2020). For example, Järvenoja et al. (2025) found that co-and socially shared regulation of motivation were linked to higher success expectancies during and after tasks. Studen ts in groups with frequent socially shared regulati on also reported more positive emotions as reasons for thei r motivatio n. In terms of teacher-student interactions, emotional support and effective classroom organizat ion are essential for fostering students' situational engagement, including motivation (Pöysä et al., 2019).

Although research has identified various aspects that influence students' motivation, studies focusing on classroom interactions at a situational level remain scarce (Järvenoja et al., 2023). While we know that long-term motivation accumulates from situati onal motivational experiences, there is no systemat ic evidence on how motivational support and regulation enacted accordi ng to situational needs shape students' in-the-moment motivational appraisals and gradually influence the long-term development of their motivational trajectories. This study addresses this gap through an intervention design by identifying classroom interaction practices that support students' motivation in the moment, in authentic le arning situations, and by examining their effects o n students' motivational beliefs in both the short and long term, as well as the role of SRL in shaping these beliefs over time.

## Methods

Intervention design The participants are secondary school subject teachers and their 7th and 8th grade students from two intervention (Nteachers=12, N students=250) and two control schools (N teachers=4, N students=100). ## Data collection and analysis

In both the intervention and control schools, self-report data is collected from students at general and situational levels to study the long-term effects on students' general motivational beliefs (Gaspard et al., 2015) and SRL skills (Wolters & Benzon, 2013). The self-reports are administered once before the intervention and three times over the next three semesters. During the three-month intervention, situational self-reports on students' motivational beliefs and SRL are collected multiple times in eac h lesson taught by the participating teachers,  using a repeated instrument with five items: emotional valence, expectancy for success, utility value, effort costs, and SRL. As part of the intervention, teachers participate i n audio-recorded reflective group discussions. This enables a qualitative analysis of the teachers' perspective s on the effectiveness of the motivational interact ion practices and allows for refinement of the intervention design during the study. Furthermore, a researcher conducts in-class observations of each teacher’s lessons three times to enable analysis of teacher-student interactions. The observation instrument is based on Turner et al. (2014) and tailored to obser ve the motivational interaction practices applied in this study. The observation categories cover the frequency and quality of teachers’ enactment of positive interaction, support for meaningfulness, and support for autonomy and belongingness. The data analysis will utilize complementary analytic approaches and triangulate qualitative (classroom observations, reflective discussions) and quantitative (students’ general and situational self-reports) data. First, the short-and long-term effects of the intervention on students' motivational beliefs are analyzed using multilevel modeling, while dynamic structural equation modeling (DSEM) explores momentary fluctuations in motivational beliefs, SRL, and their relations during the intervention. DSEM identifies how situational motivational beliefs and SRL fluctuate over time and across lessons in relat ion to classroom interactions. Second, qualitative content analysis of the reflective discussions provides a more nuanced understanding of how the motivational interaction practices work in the classroom. Classroom observations, in turn, enable the identification of potential differences between the teachers that might influence the intervention's effectiveness.

## Expected results and practical implications

The expected results will identify classroom interaction practices that support students' motivation, particularly in the moment, and contribute to a favorable motivatio nal trajectory over time.  For teachers, the results provide evidence-based knowledge on everyday classroom interactions aiming to foster students' motivational development during secondary school, a stage identified as period for  motivational challenges (Koenka, 2020). For students, the study provide s insights into managing motivational challenges, sustain ing their drive for continuous learning, and supporting one another in building and maintaining motivated learning by self-, co-and socially shared regulation of learning skills (Järvenoja et al., 2023).
