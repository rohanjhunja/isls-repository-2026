# Knowledge-Building Discourse in Generative AI-Mediated Collaborative Problem Solving: A Student–Expert Comparison

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study examines how students and experts differ in their engagement with generative AI (GenAI) during knowledge-creating dialogue. Twenty undergraduate students (10 pairs) and one pair of expert educators collaborated with ChatGPT-4o to address a socio-scientific issue. Using the Knowledge Building Discourse Explorer (KBDeX) and “Good Moves” coding, we analyzed temporal degree centrality and discourse networks to capture epistemic and dialogic processes. Results showed that students’ discourse was largely anchored to AI-generated ideas, with limited transformation through dialogue, whereas experts engaged in self-sustained cycles of problem definition, evaluation, and reframing, using GenAI selectively to extend inquiry. These findings suggest that diffe rences lie not in the availability of ideas but in how discourse is organized to support knowledge creation, highlighting the importance of scaffolding epistemic and dialogic practices for effective GenAI use.

Collaborative problem solving (CPS) in the knowledge age involves advancing ideas through sustained dialogue. Knowledge Building (KB) frames CPS as collective inquiry in which participants iteratively define and refine ideas through discourse (Bereiter & S cardamalia, 2014, 2016). Generative AI (GenAI) can support this process by providing ideas, summaries, and explanations (Essel et al., 2024), yet its effectiveness depends on whether learners critically evaluate and integrate its outputs into ongoing inquiry (Du, 2025), consistent with research emphasizing reflective dialogue and shared evaluation in CPS (Zheng et al., 2025). Students and educators differ in how they perceive GenAI’s role (Otaki et al., 2026), suggesting that such differences may shape their engagement in collaborative settings. Prior research has mainly examined the outcomes or structures of AI-supported argumentation, leaving less understood how knowledge-building discourse unfolds as a process in interaction with GenAI.

From a KB perspective, expertise is understood as progressive problem solving rather than accumulated knowledge (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 1994). Comparing students and expert educators provides a lens to examine how different epistemic frames shape engageme nt with GenAI. Accordingly, this study addresses the following research question: How do temporal and dialogical structures of discourse differ between student groups and expert educators when engaging in knowledge-creating dialogue with GenAI? Participants included 20 undergraduate students (10 pairs) and two expert educators (one pair). All participants collaborated with ChatGPT-4o on a shared monitor to address a socio-scientific issue related to genetically modified foods (GMOs). Students reported prior experience using ChatGPT for academic support, while the experts, who specialized in science education, reported extensive experience with inquiry-based teaching and daily use of multiple GenAI tools.

Each pair engaged in a 40-minute discussion followed by a brief presentation and a short reflective interview. Data sources included audio–video recordings, screen captures of AI interactions, and transcripts of human and AI utterances segmented by conversational turn. To examine epistemic and dialogic engagement with GenAI, we analyzed discourse using the Knowledge Building Discourse Explorer (KBDeX; Oshima et al., 2012). Words from participants’ discourse were used as nodes, and their temporal total degree centrality (TDC) was calculated across turns to capture how ideas gained prominence over time. TDC was interpreted as an indicator of epistemic focus within evolving discourse. Discourse was coded for “Good Moves” (Bereiter & Scardamalia, 2016), including problem definition, new ideas, promisingness evaluation, critical discourse, comparison, meta-dialogue, and higher-level ideas. Coding was conducted at the level of conversational turns, and distributions of moves were compared between student and expert groups to examine differences in how discourse was organized and how GenAI was incorporated into knowledge-building processes.

677 The analysis revealed systematic contrasts in how students and experts engaged in argumentation and knowledgecreating dialogue with GenAI. Figure 1 compares temporal degree centrality and discourse networks between student and expert groups.

First, temporal trajectories of degree centrality (DC) showed differences in how ideas were introduced and developed. In the student group, DC values for AI-generated words increased sharply following prompts, indicating that GenAI outputs quickly became c entral to the discussion. Many of these ideas were incorporated directly into the evolving discourse, suggesting that students’ engagement was largely anchored to AI-generated content rather than being progressively transformed through dialogue. This patte rn reflects a mode of interaction in which GenAI functions as a primary driver of idea development. In contrast, the expert group showed a different pattern: increases in DC were more strongly associated with ideas introduced through their own dialogue, wi th GenAI used selectively to extend, refine, or consolidate emerging ideas, particularly in later phases of the rather than as a source of direction.

Second, knowledge-building discourse networks revealed qualitative differences in how dialogue was organized. The student network was relatively sparse and centered around a limited set of connections, with few links to critical discourse or meta-dialogue. This suggests that while students incorporated AI-generated ideas, these were not extensively reworked through collective inquiry, often remaining at the level of elaboration rather than transformation. In contrast, the expert network was dense and more e venly distributed, characterized by frequent connections among problem definition, critical discourse, and meta-dialogue. This pattern indicates sustained cycles of inquiry in which ideas were continuously examined, reframed, and evaluated, reflecting a more deliberate and strategically coordinated discourse. Taken together, these findings suggest that the key difference between students and experts lies not in the availability or quality of ideas but in how discourse is organized to support knowledge creation. Students tended to engage with GenAI as a source of ideas, resulting in externally anchored discourse, whereas experts used GenAI as a resource within internally regulated inquiry, embedding AI outputs into iterative cycles of problem framing and evaluation. This distinction highlights that effective use of GenAI in CPS depends on epistemic and dialogic practices that enable learners to critically integrate AI-generated content into sustained knowledge-building processes, rather than relying on AI outputs as ready-made solutions. Pedagogically, this implies the need to design learning environments that scaffold not only access to AI-generated ideas but also the dialogic practices required to interrogate, connect, and refine those ideas over time.
