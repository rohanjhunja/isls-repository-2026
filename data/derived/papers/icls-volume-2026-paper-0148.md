# Small vs. Large Language Models: Comparing Language Models in Generating Productive Failure Math Problems

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) such as GPT-4o can generate high-quality math problems but are resource-intensive and difficult to fine-tune. Small language models (SLMs) like Phi-3 may provide a more practical alternative, as they can run locally and be adapted for specific tasks. In this study, we generated 60 Productive Failure (PF) math problems, with half produced by GPT-4o and half by Phi-3. To ensure comparability, we controlled for grade level, Common Core standard, theme, interest, and prior knowledge. Trained raters evaluated the problems across five PF dimensions. Results showed that GPT-4o (M = 13.38; SD = 1.88) outperformed Phi-3 (M = 10.67; SD = 2.73), with a large effect size (d = 1.16). These findings indicate that although GPT-4o currently generates more effective PF problems, Phi-3 shows promising potential. With further fine-tuning and task-specific adaptation, SLMs like Phi-3 may offer a sustainable, cost-effective solution for educational problem generation.

Large Language Models (LLMs) such as GPT-4o have become central to generating contextually rich and diverse math word problems (Naveed et al., 2023). However, LLMs’ use comes with considerable financial and environmental costs (Morrison et al., 2025). From  a global perspective, training LLMs consumes enormous computational resources, with datacenter-level operations driving energy demands and associated carbon emissions (Mehta, 2024). From a task-level perspective, for some specific purposes, using LLMs seems not appropriate. That is, we may use these very powerful models for tasks that could be done by much smaller models. Lastly, the use of LLMs, as of today, costs a considerable amount of money (particularly when text and image generation happen at the same time). These issues raise pressing concerns about the scalability of LLM-based AIpowered educational technology tools. Small language models (SLMs), in contrast, offer a promising alternative. Defined broadly as models under 10 billion parameters that can operate on consumer-grade hardware, SLMs are increasingly capable of handling specialized tasks with competitive accuracy and efficiency (Belcak et al., 2025). SLMs are less costly to run, can be finetuned with relatively modest computational resources (Dettmers et al., 2023), and may be deployed locally, which can enhance both privacy and sustainability. Their task-specific focus also aligns well with many educational applications, which often require a narrower scope and curricular alignment rather than general conversational ability (Lu et al., 2024).

The current study investigates whether SLMs can rival LLMs in producing high-quality math problems designed for teaching through Productive Failure (PF; Kapur, 2014; Kapur & Bielaczyc, 2012). PF is a teaching method that intentionally confronts learners with challenging problems designed for failure before formal instruction. These problems leverage failure or struggle to activate prior knowledge, encourage exploration of multiple solution pathways, and foster deeper conceptual understanding (Kapur, 2014; Kapur & Bielaczyc, 2012). Research has shown that PF supports robust learning outcomes across domains (Loibl et al., 2017) and that PF usually outperforms direct instructions (Sinha & Kapur, 2021; Darabi et al., 2018). The word problems suitable for PF are usually long, story-like, and effectively engaging. Creating these problems for teachers is not an easy task. Therefore, through detailed prompt engineering, we used LLMs and SLMs to help teachers. To operationalize PF problems’ quality, we employed a rubric based on the literature of PF which captures five critical dimensions: activation of prior knowledge (APK; i.e., a PF problem should include familiar concepts, sweet spot calibration (SSC; a PF problem should and include concepts that learners have mastered before so the problem is approachable), affective engagement (AE; a story with theme and information that aligns with students’ interests can engage learners affectively), multiple representati ons (MR; a PF problem should encourage a wide range of representations and solution methods), and open-endedness (OE; a PF problem should be open-ended and ill-structured to allow for creative exploration and non-canonical ideas). Each of these facets could be scored for each problem as 1, 2, or 3, making the minimum score as 5 and the maximum score as 15. This rubric was applied to evaluate problems generated by GPT-4o and Phi-3. Phi-3 (mini; 3.8B parameters), a 1222 fast, efficient model trained on high-quality synthetic data and optimized for real-time, on-device use. The central research question for this study is: Is there any difference between GPT-4o and Phi-3 in the quality of PF problems that they generate? If so, how large is this effect?

## Method

Problem generation We used the two models’ API to generate 60 algebra problems (GPT-4o = 30; generated by Phi-3 = 30). Both models received identical prompts and problem contexts. To ensure fairness across models, both models received the same structured prompt with six components: (1) roles and objectives, defined the model’s task as an educational design assistant generating PF math problems; (2) response rules, specified the narrative format, tone, open-endedness, and restrictions; (3) PF overview, described PF’s phases and purpose; (4) a detailed rubric, outlined six evaluation criteria for evaluating the quality of PF problems, each scored on a three-point scale; (5) human-generated examples, illustrated five example math problems to show the expected quality and  the structure of PF problems; (6) instructions for generating new problems, guided the model to create a new, open-ended narrative problem aligned with a selected Common Core Standard, grade level, theme and interest, and explicitly listed prior knowledge concepts.

In generating the 60 math problems, we controlled for confounding variables by holding constant grade level, domain, Common Core standard, thematic context, student interests, and prior knowledge concepts. We used topics from 7th grade ratios and proportional relationships (CCSS.Math.Content.7.RP.A.2), with prior knowledge drawn from unit rates (7.RP.A.1) and operations with rational numbers (7.NS.A.1). The thematic context was sports, specifically basketball and scenarios involving Stephen Curry. Each problem was assigned to a unique identifier and stored in a blinded database for evaluation (i.e., raters did not know the model with which the problem at hand was generated).

Evaluation process Evaluation was conducted by at least two trained human raters. Each problem was scored independently by two raters using the PF rubric (see Table 1). Discrepancies of ≥ 5 points triggered adjudication by a third rater (16 cases), with the final score taken  as the mean of the two closest ratings. This process ensured reliability and minimized outlier effects. Data were analyzed with independent-samples t-tests comparing GPT-4o and Phi-3 on overall and subscale scores performance, with effect sizes (Cohen’s d) and 95% confidence intervals reported. Our findings show that GPT-4o generates PF problems with higher quality than Phi-3 overall and across most dimensions, with the  largest gaps in multiple representations and open-endedness—key to PF’s focus on exploration and non-linear problem-solving (Kapur, 2014). These findings align with prior work that showed LLMs’ strength in integrating complex representational structures due to diverse multimodal training (Naveed et al., 2023). While Phi-3 performed comparably on affective engagement and only slightly lower on activation of prior knowledge, GPT-4o still produced more pedagogically sophisticated tasks. Yet Phi-3’s efficiency and smaller size highlight the promise of SLMs as sustainable alternatives. Future studies should look into enhancing SLMs’ performance by improving the prompt engineering and integrating targeted fine-tuning or retrieval-augmented generation (Borgeaud et al., 2022; Belcak et al., 2025; Shone, 2024). Moreover, instead of using only one SLM, future studies could engineer a multi-model system with multiple SLMs that support and communicate with each other for optimal performance.

The study is limited to sixty problems within one domain and grade level, suggesting the need for broader evaluations, classroom-based teacher reviews, and refinement of SLM prompts.
