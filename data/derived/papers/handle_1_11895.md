# Teachers' Strategies: Collaborative Learning in Second Language Classroom

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
The study explores strategies teachers use to design, monitor, manage, and evaluate collaborative group activities in second language learning within higher education. Twenty second language teachers from diverse educational contexts and countries participated in semi-structured interviews. A total of 68 Critical Incidents were obtained. We elaborated a coding schema and analyzed critical incidents focusing on task objectives, teacher actions, student interactions, and evaluation outcomes. Teachers found that culturally relevant tasks content led to greater student engagement, while challenges such as high learning objectives, unclear instructions, and imbalanced group participation affected group work and task success. The study emphasizes the importance of balancing group size, task complexity, and cultural context in collaborative second language learning. The findings attempt to bridge theoretical and methodological foundations of collaborative learning with strategies applied in the classroom.

## Introduction

Collaborative Learning (CL) is an “umbrella term” r ooted in Vygotskian and Piaget constructivist theories and Freire’s critical pedagogy (Yang, 2023). It  emphasizes peer interaction, joint activity, and socio-institutional context (Kreijns et al., 2003; Smith & MacGregor, 1992). Although there is no consensus, CL is broadly defined as a pedagogical approach where groups of learners work together to solve problem s, complete task s, or create products. CL is characterized by shared goals, resources, and representations among participants (Détienne et al., 2012; Mathieu et al., 2017). CL focuses on social construction of knowledge through open-ended, student-driven tasks with minimal teacher intervention. CL includes consensus groups, peer tutoring, collaborative writ ing (Bruffee, 1999), reciprocal teaching (Palincsar & Brown, 1984), learning communities (MacGregor, 1992), teambased learning (Michaelsen et al., 2002) and project-based learning (Koschmann, 1996). Language is central to CL, functioning as both a co mmunication tool and a means of group cohesion. Second language (L2) learning refers to any language learned after one's native l anguage (Richards & Schmidt, 2002). Collaborative Second Language Learning (CSLL) views  language acquisition as socially constructed (Vygotsky, 1986) and serves as a framework for impl ementing CL in the classroom. As the roles of students and teachers shift in CSLL classrooms, one-size-fits-all lessons are no longer suited (MacGregor, 1992). Teachers analyze group processes and foster knowledge construction through students’ interaction and shared responsibility (Baker, 2015).  Teachers’ strategies require (i) setting culturally relevant learning objectives and student’ task expected outcomes, (ii) facilitating L2 interaction, (iii) arranging  and managing groups, (iv) maintaining a communicative classroom environment, (v) monitoring group dynamics, and (vi) assessing lan guage comprehension and retention. These strategies draw from learning theories, instr uctional design, and educational technology, adapting to learners’ needs, task matte rs, and collaborative group goals.  Previous studies examined group arrangement, task types, communication medium s, and collaboration contexts  (Yang, 2023). However, a gap remains between developed methodologies and the ir classroom implementation. Addressing this requir es understanding how teachers adapt strategies to specific contexts and students’ needs. We collected twenty teachers’ classroom experiences  in collaborative group activities using semi-structured interviews and the Critical Incident Tec hnique. A content analysis with a developed coding grid addressed two research questions: (i) How do teache rs monitor, manage and evaluate the L2 learning obje ctive and outcomes in group collaborative work? (ii) What  contextual factors shape teachers’ strategies for CSLL? Interviews allow us to identify factors related to teachers’ preparation, management, and m onitoring of collaborative group work linking teacher-student interactions to learning objectives. Findings highlight t he importance of balancing group size, task complexity, and cultu ral context in collaborative second language learning.

## Participants

Twenty L2 teachers from diverse educational context s (universities, schools, vocational training) and countries (France n=10, Russia n=3, Czech Republic n=2, USA n=1, Poland n=1, Great Britain n=1, Brazil n=1, Argentina n=1) were recruited. Teachers' teaching experience ranged from 1 to 20 years, including early-career (1-3 years), mid-career (4-9 years), and senior teachers (10+ years). This diversity provided a broad perspective on classroom challenges and strategies across different experien ce levels. Participants received the study’s aims, consented to audio recording, with the option to withdraw. Data were (was) anonymized and stored securely. ## Data collection

The 20 interviews were carried out between April 2024 and October 2024 and lasted from 14 to 45 minutes (mean=31.29, SD=9.77). They were audio recorded and further transcribed using OpenAI's Whisper software; the large model was used for recordings over 30 minutes and the medium model for shorter sessions. The transcripts were verified and edited to remove extraneous symbols, personal information and analyzed.

## Data analysis

A total of 68 Critical Incidents (CI) were identified from reading the transcribed verbatim. A CI was defined as a report of a classroom event linked to one collaborative group t ask associated with (1) a learning objective, (2) a clear description of the context and activities, (3) teacher and student interactions within the group s, and (4) the reported outcomes related to a challenge or success. Teachers reported between 2 and 6 CI (average= 4). Example of CI by T10 (female, full time, senior teacher): “They were doing the same activities as the kids wo uld do (...) I divided them into groups (...) it was random (...) trying to come up with some solution (...) it was open they could come with lots of stuff, but they have to first agree (...) the task (...) nonsense shopping list (...) to go to the supermarket and buy a hand of sugar, two and a half  eggs (...) I wanted them to show me how they’ll buy it to cook the recipe (...) they came up with some very interesting ideas (...) they were laughing (...) we evaluated how everyone within the group fulfilled t he role they were given and how they did within the group and with th e individuals. I had my notes following them during these activities (...) draw a map you put there the dots of people who are  sitting around the table (...) two people are talking during the whole time (...) three were doing nothing (...) you have to give them the feedback from a teacher observing the whole time.” We developed a coding schema (Corneloup & Burkhardt, 2016) based  on an initial reading and content analysis on verbal descriptions of successful and challenging group work experiences depicted in CI. The resulting schema allows each CI to be categori zed according to four aspects. (1) A learning object ive of the task (projectbased, role-play or simulation, research-based or collaborative writing) focusing on reading, writing, speaking or listening comprehension, grammar, or vocabulary retention. (2) Teacher action s for monitoring and managing group task(s) and students’ actions before, during, and after, including group arrangement (random, distributed), supervision, and evaluation. (3) Student behavior and group interactions, analyzed through participation patterns, group dynamics, and student engagement across all s tages of the activity. (4) Teacher evaluation of learning outcomes and feedback strategies. Three external researchers tested the coding method  by independently coding the verbatim. The method was validated through their agreement on the conclusions, as illustrated with the CI by T10. (1) The task is categorized as a problem-solving activity that requires students to collaboratively "buy a hand of sugar". The learning objective targets vocabulary retention and speaking comprehension related to supermarket shopping. (2) The teacher managed interactions by randomly grouping students and rotating roles (leader, notetaker, mediator) to ensure equal participation. (3) Students were engaged “they were laughing”  and generated “interesting ideas” using the target vocabulary. However, some groups h ad uneven participation, with only two members contributing actively. (4) The teacher used  an observation map to track group dynamics and provided feedback to address participation gaps.

## Results

The analysis of the 68 critical incidents offers a detailed picture of the collaborative L2 learning environments where these incidents took place. It focuses on teachers’ strategies related to learning objective(s) and outcome(s) of the task, group arrangement, teachers’ actions and student interactions. Learning objective and outcome Learning objective was a central focus of the tasks, targeting specific comprehension such as speaking (32%), writing (25%), reading (24%), and listening (19%): “Students choose a professional, make contact, and ask for an interview (…) they build (interview) according to the professional.” Task design also incorporated contextual and culturally relevant content in 18% of cases: “I prepare my lesson by using local content or local image (...) I never really used textbooks.” Teachers reported success when using local content, as it resonated more strongly with students when tasks resonated with their lived  experiences. Teachers considered the task successful where students demonstrated clear understanding or progre ss with clearly observed positive student behavior and reactions, such as clapping (10%): “At the end of the class, they clapped their hands, something crystallized in their mind.” However, teachers reported that in 16% of cases, th e task was complex or failed due to high learning objective: “The group activity was a failure, the learning objective was too high, they didn't know what to do.” Students faced challenges in balancing the use of L 2 with comprehension, especially in multilingual gr oups of students with better L2 levels. Some students struggled to express their ideas in L2. The lack of clarity also often attributed to task failure (12%) to overly complex or poorly communicated instructions, highlighted a gap in teacher preparation rather than student misundersta nding: “I was explaining a theory (...) 30 students  telling me it’s the other way around”. Teachers reported that students were not focused and had reduced performance. Teachers’ actions and students’ interactions Student participation  varied across different phases of the group activit y. Before the activity  began, teachers arranged groups: “My job was to pair the stronger students with weaker students and make them retell the text to each other.” Most classrooms had 4–5 groups (59%), while classrooms with fewer groups (1–3) represented 25%, and those with more than six groups made up 16%.  During activity, group size and classroom arrangements significantly influenced students’ participation: “They told their stories  (in L2). I was actively listening and correcting, they communicated with each other”.

Smaller groups (2 –3 students) accounted for 25% of cases and generall y demonstrated balanced participation, though they lacked diverse perspecti ves. Medium groups (4 –6 students), the most common arrangement at 56%, often achieved effective collaboration with sufficient teacher monitoring (51%). In contrast, larger groups (7 or more students), 19% of cases, f requently faced uneven participation, with stronger  students dominating discussions. After activity, structured reflection processes, such as summariz ing through mind maps, contributed positively to student understanding (51%): “I divide the time between groups to do the mind map.” Teacher action played a pivotal role in mitigating challenges. Monitoring was observed in 24% of cases, with teachers actively circulating between groups t o provide real-time feedback: “I really try to go through the room (…) hear what they are saying and look at what they are doing.” Role assignment and rotation, implemented in 10% of cases: “We agreed for the  document they submitted which was a collective note (…) a grade for the oral presentation. I tried to keep track on who did what during the sessions...to be fair.” It ensured more equitable participation, with leadership responsibilities sha red among students. Feedback strategies included bo th task-based and group-based evaluations in 18% of cases, where teachers e mphasized fairness by tracking individual contributions to group tasks: “You put the dots  of people who are sitting around the table (...) two people are talking during the whole time.”

## Discussion and future work

The analysis provides conclusions and recommendatio ns for teachers' strategies in designing, monitorin g, managing, and evaluating CSLL group activities. Teachers recognized the importance of adapting inst ructional strategies to align learning objectives, task clarity, group composition, and evaluation of the  outcomes with students’ levels, needs and motivation. However, a high level of motivation  does not guarantee the transition to active engagement (Mercer & Dörnyei, 2020). In CSLL, L2 learning is a process of social constru ction on the knowledge through an extensive and repeated process of commun icative practice. Culturally relevant content is an essential requirement for successful language devel opment, especially in multilingual and international L2 learning settings.

Although there is no agreement on the optimal group size and distinguishing pair and group work for CL (Brown, 2000; Poupore, 2015). Teachers indicated that the group task objectives and student levels will vary and affect the choice of the group size. Smaller and medium groups offer positive participation dynamics while larger groups increase complexity and uncertainty, posing challenges for teachers in ensuring balanced participation and equitable performance across tasks. As Student actions varied across the phases of group activities, with uneven participation remaining a persistent issue during t asks. Teachers’ active monitoring and feedback  strategies enabled teachers to intervene maintaining task alignment and group focus. However, a more systematic approach to feedback in CSLL-particularly one that combines task and group evaluations-could further enhance outcomes by promoting accountability and fostering group cohesion. Teachers often addressed these challenges by assigning roles or pairing students with varying abilities. Role assignment and rotation strategies proved to be effective in developing positive interpersonal group dynamic behaviors between and among group members (Poupore, 2024). This finding resonates with existing literature from self-reported student’s perspective (Mathieu et al., 2017; Yang,

2023) emphasizing the benefits of clearly defined roles i n CL particularly in ensuring balanced and guided

contributions across group members.

This research is ongoing and takes part in the PhD project that aims to deepen understanding of the nature of collaborative learning and actual teachers’ strategies for designing, monitoring, managing, and evaluating collaborative group activities  in second language learning within higher education. Additional interviews are planned to expand the sample of  recent, successful, and challenging teachers experiences with group work and student’s task in CSLL. To ensure Interrater Reliability (IRR), two external raters will code and assess interview data using our coding method once additional interviews and critical incidents are collected. The results are going to be categorized and analyzed using Cohen’s Kappa method. Future research will involve classroom observations within an instrumented room developed in Grenoble (Context Aware Classroom-CAC). The CAC, featuring mobile furniture and devices to capture data on position, movement, and gaze while ensuring privacy, enables detailed observation of teacher-student interactions. Further analysis is based on obtained teachers’ experiences and stra tegies for designing collaborative second language learning lessons. The study will adapt a tool developed to measure group dynamics based on positive and negative verbal and nonverbal behavioral indicators  (Poupore, 2015). The PhD  research aims to produce open-source lesson design protocols and feedback tools  frameworks for collaborative second language learning. Expected deliverables include interaction techniques, models for synchronous collaboration, and a repository of CSLL case studies.
