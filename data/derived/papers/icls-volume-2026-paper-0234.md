Children’s everyday lives are increasingly shaped by digital technologies in communication, entertainment, and learning. Large-scale surveys and international reports show that digital media use among school-aged children is high and continues to rise (mpfs, 2024). Learners with special educational needs often show comparable or higher levels of engagement, as digital media can support accessibility and participation (Vanderloo et al., 2025). Digital participation has therefore become an important factor for social inclusion and educational equity. Meaningful engagement with digital environments requires more than operational media use. It also involves understanding digital systems, algorithmic processes, and possibilities for shaping technology. Understanding algorithmic systems and creating with technology are now core components of digital competence (Valtonen et al., 2019). Although frameworks such as DigCompEdu (European Commission Joint Research Centre, 2017) emphasize the key role of teachers, many education systems still struggle to use technology in transformative ways (OECD Digital Education Outlook, 2023). Despite increasing attention to computing education, its implementation in SEN contexts remains limited. Learners with special educational needs often have little access to structured and adapted computing curricula. Many special education teachers report low confidence in the pedagogical use of digital technologies (Montenegro-Rueda & Fernández -Batanero, 2025). Teachers’ expertise and agency are therefore central to whether computing education becomes inclusive and sustainable (Andreoli et al., 2024). Against this backdrop, this study examines how special education teachers conceptualize and experience aspects of digital and computing education in their professional contexts. It aims to contribute to a practice-informed understanding of computing education in SEN contexts and to inform ongoing discussions on the development of inclusive computing curricula.. Education in the digital world in special educational needs settings Globally, computing education for students with special educational needs is shaped by unequal access to resources, limited teacher preparation, and weak curricular integration (Bouck et al., 2021). A central reason for 1652 this gap is the lack of SEN-specific considerations in mainstream computing education frameworks. As a result, many learners are excluded from computing and digital education or are offered only low-threshold activities that focus on media use rather than core computing concepts, despite evidence that meaningful participation is possible (Starks & Reich, 2023).

Although international initiatives in teacher education and curriculum reform have emerged, they often fail to address diverse cognitive, sensory, or emotional needs (Karlin et al., 2024). Teachers frequently report difficulties in teaching abstract computing concepts when age-appropriate or linguistically adapted materials are lacking (Elliott, 2018). Recent approaches therefore move beyond assistive technologies towards learning about technology itself, for example by linking Universal Design for Learning principles with computing instruction (Hansen et al., 2016).

In the United States, SEN expertise was explicitly included in the K –12 Computer Science Framework (CSTA, 2016). In contrast, European reference frameworks and German informatics standards largely lack concrete guidance for SEN contexts (Caspersen et al., 2022; Hellmig et al., 202 5). Despite limited systematic frameworks, research highlights several inclusive approaches to computing education. Integrating Computational Thinking into existing subjects has been shown to increase engagement among learners with special educational needs (Israel et al., 2015). Multimodal designs and tangible programming tools, including educational robotics, support accessible and motivating learning experiences for diverse learners (González- González et al., 2019). Together, these approaches offer initial guidance for inclusive computing education.

## Teachers’ perspectives on computing education

Teachers play a central role in shaping the scope and quality of computing education in SEN settings. Although many recognize its importance, they often feel insufficiently prepared to implement computing education, particularly in special education contex ts (Bouck et al., 2021). Computing is frequently marginal in pre-service teacher education and treated as optional, leading to inconsistent classroom practices and uneven instructional quality (Allison, 2023). Research therefore highlights the need for pro fessional development that explicitly addresses inclusive computing pedagogy (European Commission. Joint Research Centre., 2017). Most teacher education programs with embedded Computational Thinking elements assume general-education learner profiles and insufficiently address differentiated instruction in SEN contexts (Li, 2021). Targeted integration of computing into special education coursework has been shown to strengthen preservice teachers’ confidence and perceived instructional competence (Yadav et al., 2022). Additional frameworks emphasize multimodal and structured approaches for specific learner groups, though awareness among teachers remains limited (Shah et al., 2024). Understanding how teachers navigate these demands requires a framework that captures the complexity of digital education. The Dagstuhl Triangle, shown in Figure 1, conceptualizes digital phenomena from technological, user-oriented, and socio-cultural perspectives (Brinda et al., 2016). The technological perspective refers to understanding and shaping how digital systems work, the user-oriented perspective focuses on the purposeful use of digital tools, and the socio-cultural perspective addresses the societ al implications of digitalization. This framework highlights the challenge of balancing access, application, and participation in SEN contexts, where individual support needs must align with curricular and societal goals. ## Procedure and instruments

Interviews were conducted via video call and lasted between 45 and 60 minutes. All interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim. Interview prompts addressed teachers’ views on computing-related competencies, current teaching practices, and perceived challenges in special education contexts. The analysis followed a theory-informed qualitative content approach. Based on the Dagstuhl Triangle (Brinda et al., 2016), a primarily deductive category system was used to structure the material and to identify overarching patterns in teachers’ conceptualizations of computing education. As shown in Table 1, interview segments were assigned to the three main dimensions and their corresponding subcategories. Parts of the material were coded independently and subsequently discussed across coders in order to strengthen the consistency of category application. The reported coded references indicate how often these categories appeared across the interviews and were used to describe aggregated tendencies rather thandetailed thematic variation. Teachers primarily associated computing education with the use of digital devices and standard applications to support everyday functioning, accessibility, and vocational preparation. Tablets, smartphones, and commonly used software were described as central tools in classroom practice, often framed as means to foster independence and participation in daily life. One teacher stated: “We work a lot with tablets in every lesson. We watch videos, listen to audio samples, or use them to complete tasks.” Such accounts illustrate a focus on functional digital literacy rather than on broader computational concepts. linked to isolated activities or specific projects. When mentioned, programming and physical computing were described mainly in low-threshold formats, such as block-based programming or the use of educational robotics. These activities were not portrayed as systematically embedded in the curriculum but rather as occasional additions, often dependent on individual initiative or external support. 1654 Socio-cultural aspects of computing education were addressed primarily in relation to media risks and responsible use. Teachers referred to topics such as cyberbullying, disinformation, and excessive media consumption as relevant areas of discussion. These issues were framed mainly from a preventive perspective, aiming to raise awareness of potential dangers in digital environments. Broader socio-cultural dimensions, such as participatory engagement, cultural expression, or sustainability, were rarely articulated. Overall, the findings suggest an imbalance in how computing education is currently conceptualized in the participating SEN contexts. Teachers emphasized functional and compensatory uses of digital technologies, while opportunities for students to engage more deeply with technological creation or socio-cultural participation remained limited. These patterns suggest that computing education in SEN settings is shaped more by pragmatic adaptation to immediate educational goals than by a comprehensive curricular vision encompassing all dimensions of computing education.

The findings highlight a pronounced emphasis on functional and user-oriented conceptions of computing education in special education contexts. In contrast to more traditional notions of computing education, which include understanding how digital systems work and thus a “look under the hood,” teachers in SEN contexts mainly associate it with everyday usability, accessibility, and vocational preparation. This difference does not suggest a lack of awareness of broader computing concepts but reflects context-specific pedagogical priorities shaped by institutional mandates and perceived learner needs. From a special education perspective, the dominance of functional digital literacy can be understood as a response to long-standing goals such as fostering independence, supporting adaptive functioning, and reducing risks in digital environments. Teachers appear to position computing education primarily as a compensatory tool for participation in daily life and future employment. While this focus aligns with core SEN principles, it may also narrow computing education to application skills and product knowledge that can quickly become outdated, instead of supporting more durable understanding, creative engagement, and participatory agency. This paper contributes to the field by making visible this structural imbalance in teachers’ conceptualizations of computing education in SEN settings. Rather than evaluating instructional quality, it shows how curricular, institutional, and professional conditions shape what is seen as feasible and relevant computing education for students with special educational needs. At the same time, the findings must be interpreted in light of several limitations: the study is based on a small qualitative sample of e ight teachers from one federal state in Germany, the analysis was conducted at an aggregated and exploratory level, and only teachers’ perspectives were included. Future in-depth qualitative and mixed-methods research is therefore needed to examine inclusi ve computing education across diverse SEN contexts in greater detail. This paper examined how special education teachers conceptualize computing education within their pedagogical practice. The findings suggest that computing education in SEN contexts is currently understood primarily in functional and application-oriented terms, while technological understanding and broader socio-cultural participation remain less systematically embedded. In this sense, the study highlights a gap between current classroom conceptions and more comprehensive models of computing education. Against the backdrop of first curricular enactments in Germany, such as the pilot subject “Digital World”  (Unro et al., 2026), the findings provide an empirical basis for ongoing discussions on how inclusive computing curricula can be developed in ways that are both pedagogically responsive and educationally sustainable.
