# Integrating Culture to Ignite STEM Passion in U.S. Students

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
The Asset-Based Learning Environment (ABLE) project examines how integration of cultural variables in project design enhances STEM interest and motivation among U.S. students aged 12-18. Using the Interest-Driven Creator (IDC) Theory alongside cultural frameworks like Cultural Integration and Augmentation (CIA), the study indicates significant connections between cultural elements, local STEM practices, and interest/motivation. Findings emphasize the transformative role of culturally tailored STEM education in fostering curiosity, intrinsic motivation, and sustained engagement within diverse learning environments.

## Introduction and theory

The Asset-Based Learning Environment (ABLE) (Hamilton et al.,  2022) is project provides a platform for a culturally diverse collection of students aged 12-18 to explore STEM interests, building on the Inter national Community for Collaborative Content Creation (IC4) project funded by the National Science Foundation (NSF). This study examines the impact of cultural elements  mediated over the ABLE platform on students' inter est and motivation in STEM, particularly within its global context involving students from the U.S.A., Mexico,  Brazil, Ghana, and Kenya. Findings from the first year (202 3) confirmed that cultural integration can support sustained STEM engagement, providing insights for educators a nd policymakers to develop strategies for attractin g and retaining a diverse STEM student population.

Integrating cultural elements into STEM education e nhances student engagement, especially for those from underserved backgrounds (Akumbu et al., 2023; Akumbu, 2022). Culturally responsive curricula, lik e the Local STEM (LSTEM) approach (Akumbu, 2022), whic h is situated within the Cultural Integration and Augmentation (CIA) framework, make STEM more releva nt by addressing real-world issues that resonate with students’ lives (Gay, 2015; Thevenot, 2021). Frameworks such as CIA and the Transfer and Adoption/Adaptation of Universal Principles (TAUP) emphasize the importance of incorporating local cultures and contexts into STEM education, offering a comprehensive strategy for pr omoting inclusion and engagement globally (Akumbu, 2022; Poloma & Szelényi, 2019). This study builds on these cultural frameworks by integrating them with Chan et al.’s Interest-Driven Creator (IDC) Theory (Chan et al., 2019) to explore how interest and cultural context drive STEM engagement. IDC Theory underscores the importance o f personal interests in fostering lifelong learning through interconnected loops of interest, creation, and hab it (Chan et al., 2019; Chen et al., 2020). By combi ning these cultural and interest-driven frameworks, this approach enhances STEM educ ation by aligning i t with students' cultural backgrounds, promoting intrinsic motivation, and fostering an inclusive environment where learners feel empowered to contribute meaningfully (Falk et al., 2016; Gay, 2015; Thevenot, 2021; Hite & Taylor, 202 1; Akumbu et al., 2023).

By integrating culturally responsive frameworks (CI A, TAUP, LSTEM) with interest-driven principles (IDC), our study shows how broad cultural considera tions translate into hands-on, locally relevant STEM experiences that sustain engagement. While CIA and TAUP establish the theoretical basis for embedding cultural elements, LSTEM operationalizes these ideas through  practical STEM activities linked to students’ cult ural backgrounds. Phinney (1990) highlights the signific ance of identity and recognition in dive rse contexts, and Bennett (2017) emphasizes cultural sensitivity and a sense of belonging. Chan et al. (2019) underscore  the importance of personal meaning for motivation/interest and deeper learning. Collectively, these perspectives link culture, identity, and motivation/interest in STEM education.

## Methods

The dataset for this study includes 17 U.S. adolescent participants who engaged in global meet-ups held between September and December 2023. These meet-ups were part of a larger initiative involving 28 students from Brazil, Ghana, Kenya, and the United States, who participat ed in 15 global breakout sessions with the main goa l of engaging in STEM learning. While students from all four countries contributed to the study, this paper specifically focuses on the U.S. participants to examine how cul tural variables embedded in the project design fost er interest and motivation in STEM among U.S. adolescents withi n a global learning context. By situating U.S. stud ents' experiences in a collaborative, cross-cultural environment, this research provides insigh ts into the unique impact of global exposure on STEM engagement, offering relevant takeaways for international audiences.

Using the Cultural Integration and Augmentation (CI A) and Transfer and Adoption/Adaptation of Universal Principles (TAUP) frameworks, cultural elements like beliefs, values, practices, heritage, and oral traditions were grouped into a "Culture_Combined" c onstruct due to overlapping definitions (Akumbu, 20 22; Akumbu et al., 2023; Hite & Taylor, 2021; Kelley & Knowles, 2016; Kennedy & Odell, 2014; Staus et al., 2020). The data were independently coded and moderated using a binary coding system for consistency (Shaffer, 2017). Epistemic Network Analysis (ENA) was applied to identify co-occurrence patterns among constructs, visualizing connections across 510 lines of U.S. student discourse while excluding adult input (Ruiz et al., 2019). This method provided insights into how cultural integration and  STEM interest interconnect in discussions. Table 1  presents the codes and sample utterances selected from the d ata in this study to illustrate how adolescents res ponded to ABLE’s collaborative ecosystem.  Additionally, Ordered Network Analysis (ONA) was ap plied to visualize directed connections among constructs, accounting for the sequential order of discourse events (Tan et al., 2023). Networks were aggregated and normalized, creating d irected graphs for c omparing cultural interactions and STEM interest sequences.

## Results

The Epistemic Network and Ordered Network Analysis (ENA and ONA) models highlights interview patterns among U.S. student participants, with visualizations indicating the strength of connections through line thickness and saturation and directed connections.

ENA findings, presented in Figure 1, left image, re veal strong connections between culture_combined, interest_motivation, and local STEM, emphasizing the role of cultural integration in fostering STEM engagement among U.S. learners. The strongest link is between culture_combined and interest_motivation, supported  by students' curiosity and desire to explore new exper iences. One student longed for travel opportunities, stating, "I've never left the state or not even the... continent." Another noted the influence of cultural diversity in the U.S., highlighting how local adaptations shape their unde rstanding: "It's definitely not just one culture; i t's definitely a mixture, like the melting pot we have here." One student, for his project, wants to talk “about how AI  could be a threat to society” because he is concerned that peo ple “might get so into technology that they forget about the natural world and stop understanding the natural ha bitat.” These insights underline how cultural expos ure and curiosity drive interest and motivation in STEM learning. Alternatively in Figure 1, the middle and right ima ge provide deeper insights using ONA graph. ONA shows directed connections that reveal a potential sequential flow among constructs. In the ONA graphs, each chevron’s (arrowhead within triangular lines) direction shows which code tends to appear first in the conversation. The chevron/triangle directions reveal a conversati onal “chain of thought,” where one topic naturally leads into the next. The chevron  directions and thickness collectively reveal a conv ersational flow, indicating which ideas set the stage for others to emerge. Figure 1’s middle and right panels illustrate the O NA findings, which reveal a clear sequential flow among the coded constructs. Specifically, the arrow from LocalSTEM to Interest_Motivation

(1) suggests that local STEM topics are often discussed before participants articulate what sparks their interest or

motivation. In addition, the arrow from SenseofBelonging to Interest_Motivation (2) and LocalSTEM (3) implies that feelings of community may serve as a precursor  to motivational expressions  and for STEM engagement. Finally, a connection from LocalSTEM to Culture_Combined (4) and Interest_Moti vation to Culture_Combined

(5) suggests that after expressing enthusiasm for STEM  or related topic, students often integrate cultural

considerations into their discourse. Overall, these directed links support a temporal progression in which cultural integration provides both antecedent and reinforcem ent support along several directed paths for shapin g STEMrelated interest and identity outcomes (Tan et al., 2023). The directionality of the causal chain in the disco urse between cultural references and expressions of interest should be interpreted with caution, as often the interest expressed prior in sequence to the keywords tagged to culture in a sentence was predicated on the cultural relevance of the conversation topic, with the predicate stated in the second part of the utterance. One student de clared, “personally, for me, I chose food just beca use, first of all, it's food. And who doesn't like food? And also, because it's cultural food you've never tried bef ore,” highlighting how curiosity about unfamiliar dishes fosters deepe r engagement. Two U.S. students highlighted cultural connections through food, stating, “my who le family loves white chocolate; I'm the only one w ho likes dark chocolate,” and “just going to the place where  this food originated would be really cool.” These quotes illustrate how food links cultural heritage and identity to personal interests. Another student expressed enthusiasm for new cultural experiences: “When you try somethi ng new and you end up really  liking it, that can be really awesome and a new perspective on cultures.” Cultura l discussions frequently preceded STEM conversation s, as indicated by statements such as, “if we could find out an issue going on... having to do with plants a nd animals," and, “one issue could be climate change... like polar bears and their population, how they're endangered, what we cando to help them.” Another example connects cult ure, agriculture, and technology: “We are thinking about making agriculture easier using robotics, engineeri ng, or AI. Agriculture has supported humans from th e start of civilization. Helping plants thrive with modern tec hnology can help our planet.” These directed connec tions, as visualized in the ONA graphs (Figure 1 in the middl e and far right), imply that cultural integration m ay serve as a precursor to and catalyst for heightened STEM int erest, supporting a more nuanced interpretation of the causal relationships observed in the data.

The ONA analysis reveals a clear directional flow w here cultural references often immediately follow and appear to reinforce expressions of interest and  motivation, often in instances of predicating the student’s personal STEM interests on criteria like community-centered cultural relevance. While this paper focuses on U.S. students, initial ENA findings from Brazil, Ghana, and Kenya provide valuable context for understandin g how cultural frameworks shape engagement. Students in G hana showed strong connections between cultural sensitivity and interest/motivation, particularly in STEM projects that aligned with their cultural contexts. Kenyan participants showed a notable enthusiasm for cultur al foods and traditions, with local STEM activities  strongly linked to these cultural practices, highlighting th e role of community-based traditions in fostering engagement. For instance, a Kenyan speaker stated, "I think I c hose food because through food, we get to learn abo ut other people's culture," which suggests that introducing cultural elements sets the stage for the linked exp ressions of interest. Similarly, a Ghanaian speaker remarked on the universal appeal of food, emphasizing, "who doesn't like food? Everyone likes food... eating from different cultures," indicating that cultural exposure is a common trigger for engagement. The idea that food tie learners to their heritage and family suggest a meaningful bridge between culture and motivation in ABLE STEM learning space.  Another student noted: “I love to know more about other people's culture. And see places where people lack water. Speaking of any possibility that could help gene rate water into the places and homes.” While Brazilian s tudents showed fewer connections to cultural sensit ivity or identity recognition, here also strong links emerged between combined cultural contexts, interest, motivation, and local STEM.

These close-knit relationships, as visualized in the ENA and ON A graphs in Figure 1, suggest that cultural integration may serve as a reinforcement a nd catalyst for heightened STEM interest, supportin g a more nuanced interpretation of the directional relat ionships observed in the data. These findings under score the importance of tailoring STEM education to align wit h diverse cultural frameworks while offering insigh ts into how unique cultural contexts shape motivation. Such  comparisons highlight the potential for adapting S TEM education frameworks to address specific cultural n eeds, ultimately contributing to a globally relevan t approach to STEM learning.

## Conclusion

The findings highlight that U.S. students indicate a strong relationship between cultural elements and their interest or motivation in STEM, as evidenced in the initial breakout sessions of the ABLE project. This group discussion data provided evidence in support of powerful conne ctions between culture, interest/motivation, and ST EM, illustrating how student interest and motivation for studying STEM are often predicated on exposure to culturally relevant contexts for applying STEM learnings  (Gay, 2015; Hidi  & Renninger, 2006; Jackson et al., 2021). Contextualized cultural integration, such as explor ing traditional foods and heritage, creates engagin g and relatable learning experiences that foster intrinsi c motivation. These findings suggest that culturall y responsive STEM education, when aligned with U.S. students' diverse cultural backgrounds, has the potential to significantly enhance academic engagement and long-term interest in STEM fields. Although this study primarily focuses on U.S. stude nts, findings from Brazil, Ghana, and Kenya underscore the broader implications of cultural relevance in STEM education. Ghanaian students exhibited strong ties between cultural sensitivity and STEM interest, while Kenyan participants highlighted the role of integrating cultural traditions into STEM engagement. Brazilian  students showed links between local STEM and a sen se of belonging, though connections to cultural sensitivi ty appeared less pronounced. Th ese results collectively emphasize the importance of tailoring STEM educatio n to diverse cultural backgrounds, allowing for a n uanced understanding and adaptation of STEM principles, si gnificantly enhancing student interest and long-term engagement (Akumbu, 2022; Kelley & Knowles, 2016; Kennedy & Odell, 2014). Future research should explore how culturally responsive education frameworks (CIA, TAUP, LSTEM) can be adapted and scaled across diverse contexts, leveraging both local and global perspectives to foster equitable and inclusive learning opportunities, while deeper integration with interest-driven principles (IDC) can illuminate how cultural elements and personal interests interact to create more engaging experiences.
