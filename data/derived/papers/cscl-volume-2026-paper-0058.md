# GenAI Chatbot for Socially Shared Regulation of Learning in CSCL: Impact on University Students' Learning Engagement and Outcome

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Supporting socially shared regulation of learning (SSRL) is crucial for successful collaborative learning but is challenging due to its multidimensional and context-sensitive nature. While generative AI (GenAI) chatbots have shown promise in addressing these challenges, research has largely treated SSRL and GenAI chatbots separately, with limited empirical exploration of their integration. Furthermore, existing studies on GenAI chatbots supporting SSRL are mainly conceptual or design-focused, lack ing empirical evaluation. Addressing these gaps, we developed SSRLBot, a GenAI chatbot designed to identify SSRL challenges and offer contextual strategies. We conducted a six-week experimental study with 55 Chinese university students, dividing them into two classes: one using SSRLBot (N = 27) and the other without chatbot support (N = 28). Learning engagement and outcomes were assessed through questionnaires, platform log data and products. Results showed that SSRLBot significantly improved students’ learning engagement and outcomes, extending the application of GenAI chatbots from content tutor to group moderators in CSCL.

Socially shared regulation of learning (SSRL) is a collective metacognitive process essential for successful collaboration, involving the co-construction of task understanding, shared goal setting, monitoring, adaptation, and reflection (Hadwin et al., 2011). Despite its importance, effectively scaffolding SSRL in Computer-supported collaborative learning (CSCL) remains challenging due to its dynamic and context-dependent nature (Kim et al., 2025). Traditional static scaffolds often lack the adaptability to  meet groups’ real-time regulatory needs, leading to fragmented or superficial regulation (Zheng et al., 2025; Zhou & Tsai, 2023). While Generative AI (GenAI), particularly large language models (LLMs), presents a promising approach for adaptive and dialogic SSRL support, its potential is often underutilized. First, most efforts leverage GenAI for content-centric tutoring, largely overlooking its potential as a process-oriented team moderator (Dara et al., 2024). Second, even where regulation support is attempted, most studies remain conceptual or designoriented, underscoring the need for more empirical research to establish their effectiveness. (Huang et al., 2025). Furthermore, these attempts are often fragmented, failing to scaffold the full SSRL process. Addressing these gaps, the present study developed a GenAI chatbot for scaffolding SSRL (named SSRLBot) and conducted a six-week experimental study to explore its impact on university students ’ learning engagement and outcomes in CSCL. The following research questions (RQ) were formulated. RQ1: What are the effects of selected GenAI chatbot on university students ’ learning engagement? RQ2: What are the effects of GenAI chatbot on university students’ collaborative learning outcomes? Learning engagement in collaborative learning Learning engagement refers to the quality of effort students invest in learning to achieve desired outcomes (Fredricks et al., 2004). In CSCL, engagement is shaped by social interaction and is commonly conceptualised as a multidimensional construct, which includes (Fredricks et al., 2004; Sinha et al., 2015): (a) Behavioral engagement, which refers to participation in collaborative tasks and involvement in academic activities. (b) Cognitive engagement, which refers to the knowledge construction process that  occurs as group members work toward a common goal. Previous research has identified that the ICAP framework (Interactive, Constructive, Active, Passive) provides a valuable lens for analyzing cognitive engagement levels. According to the framework, interactive and constructive cognitive activities are most likely to result in higher levels of engagement, as they involve deeper processing and meaningful collaboration (Chi & Wylie, 2014). (c) Emotional engagement represents students’ affective reactions about their collaborative experience and peers. Despite the pivotal role of engagement, existing literature identifies several critical challenges in authentic CSCL settings, notably uneven behavioral involvement, shallow cognitive processing, and negative emotional experience (Chen et al., 2024). As a growing body of research links effective SSRL to high-quality 389 engagement, scaffolding SSRL is increasingly recognized as a critical strategy to address these collaborative challenges (Isohätälä et al., 2017).

Supporting SSRL in collaborative learning with GenAI Chatbots SSRL extends self-regulated learning (SRL) from the individual to the group level. Whereas SRL concerns learners’ proactive regulation via cognitive, motivational, and behavioral strategies, SSRL emphasizes collective metacognitive regulation of shared cognition, motivation, and emotion toward common goals (Hadwin et al., 2011; Zimmerman, 1990). It is commonly conceptualized as a cyclical process of shared task understanding, shared goal setting and planning, shared monitoring and adapting, and shared reflecting (Panadero & Järvelä, 2015). Empirical studies have suggested that groups exhibiting high-level SSRL tend to achieve deeper engagement and produce higher-quality collaborative outcomes (Li et al., 2021). However, effective SSRL rarely develops spontaneously in CSCL. Prior work has therefore examined scaffolds such as strategy instruction or question-based prompts across SSRL phases, which can improve engagement, outcomes, and reflective quality (Zheng et al., 2025; Zhou & Tsai, 2023). Despite these advances, limitations remain. Most scaffolds are insufficiently adaptive to groups’ evolving needs and rarely address group heterogeneity or role differences. Additionally, they offer limited interactivity for co-constructing understanding and iteratively adjusting strategies.

Recent advances in large language models (LLMs) make GenAI chatbots a promising direction because they can sustain multi-turn, context-specific dialogue and offer bidirectional, just-in-time support aligned with core SSRL processes (Edwards et al., 2025). Building on this potential, emerging studies have begun designing SSRL-oriented GenAI chatbots. For example, Huang et al. (2025) introduced an LLM-based SSRLBot that summarizes group dialogue and generates theory-aligned evaluations and recommendations. Re lated CSCL interventions have embedded GenAI chatbots into collaborative platforms (e.g., PyramidApp) and reported improved answer accuracy and indications of better individual –group interaction quality (Sánchez -Reina et al., 2025). Nevertheless, several gaps remai ns. First, the evidence base is still limited, with many studies being conceptual or design-oriented rather than empirical.  Moreover, few studies directly test whether SSRL-focused chatbots enhance learning engagement, and existing implementations often address only parts of the SSRL cycle. It is timely to develop a GenAI chatbot aligned with the full SSRL cycle and empirically examine its effects on engagement and learning outcomes in CSCL.

## Participants and procedure

The study was conducted in a six-week Educational Research Methods course at a Chinese university, involving 55 Educational Technology undergraduates (22 male, 33 female; aged 18 –20) from two parallel classes with comparable prior knowledge. One class was randomly assigned to the experimental condition (SSRLBot-CSCL;), utilizing a GenAI chatbot to scaffold SSRL on the Feishu platform, while the other served as the control (T-CSCL;) without chatbot support. The experiment followed a three-stage design: (1) a pre-intervention week for platform orientation and pretesting; (2) a four-week intervention where both groups attended 90-minute lectures and collaborated on Feishu platform to develop group research proposals, with the experimental group receiving embedded SSRLBot scaffolding; and (3) a final week for proposal submission, presentations, and emotional engagement surveys. All participants provided informed consent following institutional ethical guidelines. SSRLBot: a GenAI chatbot supporting SSRL The design of SSRLBot was guided by established SSRL scaffolding principles (Edwards et al., 2025; Järvelä et al., 2015; Kim et al., 2025). These principles emphasize: (a) the importance of enhancing learners’ awareness of the entire SSRL process, which includes shared task understanding, shared goal setting and planning, shared monitoring and strategy adjustment, and shared reflection; and (b) the need to support students in applying appropriate strategies at each stage of SSRL to facilitate the regulation process.  SSRLBot was developed on the Coze plat form and embedded in the Feishu collaborative environment. It was powered by the Doubao large language model and designed as a team moderator rather than a general-purpose conversational assistant. SSRLBot leveraged the semantic understanding capabilities of the LLM, while its responses were constrained by the SSRL framework and supported through Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) with an expert-curated knowledge base of SSRL strategies and course content.

SSRLBot provided two main functions  (See Figure 1). First, it delivered proactive, phase-specific prompts at regular intervals to guide groups sequentially through the complete SSRL cycle, explicitly signaling the current regulatory phase and encouraging shared sense-making. Second, it offered on-demand a daptive 390 support when students queried the agent, diagnosing regulatory challenges and generating strategy-oriented guidance aligned with the group’s current SSRL phase, while avoiding direct task answers. A pilot study with five students was conducted prior to deployment to refine the prompting design and ensure system stability. To evaluate the intervention, multidimensional engagement and learning outcomes were assessed. Behavioral engagement was derived from platform logs (i.e., online learning duration and message counts) and participation equality via the Gini coefficient. Cognitive engagement was analyzed by coding posts at the sentence level using the ICAP framework (Chi & Wylie, 2014), with 15% of the data double-coded to ensure reliability (κ = 0.84). Emotional engagement was measured using a 5-item adapted scale (Xu et al., 2024; α = 0.891). Finally, learning outcomes were determined by the quality of group proposals, which were blindly evaluated by two trained raters using a 6-dimension rubric (i.e., completeness, significance, originality and novelty, feasibility, scientificity, and clarity). The maximum possible score per submission was 100 points. The final score was the average of the two raters’ scores.

Due to non-normal distributions and the small group-level sample size (n = 12), Mann–Whitney U tests were employed for all comparisons.

Comparison of learning engagement Behavioral engagement Regarding behavioral engagement, the SSRLBot-CSCL group generated significantly more discussion messages (M = 53.37, SD  = 33.77) than the T-CSCL group (M = 32.39, SD  = 31.75), U  = 207.00, p  = 0.004, r = 0.39. Furthermore, Gini coefficient analysis revealed that SSRLBot-CSCL groups exhibited more equal participation (M = 0.28, SD = 0.05) compared to T-CSCL groups (M = 0.41, SD = 0.11), U = 3.00, p = 0.016, r = 0.69. Aligned with Social Interdependence Theory, the chatbot’s prompts for shared goal-setting and monitoring likely strengthened collective responsibility and individual accountability, thereby sustaining more balanced active participation (Johnson & Johnson, 1998). While T-CSCL students spent more time viewing materials, the difference was not significant (p = 0.219), suggesting the chatbot may shift engagement from individual consumption to interactive, collective problem-solving. One theoretical lens for interpreting this pattern is Cognitive Load Theory, which suggests that learners’ limited processing capacity can be affected by coordination demands during collaboration. It is possible that the chatbot’s guidance for identifying challenges and adjusting joint strategies helped manage these demands, allowing students to allocate more cognitive resources to building on peers’ ideas rather than producing passive responses. However, cognitive load was not directly measured in this study, so this interpretation should be considered tentative.

Emotional engagement For emotional engagement, the Mann-Whitney U test indicated that SSRLBot-CSCL groups (M = 3.95, SD = 0.49) significantly outperformed T-CSCL groups (M = 3.27, SD = 1.03), U = 220.50, p = 0.007, r = 0.36. This aligns with Self-Determination Theory: adaptive prompts and actionable strategies can support autonomy, competence, and relatedness —conditions conducive to interest and persistence. Importantly, emotional gains co-occurred with more equitable participation and higher-level discourse, suggesting SSRLBot may support collective coordination rather than merely producing a novelty effect. Comparison of collaborative learning outcome For collaborative learning outcome, Mann–Whitney U tests indicated that the SSRLBot-CSCL groups (M = 90.51, SD = 1.61) significantly outperformed the T-CSCL groups (M = 87.96, SD = 1.65) in total proposal scores, U = 5.00, p = 0.037, r = 0.60. Specifically, significant gains were observed in originality and novelty (p  = 0.035, r = 0.61) and clarity (p = 0.029, r = 0.63), whereas no significant differences were found in procedural dimensions such as completeness, feasibility or scientificity (p > 0.05). This pattern extends prior work on SSRL and GenAI supports by highlighting the value of a chatbot that orchestrates group regulation  rather than acting only as a Q&A tool. The selective effects likely reflect that regulatory scaffolding most directly benefits idea advancement and articulation, whereas procedural quality may require additional domain-and method-specific guidance. Prior work has rarely integrated SSRL scaffolding into GenAI chatbots, and existing SSRL-focused chatbot studies remain largely conceptual with limited empirical evaluation. To address this gap, we designed and implemented SSRLBot to diagnose SSRL challenges and provide adaptive strategies during CSCL. Results showed that SSRLBot improved students ’ behavioral, cognitive, and emotional engagement, as well as collaborative learning outcomes. This study contributes to CSCL in three ways. First, it expands the role of GenAI chatbots beyond the tutor/assistant paradigm by demonstrating a chatbot that functions as a group-level moderator to facilitate SSRL. Second, it provides empirical evidence that SSRL-oriented chatbot scaffolding can enhance both collaborative processes and products. Third, it suggests a practical pathway for supporting learner collective agency and mitigating overreliance on GenAI through regulation-focused guidance. Several limitations should be acknowledged. While the current study focused on comparing engagement and learning outcomes across conditions, future work could deepen the process-level understanding by examining how students take up and build on specific chatbot prompts and by evaluating the relevance of the guidance over time. Relatedly, beyond coding cognitive engagement levels, subsequent studies may adopt interaction-quality frameworks that also capture how contributions connect to and advance the group’s collective work (e.g., Zhang et al., 2009). Finally, to clarify how SSRL-oriented chatbot support translates into engagement and outcomes, larger-sample studies could test explanatory pathways statistically (e.g., mediation models linking regulation, engagement, and performance).
