# Non-Disruptive Teacher Evaluation: From Case Analysis to Key Implementation Processes

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Reforming teacher evaluation is essential for promoting educational innovation and advancing sustainable development goals. Non-disruptive assessment offers a promising direction by embedding evaluation into everyday teaching practices with minimal interference. Yet, its implementation remains conceptually and practically underdeveloped. This study investigates the theoretical foundations and design principles of non-disruptive teacher evaluation through six representative cases. It identifies three key challenges: (1) technological tools support seamless data capture, but struggle with diagnostic precision; (2) online representations of teaching are expanding, yet fail to reflect critical offline practices; and (3) indicator frameworks are advancing, but linking evidence to competencies remains difficult. In response, we propose a four-step process—unify concepts, clarify standards, establish mappings, and formulate guidelines—to guide system design and support teacher growth through embedded, adaptive evaluation. The framework aims to support professional growth while contributing to more equitable and sustainable education systems.

## Introduction

Teacher evaluation is fundamental to professional d evelopment, yet conventional approaches often inter rupt instructional routines, increase workload, and rely  on narrow criteria, limiting their developmental v alue (Holloway, 2019; Sánchez et al., 2023). In  response, non-disruptive evaluation has emerged as a promising alternative. Defined as an embedded, low-interference process, it integrates assessment into  everyday teaching without requiring additional preparation, visible o bservation, or procedural interruption. Rather than  imposing external tasks or drawing attention to evaluation, it captures natural teaching behaviors through exis ting interactions and digital traces (Shute et al., 2009; McCarney et al., 2007). This approach reduces bias such as the Hawthorne effect and enables more authentic, context-sensitive data collection. Advances in AI and learn ing analytics further support real-time, unobtrusive observation of teacher practices (Yang et al., 2021, Fischer et al., 2020). While unobtrusive methods have been explored in student learning contexts (Fu  et al., 2019; Davis et al., 2022), their application in teacher evaluation rema ins underexplored. This study investigates six repr esentative cases to examine the practical challenges and desig n conditions of non-disruptive teacher evaluation, aiming to inform future frameworks aligned with teachers’ developmental needs.

## Theoretical framework

Building on prior work in unobtrusive and embedded assessments, this study positions non-disruptive teacher evaluation as a model that captures instructional e vidence without procedural interference. Rooted in the idea of unobtrusive measures (Webb et al.,  1966), non-disruptive evaluation has evolved with digital tool s and learning analytics, enabling context-aware, low-visibility data collection in real classroom environments (Shute et al., 2013; Fu et al., 2020). It shifts the focus from su mmative judgments to developmental insight, aiming to support growth rather than compliance. While these approach es have shown promise in student-centered and informal learning settings, their systematic application to teacher evaluation remains limited. To analyze and compare evaluation designs, we adopt the directness–obtrusiveness model, which categorizes assessment tools along two key dimensions: directne ss, the alignment between assessment and target competencies; and obtrusiveness, the degree of inte rference with instructional flow and teacher autono my. In teacher development contexts, obtrusiveness may inc lude time-consuming observations, external reporting demands, or behavioral changes induced by surveilla nce. By contrast, non-disruptive evaluation aims to reduce both cognitive and operational burden, embedding assessment seamlessly into professional practice. In this model, Ideal designs fall into the high-directness and low-obtrusiveness quadrant, where evaluation closely al igns with professional competencies while minimizing disruption to instructional flow and teacher autonomy. For instance, analyzing teachers’ interaction data on digital pla tforms—such as frequency and quality of peer responses —can provide valid insights into their engagement and co mpetence without requiring behavioral modification or additional workload (Lee et al.,2024). This conceptual framework provides the lens for our case analysis, helping to identify design conditions and practical tension s in implementing non-disruptive evaluation aligned with professional growth.Building on this conceptual model, we further propose a process-oriented framework to guide the design and interpretation of non-disruptive teacher evaluation systems (see Figure 1).

## Methodology

This study adopts a multiple-case study design combined with a best practices ap proach to investigate how non-disruptive assessment systems are designed and implemented to support teacher development (Yin, 1984; Schaffer & Riordan, 2003). Six representativ e cases were selected from initiatives targeting in-service teachers in China, covering a range of evaluation system types and development trajectories. Cases 1, 2, and 3 represent large-scale digital platforms implemented by local education au thorities, while Cases 4, 5, and 6 are prototype systems co-developed by academic teams and partner schools. Ea ch case focused on a distinct domain of teacher development: classroom instruction (Cases 1, 2), as signment feedback (Case 3), professional learning communities (Case 4), digital competency (Case 5), and care er advancement mechanisms (Case 6). Despite contextual differences, all cases shared the goal of embedding assessment into daily professional practices through non-disruptive data capture and analysis.Due to space constraints, detailed descriptions are omi tted, but representative features are highlighted across findings, case identifiers (Cases 1–6) are used to support cross-case comparisons in the findings.

Case selection criteria included: (1) alignment bet ween evaluation goals and teacher competency frameworks, (2) availability of embedded, process-oriented behavioral data, and (3) integration into routine instructional or professional workflows. The process framework introduced in Figure 1 structures the analysis by linking evaluation goals, empirical evidence, and implementation processes, while highlighting the dual concerns of alignment and the directness–obtrusiveness balance in system design (Peeters & Martin, 2017). Each case was analyzed using a structured coding ru bric based on three dimensions: (1) alignment betwe en evaluation objectives and the collected evidence, (2) directness of data generation, and (3) obtrusive ness of the evaluation process. These dimensions were derived from the theoretical framework introdu ced earlier. Two members of the research team independently rated ea ch case using a five-point Likert scale, followed by discussion to resolve any discrepancies. Inter-rater agreement was high, with Cohen’ s kappa (κ) calculated at 0.88, indicating strong reliability. This process e nabled comparative analysis across cases and suppor ted the identification of recurring design tensions in impl ementing non-disruptive evaluation models. ## Findings

Findings are structured around three recurring design tensions observed across the six cases, reflecting mismatches between evaluation goals, data evidence, and implem entation processes. These tensions highlight key ba rriers to realizing non-disruptive, competency-aligned teacher evaluation in practice.

## Finding 1: Advancing Automation, But Diagnostic Precision Remains Limited

Multiple systems (e.g., Cases 1 and 5) leveraged em bedded sensors, intelligent recording tools, and AI-based analysis to enable non-disruptive data collection. Some platforms integrated physiological and environmental data to monitor classroom dynamics in real time. While t hese technologies reduced teachers’ reporting burde n and minimized disruptions, they struggled to capture nuanced instructional behaviors such as improvisation, adaptive pacing, or emotional attunement. For example, in Ca se 5, although the platform continuously recorded t eacher movement and voice tone, it failed to distinguish b etween instructional explanation and off-topic talk. This gap between technical sophistication and pedagogical gr anularity raised concerns about the interpretabilit y and developmental value of the assessment results.

## Finding 2: Enhancing Online Representations, But Offline Practices Remain

Underrepresented Several platforms (e.g., Cases 2 and 4) unobtrusive ly recorded teachers’ online actions —such as assignment design, grading, and peer interaction—which were used as key evidence for evaluation. However, when platforms rely solely on online behavior, they fa il to account for core elements of in-person instruction, including spontaneous questioning, physical classroom managem ent, and student-teacher rapport. For instance, in Case 4, although teachers’ participation in a virtual PLC w as tracked, their offline collaboration, mentoring, or feedback remained invisible to the system. This disconnectio n limits the ability of such systems to support comprehensive and equitable evaluations across diverse teaching contexts.

## Finding 3: Structuring Indicators, But Behavioral Linkage Remains Difficult

some systems (e.g., Case 2), evaluation frameworks attempted to align process data with competency mod els by mapping behavioral traces to pre-defined indicators. For example, the frequency of d ifferentiated assignment design was mapped to instructional innovation. Whil e this increased standardization and consistency, i t proved challenging to validate whether these proxies refle cted genuine teacher expertise. Differences in subj ect area, grade level, and pedagogical style further complica ted cross-case comparisons. As a result, establishing robust, generalizable linkages between platform-recorded actions and developmental competencies rem ained an unresolved challenge in system design.

## Discussions

This study identified three recurring tensions in t he design of non-disruptive teacher evaluation systems: limitations in diagnostic precision, underrepresent ation of offline practices, and difficulties in lin king behavioral data to professional competencies. These findings highlight systemic challenges in aligning evaluation goals, data collection, and implementation processes. To address these tensions, we propose a four-stage process framework (see Figure 2), rooted in a competency-based philosophy and e mphasizing embedded, data-informed evaluation logic.

Figure 2 Aligning Goals, Data, and Practice: Four Steps to Effective Non-Disruptive Evaluation Step 1: Unifying Conceptual Foundations Teacher evaluation serves not only as a technical measurement process, but also as a value-laden act that defines the goals of professional growth. Thus, the foundational task of non-disruptive evaluation is to establish a competency-oriented conceptual foundation. Evaluation must be guided by a developmental vision that supports teachers’ holistic growth and aligns with the broader mission  of education (e.g., fostering student learning and well-being). Specifically, evaluation systems should pri oritize core professional capacities across career stages, such as digital literacy, instruction al innovation, classroom management, and parent-teacher communication. These reflect deep, high-level demands of teacher professional development and ensure that evaluation maintains a teacher-centered orientation.

Step 2: Clarifying Competency Standards Standards are the backbone of any evaluation system, directly shaping scientific rigor, fairness, and implementation effectiveness. Under a competency-based paradigm, standards should be derived from au thentic teaching contexts and aligned with observable, measurable forms of practice-based evidence. These include both explicit instructional behaviors (e.g., strategies,  designs, discourse) and implicit dimensions (e.g.,  innovation, inquiry, reflection). For example, to assess “instructional innovation, ” relevant evidence may include lesson plans, classroom videos, interaction data, student engagement, and learning outcomes. Intelligent eval uation systems candisaggregate these data points and match them to appropriate dimensions, providing a robust evidence base without imposing extra workload on teachers.Importantly, standards should be differentiated by teacher roles and contexts. A flexible yet generalizable evaluati on framework must account for heterogeneity across school settings and ensure both comparab ility and adaptability. Only by clearly articulatin g what matters and how it is manifested in practice can evaluation achieve both validity and usability. Step 3: Establishing Mappings Between Data and Competency Indicators To enhance feasibility, non-disruptive evaluation must embed itself in routine professional activities. This requires building structured mappings between practical evid ence and performance indicators derived from teache rs’ everyday work. These may include assignment design,  peer observation, lesson demonstration, classroom management routines, or co-teaching practices (Park et al., 2023). By embeddin g data collection into these workflows, evaluation becomes seamless and low-intrusive.The core of this step lies in revising existing performance indicators and aligning them with struc tured evidence categories. This process benefits fr om incorporating multiple perspectives, including peer assessments and student feedback. Generative AI technologies further reduce the cost and complexity of qualitative analysis,  enabling systems to identify hidden links between internal states and external actions. In doing so, non-disruptive evaluation systems move beyond simple me trics and toward diagnostic, context-sensitive representations of teacher growth. Step 4: Formulating Embedded Evaluation Guidelines Although teaching routines vary by subject and cont ext, most professional development occurs within re latively stable structures —lesson planning, teaching, assessment, and collabor ation. Establishing clear, structured workflows helps standardize evidence collection, guide teacher actions, and ensure that non-disruptive evaluation generates complete and reliable data. For instance,  integrating assignment design, feedback, and analy sis into a unified platform facilitates seamless evaluation ac ross stages. Similarly, defining unified criteria f or teaching observations or public demonstrations enhances cons istency and comparability.The process of standardiz ation should not negate teacher agency. Systems must offe r room for contextual flexibility while ensuring interoperability and scalability. This requires iter ative refinement, participatory design, and feedbac k loops that allow teachers to respond to evaluation procedures and outcomes in real time. These four stages are neither strictly linear nor s tatic. In design, standards, mappings, and norms mu st be continuously adjusted and aligned. In implementation, agile iteration and teacher participation are essential. Nondisruptive evaluation should not strive for universal uniformity, but rather for ada ptive generalizability — supporting multiple contexts and professional needs while maintaining stable procedural logic. With coordinated workflows and feedback mechanisms, non-disruptive evaluation can support professional lear ning ecosystems that are evidence-based, teacher-centered, and instructionally embedded.

## Conclusions

This study examined how non-disruptive evaluation systems can be strategically designed to align with teachers’ daily practices and developmental needs (Wilson & Sloane, 2000). Rather than viewing evaluation as an external intervention, our findings highlight the value of e mbedding assessment within authentic teaching activ ities, supported by intelligent data collection and competency-based frameworks. The proposed four-stage process— clarifying goals, defining standards, mapping evidence, and structuring workflows—offers a practical scaffold for balancing validity, feasibility, and sensitivity in  evaluation des ign. Through this lens, teacher evaluation is reimagined as a dynamic part of professional learning, not a separate bureaucratic task. Looking ahead, future research could explore how su ch frameworks perform across diverse educational settings and how they can be iteratively refined th rough teacher participation. Greater attention is n eeded to the relational and contextual dimensions of evaluation design—ensuring systems not only function technically, but also align with the values, routines, and learning cultures of school communities. Achieving sustainab le, lowinterference evaluation will require not just innov ative tools, but also long-term collaboration among educators, researchers, and institutional leaders.
