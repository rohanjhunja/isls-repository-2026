# Design Principles for Disciplinary Learning Designs with Unplugged Computational Thinking as Pedagogical Means

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper presents design principles for disciplinary learning with unplugged Computational Thinking, informed by embodied cognition, motivation research, and a Design-Based Research study in upper secondary science teaching. The resulting principles provide direction for employing embodied CT in algorithmic modeling of disciplinary processes, emphasizing task integration, bodily engagement, and strategies to reduce confusion.

## Introduction

Since Wing’s assertion in 2006 that Computational Thinking (CT) embodies a “universally applicable attitude and skill set ”, interest in integrating CT into non-computer science disciplines has increased rapidly (Grover, 2022; Weintrop et al., 2016). Despite divergences, the literature generally agrees that CT includes Decomposition, Pattern recognition, Modelling, Sequencing, Algorithm design, Evaluation, and Generalization. Dong et al. (2019) and Peel et al. (2022) suggest emphasizing unplugge d versions of  these skills when integrating CT into non-computer science disciplines. This approach enables  teachers to use CT in classrooms without spending time on coding instruction, directing more time toward disc iplinary learning objectives.  In line with Wing’s universal applicability claim, literature suggests that CT can enhance reasoning and problem-solving in various disciplines. Still, many learning designs aim at student attainment, not only of disciplinary learning objectives, but of the CT skills themselves (e.g., Dong et al., 2019; Peel et al., 2022) This paper presents design principles prioritizing disciplinary subject-matter objectives over CT. We investigated unplugged modelling of disciplinary processes, specifically through embodied enactment and/or  use of  tangible objects. This approach was motivated by the potential of embodied enactment and tangible objects to support cognition (Wilson, 2002), learning (Skulmowski and Rey, 2018), and situational interest (Renninger and Hidi, 2016). The DBR study The design principles were developed in a two-year Design-Based Researc h (DBR) study involv ing four experienced teachers across two schools. It engaged around 200 unique students in a total of 11 biology and chemistry classes. The teachers had full freedom within the constraints set by us: a) Prioritize disciplinary learning and b) Engage students in CT activities where subject-matter is modeled algorithmically (i.e., in step-by-step sequences) with embodiment and/or tangible objects. The teachers developed 11 learning activities. Some classes tried several activities. This allowed students to become familiar with unplugged algorithmic modeling, reducing initial confusion. All learning designs exhibited h igh task integration for disciplinary learning obje ctives but varied in amount of movement. For example, one design involved modeling genetic inheritance with beans, where students only moved to draw beans from a bowl representing genotypical distribution. Another had students enact molecule diffusion, formul ating rules for and enacting molecule movements  in the full classroom. All designs involved decomposition of scientific procedures, step-by-step sequencing, resulting in an algorithmic description, which was modeled by the students in a simulation  and then evaluated, and, in a few cases, generalized using pseudocode, flowcharts, or everyday language. Our focus for the DBR study was student perceptions of learning potentials and motivation, hence we used self-report questionnaires with three open questions that asked students what they found positive and negative about the activity, and whether the activity facilitated their understandin g of the content. Thematic coding was applied to identify positive/negative epistemic and motivational aspects. Across various learning designs, most students found unplugged modeling beneficial for un derstanding and remembering content. Common explanations included the clarity g ained through physical engagement, as expressed in statements such as “I could see it more clearly by having it in my hand”.  A few students unprompted emphasized the significance of algorithmic visualiz ation in embodied models, stating, “Step by step, it makes it easier to observe the change in data and in the population ”. However, for most designs, some students  reported confusion, a lack of support for understanding, or increased difficulty in grasping the content. Inter estingly, no systematic difference emerged in this regard between designs with high and low amount of bodily movement. Student motivational perceptions exhibited some variation across the 11 designs. 9 had student comments characterizing them as “fun,” 7 had comments that the activity was “new” or “different” (known to be a trigger of interest, Renninger and Hidi, 2016), and for 8 designs, students state the physical nature of the task as positive in itself. The statement “It allowed for creativity in an otherwise boring cl ass” capture the essence of many comments. One design received positive remarks from 2/3 of the students. For other activities, however, only 2 or 3 students (out of 15-20) commented positively. Negative comments, citing activities as “boring”, “tedious”, or “long and time-consuming”, were noted for 7 designs, with one receiving negative comments from 2/3 of the students. Plausibly, the divergence can in large part be explained with reference to the specific nature of the task. Resulting tentative design principles Synthesizing our findings with literature on embodied cognition (Wilson, 2002), embodied learning (Skulmowski and Rey, 2018), and situational interest (Renninger  and Hidi, 2016) allows us to propose the following design principles for disciplinary learning with unplugged CT.

1)  Activities should address disciplinary learning objectives, employing CT as a pedagogical means.

i. Subsidiarily addressing CT learning objectives is commendable if it makes disciplinary sense and the time spent on it is assessed to be reasonable from a disciplinary perspective.

2)  Utilize unplugged CT elements to algorithmically model, simulate and visualize the disciplinary

processes. i.  Decompose the disciplinary processes into subprocesses, sequence the subprocesses step-by-step, providing an algorithmic description of the processes as a result. ii.  Let the students “run the simulation” by enacting it, thereby bodily modelling the processes. iii.  Tangible objects can represent elements and traits, allowing visualization of further elements. iv.  Ensure task integration: Each bodily enacted step should be inherently related to (represent/illustrate/simulate) steps in the targeted disciplinary procedure. v.  If deemed disciplinarily relevant, ask students to explicate the algorithmic modelling, for instance in pseudocode or flowcharts, and generalize to other disciplinary processes.

3)  Consider the amount of bodily movement appropriate for modeling targeted disciplinary processes. High

degrees of bodily engagement are fun but may lead to cognitive overload. i.  Regardless of  the amount of bodily movement, discuss with the students the step-by-step simulation and how it relates to textbook descriptions and models.

4)  Include several learning designs with unplugged CT in the curriculum, so students are trained in this

form of representation of disciplinary learning, and in consequence find it less confusing. The first design principle with subclause reiterate s our overarching constraint for the DBR study. The  second design principle with subclauses is an explication of how to use unplugged CT in algorithmic modelling  of disciplinary processes. The third and fourth principles sum up the implications of theory and findings related to bodily movement and mitigating confusion. The principles are tentative, pending future independent testing.
