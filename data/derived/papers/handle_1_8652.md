# Leveraging Student-Written Chapter Summaries to Assess Understanding and Motivation in an Introductory Statistics Course

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Instructors often assign students to write summaries of what they have learned. Beyond being a helpful learning exercise, written summaries can reveal latent models of what students do and don’t understand about the instructional content. Drawing on data from an introductory statistics course, the current study assessed the relationship between written summaries and student understanding and motivation in the course. Findings revealed significant associations which can inform support for struggling learners.

## Introduction

Summary writing is an effective method for students to synthesize knowledge, identify misconceptions, and build a mental model of the learning content (Radmacher & Latosi-Sawin, 1995). While a range of research has demonstrated the benefits of summary-writing as a learning exercise, less research has assessed what the content of summaries reveals about student understanding, and how this information can be leveraged by instructors. A handful of studies have utilized advanced natural-language processing algorithms to analyze the syntactic and semantic quality of summaries (Strobl et al., 2019). However, these tools are primarily designed to help students improve their writing skills, not directly to assess understanding. Additionally, to our knowledge, prior research has not studied what a student’s written summary reveals about their motivation in the course. Frequent and unintrusive indicators of student motivation can be crucial in preventing learner burnout and dropout, particularly in STEM courses where the ‘leaky pipeline’ is especially prevalent (Burke, Mattis & Elgar, 2007). The current study analyzed data from an introductory statistics course to examine the relationship between written summaries and student learning and motivation. These preliminary findings lay the groundwork for interventions that can identify and support struggling learners.

## Methods

Data in the current study was sourced from 166 students who participated in an introductory statistics course taught in the winter of 2021 at a large public university in Los Angeles. The primary instructional tool was an online textbook developed by the course instructors (see Stigler et al., 2020, for more details). The textbook contained twelve chapters, each of which covered a different statistics concept, such as distributions and modeling variation. Prior to beginning each chapter, students self-reported their motivation by answering three questions that probed their expectancy and value for the course. These questions were abbreviated from the expectancy-value-cost scale developed by Kosovich et al., (2015). At the end of each chapter, students wrote a summary of what they learned and took a practice quiz.

For the purposes of this study, the chapter variables we focused on were the self-reported motivation scores, written summaries, and the end of chapter quiz scores. Metrics from this data were calculated for each student and for each chapter. We also incorporate the students’ final exam grade in the analyses. However, due to the COVID-19 pandemic, students were not required to take the final exam, so the sample for these analyses was significantly decreased. Two primary metrics were generated from students’ written chapter summaries: summary length and semantic content overlap. Semantic overlap was calculated using the cosine similarity between the term-frequency, inverse-document-frequency (tf-idf) of the chapter text and the students’ chapter summary. ## Results

The results showed that the length of a chapter summary was positively correlated with chapter quiz score (r(1818) =.17, p <.001), expectancy (r(1649) =.10, p <.001) and value (r(1646) =.08, p =.001). The semantic content similarity between the chapter summary and the chapter content was also associated with quiz score (r(1818) =.16, p <.001), expectancy (r(1649) =.17, p <.001) and value (r(1646) =.10, p <.001). ## Discussion

Our findings revealed that summary length and the semantic similarity between summary and chapter content was positively and significantly correlated with both chapter quiz score and student motivation. The link between summaries and quiz scores reveals that written summaries can be an indicator of student knowledge and how well they learned the material. However, due to the correlational nature of these findings, it is difficult to ascertain the direction of causality. For example, it could be that writing a comprehensive summary that drew on diverse content allowed students to learn the material better and perform better on the chapter quiz, a finding well established in the literature. On the other hand, a student who already had a good grasp of the content would write a better summary and perform better on the quiz. We also found that average semantic similarity of summaries was predictive of final exam scores. However, the association did not hold when controlling for summary length. This suggests that more sophisticated metrics of semantic analysis for summaries are necessary to better differentiate student understanding. One future direction is to use latent semantic analysis or keyword and entity extraction in order to pinpoint the exact concepts that students may or may not understand. Interestingly, our results also revealed a positive correlation between summary metrics and student motivation at the beginning of the chapter. One potential explanation for this finding is that motivated students write higher quality summaries. On the other hand, the act of writing a comprehensive summary could increase student motivation in the course. Again, the direct interpretation is elusive and future research should explore the chain of causality, but the significant correlation suggests that summary metrics may serve as a frequent and unobtrusive method to probe motivation in a course.

## Conclusion

Although course instructors will often ask students to write summaries of what they have learned, these are most often assigned as a useful learning exercise. However, the findings presented demonstrate that instructors can also derive important insights from this data. Notably, it can indicate how much students retained and their current state of motivation in the course. Instructors can apply this information to tailor instruction or to provide support for students who may be struggling with course content or who are feeling unmotivated. Future studies should explore methods that allow instructors to pinpoint exact misunderstandings from summary data and explore techniques to help instructors digest and act upon this data.
