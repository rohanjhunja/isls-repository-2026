# “I See It Less as the Icing on the Cake and More of the Cake Itself”: Educators’ Understandings of Culturally Responsive Pedagogy

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Culturally responsive pedagogy (CRP) directly embeds learners’ cultural, racial, linguistic, gendered, and classed identities into curricula, materials, and teaching practices. While CRP is increasingly recognized for its capacity to support learners’ academic and emotional growth, educators need information and training to sustainably embed CRP into everyday praxis. In response, we developed an eight-session professional development program that connected PreK-12 teachers and instructional coaches with informal learning educators to deepen their understanding of CRP and collaboratively design lessons and programs. This paper presents a preliminary qualitative analysis of participants’ descriptions of CRP principles and practices, along with barriers to implementing CRP, as evidenced in semi-structured pre-post interviews. Our findings contribute to research about culturally responsive teacher preparation by highlighting strengths and gaps in how professional training about CRP is delivered.

## Introduction

Resource pedagogies are a family of pedagogical app roaches that embrace learners’ personal and cultural identities, values, and epistemologies (Paris, 2012). Ladson-Billings (1995) introduced  culturally relevant pedagogy as an approach that  accepts and affirms students’ cultural identities  and build s academic potential, cultural competence, and sociopolitical consciousness. Similarly, Gay (2000) introduced culturally responsive pedagogy as an approach that used “ cultural characteristics, experiences, and perspect ives of ethnically diverse students as conduits for teaching them more effectively.” When introducing culturally sustaining pedagogy, Paris

(2012) proposed moving beyond acceptance  of cultures  to active nurturing of  multicultural and multilingual

identities, practices, and knowledge. Collectively, these pedagogies empower students academically and emotionally by creating meaningful connections to l earning tasks and generating intrinsic motivation t o solve personally relevant  problems, thus promoting positive conceptual and affective outcomes for learners across subject areas, including math and science (Aronson & Laughter, 2016; Esposito, 2012; Sleeter, 2012). However, teachers need information and training to implement resource pedagogies effectively. They may be unaware of their own biases about students’ backgrounds and must interrogate their beliefs, practices, and materials for implicit and explicit bias (Hawley & Nieto, 2010; Nganga, 2015; Walker, 2019). Also, teachers may oversimplify resource pedagogies  in ways that reproduce harm rather than reduce it, such as limiting cultural representation to holiday celebrations (Sleeter, 2012; Walker, 2019). Educators in informal learning environments (ILEs), such as museums and libraries,  also benefit from professional development about resource pedagogies (Bevan & Xanthoudaki, 2008). ILE educators often wo rk with visitors from varied cultural, linguistic, and socioeconomic backgrounds; yet, visitors from non-dominant cultures may sense that ILEs are “not for them” due to limited representation among exhibits, collections, and professional staff (Acev edo & Madara, 2015; Adams, 1992). To address this, ILE educators must build their own awareness of bias in their settings. In response, we began a summer program that brought  together teachers and ILE educators for professional development around culturally responsi ve pedagogy (CRP). We chose “responsive” because of the language of teacher education standards in the American state where the program was held. This paper presents a preliminary qualitative analysis of pre-post interviews with  the teachers and ILE educators  who participated, centered on the following research questions: (RQ1) How do PreK-12 teachers and ILE educators describe principles and practices associated with CRP, both current and aspirational ? and (RQ2) What barriers prevent implementation of CRP in PreK-12 classrooms and ILEs? This paper contributes to the literature about culturally responsive teacher preparation (e.g., Sleeter, 2012) by highlighting which aspects of CRP are readily understood by educators and which aspects need more preparation.

## Methods

Participants included six educators  who attended a professional development program held over Summer 2023 and gave informed consent for research. Table 1 (below) lists demographic information about participants. All had completed  master’s degrees and resided in the U.S. Midwest. Peter was a middle-school reading teacher. Jennifer and Janelle were instructional coaches at a public elementary school. Rachel was an archivist at a public library. Michelle was a manager-educator at a cultural museum. Daniel was a doctoral student, private school educator, and intern at an art museum. The eight-session program was held twice a week over four weeks in JuneJuly 2023, with one in-person and  seven online sessions. The first five sessions focus ed on building rapport, discussing CRP principles and practices, and embedding CRP across subject areas. The next three focused on collaborative lesson/program planning during which participants reflected on expectations for learning in formal and informal settings. The program was led by two facilitators (the first author and a consultant) and a graduate research assistant (the second author). Participants received 16 professional development hours and $350 stipends. This paper focuses on the  pre-post semi-structured interviews pulled from the full data set, which also includes surveys, session recordings, and artifacts. The interview protocol is available at this URL: https://bit.ly/49aCZRG. We analyzed 5 pre-program interviews and 5 post-program interviews that ranged from 15-49 minutes in duration  with a meanduration of 36 minutes. We used informed grounded theory (Thornberg,

2012) to qualitatively analyze interviews using  process and pattern coding (Saldaña, 201 6) to examine evidence

of CRP principles and practices and barriers to implementation. The coding scheme is available at the URL listed above. Participants were also asked to describe barriers to implementing CRP. The three most common barriers were Limitations of self (15 instances); Low energy, time, or drive (13 instances); and Conflicting de mands (9 instances). “Limitations of self” referred to awareness that one’s personal identity, perspectives, and experiences hindered perception of others’ identities, perspectives, and experience s. “Low energy, time, or drive” referred to low levels of resources needed to implement CRP, of ten because of “Conflicting demands” such as assess mentdriven teaching or set curricula. Less  frequent barriers included Structural impediments (9 instances); Wariness of “new” pedagogies (8 instances); Limited support from fellow educators (5 instances) or administrators (4 instances); and Funding (2 instances).

## Discussion and conclusion

Culturally responsive pedagogy (CRP) holds great promise for supporting the academic and emotional growth of learners with diverse personal and cultural backgrounds, experiences, and knowledge (Aronson & Laughter, 2016; Paris, 2012). However, educators need substantial training to embed CRP into everyday praxis  (Nganga, 2015). As such, we investigated how six teachers and ILE educators described CRP principles and practices, along with barriers to implementation, before and after a professional development program. Some strengths emerged within educators’ understanding of current CRP principles and practices: (re)designing curricula to better suit students, acknowledging and respecting cultures, and showing readiness to learn. However, gaps in understanding also emerged. For example, participants expressed aspirations to embed CRP into everyday praxis, yet they already (re)designed curricular materials on a regular basis, indicating that they did not recognize such (re)design  as embedded CRP. Also, “Respecting” occurred more frequently than “Sustaining,” which is not surprising given the history of deficit and difference pedagogies in the U.S. (Paris, 2012). We see a clear need to  help educators recognize how they enact CRP in practice and extend beyond minimal or token  representation. Educators need support with widening their repertoire of  culturally responsive teaching practices (e.g., sto rytelling, call-andresponse) and incorporating learners’ funds of knowledge into meaningful learning experiences. This paper is an initial effort to understand the s trengths and gaps in educators’ understandings of CRP in their everyday praxis. An encouraging insight was that culturally responsi ve principles and practices may be more deeply embedded into formal and informal learn ing experiences than educators realize. We plan to address the limitations of our study (e.g., small sample si ze) by expand ing recruitment efforts and offering multiple formats (e.g., one-week program), along with including more vignettes and case studies of CRP across formal and informal settings. Our aim is to continue helping  educators to recognize their strengths in practicing CRP while acknowledging and challenging barriers to implementation.
