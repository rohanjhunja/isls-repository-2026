# The Creativity Landscape Framework for Learning Design and Analysis

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper advances the understanding of creativity as a complex system through empirical validation of the Creativity Landscape framework, which conceptualizes creativity as emerging from the dynamic interplay of Creative Mindsets, Creative Environment, Creative Processes, and Creative Cognition. Through thematic network analysis of fifty highly-cited creativity research articles, the study provides empirical support for the framework's organization and its complex systems approach. The analysis revealed clusters that align with the framework's categories, demonstrating strong interconnections between elements. The findings validate the framework's emphasis on emergence, non-linearity, self-organization, adaptability, and feedback loops in creative processes. This work contributes to the learning sciences by offering a theoretically grounded and empirically supported tool for understanding and fostering creativity in learning contexts. The complex systems perspective provides new insights into the mechanisms driving creative emergence.

## Introduction

Creativity, a driving force behind human learning, has been extensively studied across diverse disciplines. While early research focused on individual cognitive proc esses, contemporary perspectives recognize creativi ty as an emergent phenomenon arising from complex interactions between individual, social, cultural, and environmental factors (Sawyer, 1999; Glăveanu & Beghetto, 2021). This paper examines creativity through the complex systems lens of the Creativity Landscape framework (Donaldson, 2016, 2017a, 2018, under r eview) and providing empirical support for its utility in understanding and fostering creative processes.

## Theoretical framework

Numerous theoretical approaches have contributed to our understanding of c reativity. J.P. Guilford's (1950) and E. Paul Torrance's (1962) work on divergent thinking established the i mportance of generating multiple, diverse ideas. The investment theory of creativity (Sternberg & Lubart, 1991) proposed that creative people strategically invest in novel ideas with growth potential, requir ing a confluence of intellectual abilities, knowled ge, thinking styles, personality, motivation, and a supportive environment (Stern berg, 2006). Teresa Amabile's (1983) componential theory e mphasized the interplay of domain-relevant skills, creativity-relevant processes, and task motivation, particularly highlighting the role of intrinsic motivation (H ennessey & Amabile, 2010). Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi's (2000) systems model introduced the interaction between individual, domain, and field, laying the groundwork for complex systems approaches. The four C model of creativity (Kaufman & Beghetto, 2009) distinguishes between mini-c (personal), little-c (everyday), Pro-c (professional), and Big-C (eminent) creativity, recognizing the diverse manifestations of creative expression across a spectrum of individual and collective endeavors (Kozbelt, Beghetto & Runco, 2010).  Complex systems theory provides a powerful framewor k for integrating these diverse theoretical perspectives and understanding the emergent, non-linear, and dynamic nature of creativity (Poutanen, 2013; Schuldberg & Guisinger, 2021). This approach argues that creativity arises not simply from the sum of parts in an individual, but from the intricate and often unpredictable interactions between multiple components within a complex dynamic system. Several key principles of complex systems theory are particularly relevant to understanding creativity.

Emergence: Novel and creative outcomes arise from the interacti ons between system components in ways that cannot be predicted solely from the properties of individual elements (Sawyer, 1999; Longo, 2009; Turner & Baker, 2020). This challenges reductionist approaches a nd highlights the synergistic effects of various factors, including individual cognition, social interactions, cultural context, and environmental influences. Non-linearity: Complex systems are characterized by non-linear relationships between inputs and outputs, where small changes in initial conditions or system parameters can lead to disproportionately  large or unexpected effects on creative outcomes (Bar-Yam, 2003; Lambert, 2020; Krippner, 2022). This explains the unpredictability of creative breakthroughs.

Self-organization: This principle describes the spontaneous emergence of order and structure within a complex system without centralized control (Sawyer, 2003; Thurner et al., 2018). In the context of creativity, selforganization is evident in collaborative processes where ideas are generated, combined, and refined through dynamic interactions among individuals.

Adaptability: Complex systems demonstrate the capacity to adapt to changing environmental conditions, a crucial aspect of creative and innovative thinking (Anderson et al., 2004; Desai, 2010). This adaptability allows creative individuals and groups to adjust their approaches, integrate new information, and transfer insights across different domains.

Feedback loops:  Iterative processes of feedback and refinement play a crucial role in shaping the development and evolution of creative ideas (Krakauer, 2024; Gabora, 2017; Amabile et al, 1996). These feedback loops can operate internally within an individual's  cognitive processes, as well as externally through interactio ns with others or the environment.

Researchers have increasingly applied complex systems theory to illuminate various aspects of creativity. Sawyer's (1999, 2003) concept of "collaborative emergence" highlights the synergistic interactions within creative groups. Poutanen (2013) provides a comprehensive overview of complex systems applicati ons in creativity research. Lambert (2020) explores the "order-chaos dynamic" in creativity, suggesting that highly creative individuals navigate the edge of chaos. Gabora's (2017) "honing theory" emphas izes the self-organizing and iterative nature of creative cognition. These advancements underscore the potential of complex systems theory to provide a more nuanced and comprehensive understanding of creative processes. The Creativity Landscape Framework The Creativity Landscape framework (Donaldson, 2016, 2017, 2018, 2020, under review) offers a holistic perspective on creativity, encompassing four interconnected categories essential for creative emergence: Creative Mindsets, Creative Environment, Creative Processes, and Creative Cognition. This framework aligns closely with complex systems theory, conceptualizing creativity not as a static trait but as a dynamic and emergent  property arising from the interplay of these elements.

• Creative Mindsets: This category encompasses individual dispositions a nd attitudes that foster creative

potential, including motivation, curiosity, opennes s to experience, tolerance of ambiguity, risk-taking, resilience, and metacognitive practices. These mind sets empower individuals to embrace challenges, explore new possibilities, and persevere through setbacks.

• Creative Environment: This category emphasizes the importance of a supportive and stimulating context

for nurturing creativity. Key elements include psyc hological safety, lowered inhibition, a generative atmosphere, diversity and inclusion, opportunities for collaboration, playfulness, and physical contex t attributes that promote inspiration and exploration.

• Creative Processes: This category outlines the dynamic and iterative st ages involved in creative work,

including problem identification, frame creation, e xploration and preparation, idea generation, experimentation and improvisation, decision-making, incubation, insight generation, prototyping, and refinement through feedback.

• Creative Cognition: This category encompasses the cognitive processes t hat underpin creative thought,

including divergent thinking, convergent thinking, abductive reasoning, metaphor creation, perspectivetaking, analogical thinking, abstraction, associative thinking, framing, conceptual combination, synthetic thinking, pattern recognition, concretization, and elaboration. The Creativity Landscape framework posits that thes e four categories are not isolated but intricately interconnected, influencing and reinforcing one another in a complex dynamic system. A supportive environment can foster the development of creative mindsets, which in turn enable individuals to engage effectively in creative processes, utilizing their cognitive resources to g enerate novel ideas and solutions. The framework em phasizes the role of feedback loops, where successes and failures within the creative process influence subsequent mindsets, environmental perceptions, and cognitive strategies. It also recog nizes the existence of attractor states – stable patterns of interaction – such as the flow state or creative block. Phase transitions, or shifts between these attractor states, can be triggered by both internal and external factors, leading to dramatic changes in creative output.

## Methodology

To empirically examine the complex systems perspect ive embodied in the Creativity Landscape framework,  a thematic network analysis was conducted. A purposive sample of fifty of the most highly-cited academic journal articles on creativity was assembled, ensuring repr esentation of seminal works while mitigating potent ial author or journal bias  by limiting our collection to only two articles fro m each author. The articles were coded using qualitative content analysis in MAXQDA Analytics Pro, employing both a priori codes based on the framework's elements and open coding for non-metaphorical characterizations, metaphors, and values related to creativity. To uncover the underlying relationships between these co ncepts, a correlation matrix was generated for all possible code pairs at a significance level of p < 0.01. This correlation matrix provided the foundation for network analysis, visualized through network maps and analyzed using Girvan-Newman cluster analysis (Girvan & Newman, 2002).

## Findings

The network analysis provided strong support for th e Creativity Landscape framework’s organization int o four interconnected categories. The Girvan-Newman clustering algorithm revealed 12 distinct clusters within the network map, alongside one isolated node  (see Figure 1). These clusters reflected the grouping of creativi tyrelated concepts in the literature into interconnec ted communities, mirroring the framework's categori zation of Creative Mindsets, Creative Environment, Creative P rocesses, and Creative Cognition. The i nterconnections between clusters further underscored the framework’ s emphasis on the interdependence of these categori es, reinforcing the complex systems view of creativity.

Clusters emphasizing individual characteris tics such as mindfulness, self-efficacy, autonomy, nonconformity, openness, and ri sk-taking strongly corresponded to the Creative Mindsets category. Similarly, the prominent Community-Inclusion cluster (black squares, lower left, 24 nodes), encompassing concepts like diversity, collaboration, and belongi ng, pr ovided robust support for the importance of the Creative Environment category. Fu rthermore, clusters highlighting creative processes  like exploration, idea generation, prototyping, and the development of tangible outcomes aligned directly w ith the Creative Processes category. The Creative Cognition category  also found strong support in the emergent cluster structure. Specifically, the Convergent-Logic cluster (light blue squares, left center, 24 nodes) and the Pr oblemSolving-Innovator cluster (pink box squares, right center, 25 nodes), two of the  largest identified clusters, highlighted the importance of cognitive processes s uch as divergent and convergent thinking, abstracti on, conceptual combination, and pattern recognition. These two large clusters demonstrated significant interconnection, further validating the framework's emphasis on the dynamic interplay between creative processes and cognitive strategies. Other smaller clusters, while not solely dedicated to cognitive a spects, also contributed to the understanding of Creative Cognition, reflect ing the interconnected nature of these elements.  Importantly, the network map’s structure, characterized by interconnected clusters and the absence of a strict hierarchical order, reinforced the framework's conceptualization of creativity as a complex and emergent phenomenon. The analysis highlighted the non-linear and iterative nature of creativity, demonstrating that it arises from the dynamic interplay of multiple factors rather than a simple, step-by-step process.

## Implications and conclusion

The Creativity Landscape framework, supported by em pirical evidence, offers a valuable tool for unders tanding and fostering creativity across diverse learning contexts. Its complex systems approach provides a nuanced perspective that moves beyond traditional linear and individualistic models of creativity, aligning wi th contemporary understandings of its emergent and multifaceted nature (S awyer, 1999; Glăveanu &  Beghetto, 2021). The framework's emphasis on the dynamic interplay of Creative Mindsets, Creative Environment, Creative Processes, and Creative Cognition underscores the i nterconnectedness of these elements in shaping crea tive potential. Unlike earlier ap proaches that primarily focused on individual cognitive processes (Guil ford, 1950; Torrance, 1962), the Creativity Landscape framework embraces a holi stic perspective, emphasizing the synergistic effects of individual, social, and environmental factors (Poutanen, 2013). The framework's practical applications span various  domains. In learning contexts, it can guide the design of learning environments that nurture studen t creativity by systematically addressing all four categories. This contrasts with traditional assessment approaches which predominately focus on individual creative potential or products (Guilford, 1950; Torrance, 1962). Instead, the framework enables e ducators to diagnose the creative potential of the context (e.g., classroom) and strategically sele ct interventions that foster creativity at the system level. In organizational settings, the framework can inform the development of innovat ion learning ecosystems, promoting employee creativity by cultivating supportive environments and fostering creative mindsets (Amabile, 1983). Moreover, individuals can leverage the framework to enhance thei r own learning through increasing their creative capacity: strategically developing specific mindsets, seeking  out enriching environments, and engaging in effective creative processes (Sternberg & Lubart, 1991). The framework's emphasis on emergence, non-linearity, self-organization, adaptability, and feedback loops (Krakauer, 2024; Thurner et al., 2018) provides a deeper understa nding of the mechanisms that drive creative processes. These principles challenge traditional linear model s of creativity (Lambert, 2020) and encourage a more dynamic and holistic approach. Future research should focus on further refining the framework, developing more precise analytical tools, exploring  cross-cultural applications (Glăveanu, 2013), and investigating the specific mechanisms through which  elements within and acro ss categories interact to produce emergent creative outcomes. This research could exp lore questions such as: How do specific environment al factors influence the development of particular cre ative mindsets? How do different cognitive processe s interact to support various stages of the creative process? And how can feedback loops be leveraged to amplify crea tive potential within complex systems? By addressing the se questions, future research can further enhance t he framework's explanatory power and contribute to a more comprehensive understanding of the complex dynamics that underpin human creativity.
