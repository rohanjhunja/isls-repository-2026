# Transformation of Habits through the Aesthetics of Agency

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper explores the affective relationships between learning and games, leveraging Thi Nguyen’s insights on the aesthetic capacity of games and Michalinos Zembylas’ pedagogical framework for habits of social transformation. Focusing on a close reading of the game This War of Mine, the analysis reveals how game-designed agency can provide valuable lessons for educational practices. Employing a material semiotics approach, the study emphasizes the interconnectedness of human and non-human elements in shaping habits. Three actionable recommendations for education researchers emerge: prioritize striving over achievement tasks, focus on the inner agency in learners, and the acceptance that habits are not designed. This paper describes and reiterates that only through thoughtfully designed environments can new agency emerge and potentially transformative habits along with it.

Learning and games Two contemporary theorists, prominent in their respective fields, Thi Nguyen in game studies and Michalinos Zembylas in educational theory, have intriguingly s imilar perspectives on  their notions of experience and the environment in which experience  takes place. Environments encompass both the material and social and take place in the past, present, and future. Environments can also be considered from the perspective of individuals, in their relationships with social groups, non-human objects and beings, and the individuals emotional responses to these relationships. Zembylas (2021) emphasizes the importan ce of this perspective o n environment when he frames the formation of habits as “implicated in the environment, the conditions of the environment have to change if there is going to be any transformation of habit s” (p. 775). Habits are the actions or thinking of actions  that emerge from agency that an environment evokes. For Nguyen  (2020), games (encompassing sports, tabletop, video and social) are pieces of art because of their aesthetic capacity to engage players in diffe rent agencies. He argues that a bookshelf full of games is equivalent to a library of agencies to try on, and the emotions experienced while playing a game allows us to pass aesthetic ju dgment on the game as art.  Within the study of games for learning, Elizabeth Gee and James Paul Gee (2017) describe the nature of humans in their environment nicely when they say, “Humans can imagine what might happen before they ac t. They can role-play other people and even other elements of their environment to imagine  the consequences of their actions” (p. 8). Their work aligns well with what I present here, in how video games can “teach us things about the real world, about language and learning, and about ourselves and our minds ” (p. 10). However, this paper extends their work to consider how Nguyen’s perspective on agency can support educational researchers in studying the transformation of habits  in any environment, supported by holistic methods  like multimodal interaction analysis. The application of such methods is outside the scope of this paper.

Having students see the world from different perspectives is the goal of educational theorist Michalinos Zembylas. He emphasizes the importance of cultivating democra tic habits considering current “top-down accountability measures” in education. Democratic habits encourage students to work together, share ideas, and take ownership of their learning. Both Zembylas and Nguyen argue for the importance of emotional affect in experiences to drive valuable personal growth, and both see this growth as arising out of “mind-body-environment assemblages ” (Bennett et al., 2013). Bennett et al. articulate how environments support the pedagogy of transforming individuals as a network of interacting human and non-human actants. Both classrooms and games happen in such a network, however game designers explicitly consider the agency a player has within a game.  Similarly, proponents of democratic education empower teachers to take similar considerations into their educational environments.

Using Nguyen’s work, I conduct a close reading of the game: This War of Mine (11 bit studios, 2022), which is being used to position players as civilians caught in the challenge of surviving war. The game world environment created by its designers is carefully crafted to bring about a desired agency. Karol Zajeczkowski, a creator of the game defines this agency by saying, “in this unfriendly reality, you have to make tough d ecisions that deeply affect the lives of the people you meet”(11 bit studios, 2014). The agency is designed provides an excellent model for Nguyen’s theory, and is also used in Polish education as a n “educational material for the core curriculum ” (This War of Mine-Games in Education, n.d.). It is being used in high school humanities courses. While games provide excellent spaces for exploring designed agency, I am interested in offering helpful prompts for designing agency in schools, from students engaging in classroom discussions to field trips to local municipal facilities.

## Analytic approach

My analysis needs to treat individuals as actants in a  broader web of human  relations and non-human actors to disentangle habit s from environments. Material semiotics is a reframing of actor-network theory that aims to unwrap webs of actants by investigating processes and practices (Law, 2019). Law argues the material semiotics approach can only be worked through cases; here, I use the close reading of a game to do this. Like Law’s material semiotics, Bennett et al. call for theorists to revisit habit s to uncover “body-society relationships” rooted in nonhuman agents. They argue: The processes through which habits are formed and r e-formed have to take account of the imbrication of human conduct within socio-material environments in which the agency of deceptively simple things, like schoolroom desks, c omplex infrastructures, like l ogistical systems, and regional ecologies have to be taken into account. (Bennett et al., 2013) Unfortunately, no model of learning effectively accounts for evaluating environmental affordances. Experiential learning, embodied learning, and situated learning all place emphasis on the phenomenal experience of the learner. Instead, by placing the environment at the center o f the study and separating any need for the mind ’s abstract understanding of an environment, I distinguish the role of the affective body. Zambyla s takes a comprehensive perspective on affect as it relates to environments, claiming that it “ranges from individual expressions of feeling to sociopolitical constructions of emotions that produce changes in what our bodies cando in connection to other bodies” (p. 769). Similarly, Nguyen (2020) emphasizes the role of environment in designing for agency when he says “Goals, abilities, and environment: these are the means by which game designers practice their art. And we experience the game designer’s art by flexing our own agency to fit”  (p. 1). Both the “aesthetic of agency” and “affective habits” place considerable emphasis on an individuals  para-linguistic interpretation of environments. As a result, a learning model that backs the material semiotic analysis of experience s needs to separate the body in an environment from the human inclination for linguistic abstraction. My experience in the game world of This War of Mine  entangled my existing knowledge and the desired orientations the  designer inscribed in the game’s characters and objects. Nguyen  (2020) proposes two forms of gameplay, striving and achievement, which are driven by how the player embodies the goals presented. In the case of This War of Mine, the goal is to survive until a ceasefire is declared in the civil war. An achievement player seeks the means to achieve the goal, while the striving player considers the goal as a “disposable ends” that allows for an aesthetic experience through the agency the game presents. The abstracted understanding of the goal offers an environment for the player where the represent ations in the game are oriented toward habits diffe rent from everyday life. Many of the objects in the game, such as food, medicine, or a lock pick, are repre sented by icons. The cognitive and embodied knowledge of those repres entations is in no way simple. These game objects call on the player’s understanding and orientation of hunger, illness, and property to orient players toward various habits. At these early stages of gameplay,  the “habit assemblages ” emerge, in Zembylas ’ terms, and t hrough striving play, the player is free to engage with the environment i n meaningful ways.  For Nguyen, a meaningful experience creates aesthetic emotions through striv ing play. For Zembylas, a meaningful experience all ows for the emergence of transformative habits.

How can environments be designed for meaningful exp erience? Nguyen (2020) continues by arguing that for striving play to happen, players need to create an inner layer of agency as part of and not separate f rom their broader everyday agency. My experience with a layer ed agency in the game was apparent when my  game home was raided at night. These raids increased the scarcity of food and medicine and made me question my priorities. Was it better to risk going hungry for a more secur e home? Should I have been more aggressive when vis iting other locations? An interview with a Polish high school teacher who was using the game in her c lassroom made these questions a key point of reflection in her classroom, saying that students “wondered what it means to win in a game like this. Does winning mean surviving? Does it make sense to survive at all costs?” (11 bit studios, 2022). The experience students had emphasize Ahmed’s (2006) notion that bodies form our environment, and environments form our bodies. She eloquently notes that “bodies are directed in some ways and not others, as  a way of inhabiting or dwelling in the world” (p. 27). Game designers don ’t want to restrict players to specific action s; instead, they create an environment where players must make their own choices. Designers of This War of Mine could have made information about the food mechanics more explicit with an instruction manual or even by prescribing where and when players should engage with food. Nor candesigners predict the knowledge the players bring with them. One player may have wa tched an online video of the game that teaches the player the inner workings of the food mechanic. In contrast, a different player may not be familiar with video game mechanic s, causing them to struggle with why the food icons ar e important. Nguyen (2020) calls this aspect of games “agential distance,” a very familiar concept to constructivist teachers who promote student-led learning. While game designers aim to balance the creativity enabled by layered agency with the challenges the player will face, ed ucators often work to reduce agential distance thro ugh clear learning goals and explicit expectations for assessment. The notion of layered agency demands a level of freedom in environments that are outside the designer’s control.

## Conclusion

Through analyzing these texts, three pertinent calls to action can be  raised  for educational researchers  when creating and evaluating environments that aim to transform habits. The first call to action is to prioritize strivin g play over achievement play. This requires that lear ning outcomes are fuzzy and that the products of ex periences are, in some way, disposable ends that encourage striving play. This can be a challenge for the evaluation of these experiences. However, continued work in multimodal methods may support observing habits in action. Second, educators need to make a considerable effort to hel p learners set aside their linguistic, classroom-learner agency and focus on the desired inner agency of the experience  for para-linguistic learning. Educators should consider how these experiences can be a reprieve from a student’s achievement-oriented life. Third, to account for the agential distance between designer and learner, it is vital that educators not aim for a specific habit to form but design the goals and objects in the environment tha t work to reorient learners towards that habit.  Games will continue to be a helpful model for novel approaches to the art of ag ency. The same challenges game designers face can be applied to the goals of learning experi ence designers  and inform how researchers evaluate these experiences.
