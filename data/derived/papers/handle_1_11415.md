# Perspective and Context Bids in Middle School Sociotechnical Discussions

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
We explore how sixth-graders engage in a sociotechnical whole-class discussion about the unequal impacts of technology and engineering on their own communities. In particular, we focus on how a group of students and one teacher try to make sense of the question: why are lower-income areas and more diverse areas more exposed to higher temperatures in their city? Through discourse analysis we identify perspective and context bids that help maintain complexity and build a systems-level understanding of the existing inequity.

## Introduction

There are increasing calls to improve engineering s tudents’ critical sociotechnical literacy in engine ering (McGowan & Bell, 2020). These calls emphasize the n eed for students to develop a systems-level perspective in engineering, where they consider diffe rent historical, social, political and economic asp ects during their design process. These include understanding how engineerin g design choices might cause, perpetuate, and exace rbate systems of inequity. A critical sociotechnical lens  can involve pushing back against taken-for-granted ideas in society that may serve to protect the status quo  (Philips, 2011). However, it is unclear how students might push back on these taken-for-granted ideas while engaging in sociotechnical discussions. In this paper, we explore how a group of sixth-grade students and one teacher collectively wrestle  with the inequitable imp acts of technology and engineering in their city, Pepperville (pseudon ym). In particular, we analyze one whole class disc ussion to identify perspective and context bids that helped s tudents adopt a systems-level understanding during a community-centered climate change curriculum unit.

## Conceptual framework

Grounded in the view that learning is accomplished through social interactions in the moment (Jordan & Henderson, 1995) we focus on the moment-by-moment reasoning of students. Within the context of  learning in sociotechnical discussions we conceptualize tw o types of micro-interactional moves that students make: perspective bids and context bids. Perspective bids involve including and considering the experiences of different groups of people, and context bids involve consider ations of technology and design in specific locatio ns and situations. We argue that the inclusion of differen t perspectives and contexts can help to maintain co mplexity (McGowan & Bell, 2020) which is necessary for expan sive thinking (i.e., can support learners in adopti ng a human-centered systems-level understanding of technology and engineering (Radoff et al., 2022)). We focus  on perspective and context bids as prior research has suggested that perspective-taking (Gupta et al., 2016) and contextualization (Kleine et al., 2021) can support  more empathetic, justice-centered stances in engineering students.

## Methods

This study is part of a larger project to design and study a community-centered climate tech curriculum unit for sixth grade students. In the 20-lesson unit, students explore the impacts of climate chang e and the effectiveness of different climate technologies on/for their comm unity, while designing video-journalistic pieces on climate science and technology. In this work, we focus on d ay three of the unit, where students explored GIS m aps of urban heat islands in their city. First, they looked at potential physical causes of urban h eat islands (varying degrees of tree coverage, surface permeability, and  road density) and then compared the locations of ur ban heat islands in their community with income and diversit y maps. We analyze a 28-minute segment of a whole class discussion between 16 students and a teacher (pseud onym Mr. S), during which time they engage in reas oning about why urban heat islands in their city, Pepperv ille, correlate with low-income and more diverse areas. Prior to this segment they discussed how low permeability, high density of roads, less tree cover are features of urban heat islands. They also discussed how living in urban heat islands can be harmful. We use discourse analysis (Gee, 2014) to explore how perspective and context bids pushed the collective discourse towards a more systems-level understanding. To identify perspective bids, we looked for markers such as “they”, “I”, “my” and descriptors for categorizi ng groups of people, such as “low-income people” or “people who are renting”; for context bids, we focused on mentions of descriptors of places, such as “Apple Hill” or “my street” (all place names are pse udonyms). While identifying these bids, we paid att ention to the underlying arguments to explore how the bids increased empathy and added complexity to the group’s reasoning, i.e., Did it raise more questions? Did it reinforce an idea? Did it add more resources or breadcrumbs?

Through their context and perspective bids, the stu dents and teacher explored the causes of why low-income families are more disproportionately exposed to urban heat islands. I n particular, they explored these causes from the perspective of low-income families, high-income families, and from their personal perspectiv es of people they know and their own neighborhoods. With all these different perspectives, they positioned families at times as making choices and at other times as not being able to afford. Their arguments ranged from how lowincome families’ use of cheaper technology contribu ted to urban heat islands, to how the designed envi ronment contributed to urban heat islands (due to high dens ity and more impermeable surfaces) leading people t oneed more air-conditioning technology. The perspective bids to re late to one’s personal experiences took a more empathic stance on why low-income people might need to use air conditioning; they deemphasized the argument that low-income families’ choice to use air conditioning is a cause of the problem. At the same time, they emphasized how this technology is also a mitigation  technology and not just a contributor to urban hea t islands. This added complexity allowed the group to adopt a systems-level understanding of why low-income people in urban heat islands are affected more: the cause of the unequal impact is not just the use of cheaper technology but also the design of the built environment, including increased impermeable surfaces to meet the needs of people in high density areas.

## Discussion

Student engagement with complexity inherent in sociotechnical systems helps them adopt more critical stances with regards to technology and engineering that inc lude considerations of the historical, political, social and economic (Gunckel & Tolbert, 2018). These considerations are especially important as often engineering problems are reduced to include only technical aspects (Gunckel & Tolbert, 2018; Cech, 2014). Such apolitical and ahistorical framings can prevent students from critically critiquing techn ology and adopting more justice centered framings. This research highlights how context and perspective bids can help maintain comp lexity and encourage expansive thinking (Radoff et al., 2022). In this case, the group engaged in expansive thinking as they focused their collective attention towards how the systems of inequity limited choices available to certain communities rather than place sole responsibility for the unequal impacts of climate change on individuals. They adopted a human-centered systems-level perspective as they made connections between ho w the built environment placed an unequal burden on low-income and diverse communities. Furthermore, this research also showcases the rich community insights that middle s chool students are able to draw on when engaging in sociotechnical discussions that are rooted in local community contexts. In this community-centered lesson, the group drew on their own commun ity insights about Pepperville and made multiple bids that added complexity to the ir understanding of why there was a correlation bet ween urban heat islands and income and diversity. The pe rspective bids made by the group not only included categorizations of specific groups of people (e.g. low-income people) but also drew comparisons between groups of people (e g. people who rent or own). These comp arative perspectives encouraged empathy and highlig hted inequity and undue burden on certain groups of peop le. For instance, when students compared people who  can and cannot afford air conditioning technology, they pushed back against common narratives that hold low-income people as individually responsible for their own we lfare (for example, people can make a choice to buy  better technology). This allowed them to humanize the people impacted and adopt a systems-level understanding of the complex relations between the built environment and  communi ties of people. Furthermore, the students also attended to their own positionings when considering  the perspectives of different groups of people in their community. Such nuances in understanding how one’s experiences  might be different from others, is critical to building allyship within critical work. Context bids also increased complexity as they related different features of specific locations and included in the discussion h istorical trends in the housing market. These conte xt bids sometimes affirmed the group’s reasoning (e.g. connections between condos that have a higher density of people and m ore impermeable surfaces) but also sometimes pushed  back against narr ow framings that emphasized individual choice in choosing where to live over critiques of an inequitable housing system. Previous research highlights the ways in which stud ents and teachers co-construct ideas and roles that can constrain students’ sociotechnical sense-making (Gupta et al., 2017). We posit that a lens o f context and perspective bids can provide valuable insight for f acilitating expansive whole-class sociotechnical discussions that maintain complexity (Wendell et al., 2024).
