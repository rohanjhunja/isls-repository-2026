# Seeing in 360°: Practical and Theoretical Insights for Video Recording in Learning Sciences Research

**Conference:** ISLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This full-day workshop addresses the theoretical and practical challenges of using 360° video and multi-camera systems in qualitative research. Drawing on a multi-year research– practice partnership in secondary classrooms, we share insights for implementing a range of recording technologies. We will address theoretical issues, including how each configuration shapes what becomes visible, invisible, and analytically possible. We will also address practical challenges researchers encounter when working with complex video data, including: managing massive file sizes, synchronizing multiple videos, and establishing data storage and processing workflows. Through hands-on activities, attendees will collect, process, and analyze their own 360° footage and practice troubleshooting common pitfalls. We invite researchers at all levels to consider how 360° video and multi-camera set-ups can support responsible, rigorous, and theoretically informed inquiry. Workshop overview This full-day workshop is intended for researchers, including graduate students and emerging scholars, who are interested in using 360° video in their research projects. The workshop organizers have engaged in a multi-year research project using a diverse array of video data collection methodologies, including 360° cameras (e.g., GoPro MAX), standard cameras, and synchronized multi-camera setups. This extensive practical experience has yielded valuable methodological insights, which we are eager to share with the learning sciences community. Workshop goals and participants This workshop is designed for learning scientists who collect, analyze, or theorize with video data, including those who are interested in multi-modal analysis, emerging recording technologies, representations of practice, and ethical considerations surrounding visibility and video-based research. No prior experience with 360 video is required, and we welcome participants at any career stage. The goals of our session are twofold: First, participants will consider the theoretical and analytic affordances of various video recording set-ups, including what is rendered visible and invisible with their methodological choices, and how those choices influence their analysis. It is tempting to think that collecting more data or richer data is always better; that is, that multi-camera set-ups are better than single-camera, or that more immersive 360° videos are preferable to wide-angle views. However, we argue that this is not necessarily so: even the best video recording renders some aspects of an event invisible and unrecoverable, and the technical difficulties of wrangling large data corpora may render some analyses impractical. As researchers, it is important to thoughtfully weigh these choices. In this session, we will engage learning sciences colleagues in deeper theorizing around these issues. Second, we will share practical knowledge and insight into how to use complex video recording set-ups. Across two research-practice partnerships — Project SIGMa (Supporting Instructional Growth in Mathematics; Horn & Garner, 2022) and Project TAU (Teaching Amidst Uncertainty) — we have spent extensive time developing and troubleshooting various video recording set-ups. Using multi-camera set-ups, especially with 360° video, presents many nontrivial challenges: synchronizing multiple cameras, managing different files and file types, as well as processing and storing large files. Building on the theoretical conversation, participants will gain practical knowledge and skills to use 360° cameras and mutli-camera set-ups — including knowledge of equipment (cameras, but also accessories such as SD cards and cables), software, data compression, and data storage requirements. We have refined and synthesized our data management processes to use with our large, multi-site research team, and we want to share this hard-won knowledge with others in the field. Theoretical background We resurrect Hall’s (2000) question around video recording teaching and learning activities: “How should we expect new technical media for recording human action, along with a growing suite of tools for their analysis, to shape the specific cultural practices of cognitive and educational researchers?” (p. 649). As video recording technology has become increasingly sophisticated and complex in the quarter-century since Hall posed this question, it is vital to consider how such technology shapes our work as researchers and learning scientists. Moreover, Hall argues, video recording is not a neutral practice, and our choices for creating video records are theory-laden. In this workshop, we will consider a) how video both captures and (re)organizes activity and b) how video shapes analytic practices. We examine this through our use of 360° video and multi-camera set-ups. How does mutli-camera 360° video (re)organize activity? Video recordings construct a particular version of an event, where decisions — around what to record and from where — frame the activity of interest in a particular way. Each camera has a fixed field of view, inevitably deleting some elements of the activity entirely; multiple cameras can offer more views, but cannot fully capture everything. Even within what is captured, some elements become foregrounded while anything at the periphery will be backgrounded. What remains in the video record then stands in for the “real” activity that can be studied, replayed, analyzed, and shared. The decisions behind what we are able to capture reflect and embed theoretical and anticipatory assumptions into the video record. These orientations then shape what becomes visible, emphasized, and relevant in any subsequent study of the interaction. When we make decisions about what to capture and what to foreground in the recording, we necessarily change what the event is. This is true for any video record, but it’s important to consider how these implications might be different for 360° video. Although 360° cameras expand the field of view, elements of activity will still be deleted, as they fall outside the audio range or line of sight. While background activity is generally visible, 360° cameras privilege what is physically closer to the camera. In other words, foregrounding is no longer a matter for framing, but of how the placement of the camera affords a particular, spatially organized perspective. How does multi-camera 360° video shape viewing and analysis? Video offers a durable record that can be replayed and edited. This allows us to rebuild fine-grained interactional detail of events, often to a level of detail that exceeds what can be captured using fieldnotes or audio recordings. While multi-camera and 360° recordings expand what is captured, they still present particular points of view. Across different cameras — or, by panning and zooming in a single 360 video — viewers follow different viewing paths can create different senses of “what happened,” producing multiple possible versions of the event. Multi-camera and 360° video can restore some dimensions of the recorded event, affording different interpretive trajectories. Yet, these trajectories are still constrained within the original, recorded theoretical frame. With any video record, what we select for analysis is theoretically and culturally structured (Derry et al., 2010). Multi-camera and 360° recordings change what counts as “selectable” because analysts choose spatial vantage points as well as temporal moments. For example, with 360° video, analysts candecide what becomes “in frame” after the fact, reshaping what becomes visible and analyzable. With a more complex record, the ways we navigate it — how we choose where to look or move throughout the record — require greater reflexivity and transparency about how we structure our analyses and the theoretical assumptions underlie those choices. Ethical considerations The use of video warrants explicit attention to ethics. Immersive and panoramic recordings expand what can be seen, potentially capturing learners who did not anticipate being recorded or activity not visible to teachers or researchers in real time. In an increasingly surveillance-oriented world, these expanded capacities raise questions about consent, circulation, data storage, and the right to opacity. Following Derry et al. (2010), we emphasize the need for transparency and care in decisions about what is captured, who views it, and how these records are used. In this workshop, we will explicitly address issues of visibility, vulnerability, and equity, and invite participants to consider how immersive media can support respectful and responsible research practices. Practical issues for video data collection In addition to discussing theoretical issues, we aim to equip participants with practical knowledge and skills to use complex video recording protocols in their research. As with any technology, there is a learning curve to using n ew cameras and data collection protocols. However, we have found that using 360 cameras, especially in a multi-camera set-up, has presented substantial challenges. Our development of these video methods stems from our own research-practice partnership projects (RPPs), in which we collaborate with secondary mathematics teachers to support their reflection and learning. In this work, we use video recordings to document classroom activity, using them as fodder for both teacher reflection and research analysis. These dual purposes have required us to find ways to make the videos usable and legible for multiple audiences. And while our data collection happens in classrooms, we argue that our processes are of interest to the broader learning sciences community — that is, the challenges we encountered and the solutions we have found are common across many learning environments. In our RPP work, our teachers frequently use groupwork as a key part of their pedagogical practice. We conjectured that a typical single-camera set-up would be insufficient for our project, as we would need to choose between framing a whole class or one specific group. In early iterations of our RPP work (Horn & Garner, 2022), we addressed this concern by using the Swivl platform. The Swivl captures one video stream with up to five separate audio tracks (one teacher and four student groups). This allowed us to select a single framing for the video (i.e., a whole-class, wide-angle view), but with more detailed audio data on student collaboration. However, the Swivl had serious limitations: Students were only recognizable if they spoke out loud. Students with similar voices were difficult to differentiate. And, of course, many aspects of interaction (e.g., gesture) were simply not available in the video record. However, with each technological innovation, we encountered new challenges. For the next iteration of our RPP work, we looked for platforms that would allow us to capture video recordings of multiple student groups. During our pilot work, we tried a multi-camera version of Swivl. Similar to the single-video, multiple-audio set-up, the multi-camera Swivl would enable us to easily synchronize multiple video streams. However, the platform requires strong and stable wifi; this is not always available in research settings, particularly in schools. We ultimately developed our own data collection protocol, consisting of: one whole-class camera (with audio from the teacher), a point-of-view camera worn by the teacher, and three 360 cameras at student groups. This multi-camera set-up generally captures the rich record of classroom interaction that we were seeking. With 360 video, individual students are visible and audible — we can more readily tell who is speaking and how often. Students’ body language and gestures are clear, allowing us to attend to nonverbal interactions. We can also better see what students have in front of them — their worksheets, whiteboards, and manipulatives. Yet the complexity of our data collection protocol presented additional challenges, requiring troubleshooting and innovation. In addition to common issues (e.g., battery life, file size), we needed to address:

1. Video joining: GoPro cameras record long sessions in smaller clips — about 15 minutes for a wide-angle

view, and about 8 minutes for a 360 view. It is impractical to view a lesson in these small pieces. To address this, we developed a Mathematica program that joins the clips together.

2. Video syncing: With our multi-camera set-up, it was crucial to develop a way to synchronize the videos

within a single lesson. To address this, we developed a second Mathematica program that allows us to synchronize and trim the videos automatically.

3. Data transfer and uploading: We needed ways to process, store and share our video data. The large files

(up to 60 GB on a single camera) are nontrivial to move (e.g., to a hard drive or the cloud). We identified the equipment that impacts transfer and upload speeds to expedite the process.

4. Data management: Coordinating and managing many data files — especially with multi-camera set-ups

and multiple data collection events across a large team of researchers — is challenging. We developed strategies for organizing, storing, and parsing our large data corpus. In this workshop, we plan to share this practical knowledge to support other researchers in using similar equipment. Participants will receive documentation (e.g., workflows) as well as hands-on experience. O utline of planned activities In this full-day workshop, we plan to spend the morning session discussing the theoretical aspects of video data collection, including comparisons of the practical and analytic affordances of various methods. In the afternoon, we will address the practical considerations and share the Mathematica programs that we developed. In the morning, we will begin with introductions — of both presenters and participants — and framing of the workshop goals. During this time, we will elicit the participants’ contexts and experiences with video, allowing us to build on their knowledge and expertise. Then we will introduce the theory underlying the workshop, including key concepts from Hall (2000) and Derry et al. (2010); we will center this discussion on how 360 video reorganizes researchers’ recording, representation, and viewing practices. Next, we will have invited speakers present how they use 360 video in their own work — aiming for 34 brief presentations from different projects (including ours), illustrating the use of 360 video and multi-camera set-ups across classroom and out-of-school learning contexts. We will facilitate a discussion with speakers and participants to share thinking across diverse analytic goals and methodological choices. To close out the morning portion of our workshop, we will offer a comparative video demonstration. Participants will view parallel video recordings of the same event using different media, including: whole-class video with targeted audio, standard wide-angle view, and a 360 view. After viewing the same event through different recording methods, we will facilitate a discussion of the affordances and constraints of each method. In the afternoon session, we will begin by leading participants in creating 360 video data to work with. In small groups, participants will engage in a brief collaborative activity and record it. Before beginning the activity, we will facilitate groups’ discussion of how to record the activity — each group candecide how to arrange the camera, themselves, and their materials (drawing on the theoretical discussion from the morning). After recording their own engagement in the collaborative activity, groups will learn how to process the video data they collected — including joining and synchronizing the files using our Mathematica programs. Groups will analyze their video data, with an emphasis on understanding how their data collection choices impact what is available in the video record. Then we will facilitate a discussion to share insights across the groups. To close the workshop, we will prompt participants to reflect on the take-aways for their research.
