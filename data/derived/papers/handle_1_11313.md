# High School Teachers’ Approaches for Integrating AI Literacy into Planned Instruction: Uniformity or Heterogeneity?

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
While various frameworks exist to promote K-12 AI education, little is known about how teachers bring AI literacy into their classrooms. In this study, we examined how two teachers designed and implemented AI literacy in their individual classes. Using epistemic network analysis (ENA) to analyze co-design records and interviews, we found that both teachers shared a common goal of enhancing student learning with AI but diverged in how they emphasized foundational and technical understanding of AI versus responsible and practical use. These varied approaches highlight the complexity of AI literacy and illustrate that a one-size-fits-all model does not apply. By examining these multiple pathways, we offer insight into how educators can tailor AI literacy instruction to meet diverse student needs and instructional contexts.

## Introduction

AI literacy as a term is being used  broadly to refer to the AI concepts and skills the general populace should understand to engage effectively with AI and make i nformed decisions. The emphasis in existing framewo rks is on students. For instance, Long and Magerko (2020) emphasize the  importance of teaching students what AI is, how it uses data to make decisions, and the potenti al biases in AI algorithms. Touretzky et al. (2019)  similarly argue for the need to integrate AI education across  grade levels, stressing the importance of teaching  students about how AI learns from data and interacts with it s environment. However, there are important ways in  which we must consider teachers and their understandings and approaches to AI literacy, as what they plan for and enact in the classroom will ultimately shape what AI lite racy develops. Educators have to navigate complex dec isions about which aspects of AI to include and how to do so effectively and developmentally. For instance, consider K12 teachers who are in situations where they need to think abou t how to integrate AI into existing subject matter curricula rather than teaching AI as a standalone topic. If t hey follow the guidance of frameworks named above, they will need to consider the kinds of AI systems (e.g., generative text tools, computer vision) and the kinds of activities (e.g., gaining new insight,  composing new artifacts) that align to their subject area learning goals. Moreover, they may be coming from very different pl aces with respect to their own background knowledge  and their own perspectives about what concepts or skill s should be more or less prioritized. Can we assume  that AI literacy, as viewed and structured by teachers will have uniformity, or should we expect noteworthy differences? Thus, for our current study, we draw from records obtained in an extended prof essional co-design experience to answer the following research question: To what extent do teachers prioritize different aspects of AI literacy when integrating AI into their curriculum?

Related literature and research perspective K-12 AI literacy Over the past few years, several frameworks have em erged to promote “AI literacy” in K –12 education. Once confined to university-level computer science, AI literacy has evolved to embrace interdisciplinary approaches (Ng et al., 2023), reflecting AI’s growing influenc e in daily life and a cross disciplines. Early frameworks emphasized technical concepts—such as automation, intelligent agents, search, and machine learning (Kandlhofer et al., 2016). Touretzky et al. (2019) proposed five “Big Ideas” to help students understand how AI works and its societal impacts. More recent frameworks (Long & Ma gerko, 2020; Mills et al., 2024) highlight ethical considerations, social consequences, and everyday uses of AI. Despite these many guidelines on what to teach about AI in K-12 education, less attention has been given to how AI literacy is actually translated into clas sroom practice—particularly in terms of what teachers teach about AI and how they implement AI liter acy in their lessons. We view teachers as designers  of instruction, whether it is through the creation of their own tea ching materials or through strategic modifications of ones that have been provided for them (Brown & Edelson, 2003). Given that, we believe there is much to learn abo ut learning experiences and design from examining how teachers engage in any form of this design work, especially for the topic of AI.

Educational co-design: Artifact creation and research activity Our study adopts a research co-design approach that involves collaboratively desig ning and developing educational innovations with teachers to address an educational need (Roschelle et al., 2006). Co-design combines the lived experiences of teachers with the professional expertise of researchers and is considered a mechanism for supporting teachers to develop their own ideas, kno wledge and skills related to addressing a problem (Potvin et al., 2023). Roschelle and colleagues argued that educational co-design typically aims to develop tangible resources that enhance learning while considering critical cl assroom contexts such as students' characteristics and community values. Moreover, engaging teachers in co-design allows researchers to develop practical insi ghts about the viability of educational innovations and leads to professional development for teachers. While co-design may have had artifact creation as a core focus, they are teacher spaces as well. For example, in co-design research focused on developing resources that integrate Resp onsible Research and Innovation (RRI) into K-12 science education, Kyza and Agesilaou (2022)  found that the co-design process facilitated mutual iterative learnin g and increased access to learning. Teachers developed the skills to decide what to teach in their science classrooms and apply innovative pedagogy. Similarly, Arastoopour Irgens et al. (2023) found that co-designing data science units helped teachers co-construct knowledge by aligning instructional goals with student needs and classroom practice. In this study, we follow an emerging trend in learn ing sciences of both organizing and studying records of co-design as a potential avenue to promote the integration of AI literacy into existing high school curricula and enhance our understanding of how teachers engage with the topic of AI. Over the course of an academic year, we engaged high school teachers in codesign sessions with university researchers with the goal of developing lessons that integrate AI literacy and meet the instruction al goals of their individual classrooms. Our antici pated impact was to disseminate AI resources that can be usable by other teachers and examine how teachers develop instruction to support their teaching of AI literacy.

## Context and participants

We used Shaffer’s quantitative ethnography (QE) app roach (Shaffer, 2017) to analyze qualitative data f rom a multi-session co-design case, combining ethnographic and computation al methods for interpretation.  The case includes five high school teachers from across the U.S. who participated in a virtual co-design fellowship focused on promoting the integration of AI literacy into K-12 curricula. During the fellowship, the teachers participated in five virtual co-design sessions with researchers at Stanford University between October 2023 and May 2024. Each session lasted about 60 minutes over Zoom and combined collaborative activities, peer feedback, a nd curriculum design aligned with participants’ instructional goals and students’ needs. The first two sessions focused on building foundational understanding of AI literacy and mapping it onto classroom goals. In the following two sessions, participants focused on designing and reh earsing their AI lesson while receiving feedback fr om peers and the research team. After the fourth session, each teacher implemented their lesson in their classroom, then used student feedback to revise their materials.  In the final session, participants reflected on the ir lesson implementation and their approaches for integrating AI literacy. The five participating teachers (3 female-identifying, 2 male-identifying) taught grades 9 –12 across diverse U.S. regions and subject areas. Their teaching experience levels ranged from under 4 to over 20 years with varying degrees of prior AI experience. The teachers collaborated with our research team comprising one faculty member and two graduate students with expertise in AI and education to co-design classroom-ready AI lessons. Data sources and analysis We drew on multiple data sources, including six pre-survey questionnaires, eighteen post-session reflections, and six post-implementation interviews. The pre-surveys captured teachers’ motivations, prior exper iences with AI, and goals for incorporating AI into their subject area. After each co-design session, participants completed openended surveys that elicited their reflections on th e co-design activities and AI concepts discussed.  Following lesson implementation, each teacher participated in  a 90-minute semi-structured interview focused on their experience designing and teaching their AI lesson. We first transcribed the Interviews data using Zoom  AI transcription and manually revie wed the transcript for accuracy. Next, we segmented  the dataset by teacher and activity type (pre-survey, reflection, interview) and applied a hybrid  coding approach. QE enables such analyses of multiple discourse from similar conversations by  modeling the data based on the time or activity in  which the discourse occurs (Zörgő et al., 2021). Using Mills et al.'s (2024) AI literacy framework, we applied a priori codes covering themes such as understanding how AI works,  ethics, problem-solving, and societal impact. We complemented this with open coding to surface addit ional themes in teachers' approaches to AI literacy. Two researchers collaboratively refined 32 initial code s into seven final categories, achieving an inter-rater reliability kappa of ≥ 0.7 (pooled kappa = 0.88). We then used these code categories to develop a codebook that was applied across the whole dataset. We quantified each code as "1" if present in a response or "0" if absent. After quantifying the coded data, we used the Epistemic Network Analysis (ENA) web tool (Marquart et al., 2018) to measure and visualize co-occurrences of these codes across teachers' discourse. Each teacher served as a unit of analysis, with sentences treated as utterances and grouped into stanzas based on activity context. For each teacher, we computed co-occurrences among the seven codes representing key AI literacy components: Understanding (Understanding AI Mechanisms, Understanding AI Ethics), Evaluation (Responsible AI Use, Assessing AI Outputs), Use (Using AI as a Tool, Enhancing Learning with AI), and AI Career Readiness. The cumulative co-occurrences for each code were visualized as a weig hted node-link network. In the ENA network, larger node sizes indicate frequent co-occurrence with multiple codes, and thicker edges s how stronger associations between specific codes within the network (Bowman et al., 2021).

## Findings

Our QE analysis revealed varying perspectives among teache rs on the AI knowledge and skills they prioritized for students in their subject area. To illustrate this, we present two contrasting vignettes. Simone, a Social Studies and AP Psychology teacher with over 20 years of exp erience, and Margot, an Honors/AP English teacher w ith under 4 years of experience. Both entered the study  with prior knowledge of AI but brought different l evels of experience and interest to the co-design process.

Simone In Simone’s network (Figure 1, left), the strongest connections were between Using AI as a Tool, Using AI to Enhance learning, and Responsible AI Use. The strong connections between these codes highlight Simone’s belief that the practical application of AI tools, whether  for task automation or enhancing learning, is a co re aspect of AI literacy that students need to develop. During the co-design, she indicated that her “main” goal was for students to “know how to use AI responsibly and ethically.” Additionally, Simone wanted students to understand how AI can “enhance their critical thinking and research skills [and] also to help improve their writing skills.” For Simone, AI literacy was primarily about encouraging student s to utilize AI for practical problem-solving while ensuring they understood the boundaries of responsible use. She elaborated, stating, “The ethical and responsible use … is a big, huge thing and then, of course, how to use it as a tool rather than a crutch. You know, it's … too easy to say, okay, write an essay on such and such.”  This comment indicated that Simone was deeply concerned with ensuring students use AI as a supportive tool rather than becoming overly dependent on it. Figure 1 Simone’s Network Graph (Left) And Margot’s Network Graph (Right) By framing AI “as a tool rather than a crutch” or s ubstitute for effort, Simone emphasizes teaching students to approach AI thoughtfully, using it to enhance their  capabilities without allowing it to replace essent ial skills. Notably, the absence of any connection to codes alo ng the Understand/Evaluate dimension in Simone’s ne twork suggests that she places less emphasis on foundational knowledge of AI mechanisms or deeper ethical nuances of AI. In sum, Simone’s network indicates an applied a nd hands-on approach to AI literacy, focusing primarily on empowering students with practical skills needed to effectively interact with AI tools and use AI to improve their learning experience in ethical and responsible ways.

Margot Margot’s network (Figure 1, right) highlights strong connections between (a) Understanding AI Ethics  and Assessing AI Output  and (b) Understanding AI Mechanisms  and AI Career Readiness. During the co-design session, Margot expressed that her goal was for stu dents to “learn the basics of how artificial intell igence works so that they candecide how to use it for different  academic purposes and when it is ethical and not e thical to use it in different situations.” This comment reflects Mar got’s comprehensive approach to AI literacy, focusi ng on foundational AI knowledge, ethical consideration, and effective use for learning. Margot emphasized that students need a foundational understanding of how AI operate s before using it as a tool for learning. She expla ined, “I want students to understand conceptually how large language models work so they have a basis for understanding how text generation creates writing through training on large sets of data and predicting the most likel y word or sequence of words.” By building this conceptual knowledge, Margot aimed to help students critically evaluate AI tools, such as ChatGPT, in terms of when and how to use them in their writing processes. Margot also expressed concern about “biases embedded” in AI systems and the potential of AI to spread misinformation which students might encounter and c onsume. As such, she wanted students to engage critically with AI outputs and understand ethical implications  such as copyright issues and the potential of AI t o “hallucinate” or fabricate information. In her less on, Margot used real-life examples of AI hallucination to demonstrate the limitations of AI and caution students about the risk of using AI or blindly trusting AI generated content. She noted, “So, I did try to embed some of  that [AI ethics] into the lessons … we talked abou t some instances like that one lawyer who used ChatGPT for cases, and then it made up a bunch of cases.” In addition to understanding AI ethics and evaluating AI outputs, Margot emphasized preparing students to use AI tools effectively in their future careers. She acknowledged the growing presence of AI in the workforce and wanted students to recognize that in addition to critical thinking and writing skills, “AI is a tool they are going to be expected to use in the workplace.” Margot’s a pproach to AI literacy also involved teaching stude nts to use AI responsibly to support their learning. Although the connections between Responsible AI Use, Understanding AI Mechanisms, and Using AI to Support Learning  were less central in her network, they were neverth eless significant. She noted that students need to “learn  the basics of how artificial intelligence works” t o use AI responsibly for various academic purposes. In sum, Margot’s network and approach to AI literacy highlight a broader perspective that integrates technical knowledge, ethical understanding, and practical application.

## Discussion and conclusion

Our aim in this study was to examine how high schoo l teachers prioritize aspects of AI literacy as they integrate AI within their subject-area curricula. Findings revealed that AI literacy is integrated heterogeneously by teachers and shaped by factors such as their own safety conc erns, awareness of ethical considerations, and thei r evolving perception of the potential utility of AI within th eir subject areas.  Although both teachers emphasized the importance of helping students use AI to support learning, their pedagogical choices diverged in important ways. Simone prioritized responsible use and practical application while Margot emphasized understanding foundational AI concepts and ethical reasoning. The varied approaches in the way the teachers integrated AI into their lessons suggest that there is not a unified understanding of how to treat and represent AI literacy in classroom instruction. AI literacy, when taken up by teachers, is far from  uniform. This insight suggests the need to extend existing AI literacy frameworks in two ways. First, they should account for the instructional contexts and priorities of teachers, who often must weave AI literacy into pre-existing curricular goals. Second, these frameworks could benefit from including design principles or practic al entry points to help educators operationalize AI  literacy in everyday lessons. Rather than prescribing a single pathway to AI lite racy, we argue that the field must embrace teacher-centered, modular approaches that allow educators to prioritize the aspects most aligned with their content area and students’ needs. Recently, Lee and Long (to appear) have proposed a meta-framework for understanding what different perspectives are being privileged in  different treatments of AI literacy. One treatment  can emphasize issues of surveillance in injustice, another might emphasize underlying computational mechanics, and still others might intentionally address both. In t he future, being able to distinguish those may help  researchers, designers, and educators better understand their re lative prioritizations of different aspects  of AI literacy and collaborate toward new understandings of trajectories, interconnections, and tensions in how this is taught across contexts and subject areas.
