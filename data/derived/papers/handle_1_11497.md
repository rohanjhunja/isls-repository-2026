# Comparing Data Science Practices between the Classroom and Real-World Projects

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Critical data literacy (CDL) is essential in data science education, requiring collaborative approaches that extend beyond quantitative reasoning. While literature on CDL is growing, more exploration is needed on how students apply data science practices to support CDL in a summer program. Using a descriptive case study, we examine differences between classroom learning and real-world applications in social justice projects. Findings indicate that practical engagement enhances students' understanding of data tools and ethical considerations.

## Introduction

Critical data literacy (CDL) has emerged as a cruci al component in data science education, transcendin g traditional disciplinary boundaries (NASEM, 2022). Promoting CDL requires collaborative approaches to enhance learners' skills in data science, going beyond mere quantitative reasoning to encompass a comprehensive understanding of data workflows and tools (Louie, 2 022; NASEM, 2022). In post-secondary settings, there is a need for advanced support mechanisms to equip learn ers with the competencies required  to effectively engage with complex data science practices (Pimental et al., 2022; Sager, 2024). Recent scholarship advocates for a nuanced approach to data science instruction, empha sizing the need to engage students with the complex ities of data variability and the impact of data contexts (L ee et al., 2021; Rosenberg & Jones, 2022). Introduc ing complexity into data science education is valuable, yet instructional design must address challenges su ch as dealing with messy data and integrating students' existing knowledge. Effective scaffolding is crucial for fostering meaningful engagement with data (NASEM, 2022; Rosenberg & Jones, 2022). This proposal explores CDL at the post-secondary level. This poster seeks to address essential questions surrounding the progression of data practices for supporting learning of CDL throughout a summer-long data science program.  With this call to action, the poster aims to contribute to the ongoing discourse on CDL, advancing our understanding of effective pedagogical strategies and tools to empower students through collaboration with the data science practices necessary to thrive in an increasingly data-driven world. Another research goal is to pave the way for a more inclusive and equitable future in data science education through rigorous i nquiry and collaborative engagement  (Sager, 2024). To this end, the research question guiding this study is:  How do data science practices taught in a classroom  setting differ from those enacted in real-world social justice projects?

## Methods

We examined a diverse group of undergraduate learners (n=9) within a data science summer Research Experience for Undergraduates (REU) as they participated in a classroom data science course and a food justice focused data science project. Most activities for the REU students were place-based, with all in-person meetings recorded on video. The first phase of the summer program includ ed ten videos from data science courses, totaling a bout 20 hours. Phase two recorded 17 videos from bi-weekly meetings between urban farm lab members and partnered students, encompassing 18 hours of content. After r eviewing the videos, we identified six key data sci ence practices used in the course and applied in a social justice project. Using a sociocultural learning perspective (i.e., Greeno, 2006), we developed a codebook to analyze student-teacher and peer interactions, focusing on collaborative knowledge exchange. This analysis employed thematic coding to compare the frequency and context of data science practices across both settings, highlighting their role in fostering engagement and understanding.

## Findings and conclusion

Our findings highlight the differences in data science practices utilized in classroom-based and food justice project-based contexts (see Figure 1 for frequency counts).  The findings are organized by data science practic e, then an example of each of the practice is provided  for the data science course and the food justice project. Due to space constraints, we will focus on two practices: understanding the problem and data storytelling. Figure 1 Frequency of Codes by Data Science Course and Food Justice Project Data science practice 2: Understanding the problem This practice explored how data science can address  real-world issues, with participants referencing potenti al applications 55 times —13 from the data science course and 42 from the foo d justice project. These discussions demonstrated a proactive approach to applying theoretical knowledge. In the data science course, under standing the problem was noted 13 times. One student's project using the World Atlas of Language Structures illustrated a gap in connecting manipulated data to real-world implications. Conversely, the food justice project recorded 42 instances of problem understanding. Students mapped 1,800 SNAP-eligible grocery stores in Northwood, refining their analysis to focus on stores selling fresh pro duce. This revealed that many identified stores di d not meet nutritional needs and highlighted that South Northwood is a food desert due to a lack of walkable grocery stores. This analysis showcased the role of spatial data sc ience in addressing socioeconomic issues and reveal ed disparities in food accessibility. While students in the data science course demonstrated technical skills, they often struggled to link their work to real-world issues. In contrast, the food justice project  illustrated a robust understanding of food accessibility through compreh ensive mapping and spatial analysis, emphasizing th e need for aligning technical work with meaningful outcomes.

Data science practice 6: Data storytelling Data storytelling occurred 72 times in total—28 times in the data science course and 44 in the food justice project. Effective visualizations are essential in data science for communicating insights and understanding data patterns. In the data science course, storytelling examples r evealed challenges in contextualizing data. For ins tance, the students’ project used Python and GeoPandas to analyze lingui stic features geographically but produced overly simplistic visualizations that overlooked the complexity of language diversity. This highlights the need to integrate real-world applications into data narratives. In contrast, the food justice project demonstrated effective storytelling through 44 presentations. Students shared their findings with the Farm Director, illustrating the urban farm's role in addressing food insecurity. Their visualizations depicted disparities in grocery store access and socioeconomic conditions in South Northwood. The group revealed gaps in grocery availability, while another member connected income and mobility data to food access issues. Overall, the data science course focused on narrative construction around analysis results but often lacked context. T he food justice project excelled in connecting find ings to community needs, showcasing how relatable narrative s candrive understanding and advocate for social change. This underscores the importance of compelling storytelling in bridging data analysis and real-world applications. The Food Justice Project built on CDL principles, e nhancing students' engagement with ethical data usage and power dynamics in data analysis as they m apped food deserts, thus understanding the societal implications of their work (Louie, 2022). In contra st to the more theoretical approach of the course, students in the Food Justice Project gained a deeper understand ing of data's role in addressing social and environ mental justice issues. Meaningful community partnerships are essential in data science educati on, providing authentic contexts that enrich learning, enhance problem inte rpretation, and improve communication of findings, while course designs should incorporate these partnerships and social justice elements to foster critical thinking, ethical decision-making, and technical skills (Avila et al., In press; Lee et al., 2021; Greeno, 2006; NASEM, 2018, 2022).
