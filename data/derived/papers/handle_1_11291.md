# Exploring Students’ Perceived Affordances of Physical and Digital Visualizations of Data in Greek Trade Lessons

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study designs a combination of physical and digital visualization of data. We analyzed eighth-grade students’ reflections on those visualizations and participation. This study highlights the value of combining and contrasting physical and digital visualizations of data to help students recognize the unique affordances of each to learn historical concepts and appreciate the benefits of using them in a complementary way.

## Introduction and background

With the increasing availability of large datasets, there is a universa l need for data literacy, including the ability to understand, use, and create data visualizations to make data-driven decisions (Wise, 2020). Our research is motivated by concerns that data literacy education focuses primarily on mathematical representations i n STEM contexts, which can exclude students less interested in math and oversh adow data literacy’s connections to other disciplinary contexts (Jiang et al., 2022; LaMar & Boaler, 2021). A key goal of this study was to  highlight the need to integrate multiple, complementary visualiza tions (Wilkerson et al., 2021). This study integrates three visualizations: a physical hallway with different color floor tiles that represent unique Greek land parcels, a digital map of the tiles, and a digital network representing floor-tile adjacencies. These were chosen in consultation with a teacher to help students explore the impact of geog raphy on trade in Ancient Greece. We added network visualization because it supports students’ recognition of data patterns (Zhou et al., 2024a). Additionally, previous work shows the promise of physical activities for scaffolding students intonetwork visualizations  (Zhou et al., 2024b). Thus, we designed a physical activity where stude nts viewed the hallway floor to collect data and then used the digital map and network to understand the situation that was being represented  to complete tasks. We analyzed their post-activity reflection, and videos of the activities to answer: RQ1: what are students’ perceptions of each visualization? and RQ2: how does the teacher mediate students’ perceptions of each visualization?

## Theoretical framework

This study draws on Gibson’s (1979) theory of perce ptual affordances to examine how students appreciate the potential benefits or challenges of each visualization (see also Greeno, 1994). Affordances refer to the actions an object is perceived to support. The hallway-floor layout is primarily designed to help students utilize the physical space to take a historical perspective by reenactin g ancient Greek trading (by identifying the kinds o f crops their “city” has and considering how they would transport them tonearby regions). The digital map is intended to afford students an overview of the entire space, which was  constrained in the hallway, so that students could  collect crop-yield data from other teams. The network visualizat ion affords students the ability to connect existin g crop data from the hallway activity and the map tonew d ata about the impacts of terrain difficulties on tr ade route efficiency. However, these conjectured affordances are only useful if they are recognized by students (Gresalfi, 2013). The extent to which these affordances are real ized depends on several factors, including students ’ ability and intention to perceive them. For example, the di gital network’s complexity may challenge students’ ability to recognize and utilize designed affordances, although the unfamiliar challenge might also resu lt in deeper engagement from participating students (Gresalfi et al., 2012). To better understand factors influencing students’ perceptions of designed affordances, we adopt the concept of mediation (Wertsch, 1985). Mediation refers to the idea that learning is shaped by contextual elements such as tools, community, rules, and division of labor.

## Method

The Greek Trade activity is a two-day intervention targeting eighth-grade history standards on the cultural contributions of Ancient Greece. The activity design builds on a standards-aligned existing lesson plan created by our partner teacher, Cole, who aimed to communicate the roots of geometry in  Athenian land parcels. In the original version of the lesson, the school’s hallwa y was divided into “cities” administered by groups of 4-5 students, each of which had parcels of different cr ops, represented by randomly sized clusters of hallway tiles in each of 5 colors or "crops" (black was for olives, tan for wheat, light gray for barley, reddish-brown for grapes) and unfarmable land. Student groups were asked to count how many harvest s of each crop they could do in each parcel (5 tiles of black olive tiles = 5 olive harv ests) and were then given a target harvest number f or each crop for the winter. If they had too few/many of one crop, they were instructed to trade with other groups. In this study, we used a network visualization tool, Net.Create (Craig et al., 2021) and identified two key elements that informed the redesign of the original lesson plan with network analysis in mind. First, network visualizations consist of two main components:  nodes/circles and edges /links between nodes. Network analysts can use a “shortest path” concept to find the fastest route between two cities that are not directly connected. To incorporate this movementof-goods-and-information element, we re-represented each crop parcel as a node in the network and created edges between adjacent parcel nodes. We manually adjusted edge weight to represent more challenging topography; higher edge weights, represented by thicker borders, indicated difficult, time-consuming travel routes (Figure 1, right). Second, network analysis offers more compact single-glance representations. Thus, we revised each parcel’s node-entry to include how many crops it contained and which city it belonged to. We also created a digital map of the hallway as an intermediate step between the physical and abstract network (Figure 1, left). ## Findings

The hallway activity was designed for students to p hysically observe the geometry of their territory, count the crops (tiles) numbers in their territory, and compi le crop totals to inform trading decisions. The hallway was perceived as the most helpful visualization for gat hering crop numbers (28), navigation (2), and under standing landscape (22). Twenty-one students found the hallway easy/helpful for collect ing their team’s crops number, while only 1 found it hard, which aligns with our design conjecture that students would be easily able to count tiles as they walked over them. Students perceived the hallway as easy for landscape information, but hard for locating other groups’ capitals, which aligns with our conjecture, in which students were confined to their own territory but could observe other territories from a distance. The physical experience in the hallway emerged as significant in 26 students’ reflections: “the hallway was the most helpful because you act ually got to see it in person. And you went through all the steps and emot ions and everything in person wher [where] eyou[you] felt everything and experienced it.” Additionally, the hallway activity served as a foundation for subsequent activities, “The hallway gave a visual representation helped me understand what we were doing for... the network.” The digital map helped students transition from a concrete experience in the hallway to the more abstract network experience. The map faithfully replicated the hallway layout, with blue lines representing the blue-taped team boundaries, a blue square marking each team’s blue-taped-X capital, and hallway classroom signs replicated on the map. The alignment between the features of the hallway and the overview in the map was designed to help students find their physical location on the map, a nd to provide information about other groups. Twenty-one students recognized the map’s landscape affordances, with 8 students explicitly noting the maps affordance of a whole-class overview (2) or their group’s entire territory (6). We also designed the map to show crop/tile numbers; 12 students noted the map’s affordances for gathering crop numbers, while only 1 found it hard. We designed the map as an essential resource for using filters in the n etwork while plotting efficient routes. 13 students found the map easy/helpful for plotting routes, while 7 found  it hard. Overall, students’ perceptions of the map  align with our designed affordances of supporting data collect ion beyond their own group and using the map as a supplementary tool for plotting routes. However, 6 students identified specific challenges in identify ing group territory (2) and capital locations (2). Moreover, 7 students found the map easy/helpful for evaluating travel time, which is misaligned with our conjecture. These misa lignments and challenges suggest a need to better f acilitate student introductions to the map and its features and to rethink reflection question wording in future research. The network visualization afforded students the abi lity to verify crop numbers collected in the hallwa y and to access information about terrain difficulty and distance for plotting routes. This last task ai med to help students practice extracting relevant data from a complex network to solve problems. Seven students found the network easy/helpful for crop numbers, and students ' reflections indicated that they did connect crop numbers to trading and team allocation tasks. The network is t he sole data source of terrain difficulty that the students were asked to assess, as indicated by the fact that stud ents only commented on it in the network reflection; 8 students found this design feature helpful. We also limited information about traveling days/distance to the ne twork only. Twelve students perceived the network positively fo r this purpose, while 6 found it hard. 24 students perceived the network positively for travel routes and only 4 found it hard. For the general network use, 5 students found it easy/interesting and 9 found it challenging. Further analysis  of codes in the network use category identified specific challenges: many buttons (2), finding the best match (1), reading and navigating (1), edges (2), object meanings (1). While these specific challenges suggest room for improvement, s tudents did note how other features, particularly the filters, were valuable. Eight students explicitly commented on positive ly, “The most helpful thing about the network was how we could reduce, filter, and view all the information, making it easier to answer questions.” Our analysis of students’ writte n reflections revealed that students ’ perception of each visualization’s affordances aligned with our conjectur e. Students valued the hallway activity as an easy a nd grounded way to gain foundational understanding of the activity, historical knowledge, and the digital  network. They perceived the digital map as providing an over view of the entire area and serving as a supplementary resource to the network for plotting routes. They r ecognized the network affordances in offering uniqu e information for plotting efficient routes and the filters function as helpful to extract data from the large dataset. Classroom video data showed that Cole used spontaneous scaffolding to mediate students’ r ecognition of designed affordances. Students’ reflections showed they valu ed the grounded way of learning history in the hallway activity. Video analysis finds that Cole supported this approach by consistently immersing students in the historical context through scenario narrative and connecting abstract concepts with concrete representations. Cole frequently prompted students to connect the tiles they were standing on to different types of terrain and their uses. For example, w hen interacting with Group 2, Cole pointed to tiles that weren't designated for crops and asked, “what does this represent?” Students guessed, “bord ers, rocks, areas where food cannot be grown.” Cole encouraged them to think about unfarmable land’s uses: “also means the city, or places where people’s homes are built, you think your area is poor because you do n ot have enough farmland. You might have actual businesses.” This spontaneous mediation suggested unfarmable land’s value and grounded abstract concepts of ancient terrain in concrete representations to sustain students’ immersive experience. Cole emphasized the rule of not physically crossing team borders to mimic the difficulty of overland trav el in ancient Greece and to highlight the value of the map and network. Students’ reflections indicated that they particularly appreciated the filter function. Prior to this Greek Trading network, students had experience with two previous networks. These activities prepared them to be able to add nodes and edges, and to apply simple filters. However, the Greek Trading network introduced a significantly larger dataset and thus more challenging task. Day 1’s class began with an introduction to the learning objectives: “we’re going to be focused solely aroun d analyzing the network, and we're gonna do that th rough trading crops.” Next, Cole gave students a clear heads-up about the complexity they would face, describing  the network as “just like a bomb going off. a beautiful mess.” This remark prepared students for the complex network they were about to work with. Cole followed this remark with an explicit comment on th e usefulness of filters: “that’s the easiest way to read the data on the network.” Next, Cole did a refresh on filters by modeling a step-bystep use of filters in a network that students used previously to guide them to condense content. Cole’s instruction on the importance of filters mediated students’ positive comments on using filters to work with complex networks.

## Conclusion

This study reports on the redesign of a history lesson to better integrate data visualizations. Our analysis revealed that students’ perceptions on each visualization’s affordances generally aligned with our conjectures, though some challenges were also identified for refinement. Our  video analysis demonstrated that the teacher media ted students’ perception by constantly immersing them in historical perspectives in the hallway activity and explicitly modeling how filters condense network complexity. This study suggests the value of leveraging physical activities to engage students in diverse ways of working with data. This study also demonstrates the promises of combining and contrasting network visualization with physical and digital visualizations to support students to recognize and value different affordances of each visualization i n a complementary manner (Ainsworth, 1999). In addition to the many studies focused on visualizations in STEM learning, we argue that exploring multiple visualiz ations of data also holds value in humanities contexts.
