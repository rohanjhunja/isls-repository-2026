# When Visuals Come First: Aligning Pre-Instruction and Embodied Pedagogies to Support Learning of Statistical Models

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Introductory statistics courses are challenging in part because core concepts such as statistical models are highly abstract. Prior work shows that visual representations and embodied pedagogies can each support abstract learning, yet little is known about how they interact. In this study, 112 undergraduates were randomly assigned to pre-instruction with or without visualizations, followed by an embodied intervention (gesture or drawing). Students who received visual pre-instruction outperformed those without visual support on an immediate posttest (Posttest 1). However, pre-instruction type did not directly predict later performance (Posttest 2), nor did it moderate the effectiveness of gesture versus drawing. Exploratory analyses revealed a moderated mediation: the relationship between Posttest 1 and Posttest 2 performance varied by pre-instruction type and students’ reported use of visual information from the embodied intervention. These findings underscore the importance of representational coherency across learning phases, offering new insights for designing instructional sequences in statistics education.

The major issues addressed Introductory statistics is one of the most widely taught college-level courses in the United States, with over 1.5 million students enrolled annually, yet nearly a third of students fail to pass (Digital Promise, 2023). A major reason is that core concepts —such as randomization, probability, and statistical models —are highly abstract. Although it is often assumed that students can intuitively grasp these abstractions (e.g., a formalism-first approach), research shows that learners frequently struggle with these foundational statistical, which in turn limits their ability to make accurate inferences  and transfer the knowledge tonew situations  (Chance et al., 2004; Garfield & Ahlgren, 1988; Kula & Koçer, 2020; Watkins et al., 2014; Zhang et al., 2022, 2025a). One promising approach to addressing these challenges is embodied pedagogy, which uses bodily actions and representations to help students learn abstract ideas (Barsalou, 2008; Nathan, 2021). Embodied pedagogies have shown promise in statistics and data science (e.g., Zhang et al., 2022, 2024). At the same time, representations, the form in which mathematical or statistical ideas are expressed and worked with —for example, equations, graphs, tables, diagrams, verbal descriptions, or other representational me dia have long been recognized as a crucial part of statistics and data science learning (Lesh & Doerr, 2003). In particular, graphs and diagrams might make abstract concepts more concrete and engage  an additional working memory channel for processing (Evagorou et al., 2015; Mayer, 2009; Corter & Zahner, 2007; Jain & Mitra, 2025). Although both embodied and visual pedagogies have been studied extensively in isolation, very little is known about how they function together—even though they share commonalities such as leveraging visuospatial information (Rau & Herder, 2021). A central limitation of studying instructional strategies in isolation is that students rarely encounter concepts as completely novel. By the time they reach an embodied activity in class, students are likely to have already been exposed to representations of variability, sampling, or models —whether through text, graphs, or other media. This means that instructional interventions never operate in a vacuum: they build on a foundation of representational knowledge that varies in type and strength across learners and may itself have been shaped by other forms of instruction.

To model this more authentic instructional sequence, the present study evaluates the combination of visualizations and embodied pedagogies by providing visualization (versus no visualization) before two widely used embodied activities (gesture versus drawi ng). Below, we will review relevant literature and present our hypotheses.

## Literature review

Benefits of visual representation 3 Theories of multiple representations and multimedia learning explain why visual representations enhance learning. Ainsworth’s (2008) Design, Functions, and Tasks framework suggests that visuals complement text by engaging different cognitive processes, con straining interpretation, and supporting the construction of transferable mental models. Similarly, Schnotz’s (2014) Integrated Theory of Picture Comprehension argues that visuals and text generate distinct but complementary internal representations —analog  and propositional—that, when integrated, strengthen understanding.

These perspectives align with Baddeley’s (1992) working memory model and Mayer’s (2009) Cognitive Theory of Multimedia Learning, which emphasize that processing information across separate visual and verbal channels distributes cognitive load and promotes learning. A review of the literature suggests that visuals are effective, especially when learners have low prior knowledge, when visuals  provide complementary rather than redundant information, and when students are guided to integrate text and visuals (Kalyuga & Singh, 2015; Schnotz & Bannert, 2003; Ainsworth, 2014; Zhang et al., 2024). In particular, the work on semiotic functioning, defined as how signs and symbol systems function in meaning-making, demonstrates the importance of learners’ ability to use, coordinate and flexibly adapt representational transformations in ways that generate meaning and new insights (Duval, 1999; Godino & Batanero, 2003). In this sense, embodiment can be introduced as an approach to help learners coordinate and make sense of representations. Benefits of embodied pedagogies Theories of embodied cognition emphasize that bodily actions can ground abstract concepts through different but potentially complementary mechanisms. The sensorimotor simulation account argues that embodied experiences construct multimodal frames in learne rs’ minds, which can later be reactivated during reasoning and problem solving (Barsalou, 2008). The cognitive offloading account suggests that embodied activities ease working memory demands by distributing information to the physical channel, freeing resources for conceptual processing (Kirsh & Maglio, 1994; Wilson, 2002). Although both mechanisms emphasize the importance of visual information, empirical evidence for the role of visuospatial representation in embodiment is mixed. (e.g., Wagner et al., 200 4). Wagner et al. (2004) found that gestures can act as propositional rather than visuospatial representations when conveying nonspatial content, whereas Wu et al. (2022) showed that visuospatial working memory is recruited when interpreting iconic gestures about concrete scenes. In addition to the debate about the recruitment of the type of working memory resources, two other gaps persist: first, much of this work assumes that embodied experiences operate independently of students’ prior knowledge. An emerging line of work suggests that just as instructional design influences how working memory is recruited (Paas, Renkl, & Sweller, 2004), students’ pre-instructional knowledge may also shape embodied learning(e.g., Aldugom et al., 2020; Cook et al., 2024; Congdon & Goldin-Meadow, 2021; Congdon et al., 2018, 2025; Zhang et al., 2024, 2025b). This consideration is especially important in statistics education, where instruction is cumulative and requires coordination across visual and symbolic representations. Second, although we gained theoretical value and practical utility by comparing an embodied pedagogy to an non-embodied one, embodied pedagogies are not monolithic. For example, in teaching gravity, instructors may use gestures to represent direction or drawings to depict force. Each approach carries distinct affordances, yet research has not fully clarified why or for whom different types of embodied pedagogies are effective. Because pedagogies such as drawing and gesturing are widely practiced in the classroom, understanding why they work and for whom is essential for making informed pedagogical choices. Without such evidence, pedagogical decisions risk being guided by habit or convenience rather than theory. The current study This study aims to evaluate the combined effect of pre-instruction visual support and embodied pedagogies in the context of learning statistics models. We chose to conceptualize visual support as a type of pre-instruction for both theoretical and practical reasons: theoretically, we are interested in whether providing visuals before embodied instruction provides the embodied activity with a representational anchor; practically, this sequencing also reflects how learning typically unfolds in real classrooms.  Students rarely encounter embodied activities in isolation; instead, they often come to class with some prior exposure—whether through readings or lectures, likely differ in their level of visual support—that shapes how they interpret and enact embodied t asks. To test how visual pre-instructions impact learning from different types of embodied pedagogies, the present study investigates how providing visualizations (versus no visualizations) before an embodied intervention (gesture or drawing) shapes students’ learning of statistical concepts. We hypothesized that learning with visual pre-instruction would outperform learning without visuals. Building on prior work that has documented moderating effects of learners’ prior knowledge and visuospatial working memory on embodied learning, we further hypothesized an interaction between the type of pre-instruction and the type of embodied pedagogy. 4

## Participants

Participants were 112 undergraduate students from a large public university in the Midwest. Of these, 67 (59.8%) identified as female and 45 (40.2%) as male. In terms of race and ethnicity, 1 (0.9%) identified as American Indian or Alaskan Native, 33 (29.5%) as Asian/Pacific Islander, 2 (1.8%) as Black or African American, 3 (2.7%) as Hispanic/Latino, 64 (57.1%) as White/Caucasian, and 9 (8.0%) as multiple/other ethnicities. Materials Two versions of the pre-instruction were developed. The pre-instruction for the No-Visual group was mainly textbased instruction about the empty model, the two-group model, and the sum of squares, with only one graph at the beginning to give the necessary setup of the data. All other instructions were text-based. The pre-instruction for the Visual group included the same text information, but with 13 additional graphs to support the text instructions.

## Design and procedure

Upon arrival at the lab, participants were seated at a laptop with printed worksheets placed beside it. The experimenter provided a brief overview of the session and launched the study through Qualtrics (Qualtrics, Provo, UT), where all materials were administered. After electronically reading and signing the consent form, participants completed a pre-survey assessing their initial understanding of statistical models. Participants were then randomly assigned to one of two pre-instruction conditions. In the visual condition, they studied materials accompanied by visualizations; in the no-visual condition, they studied the same content presented without visual aids. Following this phase, all participants completed posttest 1 to assess knowledge prior to the intervention. Next, participants viewed a series of instructional videos that combined an experimenter’s narration with a hands-on demonstration. They were randomly assigned to one of two embodied intervention conditions: gesture or drawing. After each video, participants advanced to a screen displaying the narrated sentence as text, read the sentence aloud, and reenacted the demonstrated action using their worksheet. After completing all videos and hands-on tasks, participants took posttest 2 which consists of multiple choice and short answer questions. They then completed a postsurvey reflecting on the experience, including the content they recalled from the instructional videos while solving Posttest 2 problems. A trained experimenter guided each session. During the first video and reenactment, the experimenter monitored participants closely to ensure they understood the procedure and corrected any errors  (figure 3). Once participants demonstrated that they could follow the sequence correctly, the experimenter stepped back, observing more passively while remaining available for questions or guidance. Common mistakes included forgetting to read the sentence aloud or attempting to read and perform the action simultaneously. In these cases, the experimenter reminded participants to read the sentence before reenacting the action. Figure 3

## Measures

Pretest performance The pretest consisted of three open-response questions assessing students’ basic understanding of statistical models and sum of squares before any instruction. Two trained coders scored the first two questions from 0 to 1 and the last question from 0 to 2 based on a pre-determined coding rubric blind to condition. Posttest 1 performance The pretest consisted of ten true –false items designed to evaluate students’ understanding of these concepts. Of the correct answers, seven were “true” and three were “false.” Posttest 2 performance The posttest consisted of 22 questions (15 multiple-choice questions, seven open-response questions). The openresponse questions were graded by two trained coders blind to the participants’ conditions: 0 for incorrect, 1 for correct, and 0.5 for correct but incomplete answers or answers that exhibited minor misunderstandings. 6 Self-reported visual use during posttest 2 After completing Posttest 2, participants were asked: “While answering the questions, how often did you think about the videos you watched?” and “If you did think about the activities while answering the questions, what did you think about specifically?” Responses to the second question were coded as either referencing visual components or non-visual components.

Establishing inter-rater reliability Inter-rater agreement was assessed following independent grading of all student responses blind to condition, with any scoring mismatch considered a discrepancy. A re-grading protocol—consisting of joint review of discrepant cases, rubric refinement, and subsequent independent re-grading—was specified for instances where agreement on a given question fell below 90%. Because both coders were highly experienced, no re-grading was required. Overall, the raters demonstrated strong reliability, reaching agreement on 93% of all responses. The discrepancies were resolved in a joint coder meeting.

Main effect of pre-instruction type We first used a multivariate regression to examine whether controlling for pretest performance, Pre-Instruction Type (Visualization Support vs. No Visualization) improved performance on the posttest immediately after (i.e., posttest 1). The analysis was conducted using the supernova package in R (Blake et al., 2024). There was a significant main effect of visualizations, F (1, 109) = 9.40, p =.003, Proportion Reduction of Error (PRE) =.08, such that students in the Visual condition (M = 7.14, SD = 1.24) scored higher on posttest 1 than those in the No Visual condition (M = 6.36, SD = 1.46). In contrast, the effect of pretest performance was  not significant, F (1,

109) = 1.09, p =.30, PRE =.01.

Main effects of learning with visualizations and the type of embodied pedagogy Because the type of embodied pedagogy was randomly assigned after learning with visualizations or not, we then examined the effects of both conditions using a multivariate regression that used posttest 2 (the test at the end) as the outcome variable.

Figure 4 Distributions of Posttest 2 Performance by the Two Conditions Figure 4 shows the distributions of participants’ posttest 2 scores by the two conditions. Among predictors, pretest scores were a significant positive predictor of posttest 2, F(1, 108) = 6.47, p =.012, PRE =.057. Neither pre-instruction type (visualization vs. no visualization),  F(1, 108) = 1.57, p =.213, PRE =.014, nor the type of embodied pedagogy (gesture vs. drawing), F (1, 108) = 1.12, p  =.293, PRE =.010, showed a significant effect on posttest 2. Based on Figure 4, we also did not see trends for the interaction between pre-instruction type and the type of embodied pedagogies, F (1, 108) = 0.30, p =.583, PRE =.002, which contradicted the original hypothesis.

Exploratory analysis 7 We were interested in the finding that although pre-instruction types significantly impacted participants’ posttest 1 performance, it did not significantly impact participants’ posttest 2 performance after participants went through one of the two types of embodied instructions. We also found that, although posttest 1 and 2 both assessed students’ understanding of empty and two-group models, participants’ posttest 1 scores did not significantly predict their posttest 2 scores, F(1, 110) = 1.90, p =.17, PRE =.02.

We explored two potential moderators of the relationship between posttest 1 (the knowledge participants have developed after pre-instruction) and posttest 2 (the cumulative knowledge participants acquired after pre-instruction and embodied instruction): pre-instruction type and visual use during problem-solving in posttest 2. To examine whether pre-instruction type moderates the relationship between posttest 1 and posttest 2 (visualized in Figure 5), we conducted a multivariate regression that used posttest  2 performance as the outcome, and pre-instruction type (Visuals vs. No Visuals), type of embodied pedagogy (Gesture vs. Draw), the interaction between pre-instruction type and posttest 1 performance, and pretest performance as covariates. The overall model was significant, F(5, 106) = 3.85, p =.003, PRE =.15. The complete results are shown in  Table 1. Both pretest and posttest scores were z-standardized to aid interpretation. In the table,  post1_c denotes the standardized posttest 1 score, PI denotes pre-instruction type,  EP denotes embodied pedagogy condition (gesture vs. drawing), and pre_c denotes the standardized pretest score.

Importantly, there was a significant interaction between posttest 1 performance and pre-instruction type, F(1, 106) = 6.76, p =.011, PRE =.06, indicating that the relationship between the two posttests differed by pre-instruction type. Specifically, for participants who received the pre-instruction with visuals, their posttest 1 performance positively predicted their posttest 2 performance, F (1, 54) = 9.20, p  =.004, PRE =.15; for participants who received the pre-instruction without visuals, their posttest 1 performance did not significantly predict their posttest 2 performance, F(1, 54) = 0.21, p  =.651, PRE =.00. This pattern suggests that the pre-instruction influences not only immediate learning outcomes but also how effectively students build upon prior learning.

Instead of testing this moderator separately, we want to integrate with the previously discovered effect. Figure 6 shows the conceptual diagram for the analysis to test our “representational bridge” hypothesis altogether. We specified a process model in lavaan (Rosseel, 2012). The model was estimated with maximum likelihood. Because observed interaction terms were included, global fit indices are not interpretable, and we focus on path estimates, conditional effects, and p-values. To avoid over complexing the results, we will only report on the key Figure 6 Conceptual Diagram for the Model The process analysis revealed the relationship between posttest 1 performance and posttest 2 performance was significantly moderated by both pre-instruction type (z = 2.57, p =.010) and visual use (z = 2.21, p =.027). Conditional slope analyses showed that for students without visual pre-instruction and who did not report using visuals at posttest 2, higher posttest 1 performance predicted lower posttest 2 performance (z = -3.06, p =.002). When only one moderator was visual (visual pre-instruction only but no visual use, z = 0.95, p =.344; or visual use but no visual pre-instruction, z = 0.17, p =.867), the relationship between posttest 1 and posttest 2 relation was not significant. Critically, when students both received visual pre-instruction and reported using visuals later, higher posttest 1 performance predicted higher posttest 2 performance (z = 2.96, p =.003). Indirect effects mirrored this pattern: the mediated effect of pre-instruction type through posttest 1 on posttest 2 was significantly negative without visuals (z  = -2.18, p =.029), nonsignificant when only one form of visual support was present (p >.05), and significantly positive when both were present (z = 2.14, p =.032). In summary, consistent with our prediction, we found that participants who learned with visual pre-instruction outperformed those learned without visual support. Contrary to our prediction, the type of pre-instruction did not significantly moderate the eff ectiveness of the two types of embodied pedagogies, neither did it predict participants’ posttest 2 performance. Our exploratory analyses revealed that although the type of pre-instruction alone did not significantly predict participants’ posttest 2 perfor mance, there was a significant moderated mediation: the indirect effect of the type of pre-instruction on posttest 2 through posttest 1 was contingent on the type of pre-instruction and the self-reported use of visuospatial information from the embodied intervention while answering posttest 2. In other words, the pathway by which pre-instruction influences later performance (Posttest2) through earlier performance (Posttest1) is different for different pre-instruction groups and the use of visuospatial information from the embodied videos during posttest 2. Specifically, we found that there was a positive correlation between posttest 1 performance and posttest 2 performance only for participants who received visual pre-instruction and used visual information from the embodied intervention in posttest 2. Instead, the correlation was negative for participants who received non-visual pre-instruction and did not use visual information in posttest 2.

Because learners’ use of visual information was measured via self-report, there are at least two interpretations. First, self-reported recall of visual information may approximate the mental representations 9 learners developed during the embodied intervention—that is, what they encoded and remembered. Second, it may reflect individual strategic tendencies, with some students naturally more inclined to leverage visuospatial information. The first interpretation  is theoretically more compelling, as it suggests that embodied interventions can shape the mental representations students later activate during problem solving. However, without a nonembodied control group, it is difficult to disentangle these explanations. Notably, prior research has demonstrated that participants in embodied interventions report greater recall of visual information than those in non-embodied conditions (Zhang et al., 2024b), lending support to the idea that self-reported recall indexes knowledge developed through embodiment.

Despite the exploratory nature of this process analysis, our findings suggest a new theory for how pre-instruction, embodied intervention, and problem solving interact. Because both types of embodied videos and the corresponding reenactments required learners to manipulate visual information, we speculate that whether learning at Posttest 1 carries over to Posttest 2 depends on the coherency across phases of learning—that is, the match between pre-instruction, the embodied intervention, and the internal me ntal representations students later activated during problem solving. Without such coherency, the link between earlier and later performance may weaken, making initial learning benefits less durable. The significant moderated mediation supports this view: the relationship between Posttest 1 and Posttest 2 varied systematically depending on pre-instruction type and students’ self-reported activation of visuospatial representations during Posttest 2, suggesting that these factors shape how knowledge is consolidated, retained, and transferred. Of course, given the exploratory nature of this study, the working theory needs to be evaluated systematically in future studies. Testable hypotheses and open questions This working theory generates concrete, falsifiable hypotheses for future research. For example: (1) The effectiveness of embodied interventions (compared to non-embodied controls) will be moderated by pre-instruction type, such that embodied interventions  are more beneficial following visual pre-instruction; (2) Students who receive embodied instruction will be more likely to develop and activate visuospatial mental representations during the embodied intervention and subsequent problem solving than those in non-embodied conditions; (3) The effects of embodiment on learning will be mediated by the extent to which students activate coherent internal representations at problem solving.

The theory also opens new areas for inquiry. For instance, for students without visual pre-instruction, what forms of pedagogy best compensate? Can embodied instruction alone generate sufficient visuospatial representations to offset the absence of visual scaffolding? Alternatively, if coherency is the decisive factor, could a no-visual pre-instruction combined with a non-visual intervention outperform mismatched conditions? Future work should also examine what kinds of representations learners are asked to coordinate and how they move among them during learning. Understanding might  depend on learners’ ability to interpret, transform, and connect meaning representations (Lesh & Doerr, 2003; Zhang et al., 2024c). In this view, embodied activity is not simply another support added onto instruction; it may function as a mechanism that helps students coordinate and translate across representational forms, thereby strengthening the coherence of the internal representations they construct. This framing clarifies that the benefits of embodied pedagogy may depend on whether that movement supports semiotic functioning during problem solving. Moreover, while our study manipulated pre-instruction to impose differences in representational knowledge, learner characteristics such as visuospatial working memory or pre-existing representational competencies (e.g., ability to interpret graphs) may als o explain heterogeneous outcomes of embodied learning. Past work in learning with visualization often assumes that learners already know how to interpret the visualizations presented. However, empirical research in statistics education shows that even basic representations such as the normal distribution, which instructors frequently use to teach more advanced concepts, pose difficulties (Zhang et al., 2025). In practice, visualizations play a dual role —students both learn with  them and learn about them (Rau, 2017). It is likely the case that the benefits of visual support depend not only on their design but also on students’ developing conceptual representational competencies (Mayer, 2009; Schnotz, 2014). Future work should evaluate how the charact eristics of cognitive capacity and knowledge learners bring into the learning intervention impacts their learning outcomes.

These questions are especially timely given the mixed evidence in the literature about the effectiveness of embodied learning (e.g., Congdon et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2024). Our findings highlight that embodied interventions should not be treated as stand-alone activities. Instead, they operate within a sequence of instruction that includes what students have already learned and the strategies they bring to problem solving.
