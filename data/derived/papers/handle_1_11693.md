# Teacher Co-Design for Student-Driven Authentic Research with Community Partners

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Using an interactional ethnography approach, this study traced the two-year co-design process of three teachers to support student-driven authentic research at an innovative high school, collaborating with community partners. Grounded theory analysis of teacher interviews, lesson documents, and teacher meeting observations revealed six learning design areas, including ill-defined goals, supportive materials and student-driven learning processes. These elements initialized and evolved over time through co-design by complex partnerships involving interdisciplinary teachers, researchers, and business partners.

## Introduction

To prepare students for a  world with rapid changes, schools need to cultivate collaborative  knowledge-creation practices by which real-world communities solve problems (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2014). The current research explores innovative designs for engaging high schoo l students in authentic research in collaboration w ith community-based organizations (Zhang et al., 2024). While extensive efforts have been made to design authentic research experiences for college students, educator s face a gap of knowledge about how to enable authe ntic research in K-12 settings. As a core  challenge, educators need to design research guidance and support in a way that fosters their epistemic agency over creative research processes. The existing practices often use pre-scripted procedures to guide student research, restrict ing student agency for open-ended research (Bird et al., 2023). Therefore, further research needs to explore  how teachers engage in dynamic forms of learning design (Clark et al., 2024) that address students’ emerging interests and needs as they chart their knowledge building processes. We integrate several lines of literature to underst and how teachers may approach  dynamic designs of authentic research practices. First, teachers engage in design-mode thinking to address emergent problems related curriculum, interaction, student capability, classroom structur es, and technology  (Teo, 2014). Clark and colleagues (2024) use the concept of “ designerly thinking” to highlight that teachers not only engage in the preinstructional design but also continue their  design work during the learning process as the plans, social organization, and activities are adapted and revised. As student research work proceeds, the teacher may engage students’ collaborative input to co-structuring research goals and processes. Doing so helps foster students’ highlevel agency for knowledge building (Park & Zhang, 2025). Considering that authentic research with real-world communities often goes beyond the boundaries of sub ject areas, teacher co-design becomes essential. T eachers collaborate with their colleagues (Lu, 2022), researchers (e.g., Kyza & Agesilaou, 2022), and other partners to iteratively co-design key elements of learning. The goal of this study is to analyze how a team of teachers co-designed authentic research practices for high school students in collaboration with community partners.

## Methods

Designing contexts and partnerships This research is part of a larger design-based research (DBR) (Collins et al. 2004)  project conducted at a high school in the Northeast of the United States (Zhang et al. 2024). The co-design took place as part of a senior class

– the Innovative Solutions Project. A class of 12 th-grade students worked on authentic research with real-world

professionals to tackle authentic challenges facing the local and global communities over a ten-week period. The research-based learning process was co-designed and co-taught by three teachers (Mrs. H, Mrs. M and Mrs. K), who specialized in English, arts/business alliance, and special education.

## Data collection and analysis

The data sources included the three teachers’ inter view transcripts, their design documents (e.g., rub rics) and teacher meeting observations. Semi-structured interviews were conducted annually at th e end of the project. Interviews focused on the description and reflectio n on the co-design process. Grounded theory methods  were used to guide the data analysis (Strauss & Corbin, 1998) for identifying design elements and the reasoning and actions of design team members (Merriam, 2009). The identified design elements were grouped into four design stages – initialization, preparation of iteration, implement ation of iteration and reflection of iteration.  An interactional ethnography approach (Green & Bridges, 2018) was then applied to examine how teachers and partners constructed designs over time. Data were chronologically analyzed and ordered to show when and how design elements emerged. In this study, we present preliminary findings from our analysis.

## Preliminary findings

Through the analysis, six design areas emerged: learning goals; learning products; learning materials; learning processes; learning assessment; and learning infras tructure, relationships, and contexts. These elemen ts were initiated through distributed expertise, evolved in  response to emergent problems and opportunities, a nd were envisioned through reflective observations and conversations. Co-designing an initial set of learning elements based on distributed expertise The course design began with the central idea of integrating and applying the skills students had learned, such as communication and research skills, into an authentic project-based learning experience. The three teachers decided to work together to develop it. All teachers provided pedagogical strategies and scaffoldings to students. Teachers rotated among different student groups to ensure students received diverse perspectives and tailored support, while also focusing on specific tasks based on their distributed expertise. For example, Mrs. K, the special education teacher, focused on helping students faci ng difficulty in their academic experiences. In add ition, business partners were able to offer real-world challenges and practical insights to classroo m, and researchers supported the design process with inquiry-based frameworks. In terms of learning infrastructure, relationships, and contexts, the teachers and business partners arranged several business meetings tonegotiate possible solutions and obtain feedback and guidance on students’ ongoing research plans. Ongoing co-design based on emergent problems and opportunities In the course preparation phase of iteration, the teachers and researchers met and ref lected on the first year’s designing and teaching experiences. The teachers re flected on several problems and opportunities. For example, they observed that the students lacked systematic views on the research process and had difficulties in synthesizing and using information. To address this issue, the r esearchers introduced a research cycle  that supported teachers in refining the rubric design. Furthermore, a  significant challenge for the teachers was tra cking each group's progress and emerging needs. To address this, durin g the second year’s class, the three teachers co-created a shared document called the "Student Group Tracker."  In addition, t he teachers enhanced peer collaboration activities by providing more opportunities for group discussions. Another issue the teacher identified as a learning context and infrastructure issue was that business partners dominated the business meetings. Teachers then stepped in and guided the adults to change their role as learning facilitators. Co-envisioning future improvement through reflective observations At the end of each year’s course, the teachers and researchers discussed and reflected on the students ' learning practices and design approaches. The teachers aimed to establish more appropriate ex pectations for business communication and place greater emphasis on leaders hip development.  T he teachers and researchers also discussed incorporating more research techniques the following year, such as the use of a structured template for reviewing sources, and introducing research tools a nd workshops, such as a survey workshop and a marke ting research workshop, earlier in the process.  To design a relational and collaborative learning e xperience, the teachers considered not only becoming more actively  involved in helping business partners develop esse ntial questions but also explored whether students should  co-construct these questions a longside the teachers and business partners. The teachers observed that essential questions could change and that practices could be adapted based on students’ research processes. T hey affirmed that research questions should be flex ible, allowing for reconstruction and evolution in the future.

## Discussion and conclusion

Although the teachers used pre-structured activities and ELA-based standards to initiate research-based learning in the early phase, the affordance of diverse and c omplex partnerships in co-design promoted the continuous evolution of learning design elements. Within these partnerships, three key mechanisms drove learning evolution:

(a) distributed expertise, (b) co-identification of ever-evolving students’ problems and opportunities, and (c)

reflective observations of students’ research proce ss. Co-designers in this study brought diverse perspectives to identifying and addressing students’ idea progress and problems. They also  broadened and redefine d these learning design elements, and at the same time adaptively adjust their personal and collaborative roles (Kavanagh et al., 2022). This finding also emphasizes that learning infrastructure, relationshi ps, and contexts can be profoundly changed and expanded through co-design. The results contribute to both theoretical and pr actical improvement in co-design processes, particularly those involving complex partnerships and aiming at effectively supporting K-12 students’ research experiences.
