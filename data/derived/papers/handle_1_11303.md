# Interactivity in Extended Reality: Towards A Hierarchical Model for Defining and Designing Learning Experiences

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Extended realities (XR), encompassing virtual, augmented, and mixed reality (MR), offer transformative learning opportunities through interactive experiences. However, the concept of interactivity within XR remains ambiguously defined. This work reviews interdisciplinary definitions of interactivity and constructs a conceptual definition, operationalized using Schulmeister’s (2003) hierarchical model. From this, the paper presents a taxonomy of interactivity, clarifies its role as a key learning affordance, and offers a framework for analyzing and designing XR experiences. By exploring XR's unique capabilities for learning, this work guides the development of immersive environments and proposes recommendations for advancing the field. These insights set the stage for future empirical studies examining the relationship between interactivity, affordances, and learning outcomes, ultimately helping developers and educators maximize XR's potential for effective learning.

## Introduction

The affordability and prevalence of Extended Realit y (XR) technology, encompassing virtual, augmented,  and mixed realities has led to an increased interest in  how this technology can be used for learning in bo th training and education. If properly implemented, XR  can be a potent training tool that simulates real-world experiences, provides learners with an interactive multimedia en vironment, and allows them to practice complex skil ls and procedures through experiential learning (Kaplan et al., 2021; Radianti et al., 2021). In addition, XR offers a safe and cost-effective way to engage in learning activities that  may be too dangerous or expensive to perform in th e real world (Kaplan et al., 2021). Studies suggest t hat XR can improve learning outcomes and pro vide at least comparable results to traditional training methods (Kaplan et al., 2021; Radianti et al., 2021). However, exploring the relationship between XR and learning is a complex, multifaceted endeavor, with scholars highlighting several key affordances of the technology, including immers ion, presence, agency, embodiment, and interactivit y (Johnson-Glenberg, 2018; Klingenberg  et al, 2024; Makranksky & Petersen, 2021, McGowin et al., 2023, McGowin & Fiore, 2024). McGowin and colleagues rece ntly developed a broad-based theory of I-VR learning affordances, grounded in the cognitive sciences fra mework of 4E cognition.  For this paper, we draw up on their prior research and concentrate on the relationship between interactivity and extended cognition. Interactivity is frequently cited as a n important feature in existing literature on XR and learning. However, in the XR  learning literature, it is generally underdefined and undere xplored (e.g., Andreasen et al., 2021, Petersen & Makranksy, 2021; Klingenberg et la., 2024). This paper reviews various definitions of interacti vity across multiple domains, presents a taxonomy of interactivity derived from e xisting literature, and illustrates, through exampl es, how interactivity can be applied in the diverse technol ogies within extended reality. Additionally, it off ers a set of recommendations to further advance research and development in the learning sciences. Interactivity Over two decades since McMillan noted, “interactivity means different things to different pe ople in different contexts” (McMillan, 2002, as cited in Moreno & May er, 2007, p. 163), the field still struggles with a coherent and concise conceptualization, creating multiple definitions within and across domains. For context, Powell and Leary (2021) conducted a content analysis of interactivity (in the context of learner-content interaction) in which they examined 107 articles to identify how construc ts of interaction are used. They found that from th e 107 articles, 113 separate measurement tools were used for interactivity, indicating the substantial range  of measurement tools related to interaction. They furt her noted that most of the tools were not generaliz able and were unique to each study context (Powell, & Leary, 2021). Early definitions from the communications literatur e described interactivity as “an expression of the extent that in a given series of communication exchanges, any third (or later) transmission (or message) is related to the degree to which previous exchanges referred to even earlier transmissions” (Rafaeli, 1988, p. 1 11). Steuer

(1992) expanded on this idea, placing interactivity in the context of a mediated environment (ME) and defining it

as “the extent to which users...can [modify] the form and content of a mediated environment in real time” (p. 14), consolidating Sheridan’s (1992) and Zeltzer’s (1992) models into a single dimension centered on user control. Steuer (1992) outlines three aspects of interactivity, namely speed, range, and mapping. Speed relates to the concern of real-time interactions, range covers the actions a user can perform, both in temporal and spatial ordering, as well as in intensity (e. g., loudness, brightness) of the ME. Mapping captures how user interactions are ‘mapped’ onto the virtual environment (VE), on a continuum from completely arbitrary, to completely natural, or also referred to in some literature as ‘congruence’ in gesture o r sensory-motor representation (e.g., JohnsonGlenberg, 2018; Kilteni et al., 2012). In terms of multimodal learning environments, Moreno and Mayer (2007) define interactivity in relation to the responsiveness of the system to the learners actions (i.e., multidirectional communication between the user and the mediated environment) with the purpose of foste ring learning. Hence, a single direction (i.e., pas sive) multimedia learning experience presented in a linea r fashion would not qualify as ‘interactive’. From this, they provide five forms of interactivity for mediated le arning environments: dialoguing, controlling, manipulating, searching, and navigating. Dialoguing is the questi on and answer, or feedback, provided from the syste m (i.e., a learner can ask a question about a topic and receiv e a predetermined response from a virtual agent). Controlling is relegated to pace and order that information is presented to the learner, somewhat aligning to Makr anksy and Petersen’s (2021) definition of agency. Manipulating encapsulates both setting simulation parameters and control of viewpoints (i.e., zoom in/out, rotation of 3D objects). Searching allows a learner to find and select information. Navigating encompasses moving through different menus to display different content. When conceptualizing varieties of interaction, Wu  et al. (2005) describe a set of differences between cognitively and perceptually mediated models, with the underlying distinction about mode of control. H ere, we can use Steuer’s (1992) continuum of arbitrary to natural user input as a frame of reference. Cognitively mediated models are controlled via arbitrary interactions (e.g., mouse and keyboard) whereas perceptually mediated models rely on ‘natural’ or visuomotor control, such as physical/direct manipulation (Barret et al., 2015). Schwier (1992) has a three-pronged approach to interactivity, labeling the com ponents reactive, proactive, and mutual. Reactive is a two-way interaction between the learner and the ME, such that the system displays a stimulus to the learner in the forms of questions or information, and the learner ‘reacts’ to said stimuli, by selecting an answer or navigating to a new area of content. Proactive focuses on the generative pro cesses of learning that go beyond just selecting or navigating and is focused on “elaborations beyond designer-imposed limits” (p.5). Finally, the highest level of interaction is mutual interaction. This level is the home to both artificial intelligence (AI) and virtual (and by extension, extended) reality (S chweir, 1992). Here the learner and system have a recursive and mutually adaptive (e.g., syste m in systems) relationship, with the main point bei ng that the system/learner interaction is capable of essentially unlimited dialoguing (i.e., an unlimited conversation between the human agent and th e machine agent). These levels are not mutually exc lusive and often work in tandem, but they are hierarchical. Schwier (1992) outlines how these concepts are interconnected statin g “ when learners generate new questions and approaches (proactive) t hey can …be used by the system to formulate new conversations (mutual). Similarly, when learners generate their own strategies (proactive) they are responding to existing stimuli at a sophisticated level (reactive)” (p. 6). Schulmeister (2003) offers a different perspective with his taxonomy of interactivity, presenting a si xlevel continuum. This framework ranges from minimal  interactivity in a mediated environment (Level I) to constructing content and receiving intelligent feed back (Level VI). To contextualize this continuum at its first level, learners can pause a video or select a text prompt. At the other end of the continuum is the ability to create or manipulate the medium and receive meaningful fee dback (See Appendix 1 for the continuum of interactivity and examples of both traditional and XR learning en vironments). Despite predating much of the theoretical and empirical work, Schulmeister (2003) still provides the most comprehensive hierarchical model of interactivity, and the one that encompasses many elements of the o thers. Thus, we base our operational definition of interactivity on Schulmeister’s (2003) work. With t his, we synthesize other definitions to distill the m into both conceptual and operational constructs. Our conceptual definition for interactivity is as follows: Interactivity is the multidirectional exchange of related information between two or more agents (i.e., persons, system, virtual agents) (Rafaeli, 1988), which can be characterized by the degree of modification, malleability, and recursive feedback between agents in a mediated environment (Schwier; 1992; Steuer,1992; McGowin et al., 2023).  This definition encompasses multiple levels of interactivity, from a person reading a book or viewing an animation to a recursive dynamical system providing virtually unlimited dial ogue or feedback. For our operational definition of interactivity, we build off the works of Schulmeister (2003), Stalph and Heravi (2021), and McGowin et al., (2023) (see A ppendix 1 for additional details). Next we provide a more in-depth examination of the continuum of interactivity including concrete examples across various XR learning environments. Level I: Viewing and receiving content To illustrate, consider reading a book: the reader gains information and interacts minimally, mainly b y turning pages or choosing a section. Similarly, this level of interactivity is comparable to watching videos w here the viewer either passively watches or actively skips to different parts of the video (i.e., skip to location, but no pause function). In an XR environment, such as VR, Level I could manifest as passively viewing immersive video s or exploring 360-degree photo environments. Here, interaction is pri marily limited to navigating one's viewpoint around the virtual space and occasionally selecting  specific locations or sequences, such as deciding the order in which to view exhibits in a virtual museum.

Level II: Watching and receiving multiple representations This level can include multiple streams of informat ion to elaborate on what the learner is consuming, such as a GIF providing supplemental information about a proc ess in which the learner is engaged. In XR, this level of interactivity (II) allows users to engage with multiple types of media or informational overlays within a VE. They can control not only the timing of their interactio ns, (e.g., pause, skip, select), but also customize the  placement and display of information streams. For example, us ers migh t engage an AR display to view additional architectural details on one side of their visual field while simultaneously accessing historical facts or on the other, possibly enhancing their sense of presence and learning experience. Note that the component representation does not allow anything beyond watching/listening/reading and only serve for illustration or information. Level III: Varying the form of representation At this level, users control how information is pre sented without altering its content. They can rotat e, scale, or cross-section objects, fostering active engagement. For example, a user may switch between first-and third-person views of a carbon molecule, but this does not change its structure or properties (e.g., it remains a carbon molecule and does not become another structure such as graphene). In XR, this interactivity (III) enables users to manipulate objects in three dimensions, enhancing understandin g through direct interaction. Users can pick up, ro tate, and resize virtual artifacts, examining them from multi ple angles without changing their properties. For example, in an MR/AR archaeology application, a student could view a digital artifact overlaid on a physical table, rota te it for detailed examination, or resize it to match a r eal-world reference. These interactions mimic real-world exploration, reinforcing understanding and making abstract concepts more tangible and concrete. Level IV: Manipulating the component content Learners can generate non-predefined outputs by selecting relevant inputs and entering them into the system. This is the first-time users are ‘creating’ content through their int eractions with the system.  For example, in an educational software application, students might in put different variables into a simulation to observ e how changes in these variables affect the outcome of an experiment, creating unique scenarios based on their choices. For XR, level IV interactivity empowers users to actively manipulate content within the virtual environment. This level involves more than simple object manipulation; it includes the ability to alter environmental co nditions, change the course of events, or interact with compl ex systems that react dynamically to user dec isions. For example, users might adjust lighting and weather co nditions to see how they affect a virtual ecosystem, or they could solve problems that alter the storyline in an  interactive narrative. These changes directly impact the virtual world, providing immediate feedback on their actions (i.e., users ‘see’ the output of the manipulations). Level V: Constructing the object or representations from user input At this level, learners are able to interact with t he system to create new objects with the help of a comprehensive toolset developed specifically for this purpose (i. e., created by the developer of the system). For in stance, platforms like Roblox, Minecraft, and Second Life provide these types of opportunities by allowing users to design and construct intricate virtual structures and envi ronments from scratch via an expansive toolset. In XR, level V interactivity is distinguished by the extensive creative freedoms provided by advanced toolsets. These tools could allow users to design, construct, and even program objects or environments from scratch. For instance, users could design the architecture of an entire virtual building, sculpt intricate artworks, or engineer complex machinery, all within the confines of a virtual or mixed space. This level of interactivity not only fosters creativity but could also enhance practical skills such as spatial reasoning, architectural planning, and artistic expression. Level VI: Constructing the Object or Contents of the Representation and Receiving Intelligent Feedback from the System through Manipulative Action This highest level of interactivity integrates AI—whether real AI or algorithmic feedback —to enhance learning across all lower interactivity levels. Adaptive learning platforms exemplify this by analyzing student interactions, adjusting task difficulty, recommending personalize d learning paths, and deliv ering targeted feedback. In XR, Level VI interactivity similarly leverages AI to re fine skills and modify learner behaviors within com plex simulations. For instance, an AR or VR surgical tra ining program could analyze a trainee’s technique, provide real-time corrective guidance, and dynamically adjust ta sk difficulty. This ensures personalized, contextua lly relevant feedback that directly informs and enhances the learner’s actions. Recommendations R1-Metrics for interactivity in XR Building on Schulmeister’s  (2003) hierarchical model of interactivity, future research should focus on creating standardized tools and methodologies for quantitati vely and qualitatively assessing interactivity acro ss different levels in XR environments. While Schulmeister’s work outlines the conceptual framework for interactivity, there is a gap in how these levels can be practically eva luated. Developing assessment metrics would bridge this gap, enabling researchers and designers to evaluate how varying degrees of interactivity (e. g., passive viewing vs. manipulative actions) are integrated into XR experi ences and their impacts on learning outcomes. These  metrics would allow for a more systematic exploration of th e relationship between interactivity and learner en gagement, supporting the advancement of XR research and the effective design of immersive learning environments. By further operationalizing Schulmeister’s framework, such tools would provide a consistent approach for evaluating interactivity in XR across various applications and learning contexts. R2-Interactivity and XR affordances Interactivity does not exist in isolation but interacts dynamically wi th other XR affordances such as immersion, agency, and embodiment. Future research should inve stigate these interactions to identify how they col lectively influence learning outcomes. For instance, how do es the degree of interactivity impact a learner's s ense of presence, or agency, and how do these affordances e nhance (or detract) from the learning experience? S tudies should focus on identifying the optimal balance of these factors for d ifferent types of learning tasks, such as procedural training versus conceptual or declarative understanding. Experimental designs could manipulate each affordance with corresponding levels of interactivi ty to begin to isolate their individual and combine d effects on engagement, knowledge transfer, and skill acquisition. This line of inquiry will provide deeper insigh ts into how XR environments can be fine-tuned for specific pedagogical and andragogical goals. R3 – CPD and Interactivity As XR environments increase in interactivity, they offer immersive learning experiences that engage us ers in novel ways. However, the complexity introduced by greater interactivity may also increase Cognitive Processing Demand (CPD), an umbrella term that encompasses various dimensions, including cognitive load and mental task load, and refers to the cognitive/mental demands placed on learners, which may hinder the learning process (McGowin et al., 2024). At a high-level, CPD theories suggest that excessive  cognitive demands diminish the ability to process and retain information effective ly. Thus, future research should explore how the in creasing interactivity of XR environments affects CPD factors. For example, how do task complexity and learner expertise intersect with XR interactivity to influence learnin g outcomes? An open question is whether adding more interactive elements in XR, especially for novice l earners, leads to excessive cognitive load that det racts from learning, or at the other end of the exp ertise spectrum, can expert learners effectively ma nage these increased demands without negative consequences. Additionally, research should focus on adaptive strategies for managing CPD in XR environments (e.g., dynamic scaffolding a nd fading) to balance task challenge with learner a bility. By examining how interactivity levels interact with CPD, we can gain insight into 'optimal' levels of interactivity for diverse learning contexts and learner characteristics.

## Conclusion

Schulmeister’s (2003) levels of interactivity outline a progressio n from passive content consumption to active manipulation and intelligent feedback. This paper demonstrates how XR can enhance learning by leveraging these interactivity levels, with future research neede d to standardize interactivity metrics, investigate the relationship between interactivity and XR affordances,  explore AI-driven feedback, and examine how cognitive processing demand interacts with different levels of interactivity. Adapting frameworks like Schulmeister’s can help establish a shared understanding of interactivity in XR, enab ling more effective collaboration, systematic desig n, and impactful learning experiences.
