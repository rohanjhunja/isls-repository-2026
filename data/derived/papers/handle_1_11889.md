# Design and Play in Real Time: A Participatory Design Approach for Autistic Children to Design Online Play with Peers

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper presents a participatory design approach for children, designers, and educators to collaboratively create inclusive digital play environments that foster interactions between neurodiverse and neurotypical children. Integrating generative AI (GenAI) empowers children to actively contribute to game design while engaging in the play with their peers without adult mediation, highlighting the potential of such methods to foster inclusive collaboration among children, between children and adults, and between children and technology.

A Call for creating inclusive social opportunities online Like other children, autistic children often desire peer friendships (Rodda & Estes, 2018) yet frequently face social challenges (Lord & Magill-Evans, 1995). These include difficulties with langu age, sensory processing, and interpreting social cues—such as jokes, eye contact, or facial expressions (Ghanouni et al., 2019; Aherne, 2023)— which can lead to reduced or atypical peer interactions. Given these challenges in face-to-face communication, online spaces offer alternative avenues for social expression. Research shows a strong preference among autistic children for screen-based media, especially video games (Mazurek & Engelhardt, 2013). As Barrington Campbell, an autistic adult, shared: “If you are autistic, this can feel more than stressful... Online gaming means  autistic people can play in an environment we feel comfortable in and can control” (National Autistic Society, 2024). However, autistic children also face heightened online safety risks compared to peers (Macmillan et al., 2020). Mainstream digital spaces rarely support neu rodiverse communication and often neglect their wel l-being in interactions with non-autistic users. Few games, whether commercial or academic, are designed to connect autistic and neurotypical children through shared interests in gameplay (Spiel & Gerling, 2021; Xiao, 2024). The research team that designed Zopy, a collaborative iPad game that successfully fostered friendship among autistic students without adult mediation, encouraged designers to create more games that reflect the diverse spectrum of social behaviors in peer collaboration (Boyd et al., 2015). Building on this, more design approaches are needed  to guide the development of such inclusive applications. This paper proposes a participatory approach to digital play, involving collaboration among children, educators, designers, and GenAI. The rationale and methods for each type of collaboration are illustrated through the design case Together Tales, a collaborative storybook making game (Xiao, 2024). Collaboration among children, educators, and designers Why involve children?

Traditionally, adults have controlled all aspects of the design process, even when the intended users are children. As Druin (2002) noted, children have distinct prefe rences, curiosities, and needs that differ from tho se of adults. Their input is therefore essential for designing effective products. Involving children not only improves outcomes but also ensures they feel valued and heard.

Despite growing recognition of children as design partners, research involving autistic children remains limited. Most games for this group adopt top-down approaches with educational or medical goals, often overlooking their interests (Spiel & Gerling, 2021). Given the social challenges autistic children face, it is vital to include them in designing social games tailored to their needs. It is also worth noting that autistic children, lik e other neurodiverse groups, may be natural design partners. They often exhibit traits commonly labele d as “gifted,” such as excelling in creativity, vis ual-spatial skills, and attention to detail (Armstrong, 2010), making them highly capable contributors to design processes. How to involve children?

In many participatory design (PD) practices, resear chers acknowledge that children often struggle with  verbal communication, particularly when dealing with abstr act concepts (Druin, 2002). This issue is likely to  be even more pronounced in collaborations between autistic children and adults. As a result, drawing is freque ntly used as an alternative form of contribution to the design process (Hunt et al., 2023). In the approach proposed here, children express ide as through drawings and then select AI-generated elements that match their vision. These are directl y incorporated into the final design. The technical implementation of this GenAI collaboration is discussed in later sections. In Together Tales, children are prompted by animations within the game to draw characters they imagine appearing in specific story scenarios. They then se lect the generated images that best match their vis ion. The selected images retain certain features and are int egrated into the subsequent storylines, influencing  the progression of the narrative.

Why involve educators?

It is a common practice to involve educators in the participatory design process involving children (Piedade et al., 2024; Ey & Spears, 2020), to leverage their experti se and insights, which help ensure pedagogical guid ance. In the context of this paper, educators primarily func tion as stakeholders, providing critical insights t hat bridge pedagogical goals with the needs and interests of the children. How to involve educators?

While understanding why educators should be involve d is relatively straightforward, addressing how to involve them effectively requires deeper consideration. Excessive involvement by educators may reduce the freedom and space for children to contribute, while insufficien t involvement risks a lack of critical information in the design. How can we achieve an appropriate level of participation? The participatory design process proposed in this p aper borrows from the concept of guided play (Weisberg et al., 2016). It highlights the strategy  that adults create a setting that emphasizes a specific learning goal while still allowing children the freedom to e xplore and interact within the environment independ ently. Research shows that compared to a free setting and a highly structured s etting, children’s active participation in discovery, combined with appropriate scaffolding from a knowledgeable adult, a higher level of engagement and better learning result (Sim & Xu, 2015).

In Together Tales (Fig. 1), educators define the overarching storylin e—the goal of the journey for two players, structure of activities in which children engage during digital play. These activities may in clude collaborative drawing, assembling puzzle pieces, organizing photos to reconstruct a story, or other creative tasks. While the framework provides guidance, the specific s—such as what to draw, the themes of the photos, or the approach to completing the activity—are shaped by the children’s prior choices and left open for their exploration. This balance between structure and freedom ensures meaningful engagement and allows children to take ownership of their creative process.

The role of designers/researchers Rather than focusing solely on the final product, d esigners and researchers should prioritize creating  innovative processes and frameworks that position users and stakeholders as active, independent contributors. They are also responsible for assessing the technical feasibility of stakeholders’ ideas to ensure the process runs smoothly. Collaboration between autistic and neurotypical children Why involve both groups?

Peers support autistic children’s play in ways adults cannot (Jones, 1991), yet few digital play environments reflect neurodiverse interests. Many games focus narrowly o n autistic children’s social challenges rather than  fostering inclusive or neurodiverge nt-centered experiences (Spiel & Gerling, 2021; Xiao, 2024). Two major misconceptions contribute to this gap.

The first is that only autistic children benefit from peer interaction. Jones (1991) identified self-disclosure, trust, and companionship as key to friendship satis faction. A global study of 2,555 autism experts emp hasized traits like morality, trustworthiness, and kindness in autistic individuals (de Schipper et al., 2016)—qualities that make them valuable friends. Without opportunities to engage, neurotypical children may miss out on meaningful relationships, thus they tooneed to learn how to interact and collaborate with autistic peers. The second misconception is that autistic children are not capable of forming strong friendships. Though their styles may differ, research increasingly shows that autistic children build meaningful relationships with both neurotypical and autistic peers (Rodda & Estes, 201 8). Therefore, c reating more opportunities for positive interaction is essential to support relationship-building between neurodiverse children. How to involve both groups?

Collaborative play—where players coordinate actions toward a shared goal—effectively fosters social interaction between autistic and neurotypical children (Reuter et al., 2014; Khatab et al., 2024). Moreover, Piedade et al.

(2024) found that creating such artifacts enhances self-determination in autistic children. While few studi es

compare artifact co-design to digital collaboration within the screen in terms of social outcomes, the approach of “collaborative making through game” shows strong potential and merits further exploration. In Together Tales, children collaborate on playful design decisions,  such as choosing what kind of backpack to include, determining  the type of animal characters they encounter, and d eciding the sequence of photos displayed on a wall. These decisions collectively weave a unique narrative. At the end of the game, children receive a co-authored, printable digital storybook as a tangible artifact of their collaboration. Collaboration between children and GenAI Why involve GenAI?

The development of AI technology has significantly transformed and advanced educational practices. For example, AI can assist teachers in designing and delivering personalized, timely, iterative, and enriching lear ning experiences (Nguyen, 2023). However, a significant portion of current methodologies view AI as an extension of roles traditionally held by educators, behavior ana lysts, or other adult experts, aiming to achieve previously unattainable efficiencies (Amiri et al., 2017; Shahamiri & Thabtah, 202 0). Few approaches aim to help children enhance their own abilities, limiting their opportu nities to participate meaningfully in the design pr ocess and reducing their agency within support systems. Moreo ver, while discussions have highlighted the potenti al of AI as a creative assistant in participatory design (Zh ao, 2024), research focusing on its application for  children— especially autistic children—is scarce.

Despite this, AI has been used in various contexts to support youth in creating original work outside the PD framework. Ali et al. (2021) demonstrated how GenAI supported K-12 students and educators in co-designing learning materials, boosting teacher conf idence. Such cases underscore AI’s potential to enh ance children’s creativity and support its further exploration in participatory design with autistic children. Through the versatile application of GenAI, generative image model (GIM) empowers children to transform their sketches into content-consistent images. Vision-language model (VL M) further enhance s this process by converting visual outputs into descriptive text, in tegrating these capabilities with large language model (LLM) and carefully crafted prompts, AI can generate adaptive visual content tailored to children’s preferences, all while maintaining alignment with educator-defined scaffolds to ensure meaningful engagement. In Together Tales, generative AI is implemented using ComfyUI combined with Stable Diffusion and ChatGPT APIs (Fig. 2). Summary This paper presents an innovative design process th rough Together Tales—a storybook game designed to foster collaboration between autistic and non-autistic children. The process unfolds across three levels of collaboration. First, among children, collaborative making fosters  neurodiverse peer-to-peer interactions, cultivating authentic and inclusive social connections. Second, between children and educators, the approach ensures that pedagogical goals are met while providing children with the fre edom to create and collaborate independently, strik ing a thoughtful balance between guidance and autonomy. Third, between children and generative AI technologies, the integration of GenAI showcases AI’s potential to en hance their creative agency and further enables the m to cocreate unique experiences and products for themselves with adults and their peers.

## Discussion

This participatory design model holds great potenti al for broader application —not only for autistic children but also for those with ADHD, dyslexia, and other neuro diverse profiles, in collaboration with neurotypica l peers. The educational principles underpinning this design, such as collaborative play and scaffolding, are rooted in general education practices, suggesting their adaptability across various neurotypes and learning contexts. While this approach emphasizes children’s contributions to one  aspect of game design, future work will explore AI integration across the full design process, buildin g toward a more comprehensive child-centered framework. Upcoming iterations will also include structured observations and post-play interviews to assess its effectiveness.
