# AI Competence in STEM Education: An Exploratory Study of STEM Educators’ Confidence, Knowledge, and Pedagogical Practices

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This exploratory study examines STEM educators’ perceptions of their confidence, knowledge, and use of artificial intelligence (AI) technologies, as well as their views on the impact of AI on teaching and learning. Using a mixed-methods survey design, data were analyzed through descriptive statistics and thematic analysis. Findings revealed substantial variation in perceived AI knowledge, confidence, and pedagogical practices. Participants described developing AI competence through self-directed learning and professional networks, with limited access to structured training. Reported AI applications in teaching included lesson planning, feedback generation, and student projects, with practices var ying depending on purpose, familiarity, and access. While educators recognized AI benefits, such as increased efficiency and scaffolds for student learning, they also expressed concerns related to academic integrity, overreliance, and equity. These findings offer preliminary insights to inform future large-scale studies on ethical and equitable AI integration strategies responsive to disciplinary practices across STEM disciplines.

Large Language Models (LLMs) have emerged as particularly influential in STEM education, offering real-time automated assessment, adaptive feedback, and structured learning resources, helping students navigate complex scientific concepts  (Zhai, 2023).  Studies demonstrate that AI-generated feedback can support conceptual understanding and reasoning in physics (Wan & Chen, 2024) and formative learning in experimental sciences (Steinert et al., 2023). However, challenges persist, including educators’ skept icism, ethical concerns about plagiarism and data privacy (Xie et al., 2021). STEM educators’ perceptions of AI integration are influenced by both individual and contextual factors. Drawing from self-efficacy theory (Bandura, 1997),  confidence reflects educators’ beliefs in their capability to effectively integrate technologies in their teaching practices.  Technology adoption research further suggests that perceived usefulness, expected benefits, and anxiety influence  educators’ intentions to adopt AI-enabled tools (Wang et al., 2021). From the AI-integrated technological, pedagogical, and content knowledge (AI-TPACK) perspective, meaningful AI integration requires not only technological knowledge but also competence to  align AI tools  with disciplinary content and pedagogical strategies (Celik, 2023). In addition, contextual conditions, such as infrastructure, institutional support, and equity of access to tools and training, play a critical role in AI adoption, often outweighing demographic characteristics (Truong & Pham, 2025). Prior research indicates that educators tend to value AI-generated explanations for straightforward content, while human instruction is preferred for complex topics, highlighting the nuanced interaction between pedagogy and technology (Lee & Song, 2024).  Building on these theoretical and empirical perspectives, this pilot exploratory study examined STEM educators’ perceived confidence, knowledge, and use of AI  in teaching and learning, as well as perceived impact on pedagogical practices. These preliminary insights were intended to inform the design of a subsequent large-scale study and guide the development of adaptive, equity-oriented AI integration interventions to support STEM educators’ practices, address ethical challenges, and enhance student engagement in STEM. This study addressed the following research questions: RQ1. How do STEM educators perceive their (a) confidence, (b) knowledge, and (c) use of AI technologies? RQ2. What are STEM educators’ perceptions of the impact of AI technologies on teaching and learning? This study employed a cross-sectional survey design to investigate STEM educators’ perceptions of AI-enabled educational technologies. Participants were recruited from the STEP UP community, which is a national network of over 4,000 teachers, researchers, and professional societies coordinated by the American Physical Society (APS) to promote innovative practices, cultural change in physics classrooms, and broaden participation in STEM disciplines.

1099 Using a snowball sampling method, I distributed the survey invitation to the STEP UP community through the APS platform in December 2024. The survey was developed to examine STEM educators’ experiences with AI in education, consisting of demographic questions, closed-ended questions (quantitative), and open-ended questions (qualitative) to capture nuanced perspectives not constrained by predefined options (Reja et al., 2003). The survey instrument underwent iterative refinement: drafted by the researchers, reviewed in my research team setting, piloted with STEM educators, and revised based on expert and practitioner feedback to enhance clarity, coherence, and content validity (Dillman et al., 2014). I collected data using Qualtrics following Institutional Review Board approval, through the community discussion board. Of 55 respondents, 27 provided complete and valid responses for analysis.

## Participants

The study included 27 educators with diverse demographic and professional backgrounds. A majority were male (63%), while 37% were female. The sample was predominantly White (74.1%), with representation from Asian (7.4%), Hispanic/Latino/a (11.1%), Black/African American (3.7%), and other groups.  Teaching experience varied: 40.7% had 20+ years, while others reported 6– 10 years (25.9%), 11– 20 years (18.5%), or 0– 5 years (14.5%). Participants taught across multiple STEM fields, including physics (96.3%), as we ll as mathematics (40.7%), chemistry (25.9%), engineering (25.9%), computer science (14.8%), and biology (11.1%). Among participants, 44% (n = 12) reported participating in formal/informal AI training programs, while 56% (n = 15) indicated they had not.  STEM educators most frequently reported using chatbots (e.g., ChatGPT, Copilot) and management tools (e.g., LMS), with moderate use of machine learning applications, virtual learning environments, and personalized learning systems.

## Data analysis

To address the RQs, I analyzed both quantitative and qualitative survey responses. For the closed-ended items, I calculated means, standard deviations, frequencies, and percentages using R software to examine participants’ perceived confidence/self-efficacy, knowledge, and frequency in using AI technologies on a 5-point Likert scale (e.g., 1= not confident at all, 5=extremely confident). I analyzed the open-ended responses using reflexive thematic analysis, a non-positivist approach that does not aim to establish inter-coder reliability and is not required to be anchored in a theoretical framework (Braun & Clarke, 2019, 2024). This inductive process focused on semantic and latent meanings  without reliance on a pre-existing framework. Following the Reflexive Thematic phases: familiarization with the data, generating codes, developing themes, reviewing themes, defining and naming themes, and producing the report.

RQ1: STEM educators’ confidence, knowledge, and use of AI technologies

a) Perceived confidence in using AI technologies for teaching and learning

STEM educators reported a wide range of confidence levels, spanning from “extremely confident” to “not confident at all.” The mean score was moderate (x ̅  = 3.19; s = 1.18). The largest group (n = 9) identified as “moderately confident,” while three educators reported being “not confident at all.” This spread suggests that although most educators hold some degree of confidence, a small portion remains uncertain or hesitant about their ability to use AI technologies (see Figure 1).

b) Perceived knowledge of AI technologies for teaching and learning

Perceptions of AI knowledge showed a similar pattern, with responses ranging from “very knowledgeable” to “not knowledgeable at all” (x̅  = 3.07; s  = 1.17). A cluster of educators (n = 10) identified as “knowledgeable,” while two reported “not knowledgeable at all.” These results indicate that while many educators feel they have some foundational knowledge, there are noticeable gaps that may affect t heir ability to integrate AI tools effectively (see Figure 2).

These responses suggest that for many educators, AI knowledge development is informal and tool-oriented, reflecting procedural familiarity gained through ad-hoc training rather than structured professional training.

c) Frequency of AI technology use in the classroom

Reported use of AI technologies varied considerably from every class to once or twice per semester (x̅  = 2.22, s = 0.97). Among the educators surveyed, 37% reported engaging in the practice every week, while 29.6% indicated they did so once or twice per semester. About 25.9% engaged in the practice once or twice per month, and only 7.4% reported doing so in every class.  This indicates that although AI use is present in STEM classrooms, its integration is not yet consistent across daily teaching practices. Educators’ descriptions of their classroom practices revealed diverse applications of AI. Some incorporated generative AI (GenAI) tools directly into student learning activities, such as asking students to critique AI-generated solutions: “I asked ChatGPT to solve the homework problem and then started class by having students compare their answer to ChatGPT’s and then give ChatGPT a grade.”  (P5). Others encouraged students to use AI for study support and verification, while still stressing disciplinary expertise: “I encourage students to use AI when studying or checking answers, and I show them how they need to know enough physics to decide whether or not they believe the answers they’re getting.” (P3). Finally, some integrated AI into creative or project-based tasks, such as design challenges: “We have used Canva’s integrated image generator for students to realize their shoe designs…rather thandrawing it out” (P13). From the TPACK lens, these applications suggest AI functions as a task-level assistant, supporting contextual integration without deeply reshaping pedagogy. RQ2: STEM educators’ perceptions about the impact of AI technologies STEM educators expressed a mix of caution and recognition of AI’s potential. (1) Concerns about student learning and academic integrity were prominent: “I am worried about student learning. I do not want them to copy the answer.” (P8); “Students are blind consumers and don’t seem to have the wisdom to know the difference between enhancing their learning and bald-faced cheating.”  (P13); “In my AP Physics C class…The advent of AI has made this more challenging to grade as I am not sure the student is writing the report and abstract or if AI is doing it for them.” (P14).

Additionally, (2) equity and access issues were noted: “My university won’t pay for the full versions of any of the tools…Since not all of my students have access to the full versions, I can’t make their use required without it becoming an equity issue.”  (P27). Finally, (3) educators acknowledged that AI supplements but does not replace teacher expertise: “AI tools do not replace the knowledge of teachers. Not all products created are higher quality than mine.”  (P21). Overall, these reflections suggest that  AI can support teaching and learning in STEM classrooms, but its effective integration requires guidance, policies, and mindful attention to equity and student learning.

This exploratory study using both quantitative and qualitative findings examined STEM educators’ perceived confidence, knowledge, and use of AI-based technologies, as well as the perceived impact of AI on teaching and learning. Findings revealed substantial variation in educators’ self-reported knowledge and confidence, reflecting patterns identified in prior research (Truong & Pham, 2025; Wang et al., 2021). Many educators reported relying primarily on self-exploration or self-directed learning, collegial networks, and earlier experience to build expertise, with limited participation in professional training. This pattern points to limited institutional mastery experiences, an essential mechanism of teaching self-efficacy development (Bandura, 1997), and underscores the importance of collaborative learning structures in adapting to emerging technologies (Avci et al., 2025; Ertmer et al., 2012).

STEM educators reported applying AI tools across practices, such as lesson planning, feedback, consistent with literature emphasizing AI’s role in enhancing personalized learning, providing timely feedback, and supporting complex STEM tasks (Wan & Chen, 2024; Steinert et al., 2023).  From a TPACK perspective, these applications suggest that AI use is positioned as a  tool facilitating tasks rather thandeeply integrated with disciplinary pedagogy (Mishra & Koehler, 2006). S tudies also highlighted concerns abo ut academic integrity, overreliance, and equity, echoing findings on ethical challenges and inequitable access to resources (Celik, 2023; Cheah & Kim, 2025).

These insights provide an initial exploratory foundation for the next research phase, which will involve a larger and more diverse sample of educators across STEM and potentially non-STEM disciplines. Future research is needed to  investigate educators’ AI experiences and identify factors that support or hinder ethical, equitable implementation across varied disciplinary contexts. Rather than offering definitive guidance for practice, the patterns observed in this study highlight promising areas for further investigation, including deeper exploration of ethical challenges caused by AI systems and professional learning resources  to mitigate these challenges. As the ISLS 2026 annual conference theme emphasizes, “Impactful learning experiences are not only technically sound— they are also culturally responsive, contextually grounded, and collaboratively shaped.” Further research is essential to ensure that AI-enhanced STEM learning supports effective and meaningful learning experiences for all individuals.

## Limitations

It is important to note that AI applications differ across STEM disciplines; this small-scale, pilot exploratory study did not measure the extent to which educators’ specific disciplines integrate AI technologies into their pedagogical practices. Although participants reported perceptions of their knowledge, confidence, and usage, these findings may not capture the nuanced ways AI is applied in STEM disciplines. The study also acknowledges that the small, not fully representative, mostly physics-oriented sample limits the generalizability  and the study’s capacity to inform practice or policy at this stage. Accordingly, the results should be interpreted with caution and viewed as preliminary insights that open a line of inquiry for replication and future, larger-scale studies on AI integration across STEM disciplines.
