# Integrating Socio-scientific Reasoning into STEM Project-based Learning

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study utilized a computer-simulated learning environment (i.e., Aladdin) to implement collaborative socio-scientific reasoning activities into a project-based learning (PBL) curriculum. Twenty-one students from a suburban middle school in the Midwestern United States participated in the study. Assigned different social roles (e.g., planning board members, residents, and a solar company), students worked together to design a profitable solar farm while addressing the controversial local issue of granting a permit for its construction. The research findings indicate that students were engaged in socio-scientific reasoning activities despite their different social roles. This study provides implications for both research and practice in STEM education, particularly for efforts to enhance students’ PBL experiences.

## Objectives and significance

In science, technology, engineering, and mathematic s (STEM) education, project-based learning (PBL) is frequently used to develop students’ interdisciplin ary problem-solving skills (MacLeod & vander Veen, 2020; vanden Beemt et al. 2020). PBL provides stu dents with an authentic and intricate real-world problem to which they need to devise a solution or create an artifac t, grounded in assumptions, investigations, and data gathering (vanden Beemt et al. 2020). The complexity of the real-world problem requires students to work collaboratively with other members, exercise team skills, and share  and integrate knowledge and insights from differen t disciplines to critically design solutions (MacLeod & vander Veen, 2020). Integrating socio-scientific reasoning into PBL can enrich students’ learning experience by emphasizing the social, ethical, economic, and political factors in  solving real-world problems. Socio-scientific reasoning (SSR) refers to the ability to make informed decisions on  controversial, socially relevant, real-world socio-scientific issues (SSI) with reasoning, problem-solving, and critical thinking skills (Zeidler & Ni chols, 2009). Introducing controversial issues is an effective method for eng aging students in SSR. Encouraging students to coll aborate in debates with different social roles proves to be a practical and impactful approach to implementing SSR activities within classroom settings. For example, Kristensen and Knain (2023) chose petroleum exploration in the Barents Sea as the controversial issue to operate SSR activ ities in promoting senior secondary students’ attit udes and knowledge on climate change. Students were divided into group s with different social roles, including the government, non-profit organizations, and lawyers. In a final mock court session, students worked in groups to present evidence and arguments either in favor of or against further pet roleum exploration in the Barents Sea. Kristensen and Knain (2023)  found that students with different social roles demonstra ted different attitudes in SSR. However, there is limited research that has integrated SSR i nto PBL and investigated the ways in which students with different social roles engage in SSR.

This study attempted to implement SSR into STEM PBL by assigning students different social roles to collaboratively address a local controversial issue. More specifically, we explored the engagement and satisfaction of students with different social representations r egarding SSR in STEM PBL. Our results yield both theoretical and pedagogical contributions. While SSR was initia lly employed as a concept delineating students' sci entific literacy, we broaden its application as an approach  to enhance the framework of PBL. Additionally, we illustrate the feasibility and effectiveness of integrating collaborative SSR into PBL, directing students’ focus toward social and societal issues.

## Participant

This study involves 21 students from a class of suburban middle school in the Midwestern United Sta tes. These students actively engaged in STEM PBL that requires interdisciplinary knowledge and integ rates the SSR approach. Out of the 21 students who participated in the entire study, one chose not to disclose his/her gender, and one chose not to reveal his/her ethnicity. The final sample, therefore, comprised 10 females and 10 males. Students are either 11 (N = 5) or 12 (N = 16) years old. Regarding students’ ethnic backgrounds, 9 identified themselves as White/Caucasian and seven as Asian/Pacific Islander. The remaining students a re Alaskan Native and African American.

Research procedures Students dedicated a total of 12 periods across 12 days to complete the entire study within the classr oom setting. On the first day, they spent 10 minutes on a pre-survey, which encompassed a consent form  and demographic information. Afterward, students allocated three periods to solar energy science exploration  (section 1)  and another two periods to iterative design (section 2). The goal of the first two sections was to activate students’ solar science knowledge and iterative design knowledge in preparing for the SSR activities. Within the SSR activities, students spent two periods in section 3 solving the local controversial issue of granting a permit to the solar company and another two periods in section 4 on proposing the best solar farm design t o address societal issues. Upon completing the entire study, two open-ended questions (i.e., How satisfied are you with the outcome of the public hearing (i.e., scientific reasoning) and why ? If there is a second round of public hearing (i.e., scientific reasoning), what will you do differently to get a m ore satisfactory outcome?) were introduced to inqui re about students’ satisfaction with the entire activity.  Moreover, five students (P3S2, P3S13, P3S17, P3S18,  P3S21) volunteered to be interviewed about their experience throughout the entire study.

## Results

Students’ interview transcripts and their responses to the two open-ended questions regarding their satisfaction and suggestions to the SSR activities (i.e., the public hearing that involves section 3 and section 4) were analyzed to reveal students’ engagement  and satisfaction with SSR activities. More specifically, we summarized the engagement and experience within each social role b ased on their responses to open-ended questions and interviews. Three major themes emerged for each soc ial role. We illustrated the experience  of students who represented the solar company, residents, and planning board below.

## Solar company: The winner who was satisfied with the final results

Students who represented the solar company won the public hearing. They were granted the permit to construct a solar farm even though the approved design was not their design. For example, P3S15 who represented the solar company said, “I am satisfied because our proposal for a solar farm got approved, even though it wasn' t specifically ours.” P3S7 also said, “We (SolarX) are making more money while satisfying  the residents and accomplishing both groups needs.”  In particular, one of the leaders (e.g., P3S21) of the solar company enjo yed the opportunity to present in the public hearing as  P3S21 said “It's really fun to speak out whatever side I knew that whatever side it would be on I could handle it. However, the solar company team faced challenges in collaboration due to the large number of team membe rs, and not everyone contributed equally to the pub lic hearing.” As expressed by P3S21, "I pretty much carried the t eam because some of the people were not working at all.” Residents: The active contributor who was emotionally engaged in SSR Students who represented the residents actively con tributed their redesign solutions. Their  voice was well-received by all social representations. For example, P3S6 from one team of residents said, “Despite the fact that SolarX won, one of our residents, Grey House, created a design that SolarX has to use which made us proud.” P3S11 indicated that “I enjoyed doing the court and preparing arguments so that the administrators would choose our design.” In addition, some students were emotionally engaged in SSR as they  expected more time and effort to generate a better design. According to P3S4, “I agree with the decision that the planning board mad e was a good one but I do still wish that my house had made  a better design that would have one, but I know our design was not good because it did not make any profit it put solar x in debt”. Planning Board: The organizer who wanted to make everyone happy The planning board members (P3S5, P3S9, P3S19) assu med responsibility for coordinating the public hear ing activities. All three expressed enjoyment in partic ipating in these activities. P3S9 shared, "I liked playing with Aladdin even though it was lagged s ometimes. I also enjoyed the debate because it was very formal and seemed realistic. I also like being on the planning board." Moreover, the planning board members were content with their instructions for organizing the public hearing activities, as highlighted by P3S19: "I think the script was very clear, and everyone was happy with our decision." They wer e particularly pleased that their decisions brought satisfaction to everyone involved in the public hearing. For instance, P3S5 stated, "The decision is going to make everyone happy, and there's going to be a good profit."

## Discussion and conclusion

This study utilized a computer-simulated learning environment (i.e., Aladdin) to implement c ollaborative SSR activities into a STEM PBL learning curriculum. Following Kristensen and Knain’s (2023) practice of  assigning different social representations to student groups,  we implemented collaborative SSR into PBL. Students were assigned different social representations (i.e., planning board, residents, and solar company) in the SSR activities to solve a local controversial issue. The study res ults revealed th at students were engaged in the SSR activities even though they represented different social groups, suggesting the feasibility and effectiveness of collaborative SSR implementation in STEM education.

Students with different social roles focused on dif ferent aspects of SSR. The solar company, tasked wi th prioritizing profits and the economy over ethical and social considerations, demonstrated a greater concern for the outcomes of SSR. In contrast, the residents were emotionally engaged in their social roles, expressing a desire for their voices and efforts to be well-received. The planning board, serving as the ultimate decision-maker, exhibited an openness to multiple perspectives, aiming to ensure  satisfaction on both sides with the final results.  These findings align partially with Kristensen and Knain' s (2023) results, indicating that students who assumed conflicting social roles exhibited different attitu des. These preliminary findings suggest the need for a more extensive study with a larger sample size to explor e differences in SSR perspectives among different s ocial representations. Additionally, further investigation is necessary to evaluate how SSR activities affect students' knowledge and skills.
