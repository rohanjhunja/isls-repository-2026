# Reconceptualize Conversational AI as a Learning Medium, From a Holistic Learning Perspective

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Current university policies predominantly restrict conversational AI use in coursework, based on assumptions that students engage with AI under transmission orientations. However, these policies overlook transactional pedagogical possibilities. This study reconceptualizes conversational AI as a medium for holistic learning through structured transactional engagement rather than information delivery. Drawing upon Miller's Holistic Learning paradigm and Computer-Supported Collaborative Learning (CSCL) principles, we conducted semi-structured interviews with 12 educators enrolled in a Holistic Learning postgraduate course to examine: (1) current perceptions and concerns, (2) capacity to distinguish transmission from transactional AI applications, and (3) necess ary implementation conditions. Findings demonstrate most educators readily recognized conversational AI's pedagogical function depends on prompt design rather than technological properties. I propose integrating CSCL script principles with Miller's six con nection types to operationalize transactional AI through multi-level prompt design. This framework moves beyond the ban-or-permit binaries toward nuanced, pedagogically-grounded implementation that honors both academic integrity and innovative learning design.

Students increasingly use conversational AI tools—Natural Language Processing systems designed for sustained, multi-turn dialogue interactions (e.g., ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini) —for both everyday inquiry and academic coursework, yet universities face divergent responses to this practice. Most institutions delegate restriction authority to individual instructors, allowing course-level bans on conversational AI use based on concerns about academic integrity, information accuracy, and passive learning dependency (Loyola University New Orleans, 2024; Northeastern University, 2025; University of New South Wales, 2025). Others, such as Stanford Graduate School of Business, explicitly prohibit instructors from banning conversational AI in take-home coursework (Stanford Graduate School of Business, 2025). These policy debates, however, rest on an implicit assumption: conversational AI use primarily involves transmission-based learning in which students passively receive AI-generated content to substitute for their own thinking. This framing positions conversational AI as either a productivity tool (to be permitted with attribution) or a cheating mechanism (to be prohibited). Yet this binary overlooks alternative pedagogical possibilities. What if conversational AI could function not as an information source, but as a medium for structured transactional engagement to support learner-driven dialogue, connectionbuilding, and inquiry across multiple knowledge domains? This paper draws on Miller's holistic learning paradigm (Miller, 2019), a theoretical framework largely absent from current educational technology debates, to reconceptualize conversational AI's role in learning. Miller distinguishes between three curricul ar orientations: transmission (transfer of information), transaction (learning through dialogue and critical thinking), and transformation (deep personal and social change through interconnection). While current university policies appropriately restrict transmission-based Conversational AI use, they fail to distinguish this from transactional applications where conversational AI serves as a collaborative learning partner facilitating meaningful connections across knowledge domains. Despite growing policy debates, no empirical research has examined educators' perspectives on conversational AI through holistic learning frameworks. We address this gap through semi-structured interviews with 12 educators enrolled in a holistic learning postgraduate course. This  gap carries particular consequence for CSCL research: without frameworks distinguishing transmission from transactional AI engagement, designers lack principled guidance for scaffolding human-AI dialogue toward transactivity, and the field lacks empirical grounding for moving beyond binary policy responses. Our study examine three research questions: (RQ1)  How do educators perceive conversational AI's current role and associated concerns? (RQ2)  Can educators distinguish between transmission-based and transactional applications of conversational AI when introduced to Miller's holistic learning framework? (RQ3)  What conditions do educators identify as necessary for conversational AI to support transactional learning rather than transmission-based information retrieval? 324 Theoretical frameworks Holistic learning orientations: Transmission, transaction and transformation Miller's holistic learning framework (Miller, 2019) provides a systematic theoretical foundation for reconceptualizing conversational AI's pedagogical function beyond transmission-based applications. The three metaorientations of holistic learning-transmission, transaction and transformation-offers a developmental framework for understanding learning progression. Transmission involves "one way of imparting information to the student by the teacher or the text," while the transaction is "more interactive" and "involves dialogue between teacher and student or student and student" with focus on "problem solving and the development of cognitive skills" (Miller, 2019). This orientation also resonates with Deweyan views of inquiry, interaction, and learning through experience (Dewey, 1938).Transformation represents the most sophisticated level, involving "connecting to and nurturing the inner, imaginative life of the student" where "the knower and known become one; what we study becomes part of our being and does not remain separate" (Miller, 2019). Miller operationalizes these orientations through six educational connections: subject, community, earth, thinking, body-mind, and soul-heart connections. Miller emphasizes that "the holistic teacher will use all three of these approaches to develop a rhythm in their teaching" (Miller, 2019). Current AI prohibition policies address transmission-based concerns but fail to recognize transactional possibilities.

Conversational AI as a Learning medium, not a tool Understanding conversational AI's transactional role requires distinguishing tools from mediums. McLuhan

(1964) established that media are not neutral conduits but active shapers of perception and cognition—the medium

itself fundamentally alters both messa ge and recipient regardless of content transmitted. Tools align with transmission—students employ them instrumentally to achieve predetermined outcomes, terminating engagement upon goal completion. Mediums align with transaction—interaction generates emerg ent learning extending beyond initial objectives. Applying this distinction, Ch'ng (2024) illustrates that treating conversational AI as a tool is inadequate, as "When you use [conversational] AI, in reading the outputs, your mind has already been changed. You have consumed the output of AI" (Ch'ng, 2024). The medium shapes thinking patterns and knowledge engagement modes regardless of information accuracy. This medium-based perspective aligns with the computers as social actors (CASA) paradigm (Nass et al., 1994), demonstrating that individuals unconsciously apply social interaction rules to communicative systems. Structuring human-AI collaboration for transactional learning Recognizing conversational AI as medium rather than tool necessitates examining how human-AI relationships preserve learner agency while leveraging dialogic capacities. Lodge et al. (2024) map these relationships across cognitive function (offloading to extending capabilities) and interaction mode (individual to collaborative), identifying four configurations: cognitive offloading, extended mind, co-regulation, and hybrid learning. For transactional learning, the collaborative-extending quadrant enables conversational AI to participate in dialogue extending thinking while maintaining transparency about its interpretive role. Yan (2025) specifies four escalating agency levels: adaptive instruments, proactive assistants, co-learners, and peer collaborators. Hi gher agency levels—co-learner and peer collaborator—prove most conducive to transactional learning through sustained dialogue and perspective-taking. Computer-supported collaborative learning (CSCL) research provides critical frameworks for operationalizin g dialogue quality in these collaborative configurations. Central to CSCL is the concept of transactivity, defined as "building on learning partner's contributions, and engaging in and maintaining coherent and joint reasoning" (Hesse, 1997). Transactive engagement involves learners actively questioning, elaborating, and integrating contributions from learning partners rather than passively receiving information. Weinberger and Fischer's (2006) collaboration scripts demonstrate how structured interaction protocols can promote transactivity through specific design principles: regulation of learning activities, process-oriented instruction, and awareness induction. Despite theoretical frameworks suggesting conversational AI's capacity for transactional learning, empirical research examining how educators conceptualize AI through holistic learning orientations remains blank.

We conducted semi-structured interviews with 12 educators enrolled in a postgraduate course on Holistic Learning: seven practicing middle and high school teachers in Ontario, two doctoral students in Curriculum and Pedagogy, two student teachers, and one university instructor, representing diverse teaching contexts and career stages. Interviews were conducted in-person, and were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim. Interviews 325 followed a two-phase structure. Phase one elicited spontaneous perspectives through open-ended questions about AI experiences and concerns (RQ1). Phase two introduced Miller's transmission-transaction-transformation model only after Phase one concluded, pr esenting participants with a brief written summary before inviting assessment of AI's potential across his six connection types (RQ2, RQ3), minimizing theoretical priming of earlier responses. We employed reflexive thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2006) using a hybrid approach: Phase one data were analyzed inductively to surface educator concerns, while Phase two data were interpreted deductively against Miller's orientations and CSCL constructs. Themes were stabilized through iterative reading, memoing, and peer debriefing.

RQ1: Educators' perceptions of conversational AI's current role and associated concerns Educators described an educational landscape where conversational AI remains largely absent from pedagogical practice despite increasing availability. As one secondary teacher noted, while "there are some AI tools in the schools already" for administrative  or teacher-facing purposes, such as tools that "help us [teachers] to give formative feedback". But the reality is that "pedagogical use of AI, I don't see many in conversational AI yet" (P1). This absence reflects both technological and pedagogical factors: schools lack systematic frameworks for implementation, and educators harbor legitimate concerns about student usage patterns. When asked about these concerns, educators articulated two interconnected apprehensions that align with transmission-based usage scenarios. First, information integrity concerns emerged prominently. One teacher captured widespread unease: "I agree some teachers are fearful of chatbots. Like presenting cheat sheets of all answers to the students" (P2). This fear reflects documented issues with AI hallucination rates and training data biases. When students approach AI as an authoritative information source —wh at we characterize as tool-based usage—educators cannot reasonably expect students to possess domain expertise necessary for systematic fact-checking. The concern is not AI's existence but its positioning as answer provider rather thandialogue partner. Second, educators expressed deep concern about learning process authenticity. Multiple participants emphasized that acquiring answers circumvents essential cognitive development. As one educator posed the question: "What matters more: the answers or the thinking process?" (P2). Another made this concern explicit: "AI can show you the results. But what is the process?" (P8). These questions reflect understanding that transmission-based AI usage—where students seek definitive responses —compromises "tolerance fo r uncertainty and independent reasoning development" that constitute fundamental learning mechanisms. One teacher articulated this concern in terms of internalization and assessment validity: "If students just jot down the answer without internalizing it, the assessments mean nothing" (P2). Another emphasized student agency: "AI cando a lot of things, but people need to learn it by themselves. AI can't learn for the students" (P7). These perspectives reveal sophisticated understanding that the problem is not AI providing information, but students treating AI as a tool for bypassing intellectual struggle. Critically, these concerns are empirically grounded and pedagogically legitimate. RQ2: Distinguishing transmission-b ased and t ransactional applications through a holistic learning perspective When invited to consider AI through holistic learning frameworks, educators spontaneously distinguished between product-focused and process-focused engagement. The educator who posed "What matters more: the answers or the thinking process?" elaborated: "We are learning it for today, for this moment, for presence, for this curiosity" (P2), suggesting a shift from answer acquisition to inquiry-centered engagement. This same educator recognized corresponding assessment implications: "The problem is the assessment format is wrong. If students just jot down the answer without internalizing it, the assessments mean nothing" (P2). Most significantly, when explicitly asked to map conversational AI onto Miller's three holistic learning orientations, one teacher state d: "Chatbot cando transmission, transactions and transformation. It just depends on how students respond to it" (P2, emphasis added). This educator elaborated transactional possibilities: "Internalization and inquiry-based learning. It is adaptive" (P2). Another emphasized sustained exploration: "Conversational AI can be quite powerful in chatting with students, exploring some topics deeply" (P5). These responses indicate recognition that AI's pedagogical function depends on prompt design and student engagement patterns rather than inherent technological properties.

RQ3: Necessary implementation conditions for supporting transactional learning 326 Participants recognized Miller's connection types as operational pathways for transactional AI implementation, extending beyond purely cognitive engagement. One educator explicitly noted: "Social-emotional part, ChatGPT can be great" (P3), challenging assu mptions limiting AI to information processing and suggesting affective dimensions. Educators implicitly mapped AI's transactional potential across six connection domains. Subject connections enable cross-disciplinary "exploration following student inquiry"  (P11) rather than predetermined sequences. Community connections provide "low-stakes" (P4) dialogue practice addressing "power dynamics" (P4) and judgment concerns inhibiting transactivity with human educators. Thinking connections leverage AI's capacity to introduce alternative perspectives, contingent upon students actively interrogating "rather than passively accepting information" (P5). For body-mind and soul-heart connections, participants acknowledged AI's capacity for prompting reflection on "embodi ed experiences and personal meaning-making" (P7) while recognizing fundamental limitations in engaging existential dimensions requiring consciousness. Participants emphasized prompt design as a critical implementation factor. As noted above, AI's pedagogic al function "depends on how students respond to it" (P2), with distinction between "exploring some topics deeply" (P5) versus answer-seeking queries. This suggests transactional implementation requires explicit instruction in engagement strategies fostering sustained dialogue rather than single-turn information retrieval. Educators' recognition that AI's pedagogical function "depends on how students respond to it" (P2) reveals critical insight: transactional versus transmission-based usage emerges not from technological properties but from prompt design. This finding aligns  with our theoretical framework positioning conversational AI as medium rather than tool (Ch'ng, 2024). When engagement terminates upon receiving information, AI functions as a tool aligned with transmission orientation. When interaction generates emergent learning through sustained dialogue, AI functions as a medium aligned with transactional orientation.

The process-product distinction educators articulated—learning "for this moment, for this curiosity" (P2) versus answer acquisition—directly maps onto Miller's (2019) transmission-transaction differentiation. This reframing transforms the concerns identifi ed in RQ1: when pedagogical objectives center on inquiry processes rather than information acquisition, integrity concerns become learning opportunities (AI errors as chances for critical evaluation) and authenticity concerns shift from independent answer generation to genuine engagement in sustained inquiry. Drawing on the computers as social actors paradigm (Nass et al., 1994), this positions conversational AI as collaborative learning partner rather than information source. Educators implicitly recognized the need for structured prompt design of conversational AI, noting students must avoid "passively accepting information" (P5) and that AI's value depends on "how students respond" (P2). However, participants' acknowledged implementation remains nascent: "it is still at its early stage of discovery" (P10). I propose integrating CSCL collaboration script principles (Kobbe et al., 2007; Weinberger, 2011; Weinberger & Fischer, 2006; Vogel et al., 2017) with holistic learning orientations to operationalize transactional AI through multi-level prompt design. Vogel et al. (2017) suggested scene-level scripts may effectively support domain-specific knowledge acquisition, while scriptlet-level scripts foster collaboration skills. Applied to conversational AI contexts, play-level design establishes overall dialogic stance positioning AI as collaborative partner rather than answer source. Scene-level prompts structure phase-specific inquiry aligned with Miller's

(2019) connection types: exploration phases for subject connections (cross-disciplinary inquiry), elaboration

phases for thinking connections (perspective examination), and synthesis phases for community connections (dialogue integration). Scriptlet-level prompts scaffold specific cognitive operations: transactive questioning ("You suggested X. However, Y. How wo uld you reconcile these?"), justification requests ("What evidence supports this claim?"), and metacognitive monitoring ("How might your framing influence my response?"), supporting body-mind and soul-heart connections through reflective engagement. This integration of CSCL scripting principles into conversational AI prompt design enables systematic instruction tailored to pedagogical objectives, transforming AI from answer source to dialogue partner through structured intervention consistent with Lodge et al.'s (2024) collaborative-extending configuration. Educational institutions face urgent policy decisions about conversational AI amid rapid technological change. This research suggests that prohibition debates rest on incomplete pedagogical frameworks.  The CSCLinformed prompt design framework I proposed operationalizes Miller's (2019) holistic learning orientations in conversational AI contexts, supporting transactional learning while restricting transmission-based misuse. This approach enables cultivation of the six connection types — subject, community, thinking, body-mind, earth, and soul-heart—through which holistic learning is realized.
