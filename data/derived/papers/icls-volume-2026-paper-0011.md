# Whiteness by Omission: Constructing Science Learning in PD

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Rosebery et al. (2016) conceptualized interpretive power as (a) teachers’ attunement to students’ sensemaking as generative for science learning and (b) teachers’ pedagogical practices that build on students’ ideas and experiences. In our professional development (PD) work, we have found that ideologies of whiteness constrain interpretive power. Here, we unpack the role of whiteness in PD discussions with in-service elementary science teachers. We use interaction analysis (IA) to trace the influence of whiteness on discourse, focusing on teachers’ construction of science and students. Following Gholson and Ma (under review), we question the IA premise that ideologies must be named by participants to be considered relevant. To make these ideologies visible, we consider not only the ways that participants carry substrate forward, but also the ways that they omit substrate in subsequent turns. Objectives and purpose Rosebery et al. (2016) conceptualized interpretive power as (a) teachers’ attunement to students’ sensemaking repertoires as generative for science learning and (b) teachers’ pedagogical practices that build on students’ ideas and experiences. Rosebery et al. argued that developing interpretive power supports teachers to see contributions from students of color as equally generative as those of their white peers. In the past decade, researchers have worked to develop teachers’ interpretive power in service of equitable STEM learning (e.g., Kayumova & Dou, 2022; Louie et al., 2025; Miller et al., 2018; Warren et al., 2020). However, in our professional development (PD) work with in-service elementary science teachers (Daniel et al., 2023; Pierson et al., 2024), we have found that ideologies of whiteness, when not explicitly addressed in PD, constrain interpretive power. Whiteness is a socially constructed ideology that produces privilege and dominance for those racialized by society as white (i.e., White privilege), while producing oppression and marginalization for those society has marked as the racialized “Other” (Battey & Leyv a, 2016; Kolluri & Tichavakunda, 2023). Whiteness shapes teachers’ constructions of science (Mensah & Jackson, 2018) and students (Ma et al., 2025). Specifically, Western Modern Science as a discipline is historically based in “Eurocentric, positivist teachings of science that reify a White, male ownership of science” (Mensah & Jackson, p. 9). Following from this perspective, certain kinds of students are seen as “smart” vs. “disruptive” based on their alignment with white norms (e.g., Gholson & M a, under review). These ideological lenses can constrain teachers’ development of interpretive power by preventing them from recognizing and valuing the contributions of racialized students and their communities. We thus conjecture that explicit attention to whiteness is necessary for teachers’ development of interpretive power. We explore this conjecture using data from a PD session with in-service elementary science teachers, facilitated by Pierson. We use interaction analysis (IA) to trace the influence of whiteness on participants’ discourse. Following Gholson and Ma (under review), we question the IA premise that ideologies must be named by participants to be considered relevant. To make these ideologies of whiteness visible, we consider how participants carry substrate forward and how they omit substrate in subsequent turns. Our analysis reveals how ideas needed for interpretive power (e.g., valuing heterogeneity in science) were omitted and transformed by teachers, reinscribing ideologies of whiteness. Literature review An excerpt from Warren et al. (2020) was foundational to the interaction we analyze. The excerpt describes a science classroom interaction that occurred between Jonathan (an African American boy), his teacher (Ms. V), and his classmates categorizing the su n as living or nonliving. Jonathan wondered aloud how a nonliving sun could produce a living flower. Ms. V perceived this as incorrect and characterized Jonathan’s contribution as “problematic.” Later, she began to recognize the ecological way that Jonathan saw the sun as a life-giving force and the ways that students might encounter words like “living” in relation to the sun (e.g., having a “life cycle” like other stars). We use this example to unpack “science as white property”: an ideology central to our analysis. Science as white property At the core of Jonathan’s experience was the interaction of “structures of race, culture, and language based in whiteness as privilege” (Warren & Rosebery, 2011, p. 98). Whiteness as an ideology maintains racial dominance 23 through structural mechanisms of property and ownership. Legal scholar Cheryl Harris (1993) first articulated this reality by naming whiteness as a form of property. The relationship between whiteness and property was embedded in U.S. society, as humans who were racialized as Black were purchased, sold, and abused as literal property, and Indigenous communities were violently removed from land claimed as the property of white settlers (Kendi, 2016). Whiteness as property has been reified through legal action throughout the nation’s history (Harris, 1993). Harris conceptualized property broadly, encompassing material (e.g., land, financial capital) and immaterial (e.g., social capital, knowledge, cultural) resources. Those who possess whiteness as property ar e afforded rights to use, enjoy, dispose of, and exclude others from these resources. Mensah and Jackson (2018) built on Harris’ (1993) work, identifying science as a specific form of white property historically based in Eurocentric, positivist teachings. Many scholars have identified how science as a discipline is framed as objective, antiseptic, and fixed; an image that emerged in service of settler-colonial and empire-building projects (e.g., Kimmerer, 2013; Mignolo, 2011). Scientific knowledge from non-white, non-male communities is generally rejected as unscientific (e.g., Bang et al., 2012; Rae, 2025; Sengupta-Irving et al., 2021). Because science as white property dictates “what science looks like, who engages in science, and what science is for” (Mensah & Jackson, 2018, p. 9), scientific culture privileges those who hold dominant identities. Individuals’ rights to access and enjoy science decrease as they belong to multiple marginalized communities. Thus, white women have more right s to enjoy science than young Black girls. Young people are generally not considered capable of science (Daniel et al., 2023), and students from marginalized communities with non-dominant cultural knowledge and practices are most often excluded. Interpretive power and whiteness Disrupting whiteness in science is critically important in teacher professional learning. A number of teacher educators have worked toward this end (e.g., Le & Matias, 2019; Shah & Coles, 2020; Sleeter, 2016; Yoon, 2012). For instance, Le and Matias (2019) demonstrated how ideologies of whiteness create seemingly benign, normative practices in science education. When teachers fail to interrogate these norms, they may contribute to exclusionary experiences for marginalized students. By contrast, Shah and Col es (2020) offered a framework of “racial noticing” to support preservice teachers to recognize and respond to race during instruction. In our PD, we hoped that foregrounding interpretive power (Rosebery et al., 2016) would implicitly challenge ideologies of whiteness such as science as white property. The tenets of interpretive power (all students bring generative resources for science learning), stand in contrast to a Eurocentric image of science as authoritative, fixed, and objective. Yet our own attempts to value ontoepistemic heterogeneity did not adequately disrupt racialized norms (Daniel et al., 2023; Pierson et al., 2024). These challenges serve as the foundation for our research question: What role do ideologies of whiteness play in constructions of science and science learning? Through our analysis, we aim to develop analytic methods that allow us to trace the way whiteness works in interaction to constrain the development of interpretive power, so that we and other teacher educators candesign and evaluate approaches to more explicitly and effectively challenge pernicious ideologies of whiteness.

## Method

To explore how ideologies of whiteness shaped constructions of science learning, we analyzed video from a PD session using IA (Gholson & Ma, under review; Jordan & Henderson, 1995). Below, we provide a methodological context of our study and our specific approach to analyzing sensemaking in our data. Methodological background: Interaction analysis IA is a common analytical approach in the Learning Sciences (e.g., Keifert & Stevens, 2019; diSessa et al., 2016; Jordan & Henderson, 1995; Philip & Gupta, 2020) that has been brought into science education and teacher learning research to focus on processes of learning rather than outcomes (e.g., Daniel et al., 2023; Walkoe & Luna, 2020). The fundamental assumptions of IA include:

1. People make sense (McDermott, 1976; Pomerantz & Fehr, 1997)

2. People create meaning by building on each other’s verbal, gestural, prosodic, embodied, tonal and other

semiotic contributions through collaborative re-use and transformation of prior turns and ideas (Jordan & Henderson, 1995; Goodwin, 2018)

3. The ‘eavesdropping analysts’ (Lynch, 2011, p. 555) can gain insight into how participants understand

what they are doing as they demonstrate this understanding to each other (Schegloff, 1992)

4. Analysts are “accountable to the methods by which participants in that activity conduct and make sense

of their interactions” (Hall, 1999, p. 190) 24 One way to restate this fourth assumption is: only what is made relevant by participants is available for scholars attempting to understand the role of race, racial bias, and white supremacy in sensemaking. The rules of whiteness and niceness dictate that certain things are not  named: politics/power (e.g., race, disability), money (e.g., economic difference), and religion (e.g., cultural difference). Castagno (2019) explained that ‘nice people’ avoid experiences, knowledge, and interactions that are potentially uncomfortable or upsetting to them. Thus, when race, disability, or gender are most likely to play a role in an interaction, they are not likely to be explicitly named by ‘nice people’ doing the work of IA (e.g., Castagno, 2019; Lewis & Diamond, 2015). We thus argue that an overlimited interpretation of assumption four is not in line with the original intentions of founding IA scholars, nor is it practical. We therefore, in our own analysis, (a) attend to the sensemaking repertoires of all participants in an interaction, (b) make connections across time and space based on participants’ prior experiences (e.g., cultural substrate; Daniel et al., 2023; Keifert, 2021; Keifert & Marin, 2018; Goodwin, 2018), and (c) attend to absence, assumptions, and unspoken ideas by looking closely at sequential sensemaking to understand what is relevant to an interaction though not explicitly named (Gholson & M a, under review).

## Research context and data sources

Our data come from a multi-year PD for elementary science teachers in a large metropolitandistrict in the Southeastern U.S. Each year, participating teachers engaged in a week-long summer workshop and four 2-hour academic year workshops, designed and facilitated by the authors along with a larger research team. Approximately 9 new teachers joined the PD community each year. The broader research team included five members who identify as white women, one who identifies as an Asian-American woman, one who identifies as a white non-binary femme human, and one who identifies as a white man. We focus on a summer PD discussion intended to develop interpretive power. This discussion took place in project year 2. Prior to the discussion, teachers read an excerpt from Warren et al. (2020) that included the story about Jonathan and Ms. V (see literature review above). This activity was designed in response to deficit orientations that emerged around multilingual learners in the first year of our study (Daniel et al., 2023), prompting us as PD designers and facilitators tonegotiate clearer definitions of equity for our team (Pierson et al, 2024) and to create opportunities for sensemaking about these definitions  for teachers. The “Jonathan and the Sun” traditional systems of science, which has particular importance for minorized learners. However, in our planning, we did not consider how to make race and ideologie s of whiteness explicit—these themes emerged in our retrospective analysis below. The “Jonathan and the Sun” discussion was guided by the following prompts, which were projected on the board throughout small group discussions: (a) “What are the goals of science teaching  and learning, and how do examples like Jonathan & the Sun fit with those goals?” and (b) “What does the Jonathan/sun example make you excited or nervous about?” These prompts were intended to help teachers connect the reading to their own goals and experiences.

Our analysis focused on a video recording that captured one group’s 25-minute discussion of the “Jonathan and the Sun” excerpt. The group included four teachers new to the project participating in their first summer PD workshop. Ruth, Ellie, and Meredith identified as white women, and Frank as a white man. We chose to focus on this group because we wanted to examine the role of a facilitator in surfacing whiteness and constructing science learning. This was the only group with a facilitator present for multiple turns of talk (Ashlyn Pierson, white woman). Though we analyzed the entire discussion, we selected this five-minute episode for the present paper as it (a) allowed us to compare race-evasive and race-explicit facilitation, and (b) illustrated a distinct ideology of whiteness at work: science as white property (Jackson & Mensah, 2018). IA (Jordan & Henderson, 1995) is designed to capture the sequential sensemaking of people in interaction with each other and their worlds. IA studies learning as “a distributed, ongoing social process” (p. 42) and is a powerful approach to understanding the moment-by-moment nature of collaborative sensemaking (Walkoe & Luna, 2020). Key to this moment-by-moment understanding is a turn-by-turn analysis of interactions (Jordan & Henderson, 1995). After creating a rough transcript, we iteratively refine d our analysis and transcript to develop an understanding of ongoing sensemaking (Engle et al., 2014). In our work, we drew on two existing forms of turns (BUILD, REPAIR) and named a new form (OMIT) to make sense of how teachers were building understanding. Prior research has established ways of characterizing sequential turns in interaction. For instance, Philip et al. (2018) and Daniel et al. (2023) identify the role of stance building turns. BUILD turns respond to prior turns by contributing to the same line of thinking (as compared to SHIFT  turns, which move away from or contradict the ideas of prior turns). REPAIR turns originated with Sacks et al.’s (1974) foundational work; “Repair 25 mechanisms exist for dealing with turn-taking errors and violations” (p. 701). For example, if person A doesn’t hear person B, they might say “Huh?” and person B would REPAIR by repeating their prior utterance more loudly. REPAIR may also happen if a speaker recognizes a misunderstanding (e.g., “Oh, when I said a snack, I meant a quick dash to the vending machines, not taking time for a full meal”). Both BUILD turns and REPAIR turns are well-established in the literature.

For the present analysis, we needed a new turn characterization: OMIT turns. In naming OMIT turns, we sought to identify when a participant responds to some, but not all, of the ideas shared by the prior speaker. OMIT turns are related to the idea of a stance shift (or SHIFT turn) as discussed by Daniel et al.  (2023), but do not include a move away from all of a prior turn, instead taking up some of the ideas in the prior turn. OMIT turns are common in everyday interaction, but have not been the focus of pr ior IA literature. In our analysis, we found a series of OMIT turns leading us to question patterns across those turns. Centrally, this is not conversational drift. Rather, it is a pattern of omissions that each similarly reflect aspects of ideologies of whiteness. In recognizing these patterns of omission, we came to understand one possible explanatory mechanism that could in the future lead to more effective development of interpretive power. The recognition of the absence of an explicitly named phenomenon  operating to shape the context of human activity is by no means new. We were inspired most directly by Gholson and Ma (under review) in their Tamir Rice. They demonstrated how key omissions from the dispatcher to the police (that Tamir was likely a juvenile and that the gun he was holding was likely a toy) and racialized substrate (t hat Black males could be dangerous) contributed to the officers’ use of lethal force, resulting in the death of a 12-year-old child holding a toy. Their analysis highlights the importance of attending to what is missing (what is omitted from turn to turn) and what substrate is necessary if we assume participants are engaged in rational-to-them sensemaking. In our analysis of elementary science teacher PD, we characterized each turn as BUILD, REPAIR, and/or OMIT to understand how ideologies of whiteness shaped the construction of science and science learning. We recognized that sometimes one turn cando both (e.g., BUILD on one part of a prior utterance while OMITTING a response to another part of that prior utterance). With these three central turn characterizations, we were able to understand how whiteness operated to shape ongoing sensemaking about the “Jonathan and the Sun” excerpt, as well as about science and science learning more broadly. Our focal episode offered participants an opportunity to develop interpretive power by recognizing the importance of heterogeneity in their classrooms, even while dealing with the tensions created by the need for standardized testing. Despite this opportun ity, ideologies of science as white property disrupted teachers’ development of interpretive power in this context. Below, we trace how whiteness operated in this context, using OMIT turns to illustrate how heterogeneity of knowledge and practices was dropped from the discussion, reinscribing ideologies of science as white property.

Following this example, Meredith shared her own ideas of how a teacher might respond to students’ unexpected answers. Her stance represents an OMIT turn —either dropping Pierson ’s characterization and her own example of multiple answers as “right,” or a choice not to continue to build on this line of thought. Instead, Meredith took a different direction, moving away from multiple “right” answers while also adding a new idea. Whereas Pierson’s initial prompt and Meredith’s own previous example positioned students’ contributions as “rich and deep” (Pierson) and test answers being “outdated” compared to what students learn (Meredith), Meredith began this utterance from an assumption that students’ answers were not aligned with those of “the greater scientific community,” elevating the authority of the standardized test over students’ knowledge and sensemaking. She said: But I think you can also be like “Most of the scientific world agrees that it’s not living.” And so “We’ve had” you know “really great discussions and in OUR classroom like that is an acceptable answer however in” however you want to say it “but in the gre ater science community” like “it’s accepted as nonliving.” I don’t know. I don’t know, it’s hard. This stance only is sensible if interpreted within logics of whiteness. First, this comment is premised on the idea of consensus in the “greater science community” that classifies the sun as nonliving. This premise rests on an image of science as fixed and  agreed-upon. Second, this stance establishes racialized hierarchies of knowledge, though race is not explicit in the utterance. The underlying prompt was built on the case of Jonathan, an African American boy. Meredith’s stance positions Jonathan’s contribution (the sun as living) as “acceptable” “in OUR classroom” but not in the outside world, because it contradicts what is “accepted” in the “greater science community.” Here, while Christine made space for Jonathan’s idea, her response still echoed racialized hierarchies by deferring to the “accepted” answer of disciplinary science (“settled” expectations; Bang et al., 2012). At the same time, Meredith’s turn dropped from the discussion the premise that multiple “right” answers exist and should be valued (i.e., a choice to OMIT that idea). Still, Meredith appeared uncomfortable with this solution, and she ended her turn by shrugging while saying, “I don’t know, it’s hard.” Noticing that the idea of heterogeneity was omitted in this utterance, Pierson then moved to REPAIR. Specifically, they present scientists as authorities that own (and have the power to update) scientific knowledge. These examples contradict the message that children have agency to change science, and they reinscribe that there is one correct answer in science at a time. Despite the facilitator’s prompts to value heterogeneity in science, teachers converged on narratives like “there is one right answer that is accepted by science,” and they constructed an image of science that is fixed and objective and that is the property of authorities (often white males). The examples that teachers shared (Galileo and Plut o) are imbued with narratives of whiteness that they were likely unaware of and that we as facilitators and researchers only uncovered in our analysis. First, Ellie shared the example of Pluto being reclassified as a dwarf planet. While the example was intended to demonstrate that students’ ideas could change science, it may instead convey that science is fixed and can only be changed or updated by authorities through formal bodies. When we looked into this example, we found that re-classifying Pluto as a dwarf planet was a decision that privileged scientific authorities with financial means (e.g., North American or European scientists representing white, Eurocentric traditions). Pluto was re-classified in response to a new definition of “planet” that was determined by the International Astronomical Union (IAU) in 2006. Though the IAU is an international body composed of 9,785 members representing all 6 habitable continents (ICHB, 2025), the vote on this definition was held in person, in Prague, on the 11th day of a 12-day conference (IAU, 2020a; 2020c). Only 2,412 members were able to attend at least a portion of this conference, and less than 450 were pres ent for the vote, representing fewer than 5% of the IAU’s membership (IAU, 2020b; 2020c). Scientists with the financial means to travel to Prague and with fewer caretaking responsibilities were more likely to be able to attend, suggesting that only certain kinds of scientists were welcome to participate. Rather than illustrating that anyone can make a contribution to science, this example underscores the ways that science as white property continues to serve as a gatekeeper in decision making. Second, Meredith shared the example of Galileo and a heliocentric model of the solar system. On face value, this is a story of a white male as a scientific revolutionary. Digging deeper, we found further ideologies of whiteness at work. Encyclopaedia Britannica (The Editors of Encyclopaedia Britannica, 2025), a resource representative of school textbooks, attributed heliocetrism to Western philosophers and scientists before Galileo, looking as far back as the 5th century BC to philosophers Philolaus and Hicetas. They particularly focus ed on Nicolas Copernicus, a Polish astronomer who popularized heliocentrism in 1444. However, they overlooked the contributions of non-Western astronomers in building this theory. For instance, there is evidence that Copernicus was influenced by Arab and Muslim astronomers, such as Ibn al-Shatir (Guessoum, 2008). Moreover, the Kerala School of Indian astronomers, including Nilakantha Somasutvan, developed a model in which planets move in orbits around the sun in the 15th century (Ramasubramanian et al., 1994). Unpacking these examples demonstrates that whiteness works to reinscribe narratives that position science as white property. These narratives invisiblize mechanisms like gatekeeping and erase the contributions of racialized scientists. Ultimately, they can limit the development of interpretive power by valuing authority over heterogeneity. Our analysis demonstrates that simply valuing heterogeneity as a facilitator was not sufficient to challenge these entrenched ideologies, as heterogeneity was repeatedly omitted by teachers as they drew upon the familiar narratives of scientific authority. Instead, we propose that research is needed to understand how to effectively and explicitly challenge ideologies of whiteness in work with teachers to develop interpretive power. Methodologically, our analysis demonstrates the potential of focusing not only on what is said, but also what is not  said to trace and later challenge the role of whiteness in PD discussions. First, we outline four IA assumptions that are often mentioned piecemeal in other literature, but have not been articulated in such a clear, ordered way in previous research. This in turn allows for an articulation of the paradox associated with the fourth assumption (analysts are “accountable to the methods by which participants in that activity conduct and make sense of their interactions," Hall, 1999, p. 190) and the demands of niceness and ideologies of whiteness. We wrestle assumption four away from strawman interpretations and instead name avenues for systematic analysis of participants’ sensemaking through relevance-based analysis (e.g., Keifert, 2021), scaled analysis (Daniel et al., 2023), and analysis of BUILD, REPAIR, and OMIT turns.
