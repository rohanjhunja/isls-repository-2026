# A Reality Check on Collaboration Skills: How Experiences During Interdisciplinary Collaboration Shape Collaboration Self-Efficacy

**Conference:** ISLS 2023

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
The role of collaboration self-efficacy for interdisciplinary computer-supported collaboration has not received much attention so far, although it may be a relevant factor. In a laboratory study that simulated interdisciplinary collaborative problem-solving with N = 72 university students, we investigated what experiences shape the development of collaboration self-efficacy and what effect a collaboration script has. The results show that experiences of technical coordination were negatively associated with collaboration self-efficacy and that a collaboration script was not particularly beneficial for fostering collaboration self-efficacy.

## Theoretical background

Interdisciplinary computer-mediated collaboration has become a reality in m any areas of modern society. However, for this kind of collaboration to be efficient and constructive, the peopl e involved need exceptionally good collaborations skills. Mastering collaboration within a given discipline is already challenging, so interdisciplinarity adds another layer of complexity as it comes with more oppor tunities for misunderstandings, potential for conflicts, as well as unshared and implicit information that can get overlooked. Accordingly, it is a central challenge to prepare students for their future by equipping them with appropriate skills for effective computer-based collaboration (Kollar, Fischer, & Slotta., 2007). However, knowledge alone is not enough. To make sure that learners apply their knowledge in practice, especially when it comes to challenging situations, self-efficacy has been shown to be just as important (Bandura, 1997). Consequently, collaboration self-efficacy, which describes the degree to  which individuals trust in their abilities to perform successfully in collaboration situations, may be another  critical factor (Mattson, 2011). However, research on collaboration self-efficacy, especially in interdisciplinary and computer-mediated settings, has been neglected in CSCL research so far. From general self-efficac y research, though, we know that mastery and vicarious experiences, as well as verbal persuasion are key factors for self-efficacy (Banurda, 1997). To foster collaboration skills, external collaboration scripts as scaffolds to  structure and stimulate effective collaboration processes, have proven to be an effective instructional support (Radkowitsch, Vogel, & Fischer, 2020). In many contexts, scaffolding has been shown to also promote se lf-efficacy (e.g., Grothérus, Jeppsson, & Samuelsson, 2019). Against this background, we investigate two research que stions: 1) How do experiences during interdisciplinary collaboration shape collaboration self-efficac y? 2) What effect does a collaboration script have on the occurrence of experiences relevant for changes in collaboration self-efficacy and on changes in collaboration self-efficacy?

## Method and procedure

We conducted an online laboratory experiment, in which a convenience sample of under graduate university students collaborated via Zoom (as this is a program that most students we re already familiar with). The participants did not know each other and were recruited from multiple German universities and studied different subjects not related to the content of the study. Participants were randomly assigned to a group of three and each group was then assigned to one of three experimental conditions. For the present study, only data of two conditions (control condition and collaboration script condition) is used. This partial data set c onsists of 72 participants working in 24 triads, divided equally between the control condition and the collaboration script condition. As the main goal was the improvement of interdisciplinary collaboration skills, participants worked on solving two problems to simulate interdisciplinary collaboration; both problems centr ed around deciding on the best way to change a kindergarten building and a visualization of the floor plan was shared in Zoom. We evoked typical challenges of interdisciplinary collaboration by having each participant taki ng on one of three roles (architect, daycare management, or fire and health protection), which cam e with a set of unshared information and affordances. The two problems required that group members pooled their unshared and partly contradicting information and discussed alternative solutions. Participants in the control c ondition received no support during collaboration. Participants in the collaboration script condition received support while  solving the first problem. During each phase, text prompts were shown how  to establish and maintain effective collaboration (based on Meier, 2007). Data on collaboration self-efficacy was collected using a pre-and posttest que stionnaire. Additionally, we recorded the groups’ problem-solving during the videoconference. Based on the dimensions of collaboration quality by Deiglmayr (Meier, 2007), we coded and counted mastery and vicarious experiences of participants that occurred during collaboration. For example, regarding the dimension information pooling, we assessed how often the participants performed (mastery) and observed (vicarious) information pooling. We also coded for verbal persuasion during collaboration, e.g. how often a participant’s contribution was applaude d or criticized. We conducted inference statistical analyses to answer the research questions and followed up with an in-depth exploratory case analysis on the eight participants with the most extreme changes in collaboration self-efficacy.

## Results and discussion

Regarding RQ 1, regression analyses revealed that mastery and vicarious e xperiences of technical coordination, mastery experience of time-management, and verbal persuasion in form of counterarguments to own contributions during the collaboration process were associated with a decrease in collabor ation self-efficacy. Regarding RQ2, technical coordination occurs more often in groups, that are supported by an external col laboration script. Additionally, our analysis shows no significant difference in the development of c ollaboration self-efficacy between the control and script condition (F(1, 70) = 1.364, p =.247). The exploratory case analysis revealed that strong positive and negative developments in collaboration self-efficacy between pre-and posttest mainly occur in learners who had rated their self-efficacy well below- / above-aver age in the pre-test. Learners with a high increase of collaboration self-efficacy perceived the collaboration task as  cognitively more demanding yet reported higher satisfaction with the collaboration process than learners with high decrease of self-efficacy. The results show that collaboration self-efficacy is a very complex aspect w ithin the development and execution of collaboration skills. First, the occurrence of technical problems, despite not being directly related to the collaboration process but e.g., to problems using the video-conference tool in general, was related to how learners evaluate their interdisciplinary collaboration skills. This may indica te that the overall collaboration experience, in contrast to specific aspects during collaboration, is crucial for th e development of collaboration self-efficacy. Furthermore, the lack of effects of a collaboration scr ipt raises further question about how we can positively influence collaboration self-efficacy. The explorative case analysis  indicates that great changes in learners’ collaboration self-efficacy from pre-to posttest may show an adjustment of learners’ self-efficacy to their actual interdisciplinary collaboration skills. We conclude that the present  study can be seen as first steps in exploring and understanding collaboration self-efficacy in the context of interdiscipli nary computer-mediated collaboration. In doing so, attention was drawn to the relevance of the construct for the success and effectiveness of collaboration processes that has so far been neglected in CSCL research.
