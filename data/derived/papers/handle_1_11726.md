# Facilitating Learning of Socio-scientific Issues through An Adaptive Critical Pedagogy Module

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study explores Grade 9 students' engagement with microplastic pollution as a socio-scientific issue (SSI) using a critical pedagogy approach in Mumbai, India. Through a two-day workshop involving Foldscope microscopes, students transitioned from limited initial engagement to active, inquiry-driven exploration. Analysis highlights the influence of cultural contexts and the potential of critical pedagogy to enhance understanding and critical thinking about SSI. Recommendations for culturally adaptive, scaffolded, and sustained pedagogical interventions are discussed.

## SSI and microplastic pollution: Context setting

Microplastic (MP) pollution has emerged as a comple x socio-scientific issue (SSI), intersecting environmental, public health, and socio-political domains. Nuanced understanding of SSI req uires critical pedagogical practices that help learners identify problems embedded in th eir everyday lives by carefully examining, reflecti ng and establishing its inter-connections with seemingly unrelated aspects of bro ader elements. This perspective is aligned with broader goals of scientific literacy t hat stress moral and ethical reasoning in scientifi c decision-making (Sadler, 2004), and the relevance of context ualized science learning (Eastwood et al., 2012). Socioscientific issues require not only scientific reasoning but also moral and cultural judgment (Zei dler & Nichols, 2009), making them especially relevant to learners from diverse socio-cultural contexts.

## Research design, method, and analysis

This is a qualitative study based on classroom enga gement, observation and intervention among 16 Grade  9 students (9 girls, 7 boys) from a School located in  a slum area of Mumbai, India. Data includes multip le videos captured through multiple cameras, audio recordings  of facilitator-student conversations, and detailed ethnographic observations of school surroundings and transcriptions of interactions with students. Dat a were analyzed through interaction analysis (Jordan & Hen derson, 1995), employing the Jeffersonian transcrip tion method, allowing nuanced capture of conversational dynamics, speech patterns, gestures, and non-verbal engagement. Keeping critical pedagogy as a theoreti cal framework, transcripts were carefully coded, id entifying shifts from passive to active engagement, conversat ional hesitations, peer interactions, and responses  to facilitator strategies. These analytical practices provided clear evidence of how students navigated t hrough the intervention and how their responses related to their socio-cultural context.

## Cultural context and linguistic engagement

Initially, classroom interactions were marked by pr olonged silence, minimal verbal engagement, and one-word responses, demonstrating the influence of Bernstein ’s (1971) restricted linguistic codes characteristi c of students' working-class socio-economic backgrounds. This initial hesitance was st arkly contrasted by lively, free-flowing peer interactions outside the formal classr oom environment, highlighting a pedagogical disconnection between home and school cultures. Usi ng critical pedagogy as a lens, we interpreted this disconnect as evidence of a ‘hidden curriculum’ per petuating socio-cultural hierarchies rather than facilitating authentic, critical engagement with socio-scientific issues. Freire (2017), Apple (2014), and  Giroux (2024) have argued that such conditions demand educators act as  transformative intellectuals who challenge dominan t structures through pedagogy. The intervention strat egically addressed this by creating a democratic, o pen, inquiry-driven classroom environment, enabling students, as  illustrated by student MP5’s significant transformation, to move beyond restrictive codes towards more reflective, elaborated modes of expression. Think like a scientist: A three-phase workshop intervention The two-day intervention was structured to foster gradual e ngagement. On Day 1, students were introduced to Foldscope; a frugal paper microscope, and explored how it could be used to observe particles invisible  to the naked eye. This hands-on orientation sparked initial curiosity, despite s ome visible hesitation. On Day 2, the three-phase module began with discussion exploring studen ts’ prior knowledge. Then, students were given Foldscopes to detect microplastics within classroom  elements i.e. dust hair strands etc., engaging the m in practical, hands-on scientific inquiry. Notably, student MP5 exempli fied a transformative shift from passive to active inquiry. Initially unable to observe MPs cle arly, MP5 demonstrated perseverance by independentl y seeking peer support, closely observing facilitator  demonstrations, and eventually succeeding in detec ting MPs (see Figure 1). His journey exemplifies how critical pedagogy, th rough responsive and supportive facilitation, can spark curiosity, foster autonomy, and drive mea ningful, engaged learning experiences, ultimately b ridging the gap between academic science and personal relevance. Students collaboratively proposed practical solutio ns reflecting their socio-cultural narratives on sustainability (e.g., plastic bans, waste management). While these  solutions indicated genuine engagement, they also highlighted limited awareness of broader environmen tal complexities, such as the ecological impacts of alternatives like cotton bags. These observations underscore the need for extended and scaffolded engagement to deepen students’ critical analysis skills, enabling  them to understand complex, systemic relationships  and implications inherent in SSI topics (Zeidler et al., 2005). A model-module: Critical pedagogy for SSI awareness Reflecting on the implementation, we propose an ada ptive pedagogical model emphasizing cultural relevance, linguistic accessibility, and scaffolded learning e xperiences. Strategic timing aligned with school ca lendars, sustained engagement extending beyond classrooms, a nd facilitator preparedness are critical for effect ive SSI education.

## Conclusion and implications

Our study underscores the transformative potential of critical pedagogy in engaging students with comp lex socio-scientific issues like microplastic pollution. It a lso underscores the significance of frugal educatio nal means like Foldscope. However, meaningful education al transformation requires culturally responsive, contextual, scaffolded, and sustained interventions, bridging the gap between learners' everyday exper iences and academic science.
