# Navigating Moments of Tension and Uncertainty in Co-Design of University Curriculum

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Student co-design promotes an alternative educational structure where students are involved in the classroom design process, honoring students’ agency in their learning. However, codesign differs from other design processes because it is a largely collaborative and symbiotic process where groups of designers and facilitators are required to work collectively while navigating disagreements and tension. Due to the unique nature of the co-design environment, moments of tension and uncertainty are common in the design process and can potentially interfere with group productivity. Common examples of tension that often impede designers from progressing include uncertainty of how to move forward or disagreements in creating the design product itself. Through data gathered from a co-design journey involving college students working on redesigning an undergraduate education course, this paper illustrates the design processes needed to navigate moments of uncertainty and tension during co-design to ultimately achieve a collaborative and authentic design outcome.

Major issues addressed Co-design is a design process in which designers collaborate as participants in the creation of a design product. Our paper focuses specifically on student co-design, a design process involving university student designers redesigning an undergraduate university course curriculum. The collaborative nature of co-design allows for the potential of designers’ experiences and insights to be equally valued and heard, disrupting the usual hierarchies that exist in curriculum design (Iversen, Smith, & Dindler 2017; Tierney, Horstman, & Tzou, 2021). Such a process not only creates a product that is reflective of the most important components of the course curriculum but also one that is also most authentic to the designers (Pitt, et al., 2019; Tierney, Horstman, & Tzou, 2021; Yip et al., 2017). However, because co-design relies on group work and healthy collaboration to be successful, unity and productivity among the designers is crucial. Yet, it is unrealistic to expect disagreements and uncertainty tonever arise in group work, creating a need for processes to navigate these scenarios. Our research questions for this work are: In moments of tension and uncertainty, what are the different ways designers and facilitators resolve tension or uncertainty? In what ways does tension positively and/or negatively impact educational co-design (put another way, is tension in codesign necessarily a bad thing)?

## Potential significance

Other studies of co-design have acknowledged its effectiveness in education and design (Camino & Zeldin, 2002; Tierney, Horstman, & Tzou, 2021; Yip et al., 2017). However, despite the praise that co-design receives, there is a lack of research regarding its processes. Specifically, there is a need for research that outlines the processes required for successful co-design to occur. While this paper contributes to education research in general, it specifically contributes to literature in Learning Sciences focused on design stakeholders and design processes. This paper highlights the nuanced practices of facilitation and group work during co-design. Through the approach to co-design used in this study, participants’ perspectives and experiences are incorporated in ways that impact the design and redesign of learning environments. Without attuning to how facilitators and groups operate during co-design, student co-design can run the risk of reifying hierarchical power dynamics when students are involved in performative ways that do not incorporate their voice or remediate the historically marginalized roles within education (Camino & Zeldin, 2002; Giroux, 2009; Kirshner, 2015; Tierney, Horstman, & Tzou, 2021; White & Wyn, 2013). Through our data, we identified some critical components in the actions of designers and facilitators in navigating moments of tension and uncertainty. We also examined whether all tension is a disruptive force and found that productive tension can be an asset that design groups should not try to avoid. These findings offer guidelines for both facilitators and designers to resolve moments of tension and uncertainty. ICLS2022 Proceedings 1173 © ISLS Theoretical approaches In our design work and in this paper, we take a sociocultural perspective on student co-design, in that we view participation and learning as an interaction between individuals (in this case university students and faculty) and the contexts in which they participate (Holland, Lachicotte, Skinner, & Cain, 1998). The process of learning, while a different conceptual category, cannot be separated from the process of emotion, belonging, or identity development (Nasir, Roseberry, Warren, & Lee, 2006). We also used communities of practice (Wenger, 1998) as a theoretical frame of how individuals participate within learning communities, examining the ways in which participation shifts over time in a community of practice.

Goals, values, and practices are in part inherited from the history of the group and its domain of practice, both from the individual histories of its members across various social worlds and through their ongoing negotiation within a community of practice. Different members of a community have different positional identities, with different kinds and levels of influence over the group’s activity (Holland et al., 1998; Holland & Lave, 2001). So too, the identity and learning of the community, as a collective, shifts over time. We used Wenger's (1998) concept of “joint enterprise” to capture ongoing negotiations of a co-design group's sense of itself, what binds members to the group, and what they accomplish together. Group members' participation in the group both determines and is influenced by this sense of “what we are about.” To this we add the construct of productive friction which Ward et al. (2011) defined as “dissonance… when two or more social worlds conflict that leads to more sophisticated practice” (p. 14). In our analysis, we use productive friction to understand moments of tension in the design process, negotiating new practices, and directions in joint enterprise.

## Methods and data sources

This work was set in the context of a university aiming to transform its education courses by centralizing just, equitable, and inclusive education. Due to the pandemic, all design sessions were conducted online over the conferencing application, Zoom. All documentation of the design process was also recorded virtually, using Zoom audio and video recordings. The designers also used Miro, an online whiteboard that was captured and saved after every design session. The design team consisted of six main designers (Katherine, Jeff, Mitch, Hannah, Vanessa, Chrissie), three feedback groups (in which Author one, Sharon, participated), and one facilitator (Author two, Gavin). All designers, including those in the feedback groups, had taken the Adolescent Development course with the facilitator, Gavin, as their professor in the past two years. This paper examines the behaviors of the six main designers and the facilitator as they redesign the undergraduate Adolescent Development course. Because all meeting sessions occurred over Zoom, ten sessions of video and audio data were collected. Miro, the virtual whiteboard the design team used, was also documented at the end of every session. Qualitative data analysis occurred in four coding phases: open coding, focused coding based on research questions, writing analytic memos looking in and across codes and data, and generating hypotheses. We used initial qualitative coding and analytic approaches that are inductive and abductive – grounded theory (Corbin & Strauss, 2008). Open coding of participants’ own words was conducted to examine how participants co-constructed the design space and the norms and practices within. The videos that were analyzed were chosen based on the amount of significant changes that occurred on the Miro whiteboard. The sessions where the most changes occurred on the whiteboard signified shifts in practice (compared to refining already established practices) and so were chosen to examine potential tension during those shifts. The videos were also analyzed in chronological order to preserve the linear co-design process. Video and audio recordings of the design sessions were analyzed by looking for recurring themes or actions taken by members of the design team. Those themes and actions were translated into codes, grouping similar moments together. These moments in the design process were then reanalyzed through the lens of tension and uncertainty, pinpointing moments where disagreement and confusion arose.

## Major findings

Through data gathered during the design sessions, we identified three processes that focused on addressing tension and uncertainty in the co-design environment: 1) Facilitator Guidance, 2) Community Building, 3) Productive Tension. Two of these findings deal with the resolution of tension and uncertainty, while the third relates to the nature of tension and uncertainty, assessing whether these moments are necessarily unproductive. Facilitator guidance ICLS2022 Proceedings 1174 © ISLS Facilitator guidance is an integral part of navigating tension in co-design due to the facilitator’s ability to act as a guide for the designers. How facilitators provide guidance is essential in moments of tension or uncertainty because they are usually the person with the most knowledge on both the subject matter and the co-design process. As such, this kind of deeper knowledge of content and design process can help designers find their way out of tension. One example of this occurs when the design group is introduced to some changes that a feedback group had made on their Miro board in design session five. The feedback group had previously left comments on the group’s Miro whiteboard for them to consider. Gavin offers the group multiple ways to alter the Miro board based on their feedback. Ultimately, Gavin leaves the final decision up to the designers. This example demonstrates the benefit of experience and knowledge in the design process. Because Gavin has more insight in the design process and the subject matter, he can guide the group, giving them options on how they should proceed. With his expertise, the design group is shown possible routes to incorporate new information into their design, aiding the group in finding the best way forward. This moment is also significant due to Gavin’s leaving the decision up to the designers, providing a space for the design team tonegotiate their joint enterprise. Here, he demonstrates that facilitators should be involved in the design process, but that designers should make the final decision to ensure an authentic design product.

Another form of facilitator guidance is their perspective as a meta-designer. Student designers often become so involved in the design process that they can no longer see the bigger picture or produce new ideas capable of pulling them out of disagreements and confusion. Facilitators can thus bring new perspectives, aiding the designers in pivoting their thinking. An instance where the facilitator uses their perspective as a meta-designer occurs when the design group is introduced to the idea of equitable and inclusive education (JEIE) in session six. As the designers take some time to look over both the ideals of JEIE and their Miro board, they struggle to combine them. When Gavin asks the group how they would merge these ideals into their Miro whiteboard, he is met with no response. Eventually, Hannah asks, “Um, do you mean incorporating JEIE towards the principles that you’ve brainstormed and added, or towards what we've done already?” Then Katherine comments on the challenges that connecting JEIE to their Miro board might pose: “I feel like we will struggle less putting JEIE into some constellations, whereas others like Biological Development…we might struggle a little more.” Noticing their confusion, Gavin offers the designers a question to redirect them: “Are the ideals of equitable and inclusive education central in the Adolescent Development course, or are they only present?” This instance demonstrates the importance of having a facilitator who is "out of the weeds” and has a "bird's eye view” of the design process. In this instance, Gavin identifies the group's struggle between incorporating and centralizing equitable education, naming space that they are experiencing tension, something the designers may not have caught themselves. The facilitator’s unique perspective led the design group to rethink their trajectory, providing an opportunity for productive friction and negotiation, eventually clearing up group uncertainty and giving them a direction to pursue. Community building and temporary leadership Community building primarily deals with designers feeling comfortable around each other. The goal is for the designers to feel safe offering opinions, challenging ideas, and questioning one another. Community building is vital in co-design because it increases designers’ willingness to step into certain roles to guide the design group out of tension. Often, the group requires temporary leadership, allowing designers to step into roles they may not always be in, and lead the group out of tension or confusion. Feeling comfortable and safe in a creative environment also allows designers to take risks, leading to more creative and innovative design outcomes. A moment that illustrates how roles centered in community building can be used to reduce tension or uncertainty occurs in session six when the design group struggles to organize their sticky notes in the constellation “classroom environment” while also incorporating the ideas of JEIE. The six main designers were in the process of incorporating JEIE into each of the main constellations from the original curriculum. However, uncertainty arises when the group struggles to organize their Miro board in a way that connects “Classroom Environment” with JEIE. Jeff offers his opinion and says, “I was just thinking we could take the JEIE stickies that we had and make a more direct correlation between the subject matter and JEIE.” After going into more depth about his idea, he asks if anybody in the group has any ideas that may be different from his: “As long as that works for everyone else, because I don’t want this to just be my own thing. Does anyone else have, like, a way they think would be better at going at it?” This moment is significant because it demonstrates how stepping into leadership roles can provide the group with direction when confronted with uncertainty. By shifting his positional identity and taking on a leadership position, Jeff changes the dynamics within the group, allowing designers to collaborate differently. Jeff also ICLS2022 Proceedings 1175 © ISLS demonstrates that leadership is not limited to decision making. Rather, Jeff opens the floor to other designers, illustrating how embodying both the leadership role and the team member role allows for more collaboration and negotiation of the group’s joint enterprise. We are currently in the process of further analyzing data to identify specific processes and tools to support designers building community and taking on temporary leadership roles. Productive tension Productive tension refers to moments of disagreement or friction in design where the outcome does not detract from the design process but rather enhances or improves the final design product. These moments should be seen as meaningful disagreements that push the design process forward rather than obstacles. One moment that illustrates productive tension occurs in session six. Hannah brought up an alternative redesign method that works backwards to centralize equitable and inclusive education in the badge design. Hannah asks the group “I wonder, because the goal of this is to make JEIE, like, the central part, right? If we relate the JEIE stickies to the concept, and then to the stickies that we’ve already made?” Here, Hannah is referring to the existing “stickies” or sticky notes that the rest of the design group have previously made on the Miro board. The rest of the group reacts positively to the idea but are unsure of how to execute it. After a moment of silent thought, Jeff introduces a proposal to the group: “We kind of did that at first with the stickies we have to the side of Educator Role. We do it in a similar way in terms of putting down quick ideas of JEIE to the whole thing while we’re thinking about it in a broader scope.” Although Hannah's proposal led to even more confusion and uncertainty, the group acknowledged the idea had merit and worked through how they could possibly implement this process into their design. This moment is significant because it demonstrates how not all conflict or tension is unproductive. While Hannah's idea spurred more uncertainty among the designers, she also added a better method for the redesign, demonstrating how uncertainty and tension have the potential to improve the design process. Designers should not actively seek to minimize all moments of tension but instead embrace them as they arise. Risk and discomfort are spaces where original ideas come from, and we should not seek to reduce them (Heath & Smyth, 1999). However, productive tension is most effective if it is related to the design work and designers feel supported by the design group. In addition, feelings of support among the designers are essential for this type of risk-taking in productive tension to occur, which is why community building and facilitator guidance are so crucial. These aspects of the design process build meaningful relationships, increasing designers’ willingness to take risks and enter spaces of discomfort and tension.
