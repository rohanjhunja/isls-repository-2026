# Four Years of AI-Powered Formative Assessment: Exploring the 2-Sigma Effect and Fairness

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Bloom’s (1984) two-sigma problem highlights the substantial learning gains achieved through individualized tutoring. With advances in artificial intelligence (AI), scalable personalized support may help approach such effectiveness while ensuring fairness across diverse learners. This stud y reports findings from a four-year deployment of an AI-powered formative feedback tool in a technical college English course. Two sequential studies were conducted: Study 1 synthesized data from seven semesters (Spring 2022-Spring 2024) to examine the longitudinal impact of AI feedback on learners’ concept learning and subsequent task performance. Study 2 used a quasi-experimental design (Fall 2024-Spring 2025) to compare learners with and without AI support, analyzing demographic differences in outcomes. Results showed that iterative AI feedback enhanced conceptual representation and improved later performance. Between-group comparisons favored AI-supported learners for concept learning, with no demographic disparities detected. Overall, AI-powered formative assessment produced learning gains approximating one sigma while maintaining equitable outcomes across learners.

Bloom (1984) reported that learners  who received individualized tutoring performed, on average, two standard deviations above those in conventional classrooms, a finding known as the two-sigma problem. This effect size represents an improvement in learning outcomes and has long served as a benchmark for what highly personalized instruction can achieve. Yet reproducing such individualized gains at scale has proven difficult. In the current artificial intelligence (AI) era, researchers have revisited Bloom’s question: Can AI-powered tools  approach the two-sigma effect? AI now enables adaptive, data-informed feedback for large numbers of learners, combining the benefits of personalization with scalability. Recent meta-analyses of Intelligent Tutoring Systems (ITS), Automated Writing Evaluation (AWE), and other AI-based feedback environments report average effect sizes ranging from approximately 0.43 to 0.86, representing moderate to large learning gains (Dai et al., 2024; Fleckenstein et al., 2023; Kulik & Fletcher, 2016; Sun & Zhou, 2024; Zhai & Ma, 2022). However, these effects remain below Bloom’s two-sigma benchmark. Building on this foundation, the present study investigates whether an AI-powered formative assessment tool can approach Bloom’s two-sigma effect and whether its benefits are distributed equitably across diverse learners. Using multi-semester data from a t echnical-college English course, this research examines both the effectiveness and fairness of AI-supported formative feedback in authentic educational contexts.  This paper consists of two studies: Study 1 examines the within-group impact of the AI tool  across seven semesters, and Study 2 moves toward causal inference by comparing treatment and control groups while analyzing whether learner background (gender, race, age) interacts with AI support. Together, these studies advance understanding of AI’s role in fostering both learning gains and fairness in higher education.  The following research questions guide the Study 1 and Study 2: Study 1

• RQ1: How does AI-powered formative assessment and feedback influence learners ’ concept learning

and their performance on subsequent activities?

Study 2

• RQ2: How does AI-powered formative assessment and  feedback affect learners’ concept learning and

their performance on subsequent activities compared to no AI support?

• RQ3: How do learner differences between AI-supported and non-supported groups influence concept

learning and performance on subsequent activities?

## Theoretical background

1382 Bloom’s two-sigma effect and personalized learning Bloom (1984) estimated that individualized tutoring could produce learning gains of roughly two standard deviations above conventional instruction. Subsequent research has sought to replicate these effects through human tutoring, computer-assisted instruction, and adaptive learning environments. Studies comparing human tutoring, ITS, and other intelligent systems report effect sizes between 0. 32 and 0.76, suggesting that computer-supported instruction can yield smaller  but consistent improvements relative to Bloom’s original benchmark (Kulik & Fletcher, 2016; Steenbergen-Hu & Cooper, 2014; VanLehn, 2011). Meta-analyses of automated evaluation systems and AI-mediated learning support these findings. AWE systems typically produce average effects on writing quality between 0.5 5 and 0.86 (Fleckenstein et al., 2023; Zhai & Ma, 2022), while studies of AI-assisted personalized learning and virtual-agent simulations report similar ranges (Dai et al., 2024; Hu, 2024; Sun & Zhou, 2024; Wang et al., 2024). Collectively, these studies indicate that, under favorable instructional conditions, AI-powered learning environments tend to yield moderate to large effects, marking meaningful but still partial progress toward Bloom’s two-sigma gains. AI-powered formative feedback and fairness in learning While AI-powered tools have capacity for individualized feedback, concerns about fairness and inclusivity remain central to the discourse. Algorithmic systems may unintentionally reproduce or amplify existing educational inequities if their design, training data, or feedback mechani sms reflect biases (Baker & Hawn, 202 2; Jose et al., 2025). Moreover, learner characteristics such as gender  and race can influence how learners  interact with and benefit from AI tools (Hadar Shoval, 2025). Even when average effects are positive, subgroup analyses are often underreported, leaving unclear whether AI tools support all learners equally. Recent meta-analyses show that contextual and demographic factors can moderate AI intervention effects. For instance, Hu (2024) and Wang et al. (2024) found that the impact of AI-assisted personalized learning varies according to learners’ educational level, and educational context. This evidence underscores the importance of evaluating AI’s dual role in promoting both effectiveness and equity. Guided by this literature, the present study adopts a dual evaluative framework that examines (a) the magnitude of AI-powered formative feedback effects across multiple semesters and (b) the fairness of those effects across demographic subgroups. This integrated approach aligns Bloom’s vision of high-effect personalized learning with contemporary concerns for algorithmic fairness, positioning AI as a potential catalyst for both pedagogical effectiveness and equitable access to learning benefits.

## Context

The studies took place in an English course at a technical college between 2022 and 2025. Within this class, the Student Mental Model Analyzer for Research and Teaching (SMART), an AI-based formative feedback tool, was implemented. SMART assignments were used as pre-class reading activities, requiring learners to write and revise summaries of assigned readings  with the support of AI-powered feedback  (M. K. Kim et al., 2024). Over the semester, learners participated in two sets of SMART writing and subsequent writing tasks. Each subsequent writing assignment was based on the same reading materials as its corresponding SMART activity. Assignment 1 required a 500-word writing incorporating direct quotes and a cited page, while A ssignment 2,  completed following the second SMART task, was  a 1,000-word argumentative writing on instructor-guided topics. These two subsequent writing tasks served as follow-up assessments to evaluate knowledge transfer and application.

## Participants

Study 1 included 110 learners who used SMART across seven academic semesters. To focus on conditions where the AI tool could provide iterative support, only submissions involving multiple attempts were retained for learners, while the second assignment required learners to select one of three possible readings. Study 2 employed a quasi-experimental design, involving four course sections across two semesters (Fall 2024-Spring 2025). Two  sections served as the treatment group s, where learners used SMART for summary writing (n = 39), while the other sections served as the control groups, where learners wrote independently without AI support (n = 18). Because of the small participation size in the second assignment from the control group, only data from the first SMART assignment were analyzed. After accounting for this and excluding learners  with incomplete demographic information, the final analytic sample consisted of 54 learners (treatment group: n = 36; control group: n = 18).

1383 SMART generates analytic indices that evaluate summaries along multiple dimensions of conceptual understanding. For this study, semantic measures encompassing both key and overall concept s and propositions relative to a reference model were utilized (see Table 1). These indices are similarity measures, ranging between 0 and 1. In addition to SMART indices, learner s’ performance on subsequent writing assignments (maximum score 100) was evaluated by the instructor and analyzed to assess transfer effects. Demographic information, including gender, race, and age, was collected through end-of-semester surveys. For Study 1, linear mixed-effects (LME) models were applied to account for repeated measures within learners and across assignments. The models included centered predictors for assignment order and number of attempts, with random effects specified for learner ID and assignment ID. For Study 2, differences between treatment and control groups were examined using independent-sample t-tests, and effect size estimates (Cohen’s d). Bayesian r egression analyses were used to further examine relationships between learner characteristics with SMART indices and subsequent activity performance. Study 1: Influence of concept learning and performance on subsequent activities To assess whether AI-powered formative feedback promotes learners’ concept learning, we modeled performance across all AI-supported learning tasks using linear mixed-effects models. Attempt, representing the number of learner-initiated re-engagements following AI feedback, was a statistically significant predictor for all four SMART outcome measures: Recall-C, Recall-P, Concept Matching, and Propositional Matching. The effects were strongest for Recall-C and Recall-P (β = 0.0458-0.0602, p <.001), indicating that repeated task engagement led to better performance in retrieving and paraphrasing key ideas. These results suggest that AI-powered feedback meaningfully improves immediate recall performance and contributes modestly to conceptual understanding, particularly when learners re-engage with feedback and iterate on their responses. To investigate whether learning from AI-supported tasks transferred tonew but similar learning contexts, we examined performance on Assignment 1 and Assignment 2. Both assignments required the application of previously learned skills. In Assignment 1, there was no significant difference in Assignment 1 scores based on AI-supported learning history. In contrast, results from Assignment 2 revealed a stronger transfer effect. Both Recall-C and R ecall-P showed statistically significant improvement in scores of  the Assignment 2, suggesting that learners transferred recall skills acquired during AI-supported tasks tonew contexts. This result may be attributed to the complexity of the assignment. Assignment 2 required  a task with twice the word limit, giving learners an opportunity to demonstrate their conceptual understanding more fully. However, Concept  Matching and Propositional Matching did not show significant differences in both assignments. This pattern was supported by marginal R²  values, which were higher for recall outcomes than for Concept  Matching and Propositional Matching.

Study 2: Effectiveness and fairness To examine differences between the treatment (personalized AI feedback) group and the control group in concept learning, independent-samples t-tests were conducted. Learners in the treatment group scored significantly higher than those in the control group on two of the four SMART indices: Recall-C (M =.79, SD =.22) compared to the control group (M =.60, SD =.12), t(52) = 3.42, p =.001; and Recall-P (M =.64, SD =.34) compared to the control group (M =.38, SD =.17), t(52) = 3.13, p =.003. For the other two measures, Concept Matching and Propositional Matching, although the differences were not statistically significant, the treatment group demonstrated descriptively higher scores. Effect sizes were also calculated using Cohen’s d, yielding d  =.99 for Recall-C, d =.90 for Recall-P, d =.47 for Concept Matching, and d =.01 for Propositional Matching. 1384 Additionally, performance on the corresponding subsequent assignment was compared between groups. As a few learners did not submit the assignment, analysis was conducted with only those who did (treatment group: n = 28; control group n = 15). Though the group differences were not statistically significant, the treatment group again showed descriptively higher scores (M = 65.46, SD = 21.22) relative to the control group (M = 62.40, SD = 22.12).

Learner differences influencing concept learning and subsequent learning were examined using Bayesian regression analyses. For concept learning, mode ls were run with concept learning scores predicted by learner characteristics (gender, race, age) and treatment condition (AI feedback vs. control): SMART indices  ~ learner characteristic × treatment condition. Bayesian regression was utilized instead of traditional regression due to the small sample size (treatment group: n = 31; control group: n = 17). All models showed convergence (R̂  = 1.00, effective sample sizes > 4,000). Nevertheless, across all measures of concept learning, 95% credible intervals for gender, race, or age group and their interactions with the treatment condition included zero, indicating no credible evidence of learner characteristic effects of interactions. However, within the treatment group, descriptive trends were observed. Specifically, female learners  tended to score higher overall, although male learners  made more revisions. By age group, older learners  tended to achieve higher scores, though this pattern was not reflected in the number of revisions.

To examine differences in subsequent assignment performance while accounting for concept learning, an additional Bayesian regression was conducted: Subsequent score ~ SMART indices × learner characteristic + treatment condition (treatment group: n = 26; control group: n = 15). All models showed good convergence (R̂  = 1.00), with effective sample sizes exceeding 8,000. Across all models, the 95% credible intervals included zero, indicating no credible evidence of learner characteristics or interaction effects on subsequent performance. As shown in previous studies (J. Kim et al., 2024; Yin et al, 2024), the longitudinal evidence (Study 1) demonstrates that AI-powered formative feedback can foster iterative revision and improve conceptual learning. While average revision attempts remained relatively low, learners who engaged more fully benefited substantially. Furthermore, the quasi-experimental study comparing the AI-supported group with the no-AI group revealed an effect size ranging between 0.90 and 0.99 for key concepts and relation identifications. This range  indicated a comparatively strong impact, as it was higher than the effect  sizes reported in recent literature on AI-assisted personalized learning (i.e., 0.43-0.70; Dai et al., 2024; Sun & Zhou, 2024; Wang et al., 2024). This suggests that under conducive instructional design,  AI-powered tools can approach a large effect, approximately half the magnitude of Bloom’s two-sigma effect.

In terms of fairness, the comparison study (Study 2) also revealed that there is no  evidence of demographic disparities in learning outcomes. This finding  supports the fairness of AI-supported learning outcomes. Although the differences in learning outcomes were not statistically significant, descriptive patterns by gender and age showed noteworthy tendencies. Gender-and age-related factors may lead to different motivational approaches for engaging with AI-supported tools, suggesting that variations in performance are more likely to reflect engagement styles rather than inequities in how AI system operates. Regarding instructional design implications, results highlight that tool efficacy depends not only on AI quality but also on integration into course structures. Low average attempts suggest that learners may require explicit scaffolding or incentives to revise multiple times. Without such design alignment, the full benefits of AI may remain unrealized.

Across four years of deployment, the AI-powered tool demonstrated that formative assessment can foster iterative revision, strengthen conceptual learning, and approach the one-sigma effect of individualized tutoring. Although between-group effects were mixed and downstream performance gains remained limited, there was no evidence of demographic inequities in AI-supported learning outcomes. These findings affirm the pedagogical value of AI while underscoring the importance of fairness-sensitive design. Achieving Bloom’s vision may be within reach, but only if AI innovations are paired with thoughtful instructional integration and sustained attention to equity in diverse learning contexts.

Several limitations should be noted when interpreting these results. First, the SMART system evolved over the course of multiple years, introducing version-related variability that limits strict comparability across semesters. Second, the relatively small sample size in Study 2 constrained the statistical power of fairness analyses, making it difficult to detect subtle demographic effects. Third, the study context, which is an English course at a single technical college, restricts the generalizability of the findings to other disciplines or institutional settings.
