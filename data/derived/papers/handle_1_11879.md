# Technology-Mediated Lesson Study to Improve Teacher Outcomes

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Professional development is crucial for teachers to build their skills and adopt three-dimensional science. Effective professional development should be ongoing, collaborative, and spread over time to ensure lasting changes in teaching practices. Rural teachers often face isolation and less training compared to their urban counterparts, limiting their opportunities for collaboration. This research explored how technology-mediated lesson study (TMLS) helped rural teachers improve their teaching abilities and three-dimensional science skills. TMLS adapts lesson study using technology, allowing teachers to collaborate remotely. Based on four years of evidence, this research developed and refined the steps of TMLS with rural science teachers. This work also aimed to improve their three-dimensional science teaching abilities by forming small, collaborative groups that co-design, implement, and evaluate lesson plans together. Overall, TMLS offers rural teachers a valuable platform to enhance their teaching skills, collaborate meaningfully, and overcome the challenges of isolation.

## Introduction

Best practices in science education require instruc tion designed around three-dimensional (3D) science: science and engineering practices (SEPs), crosscutting conc epts (CCCs), and disciplinary core ideas (DCIs; Nat ional Research Council, 2012). Central to these standards is students making sense of science using the three dimensions for richer learning experiences and connections wit h phenomenon in the world. Students integrate the t hree dimensions by modeling scientists’ and engineers’ a ctions and thought processes by highlighting that s cientific knowledge should not be detached from scientists’ skills and tools (NGSS Lead States, 2013). Professional development sessions can be a valuable  resource for science teachers to learn about and improve their teaching to be more three-dimensional. When professional learning is up-to-date, ongoing, collaborative, practice-based, and connected to local contexts, it is more effective at changing teach ing practices (Desimone, 2009; Kennedy, 2016). Professional devel opment specific to three-dimensional science must be application-based for lasting change (Marshall et al., 2016). L earning about three-dimensional science is more effective when spread over an extended period (Le, 2019). Teachers can enact better three-dimensional science elements when given time to practice these skills. Teachers alsoneed additional support, such as coll aboration with other faculty or peers (Herington & Daubenmire, 2016). In one western U.S. state, most rural teachers have  received less training on three-dimensional science standards than their urban counterparts. Isolation is a significant problem for many rural teachers as they are often the only teacher of a particular subject in a schoo l. As a result, they have fewer opportunities for meaningful collaboration, even though collaboration is a cruci al characteristic of effective professional learnin g (Svendsen, 2020). This research aimed to understand how the Technology-Mediated Lesson Study (TMLS) process helps teachers improve their teaching abilities and three-dimensional science skills. Lesson study Lesson study is an established model that helps tea chers improve collaboration, examine their practice, and enhance student learning (Cheung & Wong, 2014; Kane llopoulou & Darra, 2019). Traditionally, lesson stu dy is conducted in small, in-person groups to create lessons, review each other’s teaching, and r evise lessons. The lesson study process significantly impacts teachers’ content and pedagogical knowledge (Aoibhinn, 2016; Lewis & Perry, 2017). It increases teachers’ expectations  for student achievement (Lewis & Perry, 2015), encouraging them to consider students’ interactions with content (Martensson & Hansson, 2018). Technology-mediated lesson study Technology-Mediated Lesson Study (TMLS) utilizes technology so  teachers can use lesson study when not co-located (Hudson et al., 2024). While physically separated, r ural or isolated teachers can connect professionall y with other educators to develop and improve their teaching and pedagogical skills. The process is simple, a small group of educators come together with a shared teac hing goal. Like other forms of lesson study, TMLS g roups create lessons to be taught by multiple group membe rs. Once the lesson has been created, one group mem ber records themselves teaching it to students. A criti cal lesson study principle is having other teachers  observe the lesson implementation. By watching the lesson recor ding, observers can focus on student behavior and conversations. After the lesson has been recorded a nd shared with the group, all group members watch, review, and comment on the lesson’s implementation. Next, the group meets via online video conferencing. These virtual meetings focus on changing the lesson plan and other materials and evaluating how well the lesson met the overall TMLS goals. Groups identify changes that need to be made to the lesson before it is taught again. With a revised lesson, the next teacher in the group begins the pr ocess again by teaching it to their students while recording it. The process is repeated until all group members hav e taught the revised version of the lesson. One ele ment of TMLS that differs from other forms of lesson study —even those that also use technology to help facilitate the lesson study process (e.g., Huang et al., 2019), TMLS does not use outside facilitators or content experts to write the lessons or run TMLS meetings. The teachers become group leaders and lead the TMLS process for their group.

## Theoretical framework and research questions

This research was built on an ecological model for changing health behaviors described by Sallis et al. (2008); it is applied to changing teaching practices to incorp orate three-dimensional science teaching. Teachers choose instructional practices based on personal, social, and contextual factors. The progr am targets personal factors by supporting rural science teachers’ development of k nowledge, self-efficacy, and positive attitudes about three-dimensional science teaching. Social factors are targeted via cohorts of subject-region teams to provide a sense of community and support for the instructional changes needed. Contextual factors are selected using technology to help address the geographic isolation characteristic of rural educators. Considering thes three factors, this research aimed to address the following research questions:

1. How does TMLS help teachers build their knowledge to enact three-dimensional science teaching?

2. What role do collaborative lesson development and i terative revisions have in their knowledge growth

and capacities?

3. What role does peer observation have in their knowledge growth?

4. What roles does the evaluation of three-dimensional science lessons have in their knowledge growth?

## Data collection and analysis

This work examined data collected over four school years from 2021 to 2024. Semi-structured interviews were conducted at the beginning and end of each school y ear. Participants were asked what they knew about t hreedimensional science and how they would explain those concepts to someone unfamiliar with the terms or methods. They were also asked about their experiences with t he program and with their TMLS groups. Two focus gr oups were also conducted in each school year. Small grou ps of 3-8 teachers were aske d about their classroom experiences with the TMLS cycle and three-dimensional science. Qualitative content analysis classifies texts by identifying themes or categories (Cassell & Symon, 2004) and is based on six steps proposed by Kuckartz (201 4). This process begins with thematic coding perfor med at a high level to identify themes, followed by the identification and classification into sub-themes. Four research team members coded the same randomly chosen pre-interviews to identify themes, which were discussed, expanded, and combined based on group consensus. From this initial discussion, the additional interviews and focus groups were coded by the team. Trustworthiness was establi shed through peer debriefing, negative case analysi s, and member checking.

## Results

In our data analysis we evaluated how our teacher participants use the collaborative TMLS method to change and grow in their teaching abilities as well as their c ompetencies in three-dimensional science instruction. Growth is especially evident in those who participated in the program for multiple years. TMLS: Creating groups and co-writing lessons The first step of TMLS is to form groups  united under a common goal. By analyzing responses from interviews and focus groups, we can understand how this step in the TMLS process influenced our teachers. They valued the time to work together as a group, the friendships and comradery they formed, and the process helped them improve their knowledge of and capacity with three-dimensional science lessons. Working in groups did not come naturally or easily to all participants. One teacher observed  it took her a while to become a good “team player”  since she has never worked with other educators. Being involv ed in this professional development program for a s econd school year helped her. She said, Learning how to work with a team is so important, and it took practice. It didn’t come naturally to me. I’m a single teacher; I’m the only voice in my head before I did this program. So, to learn to communicate with teachers on making a lesson when I hadn’t done it for 20 years was really hard. But this year I feel like I’ve taken a more o f a team role … to try to make a good lesson for my team. And I feel like I’ve learned a lot going from last year to this year. I think I’m going to be a better team [member] this year than I was last year, and I think because of it, we’ll make great lessons. I mean, we did make great lessons last year, but I think it ’s just better and more healthy for me [this year.] TMLS: Teaching, recording, watching After finishing writing the lessons in person,  each team member takes a turn to record themselves t eaching the lesson to their students. Teachers frequently reflected on the impact of recording themselves, watching themselves teach, and watching each other’s videos. They found value in the experience for their pedagogical skills and their ability to teach three-dimensional lessons. For example, one teacher said,  “Teachers get in a rut  of what feels comfortable, and with the videoing, it forces us to [think], ‘maybe that really isn’t the best thing.’ It’s hard for me to give up the things that I like, or I’m comfortable with [but] I feel it’s forced me to do that.” Another common theme was the value of not being cri tical of each other or the teacher’s style while watching the lessons but instead keeping the focus on the lesson. Because these teachers do not have large, if any, cohorts in their respective schools, watching other s teach is even more infrequent. As one participant  put it, “Because I’m at a school where I’m the only science teacher, …it’s the first time I’ve been recorded and had people look at it… who have to try to teach the sam e content.” This opportunity is also the first time many of these teachers have had the opportunity to watch so meone else teach middle or high school science that  they are teaching since they completed their teaching certificates. TMLS: Lesson revision and rewriting After the lesson has been taught and reviewed by the team, they meet virtually using video conferencing to discuss the lesson by evaluating the students’ responses from the video and discussing what should be changed to improve the three-dimensional elements. One teacher commented on how it is easy for them to  stay on topic in these meetings. “We’ll have little off-topic discussions, but since we have something to f ocus on, …we can check on each other and be like, ‘Hey, let’s go back to what we were talking about.’ So we don’t have those squirrel moments too often.” Another compared these lessons to typical teacher meeting s in her school. “We don’t have to worry about our principal doing something or tha t this kid was annoying. We can focus on lesson con tent and be far more productive than we could in a setting at our home schools.” The group determines the structure of these meetings. We have found two general methods of meeting: working together liveduring the meeting or creating a list of changes and making assignments to be made afterward independently. Both methods have been success ful. Working together to evaluate and revise lessons was seen as a positive experience  in terms of teamwork and  improving general three-dimensional science knowledge and competency. Teachers also felt confid ent in the ir work and found it worthwhile to make each lesson the best possible.

What value have you found in the TMLS process?

During the post-interview at the end of the 2023-24 school year, those teachers planning on returning to the project for a second, third, or fourth year were asked why they wanted to return. One coming back for a third year said, When I was told … who I would be working with, I could see that there was still a lot for me to be able to learn …. And I was working with people that I knew I could learn from … if I could just sit back and listen to them, I’d probably just become a better teacher. If I could just be a fly on the wall in their meetings, I’d be a better teac her. So I think I could just figure out that I could still benefit from being involved given those changes.” Although our implementation of TMLS includes formal  professional development sessions in the form of workshop presentations and discussions, these are l imited compared to the time teachers spend working and learning from each other. Because of this, we can conclude that much of the growth in three-dimensional abilities comes from working with their groups. When asked why he returned, one four-time participant said, “[I’m coming back] because I like it. To be honest, I love the p ush. I like the accountabilit y that it forces me to ha ve. I enjoy this because we [write] a lesson, we see it taught multiple times, but then we perfect it at the end, so someone else can just pick it up and go. I have used every single lesson [developed by the project].” Another theme identified from interviews was the changes the te achers saw in their students regarding three-dimensional science  due to  changes they made in their classes. “My students have been a l ot better at identifying their crosscutting concepts than in past years because I’m doing better at pointing them out. They can identify [them] and point them out to each other.” Another commented that, “I have noticed changes in the way I talk to the students, too, because I’m paying more attention to the CCCs and the SEPs. I’m using those  terms more, [such as] cause and effect, analyze and interpret data, stability and change, all of that stuff. And so you see that pop up more as a theme throughout classes.”

## Discussion and conclusion

Previous research has shown that one barrier to imp lementing three-dimensional science in the classroom is the complexity of professional development needed for t hree-dimensional science instruction (Smith et al., 2024), which is even more true for rural educators. Our re search finds that using TMLS provides one way of do ing complex professional development since teachers tak e control of their learning in small groups. Doing so helps them develop their understanding and implementation of three-dimensional science in their classrooms. Teachers gained experience through developing lessons as gro ups through the TMLS process. Co-designing lessons and teaching and watching other group members teach the same lesson contributed to this learning by observing how team members utilized three-dimensional principles in their classrooms. TMLS can be used by teachers in any discipline. Iso lation in education is not limited to geography, and TMLS allows teachers to collaborate and grow person ally, make social connections, and account for any contextual factors inherent in education. It also p rovides time for professional learning and collabor ation disconnected from school-level culture. Researchers must use best practices in professional learning programs so teachers have time to build their understanding of the concepts being taught, and the  TMLS process provides an opportunity to do so. TMLS also provides various research opportunities that reciprocate data-driven information to the practical education challenges and needs teachers are tackling. The implications for rural teachers using Technolog y-Mediated Lesson Study are significant. TMLS enables rural teachers to collaborate with peers de spite geographical isolation, fostering a sense of community and support often lacking in rural settings. It provides access to ongoing, practice-based professional development tailored to their specific needs, helping them stay  updated with the latest teaching practices and sta ndards. Engaging in TMLS allows rural teachers to  refine their teaching methods through iterative les son development and peer feedback, leading to more effective three-dimensional science teaching. Overall, TMLS offers rural teachers a valuable platform to enhance their teach ing skills, collaborate meani ngfully, and overcome the challenges of isolation.  Participants saw these advantages. One teacher who has participated for multiple years summed it up this way: “We maybe didn’t realize the power of the three dimensions, but I think we were sold on the fact that they could help our students learn. And that it would develop ability in those kids that we really want to see from their futures and that we want to see in our communities.”
