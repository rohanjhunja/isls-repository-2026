# Designing a Culturally Grounded Ecology for Mediating Multimodal Literacy Experiences through Parent-Child Technology Interactions

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
The Urban Griots Playground (UGP) study investigates how culturally grounded, embodied learning environments impact early literacy and socio-emotional development in young children through a family workshop series. Using the researcher-developed Drumball-Alphariddims system—a digital tool integrating rhythmic call-and-response—UGP helped scaffold literacy and social skills in five child-parent dyads over four weeks. Findings suggest that this embodied, rhythmic and multimodal approach stimulated early literacy behaviors and social participation, though younger children struggled with the complexity of the Alphariddim code. The workshops also improved emotional understanding through storytelling and culturally relevant proverbs. While the study highlights the potential of culturally grounded, family-centered music-based interventions (MBI) for at-risk children, limitations such as small sample size and technological constraints call for further refinement and longitudinal research.

## Introduction

With the emergence of 21st century schools research  identifying children' s learning environments as a main contributor to achievement (Maltese, et al, 2010), there is growing recognition of the need for educators to equip “children with opportunities to see, touch, and fee l the learning in practice, within multiple embodim ent ways” (Tsoukala, 2021, p.96). Research in the learning sciences highlights the imp ortance of incorporating cultural practices to enhance learning outcomes and student engagement: leveraging students' cultural practices has been shown to improve learning outcomes and connection to STEM learning environments (Tia c. Madkins, N. Nasir, 2019); Africandrumming has been investigated as a cultura lly-relevant algebra teaching method (Sharp, 2007); and attentional Anchors in Balinese Dancing has bee n shown to ground mathematics learning of auxiliary  lines in Geometry Reasoning through culturally authentic movement practices (Rajala et al, 2025). However, early childhood literacy de velopment is often framed within traditional pedago gies that fail to account for culturally situated learning experiences. While rhythm-based and movement-linked pedagogical activities and games have been known to play a supportive role in children's early learning experiences – for example, with the use of clapping, music or the use of body movement for dev eloping the phonemi c awareness and aural intelligence needed for literacy development (Cole & Boykin, 2008)  – few educational interventions have embrace d the potential of drumming and drum language communication as a starting point for the design of culturally-grounded learning environments for early literacy and STEAM learning. This study explores a novel approach, leveraging African talking drums (Nketia, 1968; Finnegan, 1970) to foster parent-child interactions that mediate multimodal literacy development. By embedding culturally relev ant practices and tools within a participatory learning ecology, this study investigates how such an environment influences early literacy and STEAM engagement. The Drumball-Alphariddims system The Drumball (Tchetgen, 2017)  is a digital orality hypermedia system and tangible  interface that acts as a transducer of finger-tap and hand-stroke patterns into digital audiovisual output. The system has two components: a tangible user interface connected to a laptop/tablet and a web application that is a multi-user player environment accessible on a mobile device in stand-alone mode or in-tandem with the Drumball device. The integration of the cultural framework of Africandrumming in the Drumball design is based on 3 sensors as an analogy to the tone-slap-base drumming principles and techniques of the djembe drum. This is the main area of interaction for the user as it imitates an actual djembe drum which at its most basic level of play offers the three sounds tone (.), slap (–) and base (x). In this way, children explore writing by using the Alphariddim (Morse codelike) visual symbol system to produce letters and words as combinations of tone, slap and base sounds. The proposed technology is designed to be part of t he Urban Griot Playground (UGP)  ecosystem. UGP consists of a series of technology-driven family workshops that leverage multimodal pedagogies (oral/aural, visual and emb odied engagement) and are  informed by traits of the traditional African cultu ral system. Most UGP activities blend the practices of drumming, call-and-response and drum communication with the  affordances of computer interaction. Such multimodality is crucial to transforming parent and teacher practices by using different communication systems for different purposes.  This pedagogical framework is designed to be respon sive to the (Pre-) Kindergarten Literacy and Music/Dance standards. The research questions guiding this study were: 1) What are the impacts of culturally grounded, embodied learning environments and activities usi ng the Drumball and Alphariddims mediated in the Urban Griots Playgroun d approach to multimodal experiences on children’s literacy development particularly around aural/visual literacy skills related to letter nami ng and word spelling?

2) What is the impact of the same approach on children’s socio emotional development?  3) Finally, what are

potential impacts of this approach and intervention on children’s family relations?

## Theoretical framework

This work draws on multimodality and Cultural-Historical Activity Theory (CHAT) to examine how children and their parents engage in literacy practices within a n embodied learning ecology. CHAT (Engeström) provides a lens for examining the critical components within an activity system, such as object-orientedness, tool mediation, intentionality, the relational and dualistic nature between the elements, and contradictions (Kaptelinin et al., 1995). In the UGP’s context of multimodal literacy activit y, the object-oriented action of practicing new literacy and socio-emotional skills was most influenced by a network of three interrelated factors in the UGP activity system: cultural tools (tool), embodied learning practices (division of labor) and community involvement (comm unity). To capture micro-level interaction patterns, we integrate multimodal literacy theories to analyz e how different semiotic resources —gesture, sound, and movement —function in the co-construction of literacy practices.

## Methodology

The intervention took place in a community setting with preschool-age children and their parents, involving a series of structured workshops over the course of 4 weeks. While participants varied in cultural, racial and ethnic background, all were English-speakers or English as a Second Language learners. Data collection spanned a 6-week period and included a Pre-workshop Phase (a parent online survey on child mul timodal skills inventory, a 30-min entry zoom interview with parents about their child’s music and literacy habits at-home) and Workshop Phase (participant observation, audio-video recordings, focus group discussions, and a pre-and post-test of alphabetic knowledge, administered on the first and last day, respectively) to examine the research questions. Data analysis followed a multimodal interaction analysis  framework, focusing on how gestures, speech, and r hythmic engagement contributed to meaning making. Parent-child dyads were examined in terms of their collaborative learning trajectories, particularly how they negotiated literacy or STEM concepts through embodied interactions.

## Findings

Findings indicate that the embodied learning ecolog y fostered increased engagement in literacy activit ies among children, as well as strengthened parent-child relationships. Four key themes emerged:  1) Stimulating literacy behaviors: The Alphariddim symbol system helped engage childre n in letter recognition and spelling, but its complexity posed challenges for younger participant s. Embodied learning activities, such as body rhyth ms and digital spelling tasks, helped link symbols to soun ds and gestures. Modest gains in literacy skills we re observed, especially among children with lower initial profic iency; 2)  Cultivating social participation skills: Group activities like circle discussions and games promoted self-regulation, cooperation, and confidence. Cultural tools, such as talking drums, reinforced symbolic understa nding and social interaction. Parent-child collaboration strengthened family engag ement in learning, including at-home; 3) Enhancing emotional understanding: Children improved in recognizing and discussing emotions through visual icons and storytelling. African proverbs helped deepen emotional comprehension by connecting  feelings with meaningful sayings; 4) Consistency in Parental Engagement: Parents’ involvement was crucial for mediating lite racy and socio-emotional activities. However, limited use of digital resources at home hindered data collection and broader impacts.

## Conclusion

In this poster, I present preliminary findings on the UGP inaugural study which emphasize the role of mediation, particularly how cultural tools like the talking drum facilitate early learning and development through embodied social interaction. Case studies reveal that children successfully explored communication and language through rhythmic interaction using their body, percussion instruments and new media technologies. These findings have implications for both research and practice. For ed ucators, this study underscores the importance of d esigning culturally responsive learning environments that leverage embodied and multimodal strategies. Through rhythmic interaction and embodied play, caregivers can provide children opportunities to engage with a range of tools and applications that target different skill domains in  a gamified way.  Future research should explore how similar frameworks could be applied in diverse linguistic a nd cultural contexts. Moreover, addressing parental  concerns regarding technology use remains critical—alternative, non-digital interventions (like the GriotQuest learning kit) may further validate the cultural relevance and educational value of traditional tools such as the talking drum.
