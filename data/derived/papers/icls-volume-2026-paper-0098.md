# Bidirectional Knowledge Cuing and the Imbalanced Epistemic Games in Physics Textbook Exercises

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Integrating knowledge from multiple domains can be challenging. One example of this is physics students struggling to coordinate their mathematical and conceptual knowledge. We believe this phenomenon may be caused in part by physics students not receiving sufficient opportunities to reinforce both directions of knowledge cuing independently—from conceptual to mathematical and vice versa. In this work, we discuss how this hypothesis has implications regarding the somewhat-ambiguous mechanism for knowledge activation in the knowledge in pieces theory of conceptual change. We then report on an exploratory analysis of a calculus-based introductory physics textbook (Wolfson, 2020) to quantify the relative frequency of problem types, which would correlate to epistemic games for cuing conceptual and mathematical knowledge in opposing directions. We find that the textbook heavily favors problems with a conceptual premise and a quantitative solution, encompassing over 70% of the coded exercises.

In many disciplines, learners must coordinate knowledge of various types originating from myriad  domains into a cohesive, global whole. This is especially true in physics, where a mastery of the conceptual subject matter also requires a deep understanding of its accompanying mathematics. This inherent interdisciplinarity ha s its advantages: physics equations which have a coupled conceptual context may not need to employ cumbersome mathematical formalities (Redish & Kuo, 2015), and experienced  physicists can use conceptual knowledge to shortcut or skip complicated calculations (Kuo et al., 2013; Kustusch et al., 2014). Connecting and moving fluidly between conceptual and mathematical knowledge is a necessary skill that must be developed on the path to physics expertise (Bing & Redish, 2012; Eichenlaub & Redish, 2019).  For burgeoning physic learners, integrating and coordinating knowledge resources from both mathematical and conceptual  domains should be a central component of their educational experience.

Worryingly, physics education research repeatedly shows that physics students struggle with the mathematical aspects of physics courses—even when they have the prerequisite mathematical knowledge! This can manifest in many ways, such as students inconsistently applying the same mathematical method from problem to problem (Meredith & Marrongelle, 2008) or getting so focused on one mathematical approach that they overlook an easier alternative (Mason & Singh, 2010); however, the most well-documented examples of this phenomenon emerge when physics students can successfully perform the mathematical processing required by a physics problem  while showing  little to no understanding of the reason why such math was utilized or the conceptual physics represented by the calculation (Arnell, Swanson, Edwards, & Edwards, 2024; Hu & Rebello, 2013; Kim & Pak, 2002; Mason & Singh, 2010; Tuminaro & Redish, 2007; Walsh et al., 2007). Traditional physics instruction appears to be ineffective at addressing this disconnect, as mathematical issues persist through upper-division and even graduate-level physics courses (Manogue et al., 2006; Mason & Singh, 2010). Furthermore, traditional physics assessments mainly check students’ competence with short—sometimes single-step—calculations, which may not require conceptual understanding at all and are wholly  unreliable at measuring students’ mastery of melding mathematical and conceptual thinking (Eichenlaub & Redish, 2019; McDermott, 2004; Redish & Kuo, 2015). It appear s that the standard instructional experiences in most physics courses today are not adequately developing physics students’ ability to merge mathematical and conceptual thinking. In this work, we propose a possible factor which may contribute to the pervasiveness and persistence of these issues: we believe that physics students are, at large,  receiving an imbalanced ratio of learning experiences that require translating physical systems into mathematics versus those that ask for an interpretation or evaluation of the physical meaning of a mathematical representation. This hypothesis arises from our work on the PhysMath project (PhysMath, n.d.), in which we are developing instructional tools —digital simulations with mathematical components—f or physics educators to facilitate the latter type of experience (A rnell, Swanson, Edwards, Hart et al., 2024). In this paper, we will first explicate the theoretical basis for our hypothesis and examine how its investigation may shed light on previously ill-defined properties of our guiding conceptual  change model. Then, we will present the results of an exploratory analysis of standard instructional material—a calculus-based 860 introductory physics textbook —to lend support to our study’s assumptions and motivate continued research on this important matter.

Part one: Theoretical considerations The primary learning theory for our work has been knowledge in pieces  (KiP; diSessa, 1993), a constructivist approach that models knowledge in the mind as a large assortment of individual elements. These elements come in a variety of forms—intuitive ideas, personal beliefs, or normative facts, to name a few (Hammer & Elby, 2003; Arnell et al., 2022). When an individual engages in sensemaking, they activate knowledge elements that seem to be relevant in the context at hand. Through experience, learners build associations between facets of various sensemaking contexts and the knowledge elements which appear to be helpful or successful within them. At times, these connections may contain subtle misalignments or deviate from normative models, leading to the appearance of so-called ‘misconceptions’ (Hammer, 1996). With this model, KiP operationalizes learning as the gradual, piecemeal refinement of the activations and connections among knowledge elements to more closely match normative understandings and support productive sensemaking. The process by which a knowledge element is activated has generally been referred to  as cuing, originating in diSessa’s (1993) monograph. Elsewhere in cognitive psychology, a similar term of contextual cuing (Chun & Jiang, 1998) has been used to describe the “deployment of visual attention”—literally, how components of an interface guide visual searches. While KiP’s cuing does consider how salient features of a problem candirectly prompt element activation, it is also  interested in how elements can  cue each other. diSessa postulated that currently active knowledge elements would affect the behavior of  neighboring elements, either through stimulation or inhibition. As an example, he included a hypothetical cluster of elements that could activate during a bout of social anxiety, seen in Figure 1. In this diagram, the  pointed arrows indicate a positive or stimulating effect, whereas the flathead arrow represents a negative or inhibitory effect. Figure 1 Hypothetical Cuing Connections Diagram from diSessa (1993, p.113) diSessa cautioned that these cuing relationships would only be useful for anticipating knowledge activation over “short intervals” (p.114), as longer sequences of reasoning would include too many idiosyncratic factors to be reliably predictable. Nevertheless, the construct would provide a qualitative measure for describing the relationship between localized neighborhoods or clusters of elements. This mechanism has become a staple of the KiP theoretical framework and resonates with other constructs from cognitive psychology, such as accessibility and priming (Higgins, 1996).

Although cuing has been accepted as a baseline a ssumption in KiP research, the exact details of the mechanism have remained largely underexplored. When KiP work focuses on knowledge at an elemental grain-size, it is often to catalog which elements are activated in a given learning context (Arnell et al., 2023; Broadfoot et al., 2020; Cvenic et al., 2021); such lists are of great use to educators and instructional designers who wish to use students’ prior and intuitive knowledge as a foundation for deeper understanding, but these studies tend not to investigate how or why any particular element was activated. When KiP researchers have investigated knowledge element activation  directly, they have been primarily focused on how environmental components — like embodied actions (Lee, 2016), question types (Goodhew et al., 2021), or simulations  (Arnell & Swanson, 861 2023)—can prompt the activation of different subsets of knowledge elements. Consequently, the precise nature of how element may be cued by other currently active elements  has received little to no attention. Elements are still assumed to be able to cue each other, such as when larger knowledge structures like coordination classes (diSessa & Sherin, 1998) or epistemological frames (Hammer et al., 2005) are said to collectively prime a related subset of resources when individual members of the group are activated. Studying such ontologies has helped to identify groups of elements that belong to a shared cluster but has done little to clarify the characteristics of the cuing relationships between them.

As part of our hypothesis, we propose that element-to-element cuing is bidirectional: element A’s ability to cue element B and element B’s ability to cue element A are (at least to some extent) independent, and expertlevel integration requires that both pathways  be instantiated and reinforced.  The scarcity of research on cuing mechanisms makes it challenging to situate this claim in prior work, but we believe supportive implications can be seen throughout KiP work. As early as diSessa’s (1993) diagram in Figure 1, cuing relationships were depicted as having a directional effect. It is also a fundamental tenet of KiP that learner’s sensemaking can shift radically in response to minute changes in the learning context—even as subtle as directing the learner’s attention from the start of a process to the midpoint (diSessa, 1996). This level of contextual sensitivity suggests that the ordinality of element activation would be similarly consequential, perhaps because the cuing strength along sensemaking paths in opposing direction are not equivalent.

The starkest example of directionality in KiP research —and coincidentally, the most immediately relevant to our physics-math inquiries—comes from the work of Tuminaro and Redish (2007), who sought to categorize the problem-solving strategies of physics students. For this analysis, they used the theoretical construct of epistemic games  from Collins and Ferguson (1993), which are “a complex of rules, strategies, and moves associated with particular representations” (p.26).  When faced with a problem-solving context, learners choose one epistemic game over another based on its  alignment with the entry conditions, or “the nature of the question that motivates the inquiry and with the data or knowledge that is available to help in formulating an answer” (p. 28). For Tuminaro and Redish, this construct dovetailed nicely with KiP, as playing an epistemic game constitutes “an activation of a pattern of activities that can be associated with a collection of resources” (Tuminaro & Redish, 2007, p. 4). In their analysis, they identified six physics-specific epistemic games, three of which are relevant for our current work. The first is Mapping Meaning to Mathematics, in which students “begin from a conceptual understanding of the physical situation described in the problem statement, and then progress to a quantitative solution” (p. 6). The second is Mapping Mathematics to Meaning, in which students “begin with a physics equation and then develop a conceptual story” (p. 6). The third (which is not immediately relevant to directionality but will be applicable to later discussions) is Recursive Plug-and-Chug, a strategy in which the desired quantity in a problem is identified in an equation, after which the student checks to see if all other quantities are provided or known. This strategy has been well-documented for several decades (Larkin et al., 1980), and physics educators are strongly critical of the approach as it requires little to no conceptual understanding to perform successfully. As may be clear from their names, Mapping Meaning to Mathematics and Mapping Mathematics to Meaning (which we will refer to collectively as the ‘ Mapping’ games) draw upon the same collections of knowledge resources, but to accomplish inverse goals. Our hypothesis can be described in terms of these epistemic games: we believe physics students are playing Meaning-to-Math far more often than they play Math-to-Meaning, and that balancing this asymmetry could improve their  ability to connect physical and mathema tical ideas more consistently. Eventually, we intend to test this prediction directly by giving physics students supplemental Mathto-Meaning experiences using our  PhysMath simulations; should this prediction be supported, it would bolster the proposition that knowledge elements’ cuing relationships are indeed bidirectional, further clarifying the finegrain mechanisms of KiP’s cognitive model. For now, our empirical work sought to validate one of the underlying assumptions of our hypothesis: that physics students’ learning experiences (and thus, the epistemic games they play) are imbalanced in the first place. In the following section, we share the results of an exploratory analysis to back this assumption and pave the way for deeper investigations. Part two: Empirical exploration Our hypothesis—that the epistemic games which align with typical physics c oursework are imbalanced —was informed largely by our anecdotal experience as physics educators (and before that, physics students). However, we wished to couple our hypothesis with a  direct, empirical investigation. Thus, we conducted an exploratory (Wolfson, 2020) —to determine what percentage of the end-of-chapter problems and exercises would be well-suited for each of the Mapping epistemic games.  We chose to focus on t extbooks as they are a foundational component of educational courses across domains, in part thanks to their flexibility for both teachers and students (O’Neill, 1982). In physics, the majority of research on textbooks has focused on their epistemic or cultural 862 sophistication (Yan et al., 2023), rather than analyzing their use of mathematics. One study by Takaoglu (2018) looked at the distribution of mathematical knowledge in high school physics textbooks (e.g., how often problems used arithmetic vs. algebra), but this study only compared the mathematical questions to each other, so their frequency relative to conceptual questions is unknown.

Each chapter of the Wolfson (2020) textbook concludes with roughly 70– 100 questions covering the various topics from the chapter. While it is impossible to predict exactly which epistemic game a student would employ for a given problem (as it is entirely likely that different students would employ different games), there should be a strong correlation between the content of the problem prompt and the most-frequently used epistemic games based on the alignment of the problem to the game’s expected entry conditions. In the case of Meaning-to-Math, questions with a conceptual premise that provide the data to perform quantitative inquiries would be best suited; conversely, Math-to-Meaning would be most common on questions with mathematical premise s and which ask the students to perform qualitative inquiries.

Following this correlation, we chose to code each textbook problem on two dimensions: is the problem’s premise primarily conceptual or mathematical, and is the problem’s inquiry primarily quantitative or qualitative? A conceptual premise was assigned for problems which led with a description of a physical system (be it verbal or visual), whereas a mathematical premise was given when a problem provided the student with an explicit mathematical representation (like a graph, table, or equation). Some problems mentioned a physical system while also giving the student a  mathematical version —usually because a graph or equation w as atypical, more complicated than usual, or simply something the student couldn’t  construct or measure on their own. These problems were coded as having a mathematical premise, as the mathematical representation of the system would be the students’ starting point for the problem solving. Furthermore, any biasing effect from this coding decision would act in opposition to our hypothesis, such that any confirming results would be minimized instead of magnified. For similar reasons, problems which described a generalized ‘object,’ ‘particle,’ or ‘vector’ on a standard 𝑥𝑥𝑥𝑥 coordinate plane as their primary agent were coded as mathematical. Regarding the style of inquiry, quantitative problems were those that required the student to perform a calculation, construct a mathematical expression or diagram, or provide a numerical answer; qualitative inquiries were those which involved interpretation, evaluation, or translation—usually through open-ended responses. Some problems had multiple parts, in which both quantitative and qualitative inquiries would be performed. These problems were coded as having both inquiry types. Subsequently, chapters 2-12 are grouped together as “Part One: Mechanics” and cover the introductory topics of motion, forces, energy, momentum, and rotation. For our exploration, we coded all end-of-chapter questions from the “Part One” chapters.

We can only speculate as to why the ratios of problem categories are so lopsided. Concept-X questions may be so prevalent because they immediately make clear and salient connections to the chapter topics, or because they showcase real-world applications for the subject matter—a top priority to many physics educators. The solutions to X-Quant problems are usually single numerical values, making it easy to include answers to odd-numbered problems in the textbook without having large, cumbersome blocks of jargon-heavy text or using up a significant portion of the page count; this simplicity may also serve instructors who do not have the time to carefully evaluate large numbers of open-ended responses in courses with high student-to-teacher ratios. In any case, the motivations behind the observed problem frequencies are beyond the scope of this work. As mentioned earlier, it is not the case that each of our coding combinations represents a singular epistemic game, nor that any epistemic game is only useful in one problem category. For instance, problems in the X-Both codes may evoke a wide variety of epistemic games based on the number, nature, and order of their piecewise inquiries. However, the Mapping epistemic games described by Tuminaro and Redish (2007) each have a premise-inquiry combination that best aligns with the entry conditions for that gam e: Meaning-to-Math would tend to work best in Concept-Quant problems, while Math-to-Meaning would be best for Math-Qual problems. These associations mean that students using the Wolfson (2020) textbook (or instructional material with similarly skewed problem types) would likely be given ample opportunities to exercise Meaning-to-Math on a regular basis throughout the course, while Math-to-Meaning may hardly see use at all. These complementary Mapping games involve priming and activating similar knowledge resources but in inversed orders. If our hypothesis regarding the bidirectionality of knowledge cuing relationships is correct, then the physics students’ predominant experience of using conceptual elements to cue mathematical elements would reinforce only one side of the cuing pathway. If we wish to improve physics students’ ability to productively call upon their mathematical knowledge in diverse contexts, we may need to provide them with more balance d instructional experiences to ensure that both cuing pathways are utilized regularly.

There is an additional consequence to favoring Concept-Quant exercises so heavily in instructional material: these problems are the most likely to be solvable using the Recursive Plug-and-Chug epistemic game. As this approach does not require the activation of conceptual resources at all, its use only furthers the isolation of physics students’ mathematical and conceptual knowledge. Diversifying the problem types in physics textbooks would not only provide more balanced opportunities for a wider range of epistemic games, but it would also reduce the overall reliability of Recursive Plug-and-Chug. This could discourage physics students from coming to rely on it so quickly and universally, though Concept-Quant questions would still need to be crafted with care to encourage the use of deeper epistemic games.

One easy way to improve the epistemic quality of  Concept-Quant problems is to extend their inquiry past the calculation phase to  provide some minor opportunities for mathematical ideas to re-cue conceptual resources. When discussing the relationship between physics and mathematics, Redish and Kuo (2015) shared a diagram of a standard modeling process, seen in Figure 2, which describes a generalized pattern for translating a real-world system into a mathematical representation and back. Figure 2 Modeling Process Diagram from Redish and Kuo (2015, p.567) Concept-Quant problems typically start in the bottom-left corner of the diagram, presenting the student with a physical description and asking them to first model the system using mathematics before doing some processing 865 to discover a desired quantity. While  coding the textbook problems, it was not our goal to measure the depth or complexity of a given problem. However, we observed that nearly every Concept-Quant problem terminated at the end of the processing step: students were asked to present the number displayed on their calculator without any further analysis. Very rarely, the students were asked to compare their numerical answer to some stated value, like seeing if the calculated speed of a vehicle was above a speed limit; these post-calculation analyses were usually so shallow that we did not feel they warranted a reclassification (the very few exceptions ended up with the X-Both code).

The interpreting step of the modeling process, while perhaps not as rich as a dedicated Math-Qual problem, still requires translating a mathematical entity into something physical. Even if this problem extension only involve s interpreting a single number or a simplified expression, it would fortify in some small way the neglected cuing relationships which point from mathematics to conceptualizations; additionally, this would be another opportunity to mitigate the usefulness of Recursive Plug-and-Chug games. Physics instructors looking to give their students more balanced cuing opportunities could expanding standard Concept-Quant problems to included qualitative components after the processing phase. There are some clear limitations to our exploratory study. First, we only coded the first section of the textbook, but given the consistency of the pattern of Concept-X and X-Quant question frequencies from chapter to chapter in Part One, we felt it sufficient to demonstrate the systemic preference. Second, these frequencies only reflect the trends in the Wolfson (2020) textbook; there are many other physics textbooks which could be analyzed, though our personal experience suggests that m ost of those texts would also yield imbalanced results  (the extent of the asymmetry would likely vary from book to book). Third, students will not work through every problem in the textbook, though we feel we can safely assume that subsets of the end-of-chapter problems selected by an instructor (or student) would likely not stray significantly from the frequencies seen across the problems as a whole—and even if they did, it is highly improbable that they would diverge enough to bring the categories into balance. Finally, textbooks do not represent the entirety of learning experiences for physics students. The epistemic games which are applicable to laboratory exercises, for example, will likely be very different than those used in problem solving. That said, textbooks are a significant component of physics courses, as they can influence assignments, lectures, and even assessments. This makes them at the very least a worthwhile starting point for examination.

A great deal of physics education research has devoted itself to understanding why physics students encounter so much difficulty in effectively incorporating mathematical and conceptual knowledge. The prevalence of these hardships across every level of instruction indicates that there are broad, systemic factors that recent innovations have not yet addressed. Identifying these factors would have significant ramifications not just for physics education, but for any discipline which requires the coalescence o f many types of knowledge —especially if the research improved or expanded on the many theoretical constructs for modeling cognition and conceptual change employed by educational researchers across domains.

Our experience in physics education leads us to believe that the knowledge cuing mechanism described by KiP is bi directional, and that physics students are given largely imbalanced opportunities to develop conceptual-to-mathematic cuing over the opposite direction. Our preliminary analysis of an introductory physics textbook revealed an alarming discrepancy in the number of end-of-chapter problems which use conceptual vs. mathematical premises and which ask for quantitative vs. qualitative inquiries. While undesirable, this asymmetry in standard physics educational material provides a clear target for deeper investigation, which could provide the empirical observations necessary to make nuanced inferences about the imperceptible components of cognitive systems.

As concerning as they may be, the misalignments in physics students’ conceptual and mathematical knowledge may provide a rich opportunity for studying the relationship between student learning, instructional experiences, and interdisciplinary knowledge. Investigating this phenomenon would not only yield domainspecific advancements for physics educators but could also help develop or refine cognitive models to the benefit of the learning science field at large. Our eventual goal is to perform a larger empirical study, in which we would use PhysMath simulations to provide physics students with supplemental educational opportunities to engage in the Math-to-Meaning epistemic game. If such a study could demonstrate a significant improvement in the physics students’ mathematical and conceptual integration, it would be a strong indicator that the bidirectionality of cuing between knowledge elements deserves consideration. Furthermore, it would support the continued development of instructional material and tools capable of scaffolding bidirectional refinement between interconnected knowledge elements.
