# Exploring Convergences and Divergences in Pedagogical Judgment During Teacher Workplace Conversations

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
While teacher learning is typically studied in the context of formal professional development (PD) activities, many teacher learning opportunities emerge from teachers’ daily workplace conversations. However, these conversations are spread across time and space, difficult to analyze, and are less prominent in the field as the locus of research. In this paper, we explore challenges and opportunities for teachers to hone their pedagogical judgment, amidst convergences and divergences in their perspectives, while teachers are attending to their daily work. To do so, we integrate two frameworks for the study of teachers as agentic sensemakers. We illustrate the integrated perspective through cases of two secondary mathematics teachers’ team meetings from two schools in Israel. The analysis highlights that even when conflicts between pedagogical perspectives are explicit, some aspects of their convergences and divergences are implicit, which poses a challenge for teachers’ productive pedagogical discourse and for researchers’ analysis of workplace conversations.

Objective Our starting point for this paper echoes a call by Lefstein et al. (2020) to shift our systematic attention, as a field, from research of formal professional development (P D) activities to research of ongoing learning oppor tunities that emerge from teacher s’ on-the-job conversations (see also Coburn, 2001; Horn, 200 5; Little, 1990). One of the key features of such discourses that makes it p roductive is its being multiple-voiced with “different perspectives presented and attended to” (Lefstein et al., 2020, p. 363; see also Trachtenberg-Maslaton et al., 2023; van Es, 2012). We further elaborate this line of research by unpacking one of its complexities. Namely, we explore the notion that since pedagogical judgment has multiple domains (Horn & Garner, 2022) and argumentation levels (Herbst & Chazan, 2011), it is not always explicit nor clear which of them converge or diverge in a disagreement. For example, teachers might agree on a certain goal  or responsibility (e.g., supporting students in pa ssing a standardized test) but think differently about how this should be realized in action. Exploring these aspects of teacher discourse requir es theoretical and conceptual frameworks that guide us towards (1) aspects of pedagogical judgment we s hould point our gaze at, amidst (2) teachers’ multi ple daily responsibilities; including (3) how to operationali ze them in research; and (4) how to operationalize productive teacher workplace conversations in practice. Our claim here is not that such frameworks do not exist. Rather, our goal in this paper is to integrate two existing fra meworks that, as we  argue, together meet these demands: Pedagogical Judgment (Horn & Kane, 2022) and Practical Rationality (Herbst & Chazan, 2011). We first develop this integrated perspective theoretically, and then  illustrate its affordances based on data from two pilot studies we conducted with the goal of capturing teacher learning in their routine meetings and conversations across their learning ecologies (Ehrenfeld, 2022).

The data features two secondary mathematics teacher s’ teams from two Arabic-speaking schools in Israel, within different naturalistic professional settings. Both meetings included episodes which were rich in contrasting perspectives, an aspect of teachers’ pr oductive pedagogical discourse that is often missin g in conversations about teaching, but exceptionally com mon in conversations about assessment (Trachtenb ergMaslaton et al., 2023). The first case comes from a team of five middle-school teachers, meeting to coordinate a grading effort for a STEM excellence-track entry exam. The second case comes from a team  of two high-school teachers, meeting for counseling with a district co ach on how to prepare students for the national standardized mathematics exam. We ask what convergences and divergences in pedagogical judgment emerged from episodes of disagreements in the two discussions, with an eye towards linking the two frameworks and clarifying methodological possibilities they open for a robust study of teacher conversations across professional settings.

## Conceptual framework

We briefly describe the frameworks. The first frame work theorizes aspects of pedagogical judgment we s hould point our gaze at as action, reasoning, and responsibilities (Horn & Garner, 2022). The second framework is used to further operationalize those domains of pedagogical judgment (Herbst & Chazan, 2011). In addition to the two, we use the notion of productive pedagogical discourse (Lef stein et al., 2020) to ground our comparison of the discourses we observed to those currently labeled in the literature as productive. Firstly, Horn (2020) and Horn and Garner (2022) des cribe the evolution of pedagogical judgment in terms of the interplay between pedagogical action, pedagogical reasoning, and pedagogical responsibili ties (See Figure 1a). Pedagogical action refers to the “intentional and unintentional choice s teachers make” (p. 59). Horn and Garner highlight that the same pedagogical acti ons may have different interpretations and rational es, which constitute teachers’ pedagogical reasoning. Finally, as teachers act and reason, they engage with an array of obligations, which is framed in this framework as t heir pedagogical responsibilities. In this paper, attending to the framework of pedagogical judgment guides us to the three components and their relationships as the  focal domains of conversation and learning.

Secondly, the framework of practical rationality (H erbst & Chazan, 2003; Herbst & Chazan, 2011; Herbst & Chazan, 2023) suggests conceptual backgrou nd and methodologies for eliciting the ways mathema tics teaching rests on more than teachers’ technical kno wledge, and instead also builds on a network of per sonal commitments and institutional contexts. Understandi ng the practical rationality of teaching – tacit knowledge of what is practical in practice – can help explain why some pedagogical recommendations are perceived by teachers as feasible and desirable, while others are seen as unlikely. The goal, then, is understanding how issues of teacher agency, sense-making, and structural conditions interrelate  in mathematics teaching and teacher learning. In th is paper we build on two main notions of the practical  rationality framework. First, the articulation of four professional obligations that can organize the rationality of instructional actions: disciplinary,  individual, interpersonal, and institutional. And second, the representation of teachers’ arguments ab out instruction (Figure 1b, Herbst & Chazan, 2011) by adapting Toulmin’s (1969) argument layout, which is reconfigured to include the circumstances (as data) and action (as claim), in addition to warrants (as rationale) and backing of the warrants (often as obligations underlying the rationale).

Finally, Lefstein et al., (2020) argued that teache r judgment can be “sharpened through repeated participation in productive deliberation with colle agues about problems of practice” (p. 3; see also, Ehrenfeld, 2023; Vedder-Weise et al., 2018), and proposed six key principle s for such productive pedagogical discourse, including (1) a focus on teachers’ genuine concerns; (2) the use of evidence, explanations, and reason s; (3) rich representations of practice; (4) multiple voices wi th different perspectives presented a nd attended to; (5) generative orientations toward students, learning, content, and teaching; and (6) combining support and critique.

In this paper we pay specific attention to the seco nd and fourth point, following prior findings that reconciling multiple voices and contradicting perspectives is a  central aspect of productive pedagogical discourse (Trachtenberg-Maslaton et al., 2023; van Es, 2012).

## Methods

Phrased in terms of our theoretical framing, our analytic question is: What convergences and divergences in teachers suggested actions, warrants, and implicit obligations emerged from the two episodes of disagreement?

## Research context

The two cases of teacher team meetings presented here come from two Arabic-speaking schools in Israel, serving the Arab minority in an educational sub-system where Arabic is the official language (Cinamon et al., 2015). The first case is from a middle school, where the team meeting of five mathematics teachers focused on the coordination of a grading effort. All five teachers gave a test to select a limited number of students for the STEM honors class. The segment we bring here involves a disagreement with regards to grading for students w hose results were correct but did not demonstrate how they checked the correctness of their answer. T he second case is from a high-school where the team of two teachers met with thei r district coach to discuss daily routines and preparation for the end-of year standardized test. The segment we bring her e involves a disagreement between one of the teachers and the coach with regards to w hat students should and should not learn, and the a mount of practice tests they should take, to prepare them for the end-of-year standardized test.

## Data collection and data analysis

Data for the first case was collected with two vide o cameras recording a 45-minute team meeting, by Rima, and for the second with a recording of a 30-minute virtual meeting by Randa. Then, Rima and Randa created a content log where they identified the main topics discussed, and pointed out two episodes (in each case) which were rich in pedagogical reasoning. We started the analysis by c oding and visually representing the main argument structures according to Herbst and Chazan, (2011), relying on Toulmin (1969). For each main argument, built around a specific pedagogical action, we identified  its main circumstances, warrants, and backing. Whe n the backing was implied rather than explicitly argued, researchers proposed what it might have been (that is, what statement could reasonably provide backing for the wa rrant, as in Pinto & Cooper (2017) and we used a do tted frame to highlight this implicitness. We then considered those arguments in light of teachers implied professional obligations (Herbst & Chazan, 2011). Then, we moved  on to identify convergences and divergences in the pedagogical judgments, with an eye towards learning  opportunities in the discussions. Note that we dis cuss “learning opportunities” instead of “learning” beca use of the short nature of th ese pilot studies. Finally, we compared the discourse as it was gleaned from the analysis to what we know from the literature about productive pedagogical discourse, as discussed by Lefstein et al. (2020).

## Results

For each of the two case studies, we briefly illust rate the coding of argumentation according to the p ractical rationality representation, followed by our interpr etation of teachers' (implied) professional obligat ions, and finally present our overall interpretation of teachers’ opportunities to hone their pedagogical judgment. Study 1: “They have to validate the answer, it’s a basic skill!” The episode discussed here is four minutes long. It deals with Question 8 on the STEM excellence exam. Question 8 included two unrelated equations with one parameter. The episode started when Dina told the team that she gave two points (out of three) for those students who so lved an equation correctly but did not check  their answer, to which Asil answered, “I think we can give them three points here without deducting any.” This starting point and the ensuing conversation constitute the body of argumentation to be represented here (see Figure 2). The Circumstances for both pedagogical arguments is, then, grading a correct result without evidence of verification. Asil’s suggested Action is grading full points, and Dina’s suggested Action is reducing points. Dina provided two warrants for her suggested action. Fir st and foremost, Dina argued that verification is a  basic skill for mathematics students. And second, she argued that test instructions explicitly guided students to ver ify their solutions. She backed the first warrant, that verification is a basic skill, by invoking the example of 9th grade word problems as a mathematical context where that skill  will be useful. Dina did not explicitly back the i nstruction warrant, but it is implied (and hence dotted in the  representation) that students should follow exam i nstructions. In contrast, Asil suggested three warrants for her suggested action. First, students' grades will be low. Second, she did not teach her students that they should always check their results. And third, students solved the problem correctly. She backed the “low grades” warrant by s aying the overall exam was not easy, and by providi ng an example of one specific student who is “average,” solved Question 8 correctly, and will now lose two points. She backed her warrant of what student s’ learned by describing her pedagogy around verifi cation. Namely, that she runs through the validation verbally, rather than w riting it down and making verification visible. The  episode ended with Dina distinguishing two types of exams: “In a normal exam,  the goal is to check how well I, as a teacher, support students and how well they have understood the material [...] this exam aims to check the STEM program students.” For this, Asil responded that if that is the case, they need to co nduct a separate exam for the grade records, to which the teachers agreed. Lookin g at this short episode through Herbst and Chazan’s  (2011) four professional obligations (disciplinary, indivi dual, interper sonal, and institutional), it seems like the two diverged not only in their suggested actions and wa rrants, but also in their main obligations. While on a basic level their responsibilities converged around carin g for individual students' success in math, w e interpret Dina’s argument as highlighting disciplinary and institutional obligations. Disciplinary, as in why the mathematical skill is needed, and institutional, as in the STEM excellence tracking function of this exam. Asil’s argument highlights individual aspects of care for students’ socioemoti onal aspects of learning,  and making their work and understanding institutionally visible on record.

## Discussion

Lefstein et al. (2020) critique the tendency of looking for teacher learning only within PD activities by comparing it to that of a drunk looking for their keys under the streetlamp rather than where they lost them. In  this article, we have investigated opportunities for learning in authentic settings outside the metaphorical streetlight beam. We contribute to research on teachers’ workplace conversations by elaborating the notion of multiple voices with contrasting perspectives. The analysis highlights that even within episodes of explicit contrasting perspectives, different convergences and divergences in  t eachers’ pedagogical judgment stay implicit. The conceptual lens suggested here attend s to this challenge by seeking  convergences and divergences in actions, reasoning, and responsibilities (Horn & Garner, 2022), operationalized analytically through teachers’ suggested actions, warrants, and obligations (Herbst & Chazan, 2011). It contributes a nuanced lens for in terpreting pedagogical discourse  and the opportunities workplace conversations offer for  honing teachers' pedagogical judgment.
