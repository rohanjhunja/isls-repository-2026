# The Pemmican Experience: Representing Indigenous Knowledge and Values in K-12 Classrooms

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Issues of (mis)representation of cultures across K-12 education remain a challenge, especially for representations of Indigenous communities. As a team of Tribal knowledge holders, K-12 educators, cultural technologists, and university researchers, we created a cultural representation and teaching resources to support respectful stewardship of Indigenous representations in classroom learning. In this poster, we share insights from our design and implementation process of an Indigenous-created digital interactive narrative experience, Pemmican Experience, for K-12 classrooms.

## Introduction

Even as culturally sustaining/revitalizing approaches gain traction ac ross educational contexts, accurate representation of diverse cultures, knowledges, and histories remains a challenge (e.g., Gellman, 2024). Issues of (mis)representation of cultures across K-12 education remain a challenge, especially in the perpetuation of inaccurate and incomplete representations of Indige nous communities, which results in misconceptions o f Indigenous peoples (Journell, 2009). In part, this is due to the reality that most publicly available resources for K12 education, especially in STEM, are not created b y the Indigenous communities whose cultures are bei ng represented. Not only does this reify broad misconceptions about Indigenous peoples among all students, but these (mis)representations serve as a form of cultural identity silencing (Leigh-Osroosh & Hutchison, 2019) for Native learners specifically. In response, some scholars argue that technology stands poised to support teachers and Tribal knowledge holders to address this issue (Corbe tt, 2018; Morales-Chicas et al., 2019). To this end, our team of Indigenous and non-Indigenous educators, designers, and researchers ex plored the questions: (1) how can we support the creation of Indigenous-led representations for K-12 classrooms? and (2) how do we support educators’ respectful stewardship of these representations in their classrooms? Together, we created a cultural representation and teaching resources to support respectful stewardship of those representations. Theoretical frame Our work is framed by both culturally sustaining/re vitalizing (McCarty & Lee, 2014) and culturally dis ruptive (San Pedro, 2018) approaches. Whether a cultural representation is culturally sustaining/revitalizing or culturally disruptive is dependent on one ’s own culture and cultural competence. Thus, our e fforts are focused toward sustaining and revitalizing Indigenous culture not only of Indigenous learners but also of local Tribe s through respectful stewardship of cultural representations. Due to the systemic disenfranchisement of Indigenous peoples in STEM (Ruef et al., 2020), present forms of emerging technology do not support or engage Indigenous ways of being and knowing (Litts et al., 2021). In fact, many of the historic and current forms of emerging technologies harm, silence, and further traumatize Indigenous pe oples. In response and in resistance, scholars push  for “technological self-determination and sovereignty” (Winter & Boudreau, 2018). Building from this stance, we posit that who designs matters as it inherently determines the cultural process of design and therefore the product of what is designed. Thus, we seek to (re)engage technologies by fundamentally decolonizing by whom, how, and why these technologies are created in the first place.

## Methods

We report on our insights from engaging in an itera tive design process, which began August 2023 and re mains ongoing. The design and implementation process was guided by a Tribal Knowledge Holder (Author 3), the pedagogical design led by two sixth grade teachers (Authors 4 & 5), and the digital interactive design  led by a cultural technologist (Author 8). The products of t his iteration included: Pemmican Experience, a digi tal interactive narrative experience, and 17 lesson plans across Science, Math, Social Studies, and English Language Arts. The entire curricular experience was implemen ted in two sixth-grade classrooms over the course of three days. In this poster, we share insights from qualit ative data, including: design work, meeting notes, fieldnotes, analytic reflections, and design documentation of our process.

## Findings

We began our design process by identifying and centering two values (kinship and r eciprocity), which gave us purpose for designing, creating, sharing, and learn ing. While this value-centered approach was explicit in the design process, it was not until the classroom implementation that the value-centered pedagogy became visible to our team. The Tribal Knowledge Holder and K-12 Teachers modeled and prompted students to enact values of relationality and kinship through being in relation  with each other in collaborative and communal lear ning activities. For example, Author 4 (social studies t eacher) guided students in class-wide and paired reading of Braiding Sweetgrass for Young Adults (Kimmerer & Sm ith, 2022), which reinforced the values evident in the Pemmican Experience. Then, Author 5 (science teacher) taught about values with native l ocal plant cycles and how to gather ingredients sustainably as a framing for the communal experience of making pemmican. Thr ough centering values, both teachers integrated Indigeno us lessons (shared throug h the Pemmican Experience) and Western lessons (as established in State standards) to support students’ learning diverse knowledges. Moreover, the classroom implementation of the repre sentation was inherently interdisciplinary and connected learning across STEM and non-STEM disciplines, which resonates with Indigenous p erspectives that do not silo knowledge in disciplines as Western per spectives do. Through analysis, we identified a ran ge of interdependent content connections throughout the c urricular unit. An example of this became visible w hen students made pemmican with packaged ingredients using a traditional grinding stone (see Figure 1). This lesson transcended disciplinary content areas as well as t ime and place by connecting students with the histo rical significance of making pemmican through using the t raditional grinding stone. This culminated in a sum mative learning experience that simultaneously enacted the Tribal Knowledge Holder’ s learning goals for the students and the state learning standards as an integration of Indigenous and Western learning. ## Significance

In addition to expanding available resources for re spectful stewardship of Indigenous perspectives in K-12 classrooms, our work also provides methodological i nsights for how to create Indigenous-led STEM learning experiences. Our work offers resources and practice s regarding representations of Indigenous perspecti ves not only for science educators but also for K-12 educators across disciplines. In particular, we present a value-centered approach to bridge Indigenous and Western knowledge  through interdisciplinary and experiential learnin g. We further share how this value-centered approach can serve as an anchor for techno logy-mediated knowledge-sharing in K-12 STEM education contexts. This study provides resources and practices for integrating Indigenous and Western perspectives for K-12 educators across disciplines. The methods of representation and insights from our work deepen and expand current understandings and available resources for culturally sustaining/revitalizing learning experiences in K-12 classrooms.
