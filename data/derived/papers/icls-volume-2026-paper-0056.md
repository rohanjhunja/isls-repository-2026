# Modeling Discourse Shifts in Critical Machine Learning: How Black Girls Reimagine Surveillance through Design

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study examines how the discourse of surveillance shifted among middle school Black girls through their participation in a Critical Machine Learning (CML) project grounded in sociocultural learning theories and Critical Race Technology Theory. Using a Quantitative Ethnography research design, this study collected data through students' interaction in their groups over two consecutive sessions within a twelve-session program implementation. Ordered network analysis (ONA) reveals that students initially positioned surveillance in a binary category, either for care or control, with a weak connection between the two. Eventually, students began to position care as a primary purpose of surveillance and justified why care was important. These findings underscore the importance of integrating critical STEM education in schools, providing students with opportunities to engage and question the technologies they design.

Machine learning technologies are embedded in everyday life. Surveillance tools, such as facial recognition systems in airports, which are used to monitor people’s activities, utilize machine learning technologies (Horkay et al., 2022). In recent discussions of surveillance and machine learning, a crucial issue has been whether these technologies protect vulnerable populations or reinforce systemic inequalities. On the one hand, some scholars argue that surveillance tools are necessary to help vulnerable populations (Ball, 2001). On the other hand, critics acknowledge that these tools are trained based on biased datasets that harm marginalized communities (Lee, 2024).

Research by Buolamwini and Gebru (2018) and Canella (2018) has shown that facial recognition systems often misidentify and over-police Black communities and shape how youth, particularly Black girls, experience surveillance in schools, public spaces, and digital platforms (Buolamwini & Gebru, 2018; Canella, 2018). Despite the existing scholarship documenting the harms of surveillance (Richards, 2013; Berridge & Grigorovich, 2022), little is known about how young people themselves conceptualize and respond to surveillance when engaging with technology design. With this in mind, the research question that guides this study is: How is the discourse of surveillance shifted by students during robot design activities? Machine learning technologies are not only used to recommend music, recommend a product on social media, or filter emails; they are also central to systems that watch and police people. Facial recognition systems deployed at airports and street monitoring cameras demonstrate how surveillance has become an everyday function of machine learning technologies (Lyon, 2022; Ball, 2001). Lyon (2001) describes surveillance as having two faces: either to control people or to protect them (Lyon, 2022). Supporters of these technologies believe that surveillance provides care, for example, by assisting vulnerable populations such as the elderly, children, or people with disabilities (Ball, 2001).   However, critics acknowledge that surveillance often leads to discrimination, inequalities, and data misuse issues (Lyon, 2022; Ball,2001; Richards, 2013; Berridge & Grigorovich, 2022). They argue that because these systems are trained by biased data and shaped by social and political assumptions, they reproduce inequalities and disproportionately harm marginalized populations (Lyon, 2022; Ball,2001).

Research by Buolamwini and Gebru (2018) in their gender shade project indicates that facial recognition performed best on light-skinned males and worst on dark-skinned females, highlighting how bias is encoded into supposedly neutral technologies we use every day. Simone Browne (2015) explains that discrimination against Black communities is not a new phenomenon. Browne (2015) argues that surveillance has historically racialized Black communities, illustrating New York City’s eighteenth-century lantern laws, which forced enslaved black, mixed-race, and indigenous people to carry candle lanterns at night to make their movement visible. That early surveillance reflects today’s biometric monitoring systems that target Black communities. Together, this body of literature demonstrates how surveillance is both justified as a form of care and enacted as a form of control. 464

## Theoretical framework

To explore how Black students think about and engage with topics around digital surveillance, this study draws on two theoretical perspectives: Critical Race Technology Theory (CRTT) and sociocultural learning theories. CRTT provides a lens to understand how experiences of race, bias, and power shape students’ ideas about designing machine learning based robots (Tanksley, 2023; 2024). From this perspective, students’ ideation and conversations are not neutral, but instead reflect the broader issue of equity and representation in technology. Tanksley argues that digital spaces are not neutral and that they are racial projects that reproduce dominant ideologies and anti-black logics. CRTT advances several tenets, including recognition of racism in technology, challenging the dominant ideologies  of neutrality, commitment to social justice, and centrality of Black lived experiences.

Complementing this view, Sociocultural learning theories highlight that learning is inherently social, cultural, and mediated by tools, language, and participation (Vygotsky, 1978; Lave & Wenger, 1991). From this perspective, knowledge does not reside solely in the individual, but it is constructed by interaction and communication through meaningful activities. Learning occurs as participants use cultural artifacts, or language, technologies, and shared practices to make sense of their world. In this study, students leveraged language to share and negotiate ideas and construct the collective understanding of the concept of surveillance. We define a shift in discourse as changes in the way that students talk about and conceptualize surveillance, in terms of its purposes, and the values they consider across two activities. Taken together, CRTT and sociocultural learning theories provide a framework to analyze how students’ context, lived experience, and interaction with each other inform their evolving understanding of surveillance and technology design.

## Research design, participants, and activities

This study uses Quantitative Ethnography (Shaffer, 2017) as its methodological framework to explore the shifts in students’ discourse about surveillance during robot design. Within this framework, we employed Ordered Network Analysis (ONA) to visualize students’ discourses in two activities. The Critical Machine Learning (CML) project is a curriculum that was implemented in a small private middle school in the US, designed for girls, in twelve weeks. The project’s participants were fifteen students in grade s 6, 7, and 8. All participants’ names used in this study are pseudonyms.

Curriculum was adapted from MIT’s RAISE initiative and Critical Machine Learning curriculum (Arastoopour Irgens et al., 2022; Arastoopour Irgens et al., 2024). In this paper, we will analyze the following two activities: First, “superhero drawing,” where groups designed an initial prototype of a machine learning model and community helper robot; and second, a reflective “discussion on robot design” with students, the instructor, and researchers on their initial prototype.   In the CML curriculum, for activity one, we designed four questions for students, and we put students in groups of three or four and asked them to ideate a robot that serves their community while they were answering those four questions. Questions were: 1-Pick an issue and describe it. Who? What? Why? 2-What can your robot do? How does it work? 3-Who can use it and who can benefit? and 4-How might it be misused? Each group had a poster, and they started to interact with each other and ideate their robots based on these four questions. The main goal for this activity was to design a robot for social good. For the second activity, we organized a whole-classroom discussion to reflect on their initial prototype (ideation). We started by asking about each group’s ideas and why they wanted to make that robot. Then, we showed them a video about “lantern laws,” and we continued the discussion on the idea of Surveillance. Within those two activities, there are four projects in which students ideate a robot in their group to serve their community. Projects which I will explore more in this paper are: 1-A Robot called “RAS”, a wearable jewelry that has a camera and microphone, which, for example, kids can wear, and their parents can monitor their daily activities. 2: A robot or app called “clock them receipts”, a tool that screenshots and screen records inappropriate words that people send to each other as digital proof. 465 Figure 1 Group RAS’s Poster of Activity 1 (Initial Prototype) We audio-and video-recorded two 75-minute sessions focused on students’ designs and idea generation. For example, when students described surveillance as a tool to protect old people, we labeled it as “surveillance as care”. The data was segmented by conversational turn; each row represented a turn of talk, and each column showed a code. If a code appeared, we placed “1” in the corresponding cell, and if a code did not appear, we placed “0” in the corresponding cell. One researcher coded the full dataset. Sometimes the teacher doesn’t like them or their friends, so that can actually help them out.” Epistemic network analysis using Ordered Networks To examine the shift in students’ discourses over two activities, we analyzed 877 lines of data using a version of Epistemic Network Analysis called Ordered Network Analysis (ONA) (version 1.7.0) (Marquart et al., 2018; Shaffer et al., 2016; Bowman et al., 2021; Tan et al., 2023). The unit of analysis was defined bMarquart y a combination of activity and speaker. Thus, we created a discourse network for every student for each of the two activities for a total of 29 units of analysis.

ENA operationalizes connections in data by counting the co-occurrences of codes within a stanza (Shaffer et al., 2016; Shaffer & Ruis, 2017). Using a moving stanza window of ten lines, co-occurrences were identified between the current line (response) and nine preceding lines (common ground) (Ruis et al., 2019; Siebert-Evenstone et al., 2017). In ONA, the direct connection between nodes (codes) is represented by a unique broadcast model. Within the model, the common ground refers to codes that are in the preceding discourse within the window size or what the speaker is responding to. Visually, the common ground is located at the apex of the triangular edge representing the connection. In contrast, the response or base is the code that is in the current line of talk or what the speaker is responding with. Visually, the response is located at the base of the triangle representing the connection. To explicitly indicate the order of the conceptual flow from common ground to response, a chevron (an arrow symbol) is placed inside the triangle. The chevron always points from the ground to the response to show how ideas are built upon each other. For each pair of codes, if the connection is bidirectional, the chevron appears only on the sides that show a stronger connection, and if the connections are identical, the chevron will appear on both edges.

Larger nodes highlight the codes that are used more frequently to respond or follow up with previous ideas. The color and saturation of each node show the number of self-connections for that specific code, which means that codes occurred both in “response” (current line of talk) and “ground” (lines within the window). To visualize these high-dimensional networks, ONA utilizes singular value decomposition (SVD) and mean rotation (MR) to reduce dimensions and project the network onto a two-dimensional mathematical space (Bowman et al., 467 2021; Shaffer et al., 2016). Mean rotation aligns networks from different activities within a shared space by minimizing angular differences between them, ensuring that the comparison reflects the shift in discourse structure rather thandifferences in mod el orientation. This process adjusts the model so that the average networks from each activity are aligned along the same axis.

In this reduced space, each network is converted into a single point in the two-dimensional space (represented by a circular dot) and thus makes the comparison across multiple units of analysis visually accessible and allows for measurement between groups of units. To further visually compare the two activities in time, we also represented the mean network as a centroid (square dot), showing the mean structure of the discourse for that activity.  In this study, 19.4% of the variance of the data is in the X-axis, and 34.2% of the variance of the data is in the Y-axis. The model shows a strong Pearson goodness of fit between the visualization and the original model, with 0.95 in the X-axis and 0.98 in the Y-axis.

In the second activity, ONA shows a denser and complex discourse structure. Surveillance as control  remained the central code, but with more connection to viewing surveillance through a lens of necessity  and Surveillance as care. Importantly, in this plot, there is a shift in the students’ discourse patterns in which Surveillance as Care 469 now more often appears before Surveillance as Control. For example, Mia mentioned that their robot will help old and disabled people to protect them, which we labeled as surveillance as care. She stated, “old people whoneed to be taken care of. Also, if there, people with dementia then wander off into the streets.” Tia built her idea based on Mia’s, and she said their robot will be helpful for people who are in rehabilitation centers to prevent them from getting out of the center. She mentioned “how easy it is to get out of rehab…. if you're in a rehab for drugs, then you'll find them somewhere out there. It's not just to get caught to make sure they're doing what they're supposed to do, so they won't get in a bad situation.” In both examples, students expressed that their design would be to prevent harm, and because they aim to support these vulnerable populations, they want to build a robot to watch them. In this plot, surveillance as care discourse often happened first, followed by control, which contrasted with the first activity.

The second strongest connection in this plot is between surveillance as control and viewing surveillance through a lens of necessity, with surveillance through a lens of necessity  often occurring before surveillance as control. This pairing shows a shift in how students rationalized the need to monitor others. Viewing surveillance through the lens of necessity refers to moments when students justified surveillance as essential for those who are in vulnerable positions. For instance, in the “clock them receipts” robot prototype, Kia, in response to the instructor’s question about (this seems like an app to call people out, right?), mentioned, “What we're saying is, if somebody's being rude, they like to put it on other people. So, if somebody's putting it on other people, it could show that the other person did it and show that that person's innocent.” Kia explains that their design will create a digital proof in situations where students’ voices might be dismissed. This robot will give students a protective tool to defend themselves.

The network findings reveal that surveillance as control was central to participants’ experiences in both sessions. In the first activity, students frequently responded to surveillance as control  with justification rooted in care, reflecting the dominant narrative of surveillance as protection (Ball, 2001). However, the connection toneutrality and non-neutrality remained limited. Students drew on the regularly accepted discourse and narrative, which positions surveillance as a necessary approach to discipline people. This reflects Browne 's (2015) normalization of racialized surveillance, where monitoring is acceptable, especially in the lives of black people. Browne’s (2015) work drives us to consider how anti-Black surveillance is embedded in artificial intelligence systems and how this history shaped Black middle school girls’ assumptions about designing technologies to monitor people. In the second activity, ONA reveals a clear evolution; students more frequently connected control to care and necessity and used those ideas to explain the intent behind their design. In this activity, the flow of the discourse shifted substantially. While Surveillance as control remained central, it appears more as a response to surveillance as care. Participants like Lori and Kia shared how their application will be useful for students who are in trouble with their peers and teachers to prove that they are innocent and that they didn't send inappropriate words to others. Rather than simply reproducing the discourse of surveillance as control, they showed how their robots will resist this negative view of control by protecting and caring for vulnerable populations such as children or the elderly (Lyon, 2022).

By designing those robots, students enacted CRTT’s (Tanksley, 2023, 2024) principles of 1) pursuing social justice through technology, and reimagined their robots to support the elderly, children, and disabled people. This is reflected in the second ONA plot, which surveillance as care often appeared first in their discourse, 2) centrality of lived experiences, which students drew on their personal and family context to design their project, and 3) challenging dominant ideologies, but not in the way we designed for or expected. They didn’t focus on algorithmic bias/non-neutral, or racial oppression. By the second discussion, there was less talk on non-neutral technologies. Whereas they were challenging and reimagining the purpose of surveillance by connecting control to care and necessity in ways that aligned with their current lived experiences and their interactions with children and the elderly in their families. Findings of this study show that middle school students are capable of thinking about technology not just in a technical way, but also can question how it exists and who it serves. Importantly, this shift didn't emerge in isolation, grounded in sociocultural learning theories (Vygotsky, 1978; Lave & Wenger, 1991) and CRTT (Tanksley, 2023, 2024), students’ evolving understanding of surveillance was shaped through peer collaboration, classroom discussion, and culturally meaningful examples and lived experiences. This work offers important implications for teachers and researchers who work in STEM projects to expand their focus from integrating technology in schools to learners' lived experiences, culture, and values.
