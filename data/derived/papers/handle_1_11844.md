# The Conditions for Collaborative Groups’ Regulation of Learning: What can Multimodal Data Showcase?

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
It is argued that collaborative learning flourishes when groups effectively regulate their learning processes, but understanding the conditions enabling such regulation remains a challenge. Despite evidence that a positive socio-emotional atmosphere supports learning, little is known about its role in metacognitive monitoring that initiates regulation. Recent advances in process methods and socially shared regulation of learning (SSRL) research enable us to expand the focus from regulatory processes to the conditions enabling and supporting them. This paper demonstrates our recent research through multimodal data examples from two different studies with the aim to bridge collaborative groups’ regulatory activities with socio-emotional conditions of collaboration. Multimodal data—including psychophysiological measures, video, and audio—provides insights into how socio-emotional and metacognitive conditions within collaborative learning contexts shape regulation. Our findings highlight how multimodal methods capture detailed, temporally interconnected processes that drive group collaboration.

## Introduction

While collaborative learning (CL) has become a popular pedagogical approach in various educational contexts, it has become evident that relying solely on self-regulation of learning (SRL) for individual learning gains is inadequate, as in a group context learners share th e responsibility for their joint success. In CL, the mechanisms of regulation extend from SRL to group-level regulation of learning, including co-regulation (CoRL) and socially shared regulation (SSRL). All three types of regulation mean that students need to control and manage their own and their group’s (in case of CoRL and SSRL) learning processes. They do so by setting shared goals, planning the use of various strategies, monitoring progress,  and adjusting as needed to enhance their learning outcomes (Järvenoja et al., 2015). However, as a highly situated phenomenon, accura te group-level regulation depends on the learning context and appraisals group members make in a spec ific learning situation. Moreover, effective regulation targets not  only task-related cognitive behaviors, but also emotional and  motivational aspects of collaboration.  Emotions and motivation play a mult ifaceted role in CL, extending beyond mere  participation in social interaction (Lobczowski, 2020). While motivation reflects learners’ beliefs about their abilities, goals, and willingness to engage in learning  (Bakhtiar & Hadwin, 2022), emotions refer to affective responses to the task and social setting  that are essential for situational motivation (Pekrun, 201 6). Therefore, in process-oriented research approach, it can be meaningful to consider them in close connection.

In this paper, we argue for the need to expand research on group-level regulation of learning beyond the examination of its processes alone, as CL contains a synergy of different learning mechanisms that mak e it successful (Zabolotna et al., 2023). It is essential to explore the relationship between  these processes and other learning mechanisms and conditions relevant to coll aborative interactions and their regulation (Järvenoja et al., 2024; Zabolotna et al., 2023). That is, several internal (e.g., prior knowledge, e motional states, motivational beliefs) and external factors (e.g., time, resource s, social context) create the conditions for CoRL and SSRL to emerge (Winne & Hadwin, 2008).  In a view of collaborative learning as a complex pro cess, these conditions continuously shape how group members interpret and approach each other within the collaborative settin g. For example, e motional reactions to learning and interaction sign ificantly impact collaboration commitment, task engagement, and the interpretation of the social le arning environment (Lobczowski, 2020). Simultaneous ly, emotional and motivational experiences are constantly formed and reshaped by t he learning experience itself (Dietrich et al., 2022). Regulation of learning has a focal impact on how th is reciprocal relationship affects the process of group learning, however, this relationsh ip is only rarely studied (for exception, see Törmä nen et al., 2023).

It is also still necessary to explore and evidence empirically the actual manifestation of group-level regulation to reach a theoretically and empirically accurate operationali zation of the concept. By combining innovative process methods with theoretical develop ments, we can move forward from the collective proc esses of group-level regulation of learning to examining the situational socio-emotional conditions, including emotions, motivation, and social interactions, under which different patterns of these processes e merge to afford, e.g., collaborative knowledge construction or problem-solving (Zabolotna et al., 2023). Therefore, in this paper we describe three steps that we typically take to iden tify with multimodal data “how” and “why” group-level regulation of learning  emerges in different socio-emotional conditions during CL. These steps also op en the possibility for different analytical methods, such as process mining and multi-channel sequence mining (e.g., Törmänen et al., 2023), ordered network analysis (e.g., Zabolotna et al., 2025), or hybrid human-AI synergy (e.g., Järvelä et al., 2023).

Socially shared regulation of cognition, emotions and motivation in collaborative learning Regulation of learning does not merely indicate a general engagement with the learning task; it involves strategic and concrete activities that demand metacogniti on and enable adaptation or change to improve learning  and collaboration as well as strategic decision-making to overcome challenges (Winne & Hadwin, 2008). Recent research has identified three types of regulation t hat come into play when learners engage in collabor ative work (Hadwin et al., 2018). Firstly, each group member assumes responsibility for regulating their own learning (SRL). SRL refers to the individual learner's goal-directed process of planning, monitoring, evaluating and adapting their cognition, behaviors, motivation, and emotions to a chieve learning objectives (Zimmerman, 2002). Secon dly, group members can provide support to peers in regul ating their learning (CoRL) by temporarily assisting or influencing the regulatory processes of one or more  team members. When this regulatory process extends  to the whole group level, i.e., all group members are enga ged in regulating their learning collectively, SSRL  occurs. SSRL encompasses the transactive and intentional st rategic adaptations made by group members during ph ases of collaborative planning, task execution, and refl ection. In SSRL, group members collaboratively coor dinate, monitor, and control their cognition, motivation, emotions, and behavior through iterative negotiations, realignment, and adaptation (Hadwin et al., 2018). Therefore, genuine SSRL involves the integ ration of multiple individual perspectives in regulating cognitive, behavioral, motivational and emotional aspects of learning. In addition to different regulation types, research ers also differentiate between the target of regula tion, namely, regulation of cognition, and regulation of motivation and emotions.  Regulation of cognition, for its parts, encompasses the processes that define students’ thi nking and actions towards task completion such as changing and updating their task understanding, goals and strategies as t he task progresses as a result of metacognitive monitoring (Hadwin et al., 2018). Regulation of motivation and emotions, while closely related, often intertwine and are considered together in collaborative interactions, referring to distinct aspects: emotion regulation concerns the management of affective responses, whi le motivation regulation involves sustaining or enh ancing one’s willingness to engage in the learning process  (Wolters & Benzon, 2013). Both are grounded in socioemotional processes within the group that can enhance or impe de collaboration, and functions as a learning mechanism for groups to initiate, maintain, or restore emotional and motivational grounds, i.e., general socio-emotional atmosphere in the group, rapport between group members, for effective collaborative interactions (Hadwin et al., 2018). By engaging in socially shared emotion regul ation, group members together ensure emotionally so lid (social) ground s for completing academic tasks (Boekaerts & Pekrun, 2015). The intent is to release negative affect, dissolve emotional tension, unravel emotion al experiences, or reduce negative emotional respon ses to socio-emotionally challenging situations that could hamper the group’s learning and collaboration (Hadwin et al., 2018; Harley et al., 2019; Järvenoja et al., 2015). By engaging in socially shared regulation of motivation, learners purposefully maintain and restore a favorable motiv ational state during a learning process to achieve learning goals (Boekaerts & Pekrun, 2015; Järvenoja et al., 2015). Motivation regulation is activated when ther e is a perceived disruption of goal pursuit (Hadwin et al., 2018; Winne & Hadwin, 2008). It can be used to initiate, restore, strengthen, or redirect attributions made of the learning situation or events, efficacy belie fs, interest or (outcome) utility and perceived benefits associated with a task (Wolters & Benzon, 2013). What makes regulation socially shared is peers’ coordinated and complementary efforts to contribute to regulating their joint motivational and emotional states in a group task.

Regardless of the group-level regulation  target, the need for it is realized through metacog nitive monitoring, which is central to effective regulation. Monitoring can target various internal and external conditions, deriving from the self, the task, the social context, the learning progress or outcome. It enables learners to identify dissonance or misconceptions in their understanding  and align or alter it and/or their goals and plans  as well as the strategies they employ to achieve them (Winne & Hadwin, 2008).  In social contexts, in addition to monitoring learning progress against the standards set by the task, learners are also enabled to monitor their collective progress and to ensure that they have similar understanding of the content (Winne & Hadwin, 2008). The present study In the context of collaborative learning, group-level regulation is not a goal, but rather a necessity to support and align CL activities when there is a dissonance betw een the group members’ perceptions (Järvenoja et al., 2015). In our research, we have moved forward from explori ng purely how regulation occurs at the group level  to the question of ‘why' collaborative groups end up in a certain regulated learning path. This route is full of contextual, situational, and subjective conditions influencing the direction the groups’ learning which are often difficult to capture or explain reliably (Järvelä & Hadwin, 2024).  Different data modalities are not only a possib ility, but a necessity to grasp this variety. For example, while  learners’ introspection is critical to understand subjective appraisals, alone it is insufficient to reveal critical processes and antecedents leading to (lack of) regulation during collaboration. To grasp the complexity of group-level regulation of learning, this paper presents how we build our analysis to address the socio-emotional conditions for it in CL situations through research case examples. Through three steps, we demonstrate how we integrate different data modalities to build up a solid base for further analyses. The first step is grounding work that encompasses c areful operationalizing and coding of group-level regulation. The second step involves depicting the temporal and qualitative interconnections between motivation and emotion regulation, and cognitive regulation. The third step augments t he focus from the regulation of learning to recognizing the socio-emotional conditions that shape it and are crucial to explain it. Our research examples rely on video data that can be complemented with other data sources. Video offers a rich and complex data source to track group-level regulation from the CL process and serves as an entry to contextualized regulation processes in action and real time. It is already multimodal in nature as it provides access to behavioral and bodily reactions in addition to v erbal interaction (Zabolotna et al. 2025). In addition to  visual observation of regulation processes, video as a dat a source allow s to contextualize other data sources, such as physiological reactions or subjective appraisals. The data sets we use to showcase our analytical approach derive from our current and previous studies, where we focused on CL interactions of secondary sc hool students. The collaborative groups are typically composed of three to four students and the tasks are related to STEM subjects. The participants are from the same class and have worked together before. Our research designs emphasize authentic school tasks and real classrooms or a classroom-like setting at the university research laboratory. Multichannel da ta include high quality video recordings, audio recordings collected with high quality individual microphones,  as well as physiological data and different subjec tive data sources, such as situational self-reports and interviews. Prior to the data collection, a statement of research ethics was obtained from the Human Sciences Ethics Committee of the University of Oulu and participation is based on the learner’s (and their guardian’s, in case of minors) consent and is completely voluntary. Identifying group-level regulation of learning in collaborative interactions STEP 1: Operationalizing group-level regulation of learning Group-level regulation of learning is built in the context of interactions between the  participants of a CL task. It means that to look at the ‘how', we should first co de and analyze process data in its social context. Moreover, its definition suggests that interaction between group members should occur, thus, group-level regulation can be operationalized and coded only in relation to what happens within such an interaction, and a single ut terance is not considered sufficient to indicate whether group-level regulation takes place. Various existing definitions of group-level regulation of learning do not always clearly address the role of interaction or separate the regulatory level that is the result of metacognitive monitoring from more cognitively oriented processes, e.g., knowledge construction (Zabolotna et al., 2023). Accordingly, defining the coding criteria for it is a critical grounding phase setting stage for all  the forthcoming analyses as well as data triangula tion and integration. We define cognitive regulation happening at the group level when 1) there is an observation of a group members’ identification of an obstacle, chall enge or another clear trigger that addresses someth ing in the group's learning process and is recognized through monitoring (Criterion I); 2) this trigger is controlled with a regulatory initiation from another group member (Criterion II), and 3) the regulatory initiation leads to strate gic change and at least a small scale adaptation in the group’s learning activity (Criterion III). Motivation and emotion regulation are defined similarly, but the trigger is related to emotions or motivational state in its nature. The first criterion (Criterion I) of the operationalized definition targets metacognitive monitoring that is considered essential for strategic regulation of learning. In the interaction data, it is visible when a group member externalizes their monitoring notion to other group members (e.g., “Are you doing it the right way?”). Sometimes, however, the monitoring is not directly verbalized but embed ded in regulatory initiation  (Criterion II). This second criterion refers to the actual strategic regulatory activity, such as initiating actions of suggesting alternative strategies to overcome a challenge or reestablish s hared understanding. Simply explaining one’s own understanding or expressing emotions is not conside red regulation, though such expressions can be prom pted by regulation. Criterion III addresses adaptation, a key part of SRL theories (e.g., Sobocinski et a l., 2021). It refers to changes in behavior, thinking, or motivational and/or emoti onal state. Adaptation can be subtle and context-dependent, such as a change in collaborative strategy, or more profound, long-term effects on beliefs and strategies (Sobocinski et al., 2021). It may also occur in soc ioemotional interactions during emotionally difficu lt phases of regulation.

In addition to these, Criterion IV involves defining both the type of regulation – SRL, CoRL, or SSRL – and the target of regulation, i.e., whether it targets to control group members’ cognition or emotional and motivational states and commitment.   CoRL is coded when neither of the group members add additional strategic content following the initiating regulatory actions  but rather agree to change their focus accordingly. Instead, SSRL is coded when there is active shared strategic nego tiation between at least two group members i.e., se veral group members add on the possible strategies that c an be used to address the challenge or triggering s ituation. Empirical research suggests that motivation and emo tion regulation occur quite rarely in groups' socia l interactions (Järvenoja et al., 2020). As a result, to  facilitate a process-oriented analysis of these dynamics, it is common to combine CoRL and SSRL as group-level regulation frameworks, enabling a more compre hensive analysis. The trigger or reason for regulation is critical in identifying its target, as regulation can serve overlapping purposes depending on the context, i.e., a certain strategic activity can target the regulation of both cognition, and motivation and emotions, depending on the situation.

Finally, how the unit of analysis  is defined  matters. From a  group-level regulation perspective, a meaningful episode may span multiple turns within a certain timeframe. However, when the quality of a regulation episode is analyzed, it can be meaningful to focus on smaller segments and define the unit of analysis differently. This is especially relevant  when using multimodal data since they offer both possibilities and limitations for the granularity of the analysis.

To showcase ‘how’ SSRL appears in social interactio ns we proceed by locating the visible regulation episodes from groups’ interaction and categorize them according to one qualifier determined in Criterion IV: the target of regulation. The target of regulation refers to differentiation between cognitive and combined motivation and emotion regulation in which the collaborating group was engaged. Examples 1 and 2 demonstrate how thes e are actualized in the coded video data of the socia l interaction. The exampl es derive from a group of three 9 th grade students (1 female, 2 male) from a local inte rnational school, who were asked to collaboratively  build a robot hand. The data collection took place at the university observational laboratory with a classroom-like setting. Multichannel data were coll ected including video recordings done with a pan-tilt-zoom (PTZ) camera, audio collected with high quality individual microphones,  a nd physiological data collected with Shimmer  3 GSR+ sensors and situational self-report data. The session lasted 116 minutes and 50 seconds. To s howcase how SSRL appears in social interactions, we identified regul ation episodes from group interactions and categori zed them based on the target of regulation (Criterion IV): cognitive or motivation and emotion regulation. Example 1: Co-regulation of cognition: At the session’s start, students prepared robot par ts by waxing them. Participant B5 has already started waxing some parts and B6 was monitoring for which parts need to be prepared next. Participant B4 monitored task execution and n oticed inconsistencies w ith instructions, stating, “Check if both… [are lubricated]” (Criterion I). This caused brief confusion, leading B4 to co-regulate by clarifying the task and prompting peers to recheck instructions (Criter ion II). Initially disagreeing, B5 eventually align ed with B4’s suggestion, concluding the cognitive regulation episode (Criterion III). However, effective collaboration and problem-solving require a balanced interplay of these elements, as motivation and emotion regulation are integral to t he broader regulated learning process, supporting engagement a nd strategic decision-making in groups (Wolters & Benzon, 2013; Törmänen et al., 2021). Studies have highligh ted the importance of exploring the temporal sequen ces of regulatory events, such as goal setting, activating  prior knowledge, and adapting strategies, alongsid e their interplay during the learning process (Azevedo, 201 4). Examining these dynamics offers a more comprehe nsive understanding of how SSRL unfolds.

Our case study reveals that the group engaged in bo th cognitive and motivation and emotion  regulation at the group level (CoRL and SSRL). Cognitive regulation accounted for 8% of social interactions (total = 287.25 s, mean = 23.94 s), while motivation and emotion regulation represented 16% (total = 575.57 s, mean = 18.57 s), showing a stronger focus on motivation and emotion regulation compared to prior findings (Järvenoja et al., 2015). These results highlight the significant role of motivation and emotion regulation in collaborative problem-solving. In line with earlier studies, these temporal analyses suggest that group-level regulation is activated when monitoring identifies a need for situational adjustment. While some episodes occur simultaneously within the same discourse or actions, others follow a sequential pattern, where one typ e of regulation precedes or follows the other. In some cases, however, the temporal ass ociation appears coincidental, lacking a meaningful qualitative connection. These patterns are influenced by the situational conditio ns and the group’s appraisals of the collaborative task, under scoring the dynamic and context-dependent nature of SSRL in group learning processes.

Step 2 highlights that motivation and emotion regul ation are not only essential components of collaborative learning at a general level, but also  closely intertwined with — and sometimes even instrumental for— the regulation of cognition. It argues for the sign ificance of a growing, yet still limited, body of r esearch examining the temporal interplay between different types of regulation in group learning. Cumulative e vidence on this temporal reciprocity is needed to further unpack how these intertwined processes support engagement and enable the coordinated flow of collaborative activity over time. STEP 3: Socio-Emotional Conditions for SSRL in Collaborative Learning. The socio-emotional conditions for SSRL refer to the  motivational, emotional, and social aspects that in fluence whether and how regulation emerges in collaborative  learning interactions.  The conditions for SSRL are multifaceted and dynamic, shaped by both external a nd internal factors (Hadwin et al., 2018;  Törmänen et al., 2023). External conditions, such as task demands and available resources, frame learners’ engagement a nd influence their internal conditions, including motivational and emotional states (Winne & Hadwin, 2008). Internal conditions are not static dispositions but fluctuate over time, playing a dual role as both targets for regulation and creators of situational circumstances for SSRL (Harley et al., 2019). Understanding the socio-emotional conditions that support SSRL requires exa mining group-level emotional and motivational antecedents alongside in dividual-level factors. Prior research has highlighted the importance of social contexts, such as emotional co ntagion and physiological synchrony, in shaping SSR L emotional dynamics (Palumbo et al., 2017). Emotional states i n groups  can be characterized along two dimensions: valence (positive vs. negative) and activation (physiological arousal) (Pekrun, 2016), which together provide insights into the socio-emotional conditions of collaboration (Törmänen et al., 2023). When we broaden our view from pure focus on SSRL to  also consider the conditions, we have favored creating a general overview of the data channels and modalities and what these modalities offer for th e entity. This sort of “visualized metadata” is helpful for perceiving the different data channels, their g ranularities, and analytical possibilities in relation to each other. Figure 4 presents an example of this type of data visualization. In this dataset, 95 8th-grade students collaborated on a water purification  task in small groups. By integrating multiple data modalities, the study pro vides a detailed view of the conditions supporting SSRL. General self-report measures assessed students’ motivation toward STEM subjects (Gaspard et al., 2015) and their motivation regulation strategies (Wolters & Benzon, 2013), including specific strategies such as self-efficacy talk and emotion regulation. These measures offered a baseline for understanding their individual motivational profiles before engaging in the collaborative task.

Process data, primarily from videotaped collaborations, served as the central channel for analyzing group interactions. Video recordings captured socio-emotional exchanges and SSRL episodes, enabling the identification of how groups regulate motivation and emotions during collaboration. Emotional valence and bodily gestures observed in the videos complemented verbal  communication, offering a rich understanding of so cioemotional dynamics. Physiological data, recorded vi a electrodermal activity (EDA) with Shimmer 3 GSR+ sensors, provided additional insights into group me mbers’ physiological arousal during the task. EDA, a proxy for sympathetic nervous system activity, reflected moments of heightened emotional activation or stress (Dawson et al., 2007), and highlighted instances where students navigated cognitive and emotional challenges. Situated self-reports, administered repeatedly during the task, captured students’ momentary perceptions of success expectations, task value, and perceived costs (Gaspard et al., 2015). These measures offered snapshots of students' situational appraisals, bridging the gap between general self-reports and real-time process data. Posttask, stimulated recall interviews enabled students  to reflect on their motivational beliefs, social i nteractions and regulatory efforts. Visualized responses from situated self-reports facilitated these discussions, allowing students to revisit their collaborative experiences.

Step 3 argues that socio-emotional conditions are critical in research on SSRL, as they play a key role in shaping the emergence and quality of regulation in collaborative learning. It builds on existing work on motivation and emotion regulation but shifts  the focus from regulatory processes to the contextu al and interpersonal factors that enable them (Järvenoja et al., 2024). This per spective contributes to a broader conceptual unders tanding of how motivation and emotions are integral to success ful collaboration, and how the emergence of regulat ion is shaped by dynamic situational conditions in authentic learning contexts. Capturing these dynamic and often subtle conditions requires multiple data sources and modal ities, as no single channel offers sufficien t access to the full range of social, emotional, and contextual indicators that influence SSRL.

## Conclusion

The example studies highlight that SSRL is not a series of isolated reg ulation episodes, but a dynamic, context-dependent process shaped by socio-emotional conditions at both the individual and group levels. It demonstrates the potential of multimodal data to unravel the com plex interplay of cognitive, motivational, and emot ional processes in collaborative learning. While traditional video data remains invaluable for analyzing group dynamics, such as visible regulatory behaviors and socio-emotional interact ions, integrating physiological measures like EDA provides deeper insight into internal states and reactions. Data sources su ch as interviews and self-reports, in turn, enrich the analysis with group members’ subjective interpretations and appraisals that are meaningful for further understanding beliefs and reason influencing both the conditions and engagement in regulatory processes. Together, these modalities enable researchers to trace the temporal and qualitative connections between regulation episodes and the collaborative learning conditions that foster or hinder SSRL. Future studies could further refine multimodal appr oaches, particularly those that integrate contextualized qualitative and quantitative data in  classroom-based research, offering deeper insights into how SSRL unfolds in authentic settings and supports collaborative learning. Advancing automated or semi-automated analysis techniques supported by AI  holds potential for increasing scalability and prov iding teachers with real-time understanding of group dynamics.  Such efforts candeepen our understanding of SSRL and contribute to translating theoretical knowledge into pedagogicall y meaningful practices that foster effective collaboration and regulation in diverse learning environments.
