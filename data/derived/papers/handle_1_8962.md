# Draw a Place Where You Learn Important and Useful Things: Developing an Analytical Framework to Investigate Students’ Conceptions of Learning Environment

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In this study, we discuss the potential of a new methodological approach to assess children’s educational ecology. 1444 drawings from 11-17 years old students were collected in order to ascertain how they conceptualize their learning environments. The drawings categorization scheme was based on the three elements of the Activity System Model (Engerström 1987, p. 115) – subject, instruments, and communities. The majority of the students’ drawings portrayed traditional learning spaces like home or classroom, which is consistent with the literature on the subject. As for the analysis of the three elements of the model (subjects, instruments, and communities), the proposed coding scheme describes the community element particularly well.

This paper presents the methodological approach of analyzing drawings made by elementary school studen ts (N=1444) as primary sources of data about their conceptual understanding of learning environments. We analyzed drawings elicited from school students in order to ascertain how they conceptualize their learning environments

– including not only such spaces of formal schoolin g as schools and classrooms, but also informal lear ning

situations (purposefully organized and spontaneous), and new environments like computer-and Internet-based activities (Sawyer, 2014).

Drawing has a well-established history in education al research as  a way to document educational phenomena (Chang et al., 2020; Katz, 2017; Harris et al., 2014; Haney et al., 2004; Weber & Mitchell, 1995). For decades, draw-a-picture technic have been used to understand students’ conceptions of scientists, e.g. the Drawa-Scientist Test (DAST) (Chambers, 1983); to explore students’ mental models and conceptual understanding of science concepts e.g. internal structure of trees (Rybska et al., 2017); and to investigate attitudes and beliefs toward science and learning (Quane et al., 2021).  Hsieh and Tsai (2018) conducted a cross-sectional drawing study of 8to 18-year-old students’ conceptions of learning (i.e. students’ views of what learning is) and found that most students held traditional conception of learning-teacher-centred classroom, with students as passive listeners. The evident benefit of using drawings in educational research stems from the fact, that drawing enables the expression of complicated ideas and thoughts ev en under conditions of limited writing and reading skills (Selwyn et al., 2009). By using drawings to investi gate students’ conceptions of their learning environment, we sought to overcome certain limitations of tradition al methods of obtaining quantitative data, like wri tten questionnaires and expensive and time consuming interviews and direct observations. However, this decision in turn gave rise to further methodological issues regarding the data coding frameworks and procedures. The usefulness of draw-a-picture method in educatio nal research depends on its power of assesment. Even the well-known and frequently used DAST method has several modifications of the procedure and different coding schemes (Ferguson & Lezotte, 2020). To obtai n results that are generalizable and comparable, we developed a checklist of features the drawings may contain based on existing model of learning envirom ent. Directly invoking the concept of Learning in Activity (Greeno, Engerström, 2014), we view the learning process as a unit that is larger than an individual person – a group of people, a classroom or a community. On this approach, key roles in learning are played by the agent (stud ent), the object, and the resources which the subje ct uses to transform the object in line with his or her goal. The process happens within a given community, with its own division of labor and rules. While this model is mainly used to analyze situations in which learning occurs, it also appears useful and relevant in attempting to catego rize and analyze static concepts, i.e. the metal mo dels of learning environments illustrated by children.

The primary research questions of the presented study are as follows: ¥ How do students conceptualize their learning environment? How rich is this conceptualization? ¥ What elements make up the space as the learning environment? Which of them are recurrent? ¥ What is the structure of the presented concepts?

## Study participants

In total, 1444 primary school students (702 girls, 49%) participated in the study; they were between 1 1 and 17 years old (M = 14.00, SD = 2.00) and were students of 12 schools located in villages and small-to-medium sized towns and cities in Poland (below 130,000 inhabitants). Non-probability sampling was used to gather participants: the students who participated in this study were recruited from the schools participating in the “Science for You” educational program.

## Instruments and Procedure

The “Draw your learning environment” task was a part of a 16-page questionnaire booklet. The questionnaire in a paper-and-pencil format was administered by teachers during school hours. Children were given 45 minutes to fill out the questionnaire. Data collection was ano nymous. The school authorities and students were ve rbally informed about the study and their consent was obta ined. Participation in the study was voluntary. Ins truction stated “Draw a place where you learn things that ar e important and useful for you. Label it”. Addition al open question was placed under the drawing space (“What place is this?”) Data coding framework To explore how the learning environment is conceived by students we developed a checklist based on the Activity System Model (Engerström 1987, p. 115). Of the six-element set that makes up the Activity System Model, we chose to describe only three, shown in the table be low. We found it impossible to analyze the remainin g three elements from the perspective of children’s drawings (objects; i.e. the scope of the learning process; rules, i.e. the set of principles guiding the actors in the process; and division of labor). ## Conclusions

ICLS2022 Proceedings 1611 © ISLS Our findings indicate that the “Draw your learning environment” method, presented herein, appears to b e well suited to the study of learning environments in children. The relatively small non-participation rates (no drawing and description n=152, no drawing n =44, no descrip tion n =81) indicates that the task is suitable and understandable for 11-17 year old school students.

In the presented study, majority of the participant s presented similar interpretations of the learning environment concept-mainly as a physical space. M ost of the participants drew their home spaces as p laces related to the practice of learning. In the traditional model of formal education, students receive their learning at school and practice it at home by doing homework and preparing for tests. Although the drawings do not directly point to the home as a continuation of the school e xperience, the descriptions suggest describing this  type of activity (e.g."the desk where I do my homework"). Drawings depict ing informal areas of education and freelearning settings such as museums, the forest, and dance were in the minority. As for analysis of the three elements of the model, the proposed coding scheme describes the community element particularly well. However, it has a certai n shortcoming in the sense that it does not allow f or the assessment of processual elements and relations (de pendencies, hierarchy) between particular elements of the Activity System model. Further limitations are as follows. Secondly, we think that while drawing might provide a promising way to investigate school students’ men tal models of learning environments, in future stud ies, it should be supplemented by interviews.
