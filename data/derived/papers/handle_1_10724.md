# Measuring the Effects of Combining Field Trips with Classroom Instruction on Two Different Types of Knowledge

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Field trips can represent discovery learning at its best and their outcomes are well documented. However, more research is needed to understand how FTs can be used to support student learning in combination with other instructional models. We conducted an experiment with a group of second-grade Colombian students (N=51) to investigate the effects of field trips when used in combination with classroom instruction at two different time points (before and after). We addressed two kinds of knowledge: acquisition of landscape vocabulary and orientation skills, which were covered both in the classroom instruction and as part of the field trip. For landscape vocabulary, those who received the classroom instruction before the field trip scored higher than those who attended the field trip first and then received the classroom instruction. For orientation, the pattern was reversed. Students who attended the field trip first scored higher. These findings provide initial evidence that the timing of FTs to enhance learning may be knowledge-and skill-dependent.

## Introduction

Field trips (FTs) have a long tradition in schools. They are motivating and can help teach multiple types of content knowledge and skills (e.g., Frensley et al., 2022, Behrendt & Franklin, 2014; Jose et al. 2017).  Pre-and post-field trip interventions can help to increase the benefit s of FTs on learning (e.g., Lewalter et al., 2021). FTs often take place as a singular experience. Yet, there are reas ons to believe that the knowledge students receive in the classroom will prepare them to learn during the FT  (e.g., Orion & Hofstein, 1994’s concept of novelty space). There are also reasons to believe that the knowledge students experience during the FT will prepare them to learn in the classroom (e.g. field trips as Preparation for Future Learning, Bransford & Schwartz, 1999). In this study we are examining the effects of a field trip wh en used in conjunction with associated classroom instructio n in different ways, focusing on different kinds of knowledge. We are looking at i. no field trip, ii.  classroom instruction prior to associated field trip, and iii. classroom instruction that follows a field trip. While FTs are associated with a myriad of content knowled ge, for this study we focus on two different types of knowledge offered by FTs  and that are also part of the school curriculum: declarative and procedural knowledge (Shavelson et al., 2005). For the first type of knowledge, we chose learning vocabulary words and orientation for the second type, in this case navig ation and coordinates. While direct instruction sup ports vocabulary acquisition (Sonbul & Schmitt, 2010), there are studies that show that interacting with  the words enhances meaning and concept understanding (Moody et al., 2018). Orientation skills are at the core of our daily experiences as learners. Spatial information supports our interactions with the environment (Carlson et al., 2010) and the way we think (Levinson, 1996). During a field trip students may experience a high demand for orientation skills, for example, paying attention to where the sun is rising or where they are located in relation  to a point of reference.

In this study, we are exploring the effects of FTs and classroom instruction timing on these two types of knowledge by examining the impact of FTs on pre-post learning measures.

## Participants

Fifty-one second-grade students organized in three different classes from a school located in Bogota (Colombia) participated in this experiment. Parental consent was obtained when students registered for the field trip. Students provided oral assent before the study. The study was approved by our university Institutional Review Board. Procedures The students’ primary language of instruction is Spanish, and the field trip was conducted in Spanish. During the three-day field trip students received instruction relevant to landscape vocabulary  and orientation skills. For vocabulary, students were taught about six different landscapes present in the visited area and were able to directly observe them. For orientation skills, students had an orientation activity where they learned the different cardinal points and had to locate different points of intere st that were located on a specific cardinal point. The classroom instruction had a one-hour duration and was delivered by the same instructor for all three classes. The instruction was delivered in Spanish, but for the landscape vocabulary, one of the classroom’s learning goals was for students to learn English translations as part of their E nglish language instruction. Students were presented with a photo of a landscape and a definition in Spanish as well as its English translation. For the orientation instruction, students learned about the four cardinal points in both Spanish and English using the same format than for landscapes. Five days after the last instruction, students in the C+FT and FT+C conditions completed a post-test. The control condition completed the post-test three days after the instruction but before go ing on the field trip. This timing difference was necessary  due to FT scheduling.  Twenty-five days after the first delivered instruction, students took the delayed test. Figure 1 presents the experimental design for each of the conditions. Figure 1 Experimental Design Note. ## Measures

We designed a post/delayed-test to measure vocabulary and orientation knowledge. Post-test To assess vocabulary knowledge retrieval, students were presented with a set of six images about the studied landscapes. Student responses were coded dichotomously. They received a one if the wrote the “correct” word in English or a zero if they got the wrong word  or didn’t write a word. The overall maximum score was 6 and min

0. To assess orientation skills two items were included in the assessment. These items were constructed using the

Evidence Based Assessment Model and the Colombian Standards for Social Sciences. Items have been previously validated with fifth-grade students and were adapted for second graders.  Students received one point for each correctly performed instruction. In each item the maximum score was 4 and the minimum 0. Delayed Post-test For the delayed-test we use the same version as the post-test, but we also asked them to write the names of the landscapes in Spanish. Students first completed the Spanish version and once they were done, they completed the English version.

## Discussion and scholarly significance

Field trips have a long tradition in schools and their ou tcomes are well-documented. Different initiatives have been trying to implement solutions so field trips c an better support student learning. One example is the use of digital technologies during a field trip to support student learning. For instance, Zhang et al., (2023) implemented augment reality worksheets, so students were able to further explore plants that they were seeing during the field trip. There could be a great potential of FTs and even virtual FTs for supporting student learning, but it is important to understand better how can field trips support knowledge acquisition and skill development in association with other forms of instruction. Results from this first study show that the timing of the FT in combination with other forms of instruction (classroom instruction in this case) may support student learning in different ways. One limitation of our study is that classes were intact and therefore one possibility is that the class group is driving these differences. Given the  school’s structure for these field trips and timing limitations, we assigned each class to a condition  and were not able to administer a pre-test to measure this specific content. (Though all classes had similar scores on standardized tests of English vocabulary, F(2, 40) = 0.31, p =.738) A current study in progress is replicating this one with inclusion of a pre-test. Our first results suggest that the timing for using FTs to support student learning may be knowledge/skill dependent. More research is needed to deepen the understanding of these results.  For schools, to  fully take advantage of the benefits of FT s and VFTs, more research is needed to understand how this fo rm of instruction can be combined with other instructional forms to potentialize student learning.
