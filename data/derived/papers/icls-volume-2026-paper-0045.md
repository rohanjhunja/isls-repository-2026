# Speculative Interaction Geography: Imagining How Embodied Interactions Take Shape Beyond AI Prediction

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Recent advances in qualitative data visualization are expanding how researchers and educators analyze interactions among people and things across learning environments. Yet, these tools remain primarily retrospective, representing what has already occurred from data such as video. Bridging traditions of interaction analysis with research centering affect and speculative design, this paper explores how qualitative data visualization tools can also serve as speculative instrument s for creating data to imagine possible interactions in learning spaces. Specifically, we extend a method called interaction geography through two illustrative cases that demonstrate new technical capabilities. These capabilities enabl e researchers and practitioners to sketch imagined interactions and spatial configurations, reconfiguring interaction geography as both an analytic and speculative practice. We propose the notion of speculative interaction geography and show how speculative data visualization can serve as a human-centered counterpoint to AI-driven prediction in the learning sciences, with implications for areas like teacher education and participatory design.

For example, researchers working from traditions of conversation analysis and interaction analysis (Jordan & Henderson, 1995; Derry et al., 2010; Hall & Stevens, 2015) continue to advance  multimodal transcript visualization methods  that provide new ways to conceptualize learning and teaching as embodied, spatial, and relational processes (e.g., DeLiema, Enyedy & Danish, 2019; Marin, 2020; Solomon et al., 2022; Steier, 2014; Taylor, 2017). Likewise, scholars in multimodal learning analytics  and embodied interaction research  have developed a range of visualization tools that enable qualitative researchers to visually analyze and explore complex multimodal data (e.g., D’ Angelo et al., 2020; Davidsen & Steier 2024;  Fonteles et al., 2024). Yet, despite these advances, such tools remain primarily retrospective, representing what has already occurred from data such as video recordings. Recent research drawing from affect theory and speculative design is beginning to highlight the need for age increasingly shaped by AI-driven prediction and generation. For instance, in a recent special issue on speculative research in learning sciences, Garcia and Mirra (2023) stated “beyond simply imagining what liberating social arrangements could exist in alternative worlds, we must cultivate methodological innovations to study the efforts to design them into existence in the here and now” (p. 11-12). Likewise, Trafí-Prats and de Freitas

(2024) illustrate how techniques like speculative maps and architectural models offer powerful ways to understand

the affective dimensions of schools and generate participatory visions of alternative futures for how we design school buildings and classrooms. Still others are developing alternative artistic methods to produce data that enliven affective and interpretive dimensions of analysis  (Leander & Boldt, 2013; Vossoughi et al., 2020). Collectively, t hese perspectives ope n space for  speculative approaches  to visualization that foreground imagination, affect, and participation over automation, simulation, and prediction. Bridging interaction analysis  with research centering  affect and speculative design, this paper explores how qualitative data visualization tools can also serve as  speculative instruments  for creating data to  imagine possible movements, interactions, and participation in learning spaces. We extend a method called  interaction geography (Shapiro, Silvis & Hall,  2025; Shapiro et al., 2024; https://www.interactiongeography.org) through two illustrative cases that demonstrate new technical capabilities enabling researchers and practitioners to sketch imagined interactions and spatial configurations, a method we call speculative interaction geography. Through speculative interaction geography, data visualization can function as a human-centered counterpoint to AI-driven prediction in the  learning sciences, inviting researchers, designers, and educators to imagine possible forms of learning, teaching, and collaboration in learning spaces.  We conclude by emphasizing several limitations of this work and next steps in ways that further expand the methodological commitments and possibilities of qualitative traditions such as interaction analysis in the learning sciences (see Krishnamoorthy et al., 2021; Marin, 2020).

## Theoretical framework

360 Interaction analysis and visualizing practice Our work draws from qualitative traditions of interaction analysis  (IA), which is an important methodological approach to studying teaching and learning in the learning sciences and CSCL communities (Jordan & Henderson, 1995; also see Hall & Stevens, 2015). Scholars from diverse theoretical orientations working in settings ranging from classrooms, museums, and the natural world continue to use and expand IA to characterize embodied activity, technologically mediated interactions, and develop alternative understandings of interaction that foreground relational ontologies and histories across diverse settings (e.g., Davidsen & Ryberg, 2017; Hennessy Elliott, 2020; Kelton, 2021; Ma & Munter. 2014; Marin, 2020; Philip et al., 2018; Taylor, 2017; Vogelstein et al., 2019; Vossoughi et al., 2020).  IA researchers also continue to expand how visualizations or representations of practice, such as annotated video, can support  reflective professional practice in areas such as teacher education. For example, scholars such as Sherin & van Es (2005) and Horn & Garner (2022) continue to expand video-based reflection and structured representations of teaching moments to help teachers analyze the complexity of their classroom practice and to build shared professional vision. IA scholars use video to visually represent the situated and embodied nature of learning and teaching. Yet, much of this work operates with a retrospective epistemology, focusing on what occurred from video data to make sense of it after the fact. Our work expands this tradition by developing tools that enable  speculative visualizations to help researchers and practitioners imagine alternative interpretations and explore possible futures in ways complementary to IA. Speculative visualization of video-based data is a means of flipping after-the-fact into fiction and asking what could or might take place in future interactions  that have not yet transpired. Turn to affect and materiality Across the learning sciences and in fields such as literacy and teacher education, scholars have increasingly taken up a “turn to affect”, emphasizing the material, embodied, and emotional dimensions of teaching and learning (Jaber et al., 2024; Leander & Boldt, 2013; Vea, 2020). This shift has spurred new creative methods to engage with data. For example, researchers are expanding approaches such as painting, sketching, and performance-based approaches to offer what Balmer (2021) characterizes as an “alternative aesthetic,” or research practice that moves beyond traditional ways of presenting data. These approaches challenge conventional representational forms that focus on causal explanations  and embody what Ellingson and Sotirin (2020) characterize as making with data rather than trying to represent it.

Informed by this work, our analysis explores how visualization can function as an affective and material practice, a way to engage with the felt and emergent qualities of multimodal interaction and to reimagine qualitative data as a site of speculation and design (Kinnee et al., 2023). A focus on emergence invites researchers to analyze interactions as  unfolding over time and, in turn, to  reorient to time as enfolding pasts, presents, and futures (Gutiérrez, 2018; Lemke, 2000). We illustrate how speculative visualizations provide a means of attuning to affect, reorienting to multiple timescales of learning and teaching, and dreaming possible futures into being (Rajala et al., 2023).

Speculative design and critical visualization We draw from traditions of speculative design (Dunne & Raby, 2013; Rosner, 2018) and critical visualization (D’Ignazio & Klein, 2016; Kahn, 2020; Matuk et al., 2021; Philip et al., 2018) that conceptualize speculation as a method of inquiry. Rather than solve predefined problems, speculative design seeks to reconfigure what counts as a problem and open spaces for new  possibilities. Specifically, s peculative design aims to address not just immediate design needs but also to explore alternative futures, expose underlying values, and provoke discussion about sociotechnical possibilities through design objects like fictions  (Lindley & Coulton, 2015; Wong & Khovanskaya, 2018).

Our work extends extant and emerging approaches to speculative design and critical visualization of data in several ways. First, while speculative design has been expanded  within fields such as human-computer interaction and design research, it is only beginning to be incorporated into fields such as the  learning sciences. This paper expands interaction geography as a methodological tool for speculative research in the  learning sciences. Second, we build on original work by Kim & DiSalvo (2010) who focused on speculative visualization within a graphic design context. Particularly, we further define the notion of speculative data visualization in educational research, illustrating what speculative data visualization tools can look like and how  interactive visualizations of data and the process of generating them can provoke imagination, ethical reflection, or alternative interpretations. Finally, AI tools are allowing users to explore alternative futures and to produce visual AIgenerated fictions. Our approach resists automated fictions that “imitate imagination” by extracting our data (Kinnee et al., 2023) in favor of humanist methods that allow for intimate interactions with our data. We propose speculative interaction geography as a mode of visual inquiry that further extends qualitative data visualization 361 beyond the analysis of data and toward an imaginative, participatory practice of thinking with data to explore human possibilities for teaching and learning.

## Method

Interaction geography and technical expansion for speculation Our approach builds on a method called interaction geography, which encompasses free and open-source tools to transcribe and visualize movement, conversation, and video data over space and time. The first of these tools, Mondrian Transcription, allows one to transcribe the movement of people and things from video when automated movement data is unavailable. The second, the Interaction Geography Slicer (IGS), allows one to dynamically visualize movement data, conversation transcripts, and video data over space and time. In prior work, these tools have been used to analyze embodied activity in settings including classrooms (Underwood et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2023), museums (Shapiro et al., 2017), virtual environments (Vo & Huesemann-Odom, 2022), and early childhood education (Berson et al., 2023; Shapiro & Silvis, 2025). This paper extends this work by advancing these tools to support speculative practices of movement tracing and visualization. Specifically, we expand the technical capabilities of the Mondrian Transcription tool to allow users to load an image of a space  (e.g., a floorplan or image) and create a speculative movement trace in that space over different time periods they specify. In other words, we expand the features of this tool to allow users to not just transcribe movement data from video but engage in a process of speculative movement tracing. Users can also rewind and re-trace their speculative movement traces to imagine different kinds of movement and record multiple speculative traces for different people or things. These speculative traces can then be exported into the IGS for further exploration and refinement.

Together, these technical advancements afford opportunities  to reposition interaction geography from a retrospective analytic tool to a prospective and imaginative one that invites users to think with data that does not yet exist and to use visualization as a mode of inquiry into possible interactions and designs for learning.

## Method and data sources

Our subsequent analysis uses a comparative case study approach to explore potential interactions in two spaces from classroom and museum contexts. The first case focuses on a museum gallery space derived from prior research in a cultural heritage museum  (Shapiro et al., 2017). This prior work collected multi-perspective video recordings of visitor activity in this museum as part of a three-year research project examining how families engage with the diverse historical and cultural heritage of American Roots and Country music. While earlier analyses of these data used interaction geography to visualize and interpret real visitor interactions, the present work takes a radically different stance: not analyzing what visitors did, but rather reimagining what visitors might do in the same gallery spaces of this study. Specifically, we re-imagine how a seven-year-old girl we call Mika might explore one gallery space with her grandfather.

The second case centers on a middle school science classroom drawn from the TIMSS 1999 video study, a corpus that has been widely used for studying teaching and learning practices. Like the first case, prior work has used interaction geography to analyze real teachers ’ movement and classroom interaction patterns between teachers and students in this space  (Shapiro et al., 2024). Here, we instead use interaction geography to imagine possible pedagogical configurations  and interactions in this space  by sketching how a teacher, whom we call Jason, might move and interact during a groupwork activity. We selected these contexts for three reasons. First, both have been extensively analyzed in prior research, offering a rich foundation for  comparing speculative and analytic uses  of interaction geography. Second, they represent primary settings in learning sciences  and CSCL research, allowing us to explore  how speculative visualization extends across learning environments. Third, the floorplans and contextual imagery of these spaces are publicly available, allowing the resulting visualizations to be shared and explored interactively in future work. Importantly, speculative interaction geography shares commitments with many autoethnographic traditions that position the researcher ’s lived experience as a legitimate and generative source of knowledge. Accordingly, t he traces we create and imagine with are shaped by our own embodied histories, experiences visiting museums with children, teaching in classrooms, and observing and analyzing movement data. Case 1: Speculating museum visitor interactions Speculative movement tracing 362 The first case centers on a museum gallery space. Drawing from prior work studying visitors’ interactions in this space and personal experience visiting museums with young children, we sought to imagine how a seven-yearold girl we call Mika might move, interact, and experience this space while visiting with her grandfather. Rather than representing actual data, we used this speculative scenario to explore how the  act of tracing and visualizing interaction geographies can itself become a method for imagining interaction. We began by loading a floor plan of the gallery space into the Mondrian Transcription tool and creating a speculative trace of Mika’ s movement over this floor plan. As we traced, we imagined how she might move from one exhibit to another, what might capture her attention, and where moments of engagement or social interaction might unfold. The tracing process quickly became generative: as we drew, we imagined new possibilities such as Mika pausing for a long stretch at the art table with her grandfather, racing across the gallery toward a wall of records, or looping back to a familiar exhibit that had sparked her curiosity. Through this process, tracing became a form of  speculation. The manual, embodied act of tracing allowed us to feel the pacing, rhythms, and contours of Mika’s imagined experience. We could slow the trace to experience deep engagement or quicken it to express playful curiosity. At several points we rewound, redrew, and re-imagined movements to explore alternative pathways through the same space. This iterative process foregrounded what we describe as  tracing-as-speculation, which we define as a practice that surfaces affective, spatial, and temporal textures of interaction through the embodied act of tracing to create data. This approach resonates with foundational  research on visitor movement and engagement in museum studies (e.g., Serrell, 1994) yet shifts emphasis from documenting what visitors did from sources such as video or observation to imagining what they could do. There was also an  aesthetic dimension  to the process: Mika ’s imagined movement trace accumulated into an expressive visual form, one of possibility rather than of evidence. Figure 1A shows the final speculative movement trace in the Mondrian Transcription tool. The visualization makes immediately visible the density and quality of Mika ’s imagined movement, highlighting potential areas of engagement within the gallery. While the overall structure resembles patterns we observed in real museum data, there are several differences in the process of creating such data that afford different perspectives on visitor engagement and exhibit design.

Visually exploring speculative movement in the IGS After recording Mika’s speculative trace, we imported the data into the Interaction Geography Slicer (IGS) to explore how her imagined movement unfolded across space and time. The IGS provided a complementary mode of speculation, one that emphasized visual exploration and more detailed analytical and comparative reasoning. Rather than tracing Mika’s unfolding movement, we could now see Mika’ s complete trajectory dynamically mapped, revealing temporal and spatial rhythms and interactional patterns. Within the IGS, we noticed patterns that invited further imaginative interpretation. For example, we saw extended pauses at certain exhibits and speculated about the kinds of engagement these might represent. Perhaps Mika and her grandfather were taking  a photograph or, alternatively, collaboratively exploring an artifact. We also noticed and speculated about  transitions between longer stops and bursts of movement that might suggest  a playful alternation between attention and exploration typical of young children’s movement in museum spaces. To further explore and imagine such relational dynamics, we used the IGS to color-code and highlight portions of Mika’s movement according to moments when she might be with her grandfather. This allowed us to quickly imagine moments of  proximity and joint activity  across her complete visit to the gallery space. F or example, we imagined that the significant amount of time she spent at the art table encompassed Mika and her grandfather drawing together. Visually coding the data in these imaginative ways allowed us to further speculate about the kinds of embodied interactions Mika and her grandfather experienced. Interacting with the IGS visualization also led to speculative insights not evident during tracing alone. The IGS enabled us to see with the data we created, and consider how the layout, pacing, and affordances of the gallery might support moments of shared attention, independence, or repeated visits to an exhibit. As we interacted with the timeline, rescaled the data, and toggled data layers, we found ourselves speculating not just about Mika’s imagined visit but also about how museum spaces might be designed to invite such relational encounters. Figure 1B shows a screenshot of Mika’s speculative trace as we explored it within the IGS, color-coded to highlight moments she is with her grandfather. Case 2: Speculating spatial pedagogies Speculative movement tracing Our second case focuses on an 8 th grade middle school science classroom  from the TIMSS 1999 video study. Building on prior analyses of this space, as well as our own experiences observing and teaching in similar settings, we sought to imagine how a teacher whom we called  Jason might move and interact during a typical twentyminute groupwork activity. Using the Mondrian Transcription tool, we began by tracing Jason’s speculative path over a floor plan of this classroom as he circulated among student groups during this activity. While we began with a general sense of how Jason might move, the act of tracing again prompted us to imagine the potential characteristics of his teaching and the influence of his classroom space design. We pictured him moving between tables, pausing to provide guidance, or stepping to the front of the room to facilitate a wholeclass discussion or demonstration. In this  case, tracing became a way to feel the spatial and temporal qualities of pedagogy, the pacing of his attention, the distribution of his presence, and the embodied rhythms of instruction. Occasionally, we rewound and retraced his route to explore alternative pedagogical  trajectories, imagining what it might be like for Jason to shift his circulation patterns or stay longer with particular students and groups. Through this process, speculative tracing was not merely a representational act but a form of pedagogical speculation. It helped us to illuminate  how teaching takes shape through movement and use of space or spatial pedagogies (Lim et al., 2012), where moments of focused interaction might occur, how movement patterns afforded or constrained participation, and how the classroom ’s spatial layout supported or impeded pedagogy. Speculative interaction geography This paper proposes speculative interaction geography, extending interaction geography from a retrospective analytic method to an imaginative practice. As opposed to representing and analyzing patterns of embodied interaction from video records, speculative interaction geography engages with what could happen, treating visualization as a means to imagine possible movements, affects, and pedagogies.  Rather than viewing data as fixed evidence, this approach treats visualization as a medium for exploring the space between data, analysis, and design where imagined embodied movements can help researchers, designers, and educators consider new possibilities for learning, teaching, and design. Through our two cases, we demonstrated how the process of tracing and visualizing speculative interactions opens up new possibilities  to think and feel with potentiality. Notably, w hereas physically walking through a space offers an embodied and  present-tense experience, speculative interaction geography lets  designers and educators layer multiple imagined scenarios onto the same space, compare them visually, and examine how different configurations unfold across space and time. Furthermore, it is not always possible to physically visit the sites of  future learning designs; thus, speculative interaction geography provides an accessible means of designing for multiple possible futures. 365 Speculative data visualization vs. machine prediction in the learning sciences As artificial intelligence and machine learning models increasingly support large-scale predictive analyses  and simulations of data about teaching and learning, speculation reasserts a human-centered mode of imaginative reasoning. While predictive systems aim to model what  is likely  to occur, speculative data visualization foregrounds what  might meaningfully  occur by foregrounding curiosity, empathy, and interpretive judgment: qualities often lost when relying solely on computational approaches to studying learning and teaching  or modeling movement and spatial behavior. In other words, predictive tools privilege similarity by model ing what “should” take shape based on known patterns, whereas a speculative approach fosters diversity by envisioning what “could” take shape in an unknowable future. In this sense, the notion of speculative interaction geography offers a counterpoint to AI-driven analysis of teaching and learning data, positioning researchers and practitioners as co-imaginers of pedagogical and spatial futures.

## Implications

Implications for learning sciences and CSCL. Within the learning sciences and CSCL, this work contributes to a shift from qualitative data visualization as a mode of retrospective analysis toward visualization as a site of imaginative and critical inquiry. Speculative data visualization can help educators and researchers both understand classroom interactions and  envision alternative configurations of space, pedagogy, and collaboration.  By integrating speculative practices into methods such as interaction analysis, scholars can explore how learning environments can be organized, experienced, and felt. This orientation resonates with calls to expand the methodological diversity of the  learning sciences  beyond representation toward affective, relational, and anticipatory forms of knowing (e.g., Krishnamoorthy et al., 2021; Marin, 2020). Implications for Design. This work points tonew directions for the design of visualization tools themselves. Tools like the  Interaction Geography Slicer (IGS) or Mondrian Transcription can evolve from analytic visualization systems into speculative platforms where, in the future,  teachers, researchers, and students could collaboratively trace, visualize, and imagine interactions.  Such tools could also support new forms of reflective practice by allowing teachers to rehearse or reimagine classroom interaction  patterns or, alternatively, enabling professionals such as museum designers to collaboratively explore how visitors might circulate and engage within an exhibit. More broadly, speculative data visualization reframes visualization  as a design material  that invites creative, ethical, and situated reflection about the futures of learning, teaching,  and collaboration.

## Limitations and next steps

This paper aimed primarily to propose the notion of speculative interaction geography and open up a design space for speculative data visualization in the learning sciences and CSCL communities. Several limitations accompany the ideas and tools presented here. First, this work draws on only two illustrative cases. Significantly more research is needed to refine and test the concepts and ideas introduced here. Second, the cases presented here speculate within relatively conventional spatial configurations  including a standard museum gallery and a traditional classroom. A promising direction for future work is to apply speculative interaction geography to contexts that challenge normative assumptions about how learners move through and inhabit space.  Third, while we have outlined potential implications for research and reflective practice, these remain provisional and should be further examined through empirical studies. Finally, there are inherent limits to speculation itself. As with prior work in speculative design (Dunne & Raby, 2013), speculation is best understood not as a means of prediction or validation, but as a complementary mode of inquiry, one that can productively coexist with, but not replace, other analytic and empirical methods in the  learning sciences. While we position speculative interaction geography as a counterpoint to AI-driven methods for visualization, we also see rich potential to explore how leveraging AI tools can enhance speculative data visualization towards more  human-centered and participatory research and design. Perhaps automation and speculation will develop side-by-side, tracing imagined futures that researchers, educators, and designers are just beginning to bring into being. Balmer, A. (2021).
