# Planners and Tinkerers: A Comparative Case Study of K-12 Students’ Engagement with Data Practices in an Ag-STEM

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In an increasingly data-saturated society, fostering K-12 students' data literacy is an important issue. This qualitative comparative case study addresses this need by examining how eighth-grade students develop data literacy within a 16-week, AI-supported data-driven scientific inquiry leveraging low-cost sensor devices in an Ag-STEM context. Drawing on the frameworks of conceptual and critical data literacy, the study analyzes the distinct pathways of two four-student and three-student groups. D ata sources included project journals, data logs, AI interaction logs, and focus group interviews, which were analyzed through a multi-stage thematic and cross-case analysis. Findings reveal two archetypes, Pragmatic Planners and Messy Tinkerers, who followed distinct yet equally meaningful paths. Each group’s engagement was shaped by different forms of resistance, informational or physical, that transformed confusion into insight. The study suggests that educational designs that embrace productive struggle could support diverse students in developing a deeper understanding of the entire data-driven inquiry.

We live in a data-saturated society in which routine social interactions generate digital traces that are systematically collected and processed by corporate and state actors (Pangrazio & Selwyn, 2018; Wiesenberg et al., 2017). These conditions create an urgent civic imperative to prepare a data-informed citizenry: learners who can interpret quantitative claims, interrogate data provenance, and exercise agency in a data-mediated world (Lee et al., 2021; Rosenberg & Jones, 2024). In response, a broad movement to integrate data literacy into K-12 schooling has gained momentum, positioning data competence as a civic and equitable educational aim rather than solely a vocational one (Jiang et al., 2022; Lee et al., 2022). One promising approach is to engage students in authentic, project-based inquiries using data that are personally meaningful and connected to community issues (Wilkerson & Polman, 2020). Such designs can support learners in formulating questions, selecting appropriate analyses, and constructing evidence-based arguments (NGSS Lead States, 2013). Yet in many K-12 science classrooms, data are still handled through bounded, pre-scripted laboratory routines that position students as passive data collectors rather than active data producers who make consequential decisions about what to measure, how to sample, and how to interpret anomalous readings (Gebre, 2022; Hardy et al., 2020; Schultheis & Kjelvik, 2020). This cookbook orientation obscures the messy, iterative, and uncertain nature of authentic scientific inquiry.

This gap is especially consequential for rural youth, who often encounter agriculture as part of everyday community life even as contemporary agricultural systems increasingly rely on sensors, automation, and data-driven decision making (Mattingly & Schaefer, 2021; Oliver & Hodges, 2014). Agriculture therefore offers a place-based and locally legible problem space for studying data practices, because students can work with firsthand data tied to phenomena that matter in their communities rather than with decontextualized school datasets. Accordingly, this qualitative comparative case study examines two groups of rural eighth-grade students participating in a 16-week, AI-supported Ag-STEM inquiry using low-cost environmental sensors.

The study focuses on how students engaged with both the conceptual and critical dimensions of data practices as they planned investigations, generated and interpreted sensor data, and navigated the challenges of inquiry. Because data production and interpretation were the most sustained shared activity across the unit, they became the analytic focus of this case study. By tracing these processes comparatively, the study illuminates distinct pathways through which students made meaning with data in a place-based learning environment. This investigation is guided by two research questions: (1) How do rural eighth-grade students engage with conceptual and critical data practices throughout a data-driven scientific inquiry leveraging low-cost sensor 727 devices in an Ag-STEM context? and (2) How do the emergent pathways of engagement with data practices compare between the two groups of rural students?

## Literature and theoretical framework

The growing field of K-12 data literacy education commonly invokes two complementary but often separated aims: helping learners reason with data and helping them reason about how data are produced (Lee et al., 2021; Pangrazio & Sefton-Green, 2020). Conceptual data literacy aligns with traditions of statistical reasoning and emphasizes formal competencies such as interpreting graphs, attending to variability, and drawing warranted inferences from data (Kjelvik & Schultheis, 2019; Weiland, 2017). Critical dat a literacy, by contrast, foregrounds learners’ capacity to interrogate data as socially produced, technologically mediated, and powerladen (Kruse et al., 2024; Markham, 2018; Stornaiuolo, 2020). Within this broader tradition, one especially relevant strand, and the focus of this study, treats critical data literacy as participation: students come to understand data by taking part in the processes through which data are generated, documented, and interpreted for human purposes (Hardy et al., 2020; Irgens et  al., 2020). Although both orientations are important, curricular designs often privilege one at the expense of the other (Raffaghelli, 2023). For example, firsthanddata projects may foreground agency and participation while providing limited support for interpreting variability or constructing evidence-based claims. Conversely, instruction built around polished datasets may support formal analysis while obscuring how data are shaped by human choices, disciplinary routines, and material conditions (Atenas et al., 2023; Frisby, 2024; Lee & Wilkerson, 2018). To avoid reproducing this split, this study adopts a dual-lens framework, inspired by Noushad et al. (2022), to analyze students’ engagement with both conceptual and critical data practices within a single inquiry context. Although the broader unit included AI-supported scaffolding, AI is treated here as part of the instructional ecology rather than as a separate analytic dimension; the analytic focus is students’ engagement with data practices.  To examine conceptual data literacy, this study draws on Rubin’s (2020) framework, which identifies five foundational elements of data analysis: Context, Variability, Aggregate, Visualization, and Inference. These constructs make it possible to analyze how  students interpreted the circumstances of data collection, recognized differences within and across measurements, viewed data as a whole, represented it visually, and constructed evidence-based claims. To examine critical data literacy as participation, this study uses Hardy et al.’s (2020) framework, which characterizes data production through three interconnected perspectives: Human, Disciplinary, and Material. This lens attends to how students’ goals and interests shaped inquiry, how they engaged with the norms and procedures of scientific work, and how they encountered the resistances of tools, environments, and phenomena. Together, these frameworks support a more integrated account of how reasoning with data and reasoning about data unfolded through ac tive participation in a place-based, datadriven Ag-STEM inquiry. This study employed a qualitative comparative case study design (Yin, 2009) to examine how two student groups enacted different pathways of engagement with data practices within the same instructional setting. This design was appropriate because the aim was not to compare average outcomes across the full class, but to interpret how contrasting groups navigated a shared inquiry environment over time.

## Context, setting, and participants

The study took place in Data-Driven Solutions for Sustainable Agriculture, a 16-week unit implemented during regular weekly eighth-grade agriculture classes at a public junior high school in a rural area of the Southern United States. The school served a p redominantly Hispanic and Latinx student population in a community where agriculture was a familiar part of everyday life. This rural Ag-STEM context was intentionally selected because it provided a place-based, locally legible problem space in which students could work with firsthand sensor data connected to issues that mattered in their community. The unit was part of a larger design project exploring how rural middle school students participate in data-rich, sensor-based Ag-STEM inquiry. For the present paper, data practices became the analytic focus because generating, documenting, organizing, and interpreting sensor data was the most sustained shared activity across the inquiry. The unit was co-designed by the classroom teacher and the PI and Co-PIs. The teacher led day-today instruction, classroom management, and whole-class discussion during implementation. Members of the research team supported technology setup and troubleshooting during selected lessons, observed classroom 728 activity, and collected implementation data; their role was to support enactment rather than interpret data for students. The inquiry was supported by low-barrier tools, including environmental sensors connected to Arduino microcontrollers, Google Sheets for data organization and visualization, and a conversational AI chatbot. The chatbot functioned as an on-demand peer mentor by providing hints, troubleshooting suggestions, and process-oriented prompts. In this study, it is treated as one element of the learning environment rather than as a separate object of analysis. Across the 16 weeks, students engaged in a full cycle of inquiry around a locally relevant sustainable agriculture problem. Within that shared problem space, groups developed their own resear ch questions about how environmental conditions such as water quality, nutrient level, or temperature might influence plant health over time. The unit unfolded in five phases: (1) Problem Framing and Exploration, in which students analyzed the focal proble m and identified relevant variables; (2) Research Design, in which groups generated research questions and hypotheses and adopted disciplinary roles; (3) Data Collection Setup, in which students built, tested, and troubleshot Arduino-based sensor systems; (4) Data Collection and Analysis, in which groups collected firsthand data, cleaned anomalous values, calculated summary statistics, and created visualiza tions; and (5) Solution Development and Communication, in which groups used their analyses to propose evidence-based sustainability solutions. A key feature of the Research Design phase was students’ adoption of authentic disciplinary roles. Using Career Role Cards, groups assigned members roles such as Soil Scientist, Water Quality Specialist, Plant Pathologist, Environmental Meteorologist, and Agronomist. These roles did more than label participation: they structured division of labor, directed attention to particular variables and forms of evidence, and invited students to approach the investigation from different expert perspectives.

From the larger pool of approximately 90 students across six class sections, two groups were selected for in-depth analysis using maximum variation sampling (Patton, 2002). Whole-class observations and field notes indicated that students displayed a wide range of engagement styles. To examine this variation in depth, we intentionally selected two groups that represented markedly different approaches to the same inquiry. One group (G1) consistently approached the work in a deliberate, structured, plan-oriented manner, whereas the other (G2) was more action-oriented, prioritizing immediate hands-on engagement and trial-and-error over extensive upfront planning. This contrast provided a productive basis for examining how different pathways shaped students’ engagement with data practices. G1 comprised four students (three male, one female), and G2 comprised three students (all male).

Data sources We drew on multiple data sources to support triangulation and build a rich account of students’ engagement across phases of inquiry. Whole-class observations and field notes documented participation patterns across sections and informed case selection. The primary analytic corpus for the two focal groups consisted of project journals, data logs, AI interaction transcripts, and post-project focus group interviews. Expert Project Journals served as the main record of each group’s planning work. In these journals, students developed research questions and hypotheses, assigned disciplinary roles, and documented key procedural decisions such as logistics, data-quality considerations, and strategies for improving reliability. The journals also included an Investigation Plan section and a final Solution Development section. Two structured spreadsheets, collectively referred to as Data Logs, captured students’ data work. During collection, Expert Data Logs were used by individual role-holders to upload raw sensor readings, document procedures, and note data-quality issues. After visualizations, and formulate predictions and claims. Student-AI Interaction Logs captured students’ individual exchanges with the chatbot. Although the inquiry was collaborative, students interacted with the AI individually and were informed in advance that the transcripts would be recorded and analyzed for research purposes. These logs  provided access to students’ in-the-moment conceptual questions, troubleshooting moves, and process-related decisions that were not always visible in group products. Finally, post-project semistructured focus group interviews (30-35 mins) were conducted with each focal group, audio-recorded, and transcribed verbatim. Using a common protocol, students were asked about their experiences with data collection, data cleaning and visualization, problem-solving strategies, the technological tools used in the unit, and their overall inquiry process. Taken together, these sources made it possible to compare what students planned, what they documented while working, what they asked for help with, and how they later interpreted their experiences.

RQ1. Overarching patterns of student engagement with data practices Across the dataset, three recurring patterns characterized students’ engagement with data practices. First, inference became most visible when students encountered unexpected or anomalous data. Second, students repeatedly negotiated between the structured procedures of scientific inquiry and the unpredictable realities of hands-on work. Third, their participation was shaped not only by academic goals but also by social, pragmatic, and playful motivations. These patterns together show pathways of engagement across the inquiry more clearly than a linear learning progression.  A central pattern was that inference was especially visible when students’ expectations were challenged by their data. Inference was one of the most frequently observed conceptual practices for both G1 (7 instances) and G2 (6 instances). For G1, their initial hypothesis proposed that “clearer water supports healthier plant growth” (G1, Expert Project Journal). However, their Group Data Log showed that the condition with higher average turbidity (sandy water, 24.19 NTU) resulted in only one wilted leaf, whereas the condition with lower turbidity (clean water, 12.57 NTU) resulted in two wilted leaves (G1, Group Data Log). Interview comments suggest that the group had difficulty interpreting these inconsistent readings: …the data that we had had from the waters, they were different. So, when we put them into the chart, the chart didn't really make sense... it was like, like, the first one it was like fourteen, and then the second time we did it, it was like twenty-five... a drastic change (G1, Focus Group Interview).

In combination with the blank graph-interpretation sections in their Group Data Log, this excerpt suggests uncertainty about how to make sense of variation across readings. At the same time, the available data do not allow that uncertainty to be attributed  to a single cause; documentation demands and ordinary classroom constraints may also have contributed. Even so, the anomalous result prompted the group to revise their expectations, leading them to predict that “The clean water plant might die and the turbid water will live” (G1, H2O Group Data Log).  G2’s inferential work likewise emerged as their initial expectations were unsettled. Their hypothesis predicted a straightforward positive relationship: “If nutrients and temperature increase, then the leaf color will become green, which is good, because t he plant will be beautiful and more healthy” (G2, Crop Dusters Expert Project Journal). Yet their focus group interview suggests that working 731 through the data led them to a more qualified conclusion: “At first, we thought the soil and... nitrogen, when it was high, the leaf color would be light green, determining that the plant is very healthy. But then at the end, it came out that too much nitrogen could make the plant not healthy and die” (G2, Focus Group Interview). In both groups, then, some of the strongest evidence of inferential reasoning appeared when students had to reconcile their original assumptions with unexpected findings. A second recurring pattern was the tension between disciplinary procedure and the messy realities of firsthand data work. Both groups engaged in foundational disciplinary practices, including defining conditions for comparison and attempting to document procedures. However, these efforts were repeatedly shaped by the unpredictability of materials, environments, and tools. This was especially visible in G2, for whom the Material Perspective appeared more frequently (5 coded instances). Their Expert Data Log recorded that one plant “got eaten by something” (G2, Expert Data Log), and in the focus group students described persistent hardware issues: “one of the chords... kept popping out, yeah, and then we had to have somebody to hold it” (G2, Focus Group Interview). Students also expressed curiosity about the technology itself, as when one student remarked, “I thought that was pretty cool, how it went to the... computer all the data” (G2, Focus Group Interview). These moments show that students’ data practices were shaped not only by abstract reasoning but also by direct encounters with recalcitrant tools and environments. This tension was also visible in students’ documentation practices. G2 documented many of their material struggles, but they also described procedural lapses that later complicated analysis, such as forgetting to label data during collection: “at first we forgot to label them... and then we had to figure it out the day after” (G2, Focus Group Interview). G1’s difficulty was less about hardware and more about interpreting the data they had produced. In their Group Data Log, sections for explaining their graphs were left blank. Read alongside their interview comments about “drastic change,” these omissions suggest difficulty connecting visual displays to interpretable patterns. Rather than treating documentation as a conceptual practice in itself, these instances show how incomplete or inconsistent documentation can constrain later conceptual work such as graph interpretation and inference. Finally, the inquiry was also a distinctly human endeavor shaped by social and personal motivations. The Human Perspective was frequently observed in both groups (G1 = 9 instances; G2 = 8 instances). AI logs, journals, and interviews showed that students’ engagement was often guided by goals that extended beyond formal scientific procedure. Students in both groups used the AI chatbot for playful or off-task interactions, including jokes and questions unrelated to the investigation (G1 & G2, AI Logs). Human-centered considerations also shaped project decisions. G1 justified their choice of prediction pragmatically rather than scientifically, writing that they selected it “because it easy to do” (G1, Group Data Log). G2 framed the desired outcome of their presentation in social terms, stating that their goal was “To applause us and say good things about us” (G2, Expert Project Journal). These examples suggest that students’ data practices unfolded within a broader social world where scientific goals coexisted with curiosity, convenience, recognition, and play. RQ2: A comparative analysis of two contrasting pathways Although both groups engaged with conceptual and critical data practices, they did so through markedly different pathways. The labels pragmatic planners and messy tinkerers are used here as analytic shorthand for these observed pathways, not as fixed traits of the students themselves. G1 more often approached the inquiry through deliberate planning and internal coherence, whereas G2 more often engaged through action, troubleshooting, and revision in the midst of work.

The clearest contrast between the groups lay in how they approached scientific procedure. G1 showed relative strength in abstract planning. Their journals displayed logical alignment among research questions, hypotheses, and variables, suggesting a coherent initial approach to the Disciplinary Perspective (G1, Expert Project Journal). However, this strength in planning was not matched by equally sustained documentation during enactment. Their Expert Data Log, the primary tool for recording the data-production process, was submitted largely blank, and planning sections such as Data Accuracy & Reliability and Logistics were left incomplete (G1, Expert Project Journal; G1, Expert Data Log). These gaps suggest a disconnect between designing an investigation and maintaining the procedural records needed to support later interpretation. G2 presented almost the opposite profile. Their early planning was less coherent, as illustrated by the mismatch between the dependent variable in their research question (leaf color) and the one referenced in their hypothesis (plant height) (G2, Expert Project Journal). Yet they documented far more of the practical work of doing the investigation. Their Expert Data Log included extensive raw data, process notes, and reflections on data quality issues (G2, Expert Data Log). In other words, where G1’s pathway was anchored in upfront coherence, G2’s 732 pathway was anchored in active, iterative engagement with the unfolding work of data production.  These contrasting pathways also shaped the kinds of resistance each group confronted most visibly. G1’s difficulties appeared primarily interpretive. Their interview comments repeatedly returned to the problem that the data “didn't really make sense” (G1, Focus Group Interview), and their AI interactions show them asking explanatory why-questions such as, “why did our spearmint plant grow better with turbidity w ater than the clean water” (G1, AI Log). G2, by contrast, more often described resistance in physical and procedural terms. Their interview was filled with accounts of assembling sensors, managing loose wires, and handling damaged materials. As one student explained, “...you had to connect all the wires piece by piece... one of the chords... kept popping out, yeah, and then we had to have somebody to hold it” (G2, Focus Group Interview). The contrast was not simply between thinking and doing, but between two differently distributed sites of struggle: interpretive uncertainty for G1 and material/procedural instability for G2. A final difference involved procedural flexibility. When G1 encountered difficulty with documentation and interpretation, they were more likely to leave sections incomplete. G2, by contrast, showed greater willingness to improvise in order to keep the project moving, sometimes at the expense of disciplinary rigor. This tradeoff is most visible in their account of fabricating plausible values when class time ran short (G2, Focus Group Interview): S5:  …if we, like, had the like end, like, the class was like, about to end. We just had to, like, kind of make something up to, like, put it on there… S6:  be, like, a reasonable, yeah, like something that contributed to what we're like, researching, yeah.

Coded as an absence of a core Disciplinary Perspective practice, this exchange shows that for G2, completing the task and producing a usable result sometimes took precedence over strict adherence to data integrity. This admission complicates any simple celebration of tinkering: the same improvisational stance that supported persistence in the face of material difficulty also introduced methodological and ethical vulnerability. Together, these cases suggest that students can engage with data practices throug h different pathways, but those pathways involve different strengths, constraints, and tradeoffs. This study offers a comparative account of two different pathways through which eighth-grade students engaged with data practices in a 16-week, AI-supported Ag-STEM inquiry using low-cost sensors. Its central contribution is not simply that productive struggle matters, but that contrasting pathways into data work made different aspects of conceptual and critical data literacy visible. G1, described analytically as the Pragmatic Planners, approached the inquiry through relatively coherent upfront planning but struggled to sustain documentation and interpretation when their data became messy. G2, described as the Messy Tinkerers, engaged through hands-on troubleshooting and procedural improvisation, which supported persistence but at times compromised methodological rigor. Taken together, these cases suggest that students may enter data practices through different forms of participation, and that those pathways carry different strengths, vulnerabilities, and consequences for later analysis and interpretation.  T he planner and tinkerer pathways identified here align with literature documenting varied student stances in project-based and design-based learning (Bevan et al., 2015; Turcotte et al., 2022). G1’s approach resembles a causation-oriented stance, in which learners attempt to organize the work through coherent upfront design (Hermann & Bossle, 2020). G2’s approach is closer to bricolage, in which progress is made by working with available materials, improvising, and revising in the midst of action (Chang & R ieple, 2018). At the same time, the present cases complicate any simple celebration of either stance. Upfront coherence did not guarantee sustained engagement with the procedural traces needed for later interpretation, as illustrated by G1’s incomplete logs. Likewise, G2’s strong engagement with troubleshooting and documentation did not ensure methodological consistency; in this case, it also included procedural shortcuts such as inventing plausible values when time ran short. Rather than showing that both pathways were equally productive, the findings suggest that both supported consequential engagement with data, but through different tradeoffs.

A second contribution concerns the role of surprise and resistance in students’ inferential work. Across both groups, some of the clearest moments of inference appeared when students’ expectations were unsettled by their own data. For G1, the key challenge was interpretive uncertainty: unexpected sensor readings 733 and inconsistent values made it difficult for them to connect graphs to coherent claims. For G2, the inquiry was shaped more visibly by material and procedural resistance, including unstable hardware, damaged plants, and labeling problems that had to be resolved in the course of doing the work. These findings resonate with scholarship arguing that firsthand data and messy inquiry can make the uncertainty of scientific work more visible to learners (Hardy et al., 2020; Schultheis & Kjelvik, 2020). At the same time, the data in this study do not support attributing students’ difficulties to cognitive dissonance alone. In G1, for example, incomplete graph interpretations may also reflect documentation demands and ordinary classroom constraints. Even so, the cases suggest that anomalous results and resistance can become occasions for inference when students are supported in revisiting their assumptions, representations, and procedures. The findings also complicate a simplified distinction between conceptual and critical data literacy. We do not interpret documentation itself as conceptual reasoning. Rather, documentation functioned as an enabling practice that preserved the traces students later needed to compare conditions, interpret patterns, and justify claims. In this sense, reasoning with data and reasoning about data were intertwined through the practical work of producing, recording, and revisiting evidence. G1 began the project wi th relatively stronger conceptual planning but weaker sustained documentation, which narrowed their opportunities to engage later with visualization and variability. G2 showed almost the reverse pattern: stronger engagement with the material and procedural  work of data production, but weaker initial coherence in framing the investigation. These cases therefore suggest that conceptual and critical data literacies are neither hierarchical nor separable; students’ opportunities for conceptual analysis depend in part on how they participate in the human, disciplinary, and material work through which data are made. These findings also point to several concrete design implications for data-rich inquiry in K-12 settings. First, coherent research planning should be paired with supports for in-process documentation, such as required labeling routines, brief logging checkpoints, or prompts from teachers and AI tools that ask students to record what changed, what failed, and why. Second, when students encounter anomalous graphs or contradictory readings, they may need targeted prompts that bridge from data displays to possible explanations rather than being left to infer those connections independently. Third, tinkering should be treated as a productive resource but bounded by routines that protect data integrity when time pressure rises. In this study, students’ strongest inferential moments emerged not from clean execution alone, but from opportunities to work through mismatches between plans, materials, and outcomes. Designing for those moments requires both flexibility and procedural support.

## Discussion & Implications

In conclusion, this study suggests that there is no single pathway through which middle school students engage with data practices in authentic inquiry. A planning-oriented pathway and a tinkering-oriented pathway both supported meaningful engagement, but each introduced distinct constraints and vulnerabilities. These findings should be interpreted in light of several limitations. The analysis centers only two focal groups within one rural Ag-STEM context, so the identified pathways are analytic descriptions rather than exhaustive categories. In addition, because the study relies on artifacts, AI logs, and post-project focus group interviews, it supports claims about engagement pathways more strongly than fine-grained claims about real-time social dynamics, developmental mechanisms, or learning progressions. The absence of interim interviews or audiovisual interaction data further limits what can be said about how these pathways unfolded moment by moment, and the findings were situated within a setting that included sustained teacher and researcher support. Future research should examine whether similar pathways appear across other grade levels, disciplines, and sociocultural contexts, and how specific curricular supports, teacher moves, and AI scaffolds may help students move more productively among planning, documentation, tinkering, and interpretation.
