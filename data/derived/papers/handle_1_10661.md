# Culturally Sustaining Game Redesign: Developing Criticality through Redesign of the Tabletop Game Forbidden Island

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Gaming allows for play, creativity, and expression. This study draws from ethnographic data collected over a six-month period when students at a culturally and linguistically diverse Canadian junior high school redesigned the analog tabletop game Forbidden Island. Tabletop games are more accessible and adaptable to complex school contexts thandigital games. This study shows students engaged in critical, culturally sustaining processes through their engagement with this project. This paper explores the criticality expressed by one group and the importance of the tactile experience of tabletop game redesign to these student conversations and experiences.

## Introduction

Digital games and tabletop games have been explored in the learning sciences (Bastani & Kim, 2022; Kim et al., 2022, 2023; Kim & Ho, 2022). Yet unlike digital games, which require specialized ski lls and equipment, analog tabletop games are more accessible and adaptable to  different contexts and purposes (1). This reality renders analog tabletop games useful in school settings which may operate with limited resources, although the literature on culturally sustaining gaming pedagogies often fo cuses on digital games, not tabletop games. This re search aims to explore how students at a mid-sized urban Canadian junior high school with a diverse student population expressed burgeoning criticality through a tabletop  game redesign project. Our analysis also aims to d evelop understanding of the culturally sustaining  promise held in the accessibility, flexibility, and  tangible experiences afforded by tabletop games.

Some computational thinkers use tabletop games as steppingstones to more complex computational work (e.g., Berland & Lee, 2011). Our research reveals t hat tabletop games are valuable learning mediums wi th their own worth. Analog tabletop games — and the experiences they engender — are distinct from digital games. In board games, movements and play are more visible th an in digital games (Kim & Bastani, 2017). Further,  the flexibility of the tabletop game format enables players to change the pacing and roles in ways which are suited to players’ needs and expressions.

Game (re)design projects contain pedagogical value. In the context of culturally sustaining redesign, by engaging students in creating or modifying existing games, teachers can encourage students to explore their own and others’ cultures, identities, and v alues. Game design projects also facilitate cross-disciplinary inquiry and collaboration among students (Kim & Bastani, 2017).  Previous studies have shown that game redesign act ivities can enhance students’ learning outcomes, creativity, and engagement across various disciplines (Bastani & Kim, 2022; Kim & Bastani, 2017; Kim et al.,  2023). Building upon these studies, our research has re vealed that (re)design projects can serve as opportunities to develop students’ critical consciousnesses. Data from this ethnographic study consist of observations, artifacts, and interviews collected over a sixmonth redesign project of the game Forbidden Island, which took place in a culturally and linguistical ly diverse urban Canadian junior high school. This project rev eals insights regarding student understanding of cr iticality expressed throughout the game redesign process and provides a look into the value of analog tabletop r edesign within complex school environments.

Establishing the foundation of this redesign project This work draws inspiration from previous projects and publications along with scholarship of game redesign and analysis. It is embedded in concepts of criticality, critical consciousness, and culturally sustaining pedagogy. For this project, we created re design activities which: (1) pinpointed ways of act ing in varied cultural and social systems, fostering cultural competence; (2) helped students develop critical consciousness; and (3) si tuated diversity as positive resources for mutuality  (Kim et. al., 2022; Ladson-Billings, 1995; Paris, 2012). Though we attended to all three of these project design goals  in our work, this paper explores our second goal, which sought to aid students in developing their critical consciousnesses. Critical theorists seek to demystify the ways in which power  works within systems, dismantling oppression through their unpackaging of complexities of power with their analyses. To games scholars like Mary Flanagan (2016, 2009), critical games are ones whic h seek to challenge norms and assumptions embedded in game culture and systems. We argue that criticality  in game redesign projects contributes to the devel opment of students’ critical consciousnesses through the inte rrelated processes of thinking, doing, and cont emplating whereby learners engage with one another to unpacka ge experiences and causes of oppression (Freire, 19 70; Godfrey & Rarick, 2016).

In the context of this project, we noticed students engaging with many layers of culture— youth culture, home culture, and school culture— rendering this game redesign project and the classrooms in which it occurred as sites for the development of cultural competencies. Cultural competence (Ladson-Billings, 1995) and culturally sustaining pedagogy  (Paris, 2012)  are integral part s of the education process. With these foundational understandings, we engaged in this redesign research project. Research This research took place at a Canadian junior high school of about 450 students in grades six through eight. The school services three communities, of which two hav e publicly available census data. Compared to avera ge statistics for the rest of the city, t hese communities have higher immigrant populations and lower populations of individuals who speak English most often at home (2). Individual families in these communities generally have a median total household income lower than the city average. For this research, we collaborated in September 2022 with teachers at this school to create design works hops. Utilizing a model and student workbook, we ai med to merge game redesign and current teaching practices already existent in the students’  Humanities and Science/Mathematics courses. Data collection occurred from December 2022 to April 2023.

In this paper, we focus on one participant group co mprised of three girls (Grace, Saabira, and Shaza [pseudonyms]) to illustrate their engagement with t heir cultural backgrounds and lived contexts, as th e diversity of their group and engagement with their respective cultures is illustrative of the varied demographic composition of the school. These participants have ties to Cameroon, Lebanon, and Sudan respectively. Grace and Shaza were most often present for redesign sessions. Saabira was frequently absent. Survival Islands and colonial power Colonialist games reinforce ideas inherent in colonialism such as the subjugation of nature, emphasis on conquest and extraction, and oppression of Other (Flanagan, 2009, 2016; Flanagan & Jakobsson, 2023). Participants in our project intuited the colonial qualities of Forbidden Island  (e.g., the game’s focus on resource extraction) and attended to these colonial elements in their redesigned game, a demonstration of their critical engagement. Grace and Shaza decided early in the design process to si tuate their redesigned game within the context of colonialism, with Grace mentioning several times that the player s of their game will be ‘colonizers.’ To articulate  their ideas of colonization, th e group chose to focus on countries. Their game, Survival Islands, features countries and treasures which are collected by the players who are colonizers traveling through the countries while escaping the rising heat of the “sunrise cards” (see Figure 1).

The group held awareness of colonial complexity tac itly tied to games like Forbidden Island (i.e., that players travel to the game from lands outside it to extract resources and destroy terrain) and sought to reveal some of this nuance within their work. However, detailed analysis indicates the groups' ideas of colonization remained ill-defined over the course of the project. Though the group he ld a basic understanding of coloniality as control and passing understanding of the history of colonia lism (e.g., Grace mentions multiple times Canadian colonization and the “horrible” experiences of Firs t Nations people), the group did not naturally build upon their understandings to dive deeper into more critical co nversations about power and oppression as their wor k progressed.

Grace and the migration narrative One of the most poignant moments for the group was when Grace sought to defend a game mechanic she designed requiring players to “pass through all these countries” to get back to their country of origin after collecting all the treasure hidden in the count ry tiles. Shaza indicated that this mechanic was ov erly complicated, and she did not understand the purpose of making game players walk back to their home countries when they could take a  boat or plane.

To speak to Shaza’s criticism and demonstrate the importance of walking, Grace shared a narrative told to her by a Cameroonian friend whose father and unc le, as migrants, had walked for days through the de sert to reach a ship and eventually escape to Italy. During the journey, this friend’s uncle died and was buried at sea when her father finally made it to the “ocean,” presumab ly the Mediterranean. Grace’s passion as she shares  this narrative is palpable, and her narrative was taken up by the group in Su rvival Islands in that “walking” through all countries to win the game was requisite. Grace also mentions at other points in the redesign  sessions that the colonizers are taking these resources from other co untries because they have destroyed their own count ries through war and over-extraction, though these insights are not always shared by Shaza or explored in detail. Conversation facilitation through tactile experience We also noted that the tangible experience of hands-on project participation and tabletop game creation  were triggers for deeper conversation and understanding (Bastani & Kim, 2022). The group seemed to enjoy the tactile experience of drawing their game pieces (see Figure 1). We often noticed participants holding, using, or fidgeting with game cards and tokens as they worked during th eir design sessions, and these tactile experiences seemed to empower students’ enhanced engagement with critical themes of the project. Saabira and enhanced engagement Regarding the tactile nature of this redesign proce ss, one moment of note was Saabira’s shift in engag ement. At this point, Saabira had already missed many redesign sessions. When she was present, she was not engaged, often ignoring the attempts of Grace, the de facto group leader, to guide her in participation. We not ed a significant change in Saabira’s engagement on the day when she realized countries were the focus of their redesign  (see Figure 2a). Saabira says, “Oh, we’re doing countrie s? Is Lebanon  ((her country of origin))  one of them?” and stands up to engage with the game pieces. We observed her spending a good portion of the session moving game pieces and working on the group’s physical design e lements, after developing a connection to the game components.

Grace and Shaza shift to an interview format Another interesting moment for this group regarding  tactile movement was their turn to an interview fo rmat (see Figure 2b). As part of this project, participants h ad their game redesign sessions, often guided by wo rksheets, video recorded. Grace, without prompting, moved the  camera (a tablet) and adjusted it so that Shaza’s face was in full view. Then, rather than reading from worksh eet questions aloud, Grace began asking Shaza quest ions in an interview format, altering the worksheet questions and eventually diverging from the preformulated questions entirely, giving Shaza space to answer in ways whic h were more engaging and natural. Here, the camera, in an unexpected way, became a medium for deeper continuous conversation.

## Discussion and conclusion

The experiences of the group and our observations through this project illustrate that tabletop games are valuable mediums for critical learning. Regarding colonialit y and power, the natural insight of student partici pants illustrates promise for explicitly embedding redesi gn projects, entailing deep evaluation of an existing structure, in critical game design. The group’s nascent unders tanding of colonialism indicates they would have be nefited from more sustained, meaningful pedagogical guidance. The narrative Grace shared from her Cameroonian friend and her emphasis on the hardship of fleeing to escape oppression opened a meaningful space where facilitation of conversations about power and development of critical cultural competencies could have occurred. Since tabletop games do not require specialized ski lls or equipment in the same way as digital games, tabletop games as tools for complex, culturally sus taining learning in classrooms — like the linguistically and economically diverse classrooms in this project— deserve further exploration within the field of learning sciences. Future work regarding these topics should include r obust instructional guidance for teachers on facilitating critical, culturally sustaining pedago gy using tabletop redesign. We must also more readi ly and explicitly attend to our design goals of creating s pace for critical discourse and the development of critical consciousness.
