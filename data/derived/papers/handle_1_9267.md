# Differential Effects of a Script and a Group Awareness Tool on the Acquisition of Collaboration Skills

**Conference:** ISLS 2023

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Exploring ways to support learners in becoming effective collaborators is a core challenge of CSCL research. Collaboration scripts have been shown to be quite supportive, but they are also directive and can cause reactance in learners. Research on more implicit forms of support, such as promoting reflection, is still scarce. In the present study we compare the effectiveness of a collaboration script and a collaborative reflection phase utilizing a group awareness tool, on learners’ explicit knowledge about beneficial interaction and the quality of their collaboration. In a laboratory experiment, 150 higher education students collaborated in groups of three on solving two information-pooling problems. The results suggest that collaboratively reflecting about the previous collaboration fosters explicit knowledge more than collaborating with a collaboration script. However, the conditions did not differ regarding collaboration quality. We discuss these findings against the learning mechanisms behind scripting and collaborative reflection.

## Introduction

To tackle complex societal problems of the 21st century such as the climate crisis, the capability to collaboratively learn, work, and solve problems is a vital skill (van Laar et al., 2017). While collaborative learning is widely used in teaching domain-specific knowledge, ways to deliberately fostering collaboration skills  have received far less attention. This is especially relevant at career entry when collaboration skill s become essential. To design instructional support for the acquisition of collaboration skills and for reliably ass essing learners’ collaboration skills, a clear understanding of what collaboration skills entail is necessary. A pproaches for describing core components of collaboration skills in CSCL research often focus on collaboration qua lity, such as the rating schema proposed by Meier et al. (2007). This rating schema contains nine proc ess dimensions that contribute to productive collaboration: sustaining mutual understanding, dialogue management, information pooling, reaching consensus, task division, time management, technical coordination, reciprocal inte raction, and individual task orientation. While the primary goal of the rating schema is the assessment of collaboration quality, it also allows to derive skills that are necessary to achieve a high level of collabora tion quality. Further models of effective collaboration have been formulated in frameworks that focus on the large-scale assessment such as PISA 2015 (Graesser et al., 2018) or the Assessment and Teaching of 21st Century Skills (ACT21S) (Hesse et al., 2015). The framework underlying PISA (Graesser et al., 2018) proposes that collaborative problem solving requires three skills: establishing and maintaining a shared understanding, taking appropriate action to solve the problem, which includes problem solving actions and communication, and establishing and maintaining group organization. The ACT21S framework (Hesse et al., 2015) specifies five problem solving proces ses (problem identification, problem representation, planning, executing, monitoring) and two sets of skills, namely social skills (collaboration, i.e., participation, perspective taking, social regulation) and cognitive skills (problem solving, i.e., planning, executing, monitoring, flexibility, learning). In comparison, all three framew orks highlight the importance of a mutual understanding, the use of unshared information, joint decision ma king, as well as monitoring and regulation. In the present study we adopt the rating schema by Meier et al. (2007) as our model of productive collaboration and in order to determine core components of collaboration skills because the schema encompasses the skills described by PISA 2015 and ACT21S but also specifies how these skills can be observed. Research showed that learners rarely engage in productive interaction with peers when receiving no dedicated collaboration support (Rummel et al., 2009) and that computer-support can promot e effective interaction (Chen et al., 2018; Jeong & Hmelo-Silver, 2016). Meta-analytical evidence suggests that external collaboration scripts effectively support the acquisition of collaboration skills. Yet, it is debated whether external scripts restrict learners’ autonomy too strongly (Wise & Schwarz, 2017). A further challenge when using collaboration scripts is tailoring them to learners’ current proficiency in orde r to minimize negative effects that stem from learners’ prior knowledge about collaboration, such as over-scripting (Dillenbourg, 2002). Group awareness tools are another way of administering information about beneficial collaboration to the group. Yet, to CSCL 2023 Proceedings  © ISLS 76 the best of our knowledge it has not been investigated whether these tools are suited to foster the acquisition of collaboration skills and helping groups engage in fruitful collaboration. Thus, in the present study we investigate to which extent group awareness tools can affect the interaction in a group and help learners gain knowledge about effective collaboration.

## Theoretical background

Acquiring the skills for effective collaboration According to the script theory of guidance (Fischer et al., 2013), knowledge about colla boration is encoded in internal collaboration scripts. Like cognitive scripts for everyday situations (e.g., th e “restaurant script”, Schank & Abelson, 1977), internal collaboration scripts encompass knowledge and expectations about the structure of the situation, important phases, roles as well as the actions necessary to acc omplish the collaboration’s goals. Like other cognitive scripts internal collaboration scripts form over time by particip ating in collaboration repeatedly. The script theory of guidance proposes that a learner constructs a new configur ation on an internal collaboration script by recombining available script components (internal script induction) or  by modifying an existing configuration if it does not lead to the expected outcome (internal script reconfigur ation). One way to promote effective collaboration and help learners develop internal collaborations scripts is  to provide them with external collaboration scripts which we will briefly describe in the following. Scripting the interaction during collaboration External collaboration scripts guide interaction in groups by specifying objectives of the interaction, sequences of actions, or roles (Vogel et al., 2017). Doing so, external scripts reduce the cognitive capacities that learners need to invest on roles or activities during collaboration (Nokes-Malach et al., 2015). T hese cognitive capacities are then available for problem solving, processing domain-knowledge and internalizing eff ective collaboration strategies (Vogel et al., 2017). To benefit from an external collaboration scrip t, the learners of a group need to pick up the scripted elements (e.g., roles, action sequences) and enact the m, which is assumed to help learners internalize the scripted elements. Indeed, research showed that groups can leverage external collaboration scripts and engage in the scripted patterns of interaction which affects the quality of the interaction in the group (Rummel et al., 2009) and supports learners in internalizing the external collaboration scrip t (e.g., Kollar et al., 2007; Tsovaltzi et al., 2014; cf. Rummel et al., 2009 for a different result). This finding is corroborated by meta-analyses which found a medium positive effect of external scripts on collaboration skills  such as negotiation skills, information sharing skills, coordination skills or a combination of different ski lls (Vogel et al., 2017). Taken together, there is ample evidence that external collaboration scripts support collaboration and foster collaboration skills. Besides the challenge to tailor external collaboration scripts to lea rners existing skills (i.e., their internal collaboration scripts) in order to reduce negative effects that cause proce ss-losses, such as over-and underscripting (Dillenbourg, 2002; Vogel et al., 2017), there is an ongoing debate about whether and  to what degree external scripts restrict learners’ autonomy and whether this is compa tible with the core idea of collaboration which is providing learners with autonomy over their learning (see Provoc ation #2 in Wise & Schwarz, 2017). This debate raises the question about the effectiveness of less directive means of support that transfer the autonomy about the regulation to the group instead of providing explicit guidance. One such form of support are group awareness tools.

Group awareness tools: Monitoring and reflecting on the quality of interaction Instead of explicitly guiding groups how to interact during collaboration, group awarenes s tools (GATs) collect digital data about the group and visualize this data to the group (Janssen & Bodemer, 2013). The data can consist of behavioral traces from the learning environment or self-report data from questionnaires or tests (Buder, 2011). Thus, GATs afford monitoring of the interaction and subsequent reflection of the effectiveness of the interaction. We propose that GATs represent a way to administer elements of beneficia l collaboration such as behavioral models or external collaboration scripts (see Rummel et al., 2009). This argument rests on the assumptions of how GATs support monitoring and regulation. Firstly, GATs visualize information about  certain aspects of the collaboration and thereby increase the salience of these aspects. This allows the group to focus their attention. Here, the designer of the GAT decides which aspects of the collaboration are visualized in the GAT and thus are more likely to receive attention from the group. Secondly, GATs provide feedback (Jermann & Dillenbourg, 2008) which serves as a basis for collaborative reflection. Reflection is a cent ral component of regulation (e.g., selfreflection phase, Zimmerman, 2000) in which learners review their learning pr ocess and assess which strategies were beneficial and which should be changed. Collaborative reflection includes three  behaviors: evaluating performance (present or past) (e.g., by collectively discussing the group's functioning in terms of performance or CSCL 2023 Proceedings  © ISLS 77 results), looking for alternatives, and making decisions which includes enacting  strategies (Gabelica et al., 2014). In line with the synthesis of feedback mechanisms by Hattie and Timperley (2007), Gabelica et al. (2014) suggest that groups leverage feedback by taking up feedback, assigning meaning to the informa tion, extracting cues for future strategies, developing intentions and plans, and eventually acting out strategies. Up until now there is a lack of research on the effects of GATs or reflection interventions on the quality of collaboration or the acquisition of collaboration skills. However, there are findings that suggest that groups adopt beneficial interactions when working with GAT or a reflection intervention. Yet, studies  suggest that leveraging information from a GAT and reflecting about the collaboration is challenging (Dehler et al., 2009; Strauß & Rummel, 2021). Thus, scaffolding these processes is advised, for example by prompting students to recollect their experience (vanden Boom et al., 2007) or by guiding the reflection process (Phielix et al., 2011). For instance, Phielix et al. (2011) visualized students’ self-and peer-assessment of the collaboration and asked students to reflect upon this information. This collaborative reflection subsequently stimulated students to develop plans regarding aspects of the collaboration that are related to the dimensions visualized in the GAT. However, the study did not encompass a subsequent collaboration phase in which learners could exhibit changes in interaction. In another study of synchronous collaboration, Gabelica et al., (2014) found that a reflection activity that took place between two subsequent problems was associated with better performance in the second task, while reflecting during the task did not affect performance. Taken together, while a GAT does not prompt beneficial actions it can administer elements of beneficial collaboration by suggesting the group dimensions of their interaction that are effective targets of monitoring and reflection. However, it remains open whether learners internalize the knowledge about beneficial collaboration that is encapsulated in GATs.

## Research question and hypotheses

External collaboration scripts and GATs are different approaches to support collaboration. The effectiveness of external scripts is based on their directiveness, while GATs leave learners with more autonomy about their collaboration and require active processing of the information about the collaboration. External scripts offer explicit knowledge about collaboration, fill potential knowledge gaps and help learners engage in beneficial interaction. GATs, by contrast, draw learners’ attention to specific aspects of the collaboration and help learners activate their implicit knowledge about collaboration. Thus, GATs require a more active processing of the information about beneficial interaction. For external scripts, there is evidence about their potential to foster collaboration skills (e.g., Radkowitsch et al., 2020), while there is still a shortage of studies that investigate whether learners can acquire knowledge about collaboration with a GAT. Therefore, the present study investigates two questions: RQ 1) How do an external collaboration script and a GAT (including guided reflection) compare in fostering students’ explicit knowledge about effective collaboration? And similarly, RQ

2) How do a fixed collaboration external script and a GAT compare in their effects on the quality of the

interaction in the group? Given the meta-analytical evidence on external scripts (Vogel et al., 2017; Radkowitsch et al., 2020), we expect that an external collaboration script is more effective than a GAT with guided reflection in fostering the acquisition of collaboration skills, while both types of collaboration support are more effective than receiving no support (H1). With respect to the quality of the collaboration, there exists some evidence that an external script can increase the quality of the collaboration (Rummel, et al., 2009). For GATs this question has not yet been investigated. Given the scarce evidence, we treat RQ 2 as an exploratory analysis and thus did not formulate a hypothesis.

## Methods

To answer our research questions, we conducted an online laboratory experiment in  which participants collaborated to solve a joint problem. Our sample consisted of 150 German university students, aged 18 to 45 (M = 23.61; SD = 4.69). The students were assigned to groups of three, resulting in 50 groups. Participants were matched in groups depending on their temporal availability. Groups were then randomly assigned to one of three experimental conditions: control condition, collaboration script or GAT. Due to technica l issues, the video recordings are missing for three groups. Recordings were therefore analyzed for n = 47 groups.

## Procedure

The experiment lasted approximately two hours and comprised a pre-questionnaire, a short video about general strategies for effective collaboration, the first collaborative phase (learning phase) with or without collaboration support depending on the experimental condition, a second questionnaire followed by a short bre ak, the second collaborative phase (testing phase), and a post-questionnaire. CSCL 2023 Proceedings  © ISLS 78 Collaboration task The groups collaborated via the video-conferencing platform Zoom to solve two infor mation-pooling problems that lasted 30 minutes each. The sequence of the two problems was counter balanced over the two collaboration phases. The collaborative problems contained a civil engineering problem that required finding ways to integrate new demands into a 2D-model (floorplan) of a daycare center. Each group member was assigned a role (architect, daycare management, or fire and health protection) that included the joint task as well as unshared, complementary information. To arrive at a solution, the group members had to pool their unshare d information to develop alternative approaches. During collaboration, the group had access to a 2D-model (floorplan) of a daycare center (see Figure 1). All groups were encouraged to use the annotation tool of the video-conference system to draw on the 2D-model which was shared by the instructor while evaluating ideas and fin ding a joint solution. During collaboration, groups documented their information sharing and decision-making processe s in a shared virtual notepad. The collaboration phases were video recorded.

Collaboration support Depending on the experimental condition, groups received (a) no collaboration support during th e first problem (n = 45, control condition), (b) an external collaboration script that structured the  problem-solving process, prompted students to use the collaboration strategies, and provided explicit descriptions of collaboration strategies (n = 54), or (c) no support during collaboration, but a group awareness tool in conjuncti on with a collaborative reflection activity after working on the first problem (n = 57). The interve ntions included the dimensions of successful collaborative problem-solving processes proposed by Meier et al. (2007), namely sustaining mutual understanding, information pooling, reaching consensus, task division, time management, and dialogue management. In the external collaboration, these processes were explicitly prompted, while in the GAT they were queried and discussed subsequently. Figure 1 illustrated how the script and the GAT covered identical processes. Figure 1 Left: External script during the information pooling-phase and floorplan. Right: Spider-plot from the group awareness tool (dimension: information pooling).

The external collaboration script was presented in the margin area of the shared screen in the vide o conference. The script scaffolded the collaboration by specifying a sequence of actions (macro scripts) and within each phase providing the group with prompts on beneficial actions for the respective phase (micro script). The script consisted of four consecutive phases that were introduced with a short description of the  general purpose of this collaboration phase, followed action prompts for the interaction during this phase. Each phase was highlighted in a different color and contained a progress bar for visual feedback about how  much time was left in the current phase. Phase changes were indicated by an audio-signal. The four phases of the script were coordination (5 minutes), information pooling (7 minutes), discussion (10 minutes), and decision making (7 minutes). The group awareness tool (GAT) consisted of a self-assessment questionnaire that the group members filled in individually after the first colla boration phase. The questionnaire was expected to foster reflection about the group’ s collaboration strategies by asking them to compare their strategies to strategi es that have proved to lead to successful collaboration. Specifically, the questionnaire consisted of 28 questions which were based on the phases and actions included in the external script. For example, for the informati on pooling dimension, group members evaluated their information pooling strategies with items like “We have exchanged all relevant information”. The students rated their groups’ performance on a 7-point-Likert-scale (1 = completely disagree, 7 = completely agree). The individual answers were then averaged and mirrored to the group with spider-plot-diagrams. These diagrams provided the basis for the collaborative reflection phase. Here we invited the groups to reflect jointly on CSCL 2023 Proceedings  © ISLS 79 their self-assessment of the first collaborative phase regarding how they would like to improve their strategies in the second collaborative phase. We asked the groups to write down their intentions in a shared digital notepad.

## Measures

We assessed the participants’ internal collaboration script in the form of explicit knowledge about collaboration in the pre-test and after the first collaboration (i.e., learning phase). The measurement was based on the method of Rummel and Spada (2005) and consisted of two open-ended questions. First, participant s were asked to describe the phases that a group should perform while solving a joint problem, and second what should generally be done to achieve a “good” collaboration. We scored participants’ r eplies against the background of the rating schema by Meier et al. (2007). Two coders independently coded participants’ answers and counted the number of characteristics of productive collaboration that were mentioned. Each character istic of productive collaboration mentioned was awarded with one point when it matched our coding manual. During coding, the coders achieved a satisfactory agreement (Cohen’s kappa κ =.74). The points achieved in both questions were then summed. The quality of the collaboration, which served as an indicator for implicit knowledge about effective collaborati on was determined for the video-recorded interactions by using a rating-schem a based on the nine dimensions of effective collaboration (Meier et al., 2007). The coders assigned at least one of the nine codes to each individual utterance during the collaboration phases. The data was coded by three coders indi vidually who reached an agreement of 74.51% on 20% of the data and resolved conflicts by discussion. The quality of the interactions was then rated on a three-level scale (0, 1, 2) by splitting up the recordings in segments of three minutes and rating the quality of every dimension that was coded at least once during one of these segments. Rating the quality reached very good interrater agreement of Cohen’s weighted κ =.87. To assess the overall quality of the groups’ interaction, we averaged the quality-scores for each dimension and calculated a grand mean for each group in the learning and the testing phase, respectively. As a control variable, we asse ssed students’ cognitive load after the learning phase using two self-report items adapted from Stebner (2013): “How easy or difficult was it for you to work on this task?”, “During the task, my mental effort was on average…”.

## Discussion

In the presented study, we investigated the effects of two means of  collaboration support and their effects on the acquisition of collaboration skills. Research has shown that on external collaborati on scripts can effectively scaffold collaboration and subsequently foster the acquisition of collaboration skill s (e.g., Radkowitsch et al., 2020). GATs are a less directive approach to administer information about effective collaboration. While there is evidence that group awareness and reflection can enhance collaboration, it has not been investigated whether learners can acquire collaboration skills by reflecting using a GAT. Our results suggest that both, a collaboration script, and a GAT, but especially the GAT and the subsequent reflection, helpe d learners to gain explicit knowledge about beneficial interaction during collaboration (RQ 1). Regarding collaboration quality in the groups (RQ 2), which we understand as a proxy for implicit knowledge about effective colla boration, our analyses showed that providing groups with collaboration support had no effects. This result is mo st striking in the script condition where the script did not even affect the collaboration quality in the lea rning phase. In summary, our results suggest that learners benefitted from collaboration support in acquiring e xplicit knowledge about collaboration while they did not transfer this knowledge to a subsequent collaboration phase. We will discuss this in the following. The result that collaborative reflection using a GAT led to a larger increase in explicit knowledge than a script was unexpected given the ample evidence of the effectiveness of external scripts. One reason for this finding may be differences in cognitive load during the learning phase. While the script provided explicit guidance in the sense that the collaboration was divided into phases for which the learners received explicit action prompts, learners received the GAT after the collaborative task where they rec eived the information about productive interaction in the form of questions. However, while all students reported modera te cognitive load, they did not differ significantly. Thus, the difference between the conditions cannot be att ributed to differences in cognitive load. Our main hypothesis why reflection fostered knowledge acquisition is that the reflection triggered a more (inter)active processing of the information about beneficial collaboration as com pared to following the prompts by the collaboration script. This assumption is in line with the assumptions proposed in the ICAP-framework (Chi & Wylie, 2014) and should receive attention in future studies. Findings similar to ours were reported by Rummel et al. (2009) who reported beneficial effects of requiring additional elaboration when receiving support. Despite the increase in explicit knowledge about effective collaboration, groups were not able to increase their collaboration quality. This finding is in contradiction to findings by Rummel and S pada (2005) who found that following a script improves students' subsequent unsupported collaboration as w ell as their knowledge of good collaboration. However, these findings could not be reproduced in a follow-up study (Rummel et al., 2009). Three explanations are conceivable as to why the support did not help groups inc rease the quality of the collaboration. For learners who received a script, the question arises to w hat extent the groups paid attention to and followed the script. As Kirschner et al. (2008) found, a coercive collaboration script can yield better outcomes than a non-coercive script. Learners in the collaboration script condition ma y have acted on their internal script components available for this specific situation like the students in the two  other conditions. Secondly, learners may need repeated applications of the newly acquired internal script components  to build and apply the new CSCL 2023 Proceedings  © ISLS 81 configuration of the internal script successfully (Fischer et al., 2013) and achieve self-regulated performance. This conjecture could be tested with a delayed assessment of the quality of interaction. For the students who reflected on the collaboration using a GAT, it can be discussed whether the students were able to assess the quality of their collaboration accurately. The G AT visualized the self-assessment scores as a basis for joint reflection. Two explanations seem possible why reflec tion might not have affected the collaboration quality in the testing phase. First, the groups might have overestima ted their own performance and seen noneed to adapt their behavior in aspects that would benefit from attempt s for improvement, which seems likely given the average score of M = 6.1 with a maximum of 7 in the self-assessment-questionnaire. If groups produced inaccurate internal feedback about the quality of their interaction, they might not seek to improve their interaction during a subsequent collaboration (van Laar et al., 2017). Secondly, groups which showed accurate self-assessment may have deliberately decided not to alter their intera ction regarding specific aspects that they evaluated as irrelevant in the subsequent collaboration phase. The present study and results need to be interpreted against several limita tions. One set of limitations concerns the measure and analysis of collaboration quality. As collaboration quality can only be assessed for the group, the group is the unit of analysis (RQ 2). Consequently, the sample size av ailable for analysis decreases which is associated with lower statistical power to detect (null-)effects reliably. This limitation can be overcome by replicating the study with more groups, but this solution comes with larger cost s and increased coordination efforts. Further, in the present study, we adapted the rating schema from Meier et al. (2007) and rated the collaboration quality on a 3-point scale instead of a 5-point scale. Given that other studies reached a larger variance of interrater reliability (Kahrimanis et al., 2012; Meier et al., 2007; Rummel et al., 2009), our decision was a tradeoff between high interrater reliability and variance available for analysis. C onsequently, our analysis may have lacked sensitivity to detect small differences between groups. Taken together, our study presented a side-by-side comparison of an external c ollaboration script and reflection about collaboration using a GAT. Most importantly, the study addressed a ga p in research on group awareness tools. Our study also highlights several interesting avenues for future research. For example, it appears worth investigating, how groups process the prompts by a collaboration script or a GAT and how this affects skillacquisition and collaboration quality. Further, groups’ ability to moni tor and assess their interaction and how it affects reflection, planning and regulation of the interaction is an interesting ave nue for future research. With respect to the discussion regarding the role of collaboration support that transfers autonomy to the learners (Wise & Schwarz, 2017) our results are promising in that GATs may be an alternative way of administering knowledge about productive interaction. Finally, future studies may add a modeling condition as implemented by Rummel et al. (2009) and investigate the cognitive processes involved in acquiring collaboration competence over time.
