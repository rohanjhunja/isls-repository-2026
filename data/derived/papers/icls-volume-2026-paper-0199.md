# Entangled Learning: Farmers, Phones, and the Politics of Informal Agricultural Learning in Rural Jamaica

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study examines how smallholder farmers in rural Jamaica engage with mobile phones and digital platforms as part of ‘ informal’ learning apparatuses. Drawing on Barad’s (2007) theory of agential realism, it conceptualizes learning as a dynamic, relational process shaped by entangled human and non-human agencies. Based on interviews and observations with 18 farmer s, the analysis explores how mobile devices, algorithms, social networks, ecological feedback, and experiential knowledge co-produce authority and shape learning. Central to this process are agential cuts —material discursive boundary-making practices through which farmers distinguish between valid/ invalid and traditional/non-traditional knowledge. Farmers actively sift, interpret, and reconfigure knowledge through iterative intra-actions with platforms, people, and place. The findings challenge binary framings of digital learning as either human-centered or technologically deterministic, revealing instead the recursive and negotiated nature of learning. This research offers critical insights for developing inclusive and context-responsive agricultural mLearning interventions.

Mobile phones are increasingly used for learning within rural agricultural contexts. With the growing ubiquity of mobile devices, farmers are engaging not only with ‘formal’ learning platforms —such as those developed by international agencies and governments—but also with ‘ informal’ learning through popular digital platforms like YouTube, WhatsApp, or Facebook. While there has been significant research on learner agency in relation to digital tools, it is not clear how agency emerges in mobile l earning (mLearning) contexts, through farmers’ everyday entanglements with mobile learning technologies, related platforms and algorithmically shaped content. This study explores the concept of agenc y in ‘informal’ mLearning within rural agricultural settings, foregrounding the relationships between diverse actors. Rather than treating agency as a property of either the human actor (learner autonomy) or the technology (algorithmic control), the analysis draws on Barad’s (2007) agential realism, conceptualizing agency as emergent, fluid and relational, produced through intra-actions among human, non-human, and more-than-human actors (2007).Within this framework, mobile phones, algorithmic structures, farmers, community members, and ecological environments are understood not as discrete entities, but as emergent components of a ‘learning apparatus’, a configuration of entangled actors through which knowledge and authority are discursively and materially produced. Based on observations and interviews with 18 farmers in St. Andrews Jamaica, this study interrogates how agencies are negotiated and configured in practice. The findings show how agential cuts or boundary-making practices delineate valid vs. invalid knowledge, temporarily stabiliz ing what counts as credible  or useful; and traditional vs. non-traditional practices, which navigate s the cultural legitimacy of knowledge in relation to traditional epistemologies. This study challenges both technologically determinist conceptualizations of learning that position farmers as passive consumers of algorithmically-curated content, and humanist narratives that center farmers as entirely autonomous decision-makers. By revealing how entangled agencies  and agential cuts  shape everyday learning in rural agricultural contexts, this study offers new insights with both theoretical and practical implications for the design and implementation of mLearning. Contextual background Mobile phone adoption in Jamaica is extremely high (exceeding 100%) and continues to grow as a result of ongoing improvements in digital infrastructure (Poushter, 2016). Amongst smallholder farmers, access to mobile phones is widespread, though the type of device and internet connectivity varies  (K’nlfe, 2012). To leverage this growing access, the Jamaican government and its associated extension agency, the Rural Agricultural Development Authority (RADA), have implemented several mLearning initiatives, including SMS messages, multimedia learning content shared via official social media channels, and a mobile app to build farmers’ capacity in agriculture.

1477 While research on mLearning in Caribbean agricultural contexts remains limited, it is increasingly recognized that farmers all over the world are engaging in informal  learning with mobile devices on platforms like YouTube, WhatsApp, and Facebook (Devesh & Mahesh, 2018). In contrast to formal learning, which is highly structured, institutionalized and instructor led, informal learning is spontaneous, self-directed and embedded in daily life. Informal learning practices often occur outside of institutional oversight, and the sources farmers draw upon are varied, ranging from local peers to international  organizations or influencers. While this self-directed learning creates new opportunities for knowledge sharing and co-construction, it also introduces a tension between ‘ formal’ and ‘informal’ systems of agricultural learning (and complicates this binary). One extension officer from RADA described social media and digital platforms as “a competing extension system.” This sentiment reflects a concern about the accuracy of digital content, the influence of algorithms in determining what information farmers see, and the ways t hat pre-existing beliefs or assumptions might s hape how farmers search for and interpret content.

Conceptual background Digital technologies are not neutral. They contain "built-in and often hidden automated and algorithmic properties that affect how we feel, what is possible to do with our bodies, and what kind of learning and social interaction is possible" (Smythe et al., 2022). Increasingly, scholars recognize that technologies shape learners’ actions, thoughts, conduct, and subjectivities in ways that are often opaque (Williamson, 2015). In agricultural contexts these dynamics are especially significant, as farmers are navigating a rapidly evolving learning ecosystem within a complex context of political and climate precarity. While there is growing acknowledgment of the influence of digital technologies in farmers’ learning, much of the existing research in agricultural contexts is based on technologically deterministic or anthropocentric paradigms. Technological determinism tends to position the device or platform —often driven by algorithms —as the dominant actor in shaping learning. On the other hand, human-centered/humanistic perspectives assume the learner is completely autonomous, minimizing or ignoring the agency of non-human actors such as mobile phones, platforms, algorithms, or environmental forces. Situated within a sociomaterial perspective, this study understands learning as emerging from the entanglement of social and material elements, where technologies, actors, and practices are inseparable in shaping knowledge processes Through the lens of  agential realism (Barad, 2007) learning is further conceptualized as an emergent, relational, and materially-discursive apparatus. Agency is not ‘possessed’ by individual entities; rather, it is produced through intra-actions, defined by Barad as the mutual co-constitution of actors in specific practices and moments (2007). From this perspective,  a learning apparatus is composed of human and non-human actors that are continuously reconfiguring what counts as knowledge  and how learning emerges.  Within this complex apparatus of learning, the concept of agential cuts is particularly relevant. Agential cut refers to the boundarymaking practices through which some distinctions are temporarily stabilized, making others invisible or illegitimate. Agential cuts are no t just a description of boundaries, “they are part of the ongoing reconfiguration of the world” (Barad, 2007). This theoretical framing enriches an understanding of how informal mLearning unfolds in rural agricultural contexts, where farmers interact with a variety of platforms, resources and actors, often navigating conflicting information and epistemologies (Carr et al, 2025). The interviews and observations presented in this paper were conducted as part of a doctoral research project examining learning and knowledge practices amongst  smallholder farmers in Jamaica, including their use of technology in these practices. Fieldwork involved a cohort of 14 farmers from the Mount Salus community in rural St. Andrew’s parish, as well as four farmers from nearby communities who were referred through snowball sampling. Initial connections were made with the support of RADA, which facilitated the introduction to farmers' group, provided key documents, and supported ongoing engagement. In-depth, semi-structured interviews were conducted to explore how farmers access information, address practical farming challenges, and make decisions to improve their agricultural practices. Interviews typically lasted 40–60 minutes, were audio-recorded with consent, and later transcribed  for analysis. We used an iterative coding process for data analysis  with a focus on open coding to identify key themes, practices, and discourses, particularly around themes of learning, knowledge  and agency.The analysis  was further supported by field observations, as the lead researcher participated in and observed a number of farmer group meetings, training events, and informal interactions between farmers and extension officers. O bservational memos helped to enrich and contextualize the interpretation of interview data. This study is primarily grounded in interviews as they capture participants’ narrated moments of judgment and boundary-making (agential cuts), offering insight into how knowledge is evaluated and distinctions are drawn.

1478 The analysis generated two interrelated thematic categories —Entangled Agencies and Agential Cuts —each comprising themes that capture, respectively, the intra-actions between learners, technologies, and communi ty members, and the boundary-making practices through which farmers distinguish valid/invalid knowledge and traditional/non-traditional practices.

Entangled agencies in farmers’ mLearning In anthropocentric accounts of mLearning, humans are often positioned as ‘users’ or ‘learners’, and mobile phones as passive ‘tools’ (Zhao, et al., 2022). However, the interviews with farmers  in this study complicate such a distinction. Mobile phones emerge as active agents in knowledge production, prompting, suggesting, filtering, and surfacing information. Farmers often describe being directed to information by functions of specific apps or platforms. Mr. R described, “You see something pop up, and you start follow ing it” while Ms. D expressed a similar experience saying, “the phone just divert to [the content] and then [I] read upon, and me just read”. Digital prompts, like pop-ups, autoplay videos, and recommended  content, participate in directing farmers’ attention, shaping their pathway through the nearly infinite amount of content available to them. In this intra-action, learning is not solely configured by human agency, it is also algorithmically influenced. While platforms and their algorithms exert considerable influence, farmers are not just passive recipients of content. The agency of the farmer and the phone are, rather, entangled.  Farmers respond to the prompts and cues from the platform and/or device, which in turn further shapes the algorithms. Mr. M explains how he engages with YouTube content, “Since YouTube come about, I'll just go home and let something pop up, but I just go to the video: how to plant banana, how to plant plantain, how to plant yellow yam.” While the platform “pushes” content, Mr. M’s choice to view specific videos  recursively shape s the algorithm itself. By selecting content relevant to his interests and context, he participates in a feedback loop where his actions not only reflect but influence the learning apparatus. His situated interest in specific crops intra-acts with the algorithms in a recursive entanglement of agencies, making the apparatus of mLearning dynamic and fluid. Intra-actions with other humans further complicate and expand the entangled network of agencies at play in agricultural mLearning. Mr. D shared how he recently began using charcoal as fertilizer: “I have a friend nearby... he found it on YouTube and was te lling me about it.” In this case, learning  was shaped by the friend’s intra-action with  platform algorithms; but, the knowledge  in this  initial intra-action, was further configured through the social, human-human intra-action between Mr.D and his friend. Mr. D, in turn, tested this knowledge by applying the technique in his own farming practice, where the land itself also became an agent in the learning apparatus-providing feedback and validation, and further shaping his learning. Similarly, Mr. R described a process of multiple intra-actions in which he first receives algorithmically curated content but  then seeks validation in dialogue with community members: after “googl[ing] it,” he often “asks somebody verbally” to confirm. These examples demonstrate how knowledge is not simply consumed; it is evaluated, contextualized, and shared through entangled intra-actions among st technologies, humans and even natural  environments. Algorithmic prompts generate interest  and direct attention, social interactions shape interpretation, and environmental engagement (for example testing out a new practice on their land) validates the knowledge. Making the (agential) cut: Sifting. interpreting and reconfiguring knowledge Farmers are constantly performing agential cuts, creating boundaries and stabilizing what counts as credible, valid, or useful information. Understanding how these cuts occur is crucial because they determine which knowledges are legitimized or excluded, and ultimately shape the possibilities for action within agricultural systems. The cut to distinguish valid from invalid knowledge often occurs as farmers weigh new information against their own lived experience and expertise. Mr. D describes his engagement with YouTube to “find out how other people are doing things,” but quickly adds, “Some of them make no sense”. Similarly, Ms. C explains how she navigates various sources on Youtube:  “I have different variety of persons that may post something, that they do it this way, or somebody else may do it another way. And, you know, sometimes you can choose which way seems easier, better to you, to work with this”. Both examples reflect an agential cut where the farmer discerns what is credible based on their own experiential knowledge and reasoning. Similarly, Mr. M refers specifically to the importance of context in this ‘sifting’ process:  “I'll see different approaches from different people around the world, and also Jamaicans, how they deal with it. And then I pick up the thing that I think suits my application.” He distinguishes between valid and non-valid, considering his local context and geographic specificity. Farmers also redefine dominant conceptualizations of valid/invalid knowledge through intra-actions with digital platforms  that may resurface previously illegitimate or even illegal knowledge. Despite his formal 1479 agricultural education, Mr. T note s that practices like ganja  (cannabis) cultivation—omitted from institutional curricula—become seen as legitimate  through digital platforms like YouTube: "Now people like me who went through all these agricultural institutions, we didn’t learn anything about cultivating ganja…it’s the one they don’t teach us—because it’s illegal." He recount s finding out about different growing practices and techniques  from videos of other farmers on digital platforms, and then implementing those practices. His experience illustrates how intra-actions between farmers (along with their pre-existing beliefs and experiences) and the digital platform can resurface and legitimize historically invalidated knowledge. Another agential cut occurs along the lines of traditional/non-traditional knowledge. Many farmers in this study expressed a clear preference for traditional, organic farming methods, which they expressed aligns with their values of environmental stewardship and physical health (Carr et al, 2025). For example, Mr. D described a clear distinction – and even opposition-between synthetic fertilizers and traditional organic methods asserting, “I will not use synthetic fertilizer, because I am a firm believer in synthetic fertilizers damaging the soil. What we need to do is to replenish the soil, rather than synthetic fertilizer.” This material-discursive cut between traditional and non-traditional methods also shapes the way that farmers engage with digital platforms and algorithms as they often seek out farming methods that align with traditional values and practice. Mr. T described his process of navigating digital content as initially “magnetizing” information (being drawn to it),but then exercising discernment: “We have to sift through and select from the non-traditional to select the traditional.” The agential cut to stabilize the distinction between traditional and non-traditional knowledge is both discursive (how farmers frame certain practices as traditi onal or non-traditional) and material, as it shapes their actual farming practices, such as the choice to use organic rather than synthetic inputs. While mobile phones offer the promise of nearly infinite access to  content-a “competing extension system” - farmers are not passive recipients  of information. They actively participate in shaping learning, selectively engaging with content, deciding what to trust, and integrating it with their  values and lived experiences. Rather than signaling a loss of human agency, the  concept of agential entanglement suggests a reconfiguration, and echoes calls within posthumanist perspectives to rethink agency as emergent (Barad, 2007). Farmers, in this view, are negotiators within complex systems, simultaneously shaping and being shaped by technologies, social relationships, ecological contexts, and historical/political legacies. What constitutes valid knowledge for farmers is determined by perceived usefulness, contextual fit, and alignment with lived experience and values. The agential cut that differentiates legitimate from illegitimate knowledge emerges within specific relational and material contexts, rather than being dictated by top-down systems of validation  or expert endorsement. Digital platforms do not simply reproduce dominant knowledge structures; they  may also create space for marginalized or suppressed forms of knowledge to reemerge. For example, colonial and legal epistemologies that historically rendered traditional practices, such as ganja farming, illegitimate, become contested as farmers to reengage with these practices  through digital platforms. However, this also raise tensions around assessment, accuracy, safety, and efficacy, especially in agricultural contexts where misinformation can have serious consequence s. These tensions mirror broader concerns in education about misinformation, trust, and the limits of user discernment in unregulated learning environments (Kenedou & Johnson, 2023), underscoring the need to support learners to critically evaluate and interpret diverse knowledge sources.

The findings of this study have implications for mLearning design that align with calls for contextual and socially-just design approaches (Costanza-Chock, 2020; Noble, 2018) and support participatory, contextually responsive forms of mLearning.  Selwyn (2023) suggests that educational technology use should be guided by “shared stewardship of the technology resources and practices that a community decides is appropriate ”. Similarly, Costanza-Chock (2020) asserts that design justice is not just about involving communities in the design process (like asking for farmers’ input into mLear ning design); instead,  it involves “ listening to community organizers, learning what they are working on, and asking what the most useful focus of design efforts would be.” Or, as the author succinctly puts it: “Don’t start by building a new table; start by coming to the table”. This study contributes to that aim by offering a grounded understanding of the current ‘table’ and emphasizing the need for mLearning design to be responsive to the realities of rural communities.
