# The Adventures of Pauline and Paul: How Game Design Elements Can Influence Cognitive Performance Development

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Gamification, that is, the implementation of game design elements (e.g., stories) within learning tasks, has the potential to foster learners’ cognitive performance. However, results regarding the positive effects of game design elements, such as stories, are often ambiguous. This may be due to their adaptivity and, consequently, differences in feedback learners receive within gamified learning tasks (e.g., with stories, in terms of narrative progress). This study presents preliminary results on how implementing a story with different narrative conditions (without narrative, with progressing narrative, or with stagnating narrative) in a cognitive performance task affects learners’ performance development. The results showed no significant differences between the three conditions in terms of cognitive performance development, which is, to some extent, in line with previous research.

## Introduction

Gamification offers the possibility of using different game design elements in learning contexts  that are usually not directly associated with (computer) games (see Deterding et al., 2011). For this purpose, it is, for example, possible to use game design elements such as leaderboards (i.e., ranking systems that indicate learners’ relative success in comparison to other learners; e.g., Crumlish & Malone, 2009) or stories. In contrast to leaderboards, the implementation of stories within a particular learning task is usually less focused on displaying learners’ actual performance (see Sailer et al., 2017). However, it offers a narrative that provides context for individual activities in a learning task (e.g., Kapp, 2012). Although there is a certain amount of existing rese arch on implementing stories in a learning context, their effect on cognitive performance and motivation is, to some extent, inconclusive (e.g., Bai et al., 2022; Sailer & Homner, 2020; Wouters et al., 2013). One reason for th e ambiguous state of research may be that, so far, little attention has been paid to  the adaptivity of game design elements, in general, and of stories, in particular.  Therefore, the effectiveness of story implementation may depend on how a story progresses, which sometimes depend s on players’/learners’ choices or performance  within a gamified learning task. In a different gamification context  (i.e., leaderboards), it has already been indicated that gamified learning tasks are more motivating when the received performance-based feedback is positive (Ninaus et al., 2020). Similar results might also be possible for stories.

Consequently, this study presents preliminary results on how differences in performance-based feedback learners receive based on  a specific game design element  (i.e., story) might affect their cognitive performance. By implementing different forms of narrative condit ions within the story  of several task rounds of a cognitive performance task and measuring cognitive performance development, this study investigates whether interactions between task rounds and narrative have an effect on learners’ cognitive performance.

## Participants

So far, participants were 119 students (age: M = 20.33, SD = 2.59; gender: 77% female, 21% male, 2% divers e) from a German university who participated during a university course and provided written consent. They participated using their own mobile phones or tablets.

Design and independent variable The design was a 3x1 design with narrative being manipulated within the cognitive performance task. All participants were randomly assigned to one of three  conditions: the control group  (without any narrative elements), the progression group (with progressing narrative), or the stagnation group (with stagnating narrative). Narrative elements were presented in text and comic-style drawings. In the progression group, participants repeatedly received positive feedback, indicating that they had completed the story goals. In the stagnation group, participants repeatedly  received negative feedback, indicating that they had not completed the story goals. Feedback was “performance-based” but manipulated by design and, therefore, independent of participants’ actual performance. A treatment check (one item measuring perceived performance and one item measuring perceived performance development scored on a 5-point Likert scale: “I was very good at this task.” and “I have steadily improved myself in this task.”) was implemented to analyze whether participants were actually processing the received feedback and whether the received feedback was considered authentic. Measurement of the dependent variables Cognitive performance  was measured using the Attention Swiping Task (AST;  Koch et al., 2021). The task measures sustained attention by requiring participants to di stinguish between targets (i.e., drawn flowers whos e petals and centers are colored/shaped according to a given rule) and non-targets (i.e., drawn flowers whose petals and centers are not colored/shaped a ccording to that rule)  by swiping up targets and swip ing down non-targets. Flowers had to be swiped one after the other  until the given time limit (i.e., three minutes) expired; a symbol always marked the current position (see Figure 1). The task was repeated for five consecutive three-minute task rounds. Cognitive performance was operationalized separately for all five task rounds by subtracting the number of targets not identified correctly (misses) and the number of non-targets falsely identified as targets (false alarms) from the correctly identified targets (hits).

## Results

Treatment check: ANOVAs with a Tukey-corrected post-hoc test were conducted. The results showed significant differences in perceived performance (F2,110 = 1 4,12, p <. 001, η 2 =. 20) but not  perceived performance development (F2,111 = 0.88, p =.419, η2 =.02) between the three narrative conditions. The post-hoc test showed that perceived performance was significantly higher in the progression group (M = 3.51, SD = 0.91) than in the stagnation group (M = 2.47, SD = 1.08), but not the control group (M = 3.34, SD = 0.74). On a descriptive level, perceived performance development was also the highest in the  progression group, indicating that the manipulation was effective, albeit with some reservations. A repeated measures ANOVA with Greenhouse-Geisser correction was conducted. The results showe d no statistically significant interaction between task round  and narrative condition on participants’ cognitive performance (F3.15,182.63 = 1.17, p =.325, η 2 =.01), indicating a similar cognitive performance development in all three narrative conditions over the five rounds.

## Discussion

Results of the presented preliminary analyses are, to some extent, in line with previous research, as we did not find any differences regarding performance development between the narrative conditions (e.g., Sailer & Homner, 2020). Arguably, this might be affected by the feedback received  being independent of participants’ actual performance, despite the treatment check indicating that the manipulation was largely effective. In the current state, the study is limited by its s mall sample size. It is, therefore, possible that we could not identify  some existing effects. Additional data will be collected  to address this issue. Additionally, participants’ motivation was measured but has not yet been included in the analyses. Overall, the presented results, although limited by their preliminary status, can p rovide initial insights into how research should consider the adaptive nature of stories when investigating their effect on learning.
