# Organism-Level Ideas as Productive Resources in a Student’s Evolutionary Reasoning

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
We present a microgenetic analysis of an interview with a student, Charlie, whose thinking shifts between population-level and organism-level explanations for natural selection. Population-level explanations are typically considered correct, while organism-level explanations have been characterized as containing misconceptions. We argue that this case illustrates how organism-level ideas contributed to productive conceptual dynamics that ultimately supported Charlie in developing a more elaborated and integrated explanation for evolutionary change. Using a combination of knowledge analysis and interaction analysis, we propose that the interview dynamics —which established a shared framing of the conversation as focused on sensemaking— allowed Charlie to activate, examine, and ultimately integrate organism-level ideas into a more coherent explanation for natural selection.

According to fine-grained, dynamic theories of constructivism, learning is an evolutionary process of micro-level refinements and extensions of knowledge systems (Brown & Hammer, 2008; diSessa, 1993, 2002, 2014; Sherin, 2006). As in biological evolution, short-term dynamics tend to be variable and unstable, but ultimately contribute to longer term patterns of change (Svoboda, 2023). For this reason, researchers interested in conceptual change have advocated for the study of short-term conceptual dynamics to  better understand the mechanisms that contribute to conceptual learning in the longer term (diSessa, 2014; Kapon, 2017; Parnafes, 2007; Sherin et al., 2012). Such studies have revealed that learning involves reorganization of intuitive knowledge, meaning that this knowledge often plays a productive role in learning (e.g., Sherin, 2006) contradicting claims that misconceptions block learning (Smith et al., 2014). Yet, empirical accounts of the role of intuitive knowledge in conceptual dynamics are still relatively rare. In this work, we contribute to this line of research by studying the conceptual dynamics of a student's reasoning about evolution by natural selection—a domain that has a long history of study in the conceptual change literature.

Much of the research on students’ conceptions of natural selection has focused on identifying misconceptions proposed to interfere with learning (Bishop & Anderson, 1990; Brumby, 1984; Gregory, 2009; Nehm & Reilly, 2012). Evolution by natural selection is  a population-level process, and prior work has argued that students often incorrectly invoke ideas about change within individual organisms to explain evolutionary change (Ha & Nehm, 2013; Nehm & Ha, 2011).

At the same time, prior work has provided empirical evidence that students’ reasoning about natural selection is context-sensitive and dynamic (Ha & Nehm, 2013; Nehm & Ha, 2011; Settlage, 1994; Southerland et al., 2001). For example, Nehm and Ha (2011) found that students’ written responses are more likely to include canonically correct ideas when students are asked to explain evolution involving a gain of a new function compared with the loss of a function. Interview studies have found shifts between canonical explanations and informal intuitions over the timescale of minutes in response to interviewer prompts and follow-ups (Settlage, 1994; Southerland et al., 2001). In our own prior work we have shown that students switch from population-level explanations to organism-level explanations when they are trying to construct mechanisms that could explain the physiological steps involved in trait change (Svoboda et al., 2025). These patterns make natural selection a particularly interesting domain in which to study conceptual dynamics. In this study, we contribute to this program of work by identifying and analyzing an episode of conceptual dynamics featuring a learner whom we call Charlie. Over the course of a single interview, Charlie switches between two types of explanation for trait evolution: one that aligns with correct population-level explanations of trait gain and one that, at first, appears to align with descriptions of organism-level misconceptions of trait loss evolution. By the end of the 37-minute interview, Charlie provides an elaborated and integrated explanation that can account for both the evolution of trait loss and trait gain. These patterns motivated us to ask two questions:

1. What explains why Charlie shifted between two different sets of ideas?

To address these questions, we conducted a microgenetic analysis of Charlie’s interview attending both to shifts in knowledge and shifts in interactions between Charlie and the interviewer. We argue that a shift into a framing of the interview as about sensemaking contributed to Charlie activating and ultimately integrating organism-level ideas into a coherent explanation of evolutionary change. Rather than function as misconceptions that blocked his learning, organism-level ideas played an important role in the conceptual dynamics that led Charlie to make progress in his sensemaking.

## Theoretical framework: Conceptual dynamics

To study shifts in moment to moment thinking, we draw on Knowledge-in-Pieces perspectives that model thinking as emerging from complex systems interactions (diSessa, 1993; 2014). Sherin et al. (2012) model conceptual dynamics in terms of the activation of fine-grained constellations of knowledge —what the authors refer to as dynamic mental constructs (DMCs). DMCs vary in their stability and coherence. When reasoning about something familiar, a learner may recall a relatively coherent set of ideas. When confronted with a novel problem to make sense of, a learner may search their existing knowledge and assemble explanations (temporary DMCs) in the moment.

Crucially, patterns of DMC construction and use emerge from interactions with the surrounding environment (Scherr & Hammer, 2009; Sherin et al., 2012). In interviews, that environment can include the questions being asked and interactions with the intervie wer that together can align with different interpretations of the kind of activity that is taking place, or the co-constructed epistemological framing (Russ et al., 2012). As Russ and colleagues (2012) propose, interactions that suggest an interview is an examination of a learner’s knowledge can prompt a student to try to recall correct answers, whereas interactions that suggest the purpose of the interview is to explore ideas can support in-the-moment sensemaking. Shifts in interactions and associated framings are mechanisms that explain transitions in the knowledge that a learner is using. Further, it has been proposed that moments of active construction are important for learning. During these moments, as students are working to construct rather than recall, new patterns of activation can arise, linking knowledge elements together or expanding or shifting the contexts of their activation (diSessa, 2014; Kapon, 2017; Parnafes, 2007; Sherin, 2006; Wagner, 2006). In this work, we use and extend these ideas to account for shifts and eventual integration of ideas operating at two different levels of organization: individual organisms and populations.

## Interview data collection

This interview was collected as part of a larger study focused on understanding the conceptual dynamics of students’ reasoning about natural selection. One purpose of these interviews was to intentionally vary the question context by asking students to discuss examples of how natural selection could explain gains of function and losses of function. The interviewer posed the example of evolution of running speed in cheetahs as an example of a gain of function and the evolution of blindness in cave salamanders as an example of loss of function. All other shifts in context emerged in response to examples raised by the interviewee. Interviews were semi-structured, and the interviewer made intentional moves to support sensemaking such as deemphasizing correctness  and encouraging imagination and speculation. Charlie’s interview was video-recorded and transcribed to include pauses (lengths indicated in brackets), filler words (e.g. um), laughter, prosody, gestures, facial expressions, and gaze.

## Analytic approach

We used a microgenetic case study approach to analyze Charlie’s interview (Parnafes & diSessa, 2013). To identify shifts in Charlie’s reasoning, we first broke up the 37-minute interview into episodes, each capturing a included attention to both knowledge and interactions between Charlie and the interviewer. Knowledge analysis Through rounds of collective viewing of the video and transcript we identified the knowledge Charlie activated in each episode (Parnafes & diSessa, 2013). This knowledge analysis phase was informed by prior work identifying core ideas of natural selection that are likely to arise from instruction as well as intuitive knowledge in this domain (Gouvea et al., 2023; Nehm & Reilly, 2007, Southerland et al., 2001). Ideas considered core to a 690 canonical understanding of natural selection include genetic variation, differential success, and inheritance. Intuitive ideas included use/disuse as a mechanism of change—the idea that body parts (e.g. muscles) can change through the repetitive use or disuse (e.g. grow or atrophy) and useful traits increase in frequency—the idea that a trait must have a useful function in order to evolve. Note that like p-prims in physics, these ideas capture abstract biological relationships and cannot be considered correct or incorrect independent of context (diSessa, 1993). We also characterized whether the explanations provided in each episode included mechanisms at the population level (i.e. natural selection) or organism-level (i.e. growth) (Table 1). Interaction analysis We used verbal, paraverbal, gesture and facial expressions to make interferences about shifts in framings of the interview interaction (Gupta et al., 2015; Jorgan & Henderson, 1995; Russ et al., 2012). We also analyzed interviewer moves including specific bids to change topics, to shift or stabilize framings, or to relate to Charlie (e.g. though laughter or affirmation). Our analyses of these interactions helped us characterize interview framings as either situations in which to report knowledge (recall) or  to explore, construct, or refine knowledge (sensemaking).

Over the course of the interview, Charlie and the interviewer discussed different organisms and traits, some of which evolved in the direction of a gain in function and others in the direction of loss of function (Table 1). Across these shifts in context, Charlie’s explanations switched between population and individual organism levels, each composed of different knowledge elements. We now return to the questions of how to explain the shifts in Charlie’s thinking across episodes and his eventual integration of population-and organism-level ideas to account for the evolution of both trait gain and trait loss. Consistent with prior work (Ha & Nehm, 2013; Nehm & Ha, 2011), trait gain scenarios seemed to activate population-level ideas about change by natural selection whereas trait loss scenarios activated organism-level ideas. However, this pattern alone does not explain why the context ual features of trait loss or gain activated different sets of ideas.

Our analysis of Charlie links these contextual features with different epistemological framings. When explaining trait gain, Charlie’s explanation emerged from his recall of a fairly coherent DMC. In contrast, when discussing trait loss, Charlie’s thinking  included evidence that he was engaged in active, in-the-moment explanation construction. During the search for a possible explanation, Charlie reached for everyday ideas about 695 trade-offs that could account for how the body could reallocate resources from one function to another. By examining the micro-level transitions across episodes, we can see how knowledge, context, and interactions contributed to shifts between these two types of explanation. First, it appeared that the idea that useful traits are favorable played a pivotal role in Charlie's application of the population-level natural selection DMC. In episode 1, Charlie mentioned that gain in running speed must have been “favorable” for cheetahs. He did not elaborate on this, perhaps because the reason was fairly obvious to him (i.e., faster cheetahs catch more prey). When the context switched to considering eyes in episode 2, Charlie began by stating that eyes are useful and then expressed confusion over why an organism would evolve to lose something so useful. Without a rationale for why losing eyes would be favorable, Charlie did not continue to activate the rest of the population-level DMC. Moreover, rather than say that the change was “somehow favorable” as he did with the cheetah, Charlie had identified something that “didn’t make sense” to him (“why would you get rid of eyes?”). His attention was drawn more to this confusion than to providing the correct population-level explanation, which he had already demonstrated that he could do. With encouragement from the interviewer to imagine possibilities, Charlie began to activate organism-level ideas, first imagining a bat and then raising ideas about trade-offs in resources and energy using the example of his own body (episode 4), which he used to make sense of how a trait could be physically reduced. Charlie’s exploration of organism-level mechanisms was a response to a shift into sensemaking encouraged by Charlie’s interactions with the interviewer who had been making bids to frame the int erview as about what Charlie thinks and encouraging him to “imagine” what could be happening whenever he was unsure. Charlie, for his part, seemed comfortable taking up this role, sharing his ideas as well as identifying gaps in his understanding.

To address our first question then, Charlie’s shift from population-to organism-level seemed to involve

1) Charlie identifying something that did not make sense in the population-level explanation and 2)

encouragement to make sense of how a trait might be reduced. These shifts led Charlie to activate organism-level ideas including everyday knowledge about how bodies can change through use and disuse. Next, we consider what contributed to the eventual integration of explanations. In episode 4 Charlie activated knowledge of physiological trade-offs to explain changes to his own body. While the idea of a trade-off is useful for population-level explanations about evolution, he had not activated these ideas earlier when asked to explain the loss of eyes. A first step toward integration happened when the interviewer made a bid to think about muscle changes at an evolutionary timescale (episode 5). Shifting to the population level, Charlie looked for a trait’s benefit and was once again confronted with a situation in which loss of a useful trait (hair) did not make sense. When the interviewer offered the “pretend” rationale of overheating, Charlie very quickly filled in the rest of the story and extended his thinking to include bats. The benefit of losing hair due to overheating solved the problem of too much heat. Charlie then proposed a rationale for loss of eyesight that involved solving the problem of too much sensory input.

We propose that Charlie’s use of the analogy was likely supported by at least two mechanisms. First, Charlie had already been thinking about trade-offs at the organism level, making these ideas more available. Second, the sensemaking framing of the interview supported Charlie in seeking connections. Charlie started off the interview bothered by a lack of rationale for trait loss. With an example provided, he was easily able to integrate the rationale into the population-level DMC and then continue reasoning with these ideas by proposing specific mutations that could underlie trait loss (i.e., a slower rate of hair growth).

In conclusion, this case illustrates how ideas that seemed consistent with misconceptions described in prior research emerged from Charlie's efforts to make sense of natural selection and construct a coherent explanation. This work underscores the importance of interactions that support sustained sensemaking for facilitating short-term conceptual dynamics, which can foster knowledge refinement and contribute to longer-term conceptual change. The case also highlights that both Charlie's willingness to raise and explore his confusions and the interviewer's encouragement to engage in sensemaking enabled the sustained exploration that led to a more integrated explanation.
