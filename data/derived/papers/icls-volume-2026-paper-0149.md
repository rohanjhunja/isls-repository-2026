# “They Have to be More Specific, or AI isn't Gonna Get What You Are Saying”: Exploring AI with Children in an Online AI Literacy Workshop

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Children’s growing interactions with AI often occur without an understanding of its underlying mechanisms and potential biases in decision-making. Adopting a socio-constructivist approach, we explore the AI competencies that children (ages 7 –12) developed as they engaged in a five-day virtual workshop. Activities included training AI models with Teachable Machine, producing media with generative AI tools such as Canva, and participating in guided discussions about the societal implications of AI. Findings show that children developed competencies in identifying AI technologies, understanding how AI operates through data, and evaluating AI outputs. These findings suggest that providing opportunities for early hands-on engagement and guided discussion can empower children to reason thoughtfully about how AI works, engage with it in both critical and creative ways, and reflect on its broader societal implications.

Children interact with AI-driven technologies daily, including voice assistants (Alexa, Siri), personalized recommendations (YouTube, Netflix), interactive chatbots, and AI-powered media filters. These frequent interactions shape children's understanding and perception of AI, often without explicit awareness of the underlying mechanisms such as algorithms  and data collection practices that drive such technologies (Charisi et al, 2022; Grimes et al., 2024). Children’s limited understanding in recognizing how AI systems function exposes them to risks including inappropriate or biased content, susceptibility to persuasive algorithms that reinforce stereotypes, and an impaired ability to critically assess the reliability of the output from AI technologies they may encounter (World Economic Forum, 2023).

Consequent to these concerns, there has been global calls for AI literacy in K-12 settings within and out of school environments (Arn & Huang, 2024) in ways that foster a deeper understanding necessary for meaningful engagement and ethical awareness of AI’s role in society. While there have been considerable efforts to promote AI literacy among children (Famaye et al., 2025), research highlights challenges children face in understanding AI concepts, because of the abstract nature of such concepts and limited exposure to ethical ideas such as bias and fairness (Kajiwara & Kawabata, 2024; Crompton et al., 2022). Our study was grounded in the socio-constructivist theory, which argues that children learn and construct meaning through active engagement with tools and their interaction with other people. The theory suggests that engaging children in collaborative, hands-on activities foster deeper understanding and allows them to actively build knowledge in meaningful ways. We adopt a prominent AI literacy design framework “AI4K12 Five Big Ideas” (AI4K12 Initiative, 2019) which outlines five concepts that students should learn about AI including Perception, Representation and Reasoning, Learning, Natural Interaction, and Societal Impact to design how children engage with AI tools. We focused on introducing children to AI concepts and tools through interactive tasks and peer discussions, enabling them to construct their understanding while  exploring their practical and societal implications. Consequently, we seek to answer the following research question: What AI literacy skills do children develop as they engage in a structured, online AI literacy workshop?

## Context

This qualitative study involved a five-day virtual workshop, designed to explore participants' understanding and engagement with AI concepts. The workshop sessions, approximately 60– 70 minutes each, were conducted via Zoom. Recruitment materials, including  program flyers, were distributed to parents through social media channels, targeting interested families. A total of seven children (five girls and two boys) all identify as black and resident in the US participated, with two of the three  researchers facilitating discussions, guiding activities, and providing individualized support as needed. This study received IRB approval prior to the beginning of the study. 1227 Parents of selected participants provided written informed consent, and children gave verbal assent prior to participation. The workshop was structured as a series of interactive sessions, each aligned to introduce participants to the Big 5 AI concepts (Ta ble 2). The researchers leveraged existing open-access AI literacy resources from Day of AI (https://dayofai.org/curriculum/). Drawing on socio-constructivist principles, the workshops were designed to promote collaborative knowledge-building through peer interaction and facilitated dialogue.

In this section, we explain how children developed AI knowledge and skills as they engaged in activities. 1228 Emerging knowledge of AI and AI concepts By interacting with various AI tools and examining how AI systems differ from non-AI ones, children in our study demonstrated early  understanding of how AI operates and makes decisions in the real world. During reflections, one child characterized AI as "a type of generating machine that is used to do a lot of different stuff.”  Another child explained, “AI is...like a robot that you can put data in, like make the robot move...it's a thing that can identify, like pictures, like ChatGPT.” Furthermore, the children reflected on characteristics that define AI, distinguishing it from programmed machines. A child remarked, “The difference between AI and nonAI is that AI is not programmed to do a specific thing. It learns over time with the information it’s fed.” The children deba ted whether Alexa could be considered an AI, eventually agreeing that its ability to “learn,” “communicate,” and “follow orders” aligned with their understanding of AI characteristics. One child articulated this, stating, “Alexa can access and learn from t he newest information, so it is AI.” These remarks illustrate the children’s developing and socially negotiated  ideas about AI, reflecting early conceptual awareness of AI as a dynamic and evolving data-driven technology that can adapt tonew information and contexts. Emerging understanding of how AI operates and relies on data As the children designed their AI models using the teachable machine tool, they developed an understanding of how AI systems learn from data and how their data choices affected the functionality of their AI systems. Reflecting on their experience with Teachable Machine, one child explained the process of training her own AI model, noting that she "had to go and, like, get a lot of data...pictures of dogs and cats," and emphasized the importance of using a test image "completely different" from the training data to avoid simple recognition of a previously seen image. She further used an analogy to explain the need for new data in testing, comparing it to recognizing their mother. Another child further articulated the significance of data quantity and quality, stating that with more images, the AI "keeps the traits in mind" and becomes more accurate in recognizing specific animals. These children reasoned that if the AI were shown the same images it had been trained on, it would merely recognize those specific pictures rather than generalize the learned concept. Creating their own models also allowed children to tinker and assess their models under several conditions while observing how they respond. For example, one of the children observed that initially training his cat and dog classifiers with “one picture each didn’t work.” He increased his training set by adding “four more pictures to each category,” including putting “different pictures of cats and dogs,” which then improved his model capacity. This child’s observation that a single image led to poor classification and his subsequent decision to expand the training set demonstrates an emerging grasp of foundational AI principles like model training and the relationship between data quality and model accuracy. This understanding of how AI learns from data enabled the children to predict how their model might function in different scenarios, with one child speculating: “I didn't add all types of dogs, so [the model] probably won't recognize all types [of dogs].

Producing media with AI and critically evaluating AI output The children’s use of generative AI in Canva for storytelling presented them with opportunities to explore how AI might be able to support their creative ideas as well as assess its limitations. While working in groups, they wrote several prompts, iterating these prompts until the AI-generated media matched their imaginative ideas. One of the children noted they had to be “a bit more specific” in order to get the AI to produce the best result. As they examined each other’s works, the children noticed how their prompting approaches affected the quality of images generated by the AI. One child attributed her group’s success to maintaining consistency in the main characters' appearance across images. She contrasts this with another group’s project, where incons istencies in skin color, hairstyles, and other visual features disrupt the story’s continuity and clarity (Figure 1). To address these flaws in the AI-generated images, she emphasized the need for precise instructions when prompting AI, such as specifying character features (e.g., skin color) and intentionality when using AI output. She explained: “They have to be more specific, or the AI isn't gonna get what you're saying … you also have to pick the same … picture. At least try to have like a girl with the  same skin color … I feel like making it more specific will make it more clear and less fleeting.” This comment demonstrates children’s emerging understanding of how to use generative AI tools effectively for creative purposes. Additionally, it indicates a n understanding of the need to critically assess the quality of AI-generated images and identify potential limitations or errors. ## Emerging awareness of the broad implications of AI in society

As children interacted with AI tools and created their own AI models, they reflected on the potential utility of AI and how AI could have both positive and negative impacts on society. Many of the children noted that while AI can be useful in completing tasks in many aspects of life, becoming “too reliant” on AI can make people become “super lazy.” They also identified broader inequities that could arise from the high cost of many AI-driven systems. One child highlighted that technologies like self-driving cars are “really expensive,” making them unaffordable for many people and thus exacerbating social inequities. The children also raised critical concerns about security and ethical risks associated with AI. They worried about vulnerabilities in AI systems that could lead to hacking, using AI for malicious purposes, and the threat of job displacement due to automation. One child described this as an “assassination on jobs,” reflecting an understanding of the potential economic implication of AI’s ability to replace human labor in various industries. The children further illustrated these anxieties as they addressed the dangers of AI in media manipulation. One child described society as being in “dangerous times” due to AI’s increasing sophistication in creating and altering media, making it harder to discern whether media content is created by humans or AI-generated. This perspective points to an emerging awareness of how AI-generated misinformation could undermine trust and authenticity in media. Their reflections demonstrate the children's growing ability to critically evaluate the broad implications of AI’s integration into society, including accessibility issues, ethical use, and the preservation of human agency. This finding shows that even at a young age, children have the capacity to interact with AI in ways that go beyond passive usage. They can actively explore and utilize AI for creative projects while critically analyzing its functionality, ethical implications, and societal impact. Their ability to recognize and describe examples of AI around them, such as ChatGPT and Alexa, highlighted a growing awareness of the presence of AI in everyday technologies. The children's debate about whether Alexa qualifies as AI indicates an emerging capacity to assess AI’s ability to “learn” and adapt, suggesting that even at a young age, children can reason about AI’s functionality in a real-world context. Similar to previous literature (Ali et al., 2021; Su et al., 2023, Famaye, 2025), our findings highlight how hands-on interaction with AI tools fosters a better understanding of AI concepts. The children demonstrated a more nuanced understanding of how AI learns and makes decisions as they created their own AI models. This was evident in their efforts to improve AI decisions by adding more varied data and being “more specific” in their prompting of AI. These engagement patterns indicate how practical hands-on activities that allow for exploration and encourage creativity, like creating a picture story with AI, candevelop their understanding and critical use of AI. As the children created multiple models and generated different images, they engaged critically with the AI decisions/outputs, identifying errors and inconsistencies and addressing them by repeatedly modifying their data choices and prompts. Moreover, children’s ability to thoughtfully identify and articulate the benefits and risks associated with AI indicates how young children are capable of grappling with the complexities of AI when their engagement with the technology is grounded in social and real-world contexts that emphasize the issues that are evident in children’s lives and environments. Yet children’s speculative remarks about how AI could upend humanity reflect thei r ability to articulate widespread societal misconceptions and anxieties about the technology, underscoring the need to increase early AI literacy among young populations. In sum, our study reveals that providing opportunities for hands-on engagement and guided discussion about AI can empower children to reason thoughtfully about how AI works, engage with it in both critical and creative ways, and reflect on its broader societal implications.
