# How Active User Count Relates to Group Outcomes in Collaborative Programming: An Implicit Scripts Perspective

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Collaboration scripts structure how learners work together by prescribing roles, tasks, and expected contributions. When scripts are implicit rather than explicit, learners decide how to enact them, which can shift the number of active participants and potentially outcomes. Prior work suggests sequential tasks may not benefit from multiple simultaneous actors due to the misalignment with the implicit script. Building on this, we test how the number of active users relates to time-on-task and group performance. Using panel data from three sequential Jupyter-notebook assignments, we estimate within-group fixed effects models to examine early-stage and aggregated activity signals. We find that a ctive user count is significantly associated with both outcomes, while early signals show a modest correlation with group scores.

While group size remains a visible design variable in collaborative learning, evidence converges on the view that patterns of interactional dynamics are the key determinants. Larger groups often introduce coordination costs that depresses engagement and motivation, inviting social loafing (Latané et al., 1979) or free riding (Kerr & Bruun, 1983). However, headcount alone is a weak predictor of outcomes, if not taking into consideration the contextual factors like task structures or technical constraints. What truly matters is how many members are actively engaged within a shared problem space and how their contributions converge meaningfully (Dillenbourg, 1999)—through transactive talk, turn-taking, mutual regulation, etc. (Barron, 2003). In short, the effective size of a group is the subset of members, the active learners, who sustain task-focused, responsive participation. In CSCL, small groups can look very different. Some dyads or triads co-construct on a shared screen or document with continuous discourse; others “split and merge”, parallelizing work with minimal communication. A key differentiator is  the alignment between pedagogical task design with the tool’s affordances. If the task doesn’t require interdependence—or the technology resists it —collaboration collapses.  CSCL tools that provide group awareness, or what Suthers (2006) calls “representational guidance”, such as shared visual references, are therefore essential. Well-designed environments often make collaboration feel natural and effortless. Beyond tools, collaboration scripts address recurring coordination and participation problems by specifying roles, phases, and expected contributions. Scripts convert mere headcount into functional interdependence, increasing the likelihood  that learners engage with each other  rather than silently  parallelize (Kollar et al., 2006). Prior distinctions between micro-scripts and macro-scripts clarify how scripts can reduce coordination costs while fostering transactivity, grounding, and regulation—mechanisms that matter more than size per se for learning (Dillenbourg, 2002; Fischer et al., 2013). Some scripts are intrinsically dyadic because they rely on complementary roles. The ArgueGraph script, for instance, pair s learners with opposing positions, and guides them through a cycle of comparing, discussing, and reconciling views (Jermann & Dillenbourg, 2003). Recent work introduces implicit scripts — structural affordances embedded in  learning materials that guide collaboration without explicit instruction  (Cai, Davis, Tormey, & Dillenbourg, 2025). In computational notebooks, graph-based methods can operationalize task dependencies and surface teamwork strategies. Notably, for notebooks that lack real-time co-editing capabilities but possess sequential dependencies, the effective strategy observed was to open a single notebook and have the team collaborate on one screen—yielding one “active user” at a time. While active user counts helped verify groups’ adherence to the implicit script, the study did not examine whether this approach improved outcomes, leaving a critical question about the pedagogical value of such alignment. In response to this, w e treat this dimension as a latent factor of implicit script adherence and explore its association with time-on-task and group performance. Time-on-task Time-on-task reflects how much time learners actually engage  with learning activities. It has long been used in educational research to estimate learning effort, assess instructional design,  and explain outcome variance (Stallings, 1980). In learning analytics, time-on-task is typically paired  with frequency measures  (e.g., event counts), although it might be operationalized differently depending on the types of trace data. In this regard, 444 Kovanovic et al. (2015) compared time-on-task estimation strategies  and suggested careful method selection. Following recent practice (Gao et al. 2025), we use wall-clock time and active time as our estimates.

## Research questions

In this paper, we address a gap in work on implicit scripts  by testing how the number of active users relates to group outcomes  in collaborative programming. We replicat e prior settings with  panel data from three non-collaborative, sequential Jupyter notebooks —the same context in which  an implicit script encourages a single-notebook workflow. We estimate within-group associations using two-way (group × assignment) fixed effects (FE) regressions with group-clustered standard errors to partial out time-invariant factors. Accordingly:

• RQ1: To what extent does the number of active users in computational notebooks associate with group-level time-on-task and assignment performance?

• RQ2: Can early counts of active users serve as a practical indicator of subsequent time-on-task and group

performance?

## Participants and data collection

The study took place in a second-year undergraduate mathematics course for life-science majors at a European university. In addition to weekly in-person lectures, students had optional lab sessions using Jupyter notebooks. Five group assignments were submitted during these sessions across the semester. Within the university-hosted platform, there was no collaborative version of Jupyter notebooks, so student groups either worked on a single notebook on one device or used separate copies on different devices and merged them later. Of the 187 enrolled students (46 groups: 43 with four members, the rest with five), 147 (78.61%) consented to data collection. Ethics approval was obtained from the university. P articipation was voluntary and had no impact on grading. Groups were included in the analysis only if all members who generated log data had provided consent, resulting in 33 groups being analyzed. An event tracking system  (Cai, Davis, Mariétan, et al., 2025)  was installed for collecting  students' interaction data in Jupyter notebooks, including clicks on notebooks and cells, cell creation and removal, and cell execution with output and status. Group-level scores (0. 00–1.05) for each assignment were also collected. To support reproducibility and replicability, the  panel dataset, documentation, preprocessing scripts, and individual data (presence records and final exam grades) are available at https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.19316212.

To address RQ1, we ran FE regressions using the number of active users —user_count, defined as the total number of users who executed  at least one cell  (rather than just click ed) within the time window —as  the predictor, and the group-level time-on-task and assignment raw score as outcomes. Specifically, we employed two time-on-task metrics: time_login measured the interval between the first login by any group member and the assignment submission, representing the total duration of engagement; time_exec was derived from the first to the last timestamps of cell executions prior to submission, indicating the period of active programming work. Both group and assignment FEs were controlled to isolate the models from any systematic differences across groups or 445 across assignments. Standard errors (SEs) were clustered at the group level to allow for arbitrary correlation of residuals within each group across assignments.

For RQ2, the dependent variables were determined based on the results from RQ1. We divided the 1.5hour programming session into three 30-minute segments  (08:30–09:00, 09:00 –09:30, and 09:30– 10:00) and counted the number of active users within each time window. Within-subject t-tests were conducted to compare these segmented measures with the previously aggregated one, and a bar plot was generated to visualize the overall changes. Subsequently, w e used FE regression models to examine the association s between these factors and outcome variables, assessing their predictive potential by comparing model significance with the RQ1 results. Taken together, user_count  in the early session  (08:30–09:00) appeared to be a  leading indicator of lower group performance, while in later sessions it reflected teamwork inefficiency (i.e., longer timeon-task) more than additional score losses. Building on earlier work on how learners collaborate on sequential tasks through the lens of implicit scripts, our study advanced the analysis by testing how the number of active users relates to group outcomes: time-on-task and assignment performance. Addressing RQ1 and RQ2, we found that: (1) lower concurrent active users are associated with higher group scores and less time-on-task; (2) this same metric, when measured  in the first 30 minutes, provides an actionable predictive signal for these outcomes. This aligns with the prior observations (Cai, Davis, Tormey, & Dillenbourg, 2025): when the task is in divisible, the optimal strategy to prevent delays and underperformance is for the team to collaborate using a single, shared reference. Future work could operationalize early active user counts as a group performance indicator.
