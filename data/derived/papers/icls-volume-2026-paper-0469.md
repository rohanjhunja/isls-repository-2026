# Metacognition, Epistemic Framing, and Knowledge Coordination: A Longitudinal Case Study of Learning in an Intro Biology Course

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This longitudinal qualitative study examines learning mechanisms underlying conceptual knowledge development in a semester-long, high-structure undergraduate biology course. Drawing on coordination class, metacognition, and epistemic framing theories, we characterize how students developed knowledge underlying the core concept of gradient-driven flow (flux), a broadly applicable principle of physiological systems. Findings reveal a reciprocal relationship in which metacognitive actions and sensemaking epist emic frames promote knowledge development, while growing conceptual understanding fosters metacognitive regulation and sensemaking.

By focusing instruction on the core concepts of a discipline, learners candevelop knowledge of scientific principles that they are able to apply across a diverse range of problem-solving contexts (AAAS, 2011). For instance, the core concept of gradient-driven flow (flux) in physiology supports reasoning in many systems of the body from neural signaling to circulatory dynamics (Michael et al., 2017). However, we know little about the learning mechanisms that enable students to structure their developing knowledge of a core concept in ways that support its effective application in varied contexts. Patterns in a learner’s metacognitive actions and their tendencies toward certain epistemic frames likely influence their learning of disciplinary core concepts (Odden & Russ, 2018; Bao & Koenig, 2019). In this longitudinal case study of student learning in a natural setting of an undergraduate biology course, we characterize interactions between students’ metacognition, epistemic framing, and their learning of flux, a core concept in physiology. Interactions reveal learning mechanisms that shape how students develop core concept knowledge in ways that either enable or constrain its broader application. Theoretical frameworks To characterize students’ knowledge of flux, we used the lens of coordination class theory (CCT; diSessa  & Sherin, 1998). A coordination class is an organized but complex system of fine-grained knowledge elements underlying a learner’s ability to “see” and use a concept in diverse contexts. We assert that the principle of flux is a coordination class because it has many different components (e.g., gradients, resistance, rate of movement) applicable across a wide range of contexts (Michael et al., 2017; Doherty et al., 2023a; Doherty et al., 2023b). CCT provides a framework for studying how students reason about the same concept in different contexts via framing knowledge as fine-grained elements activated in a context-dependent manner during problem solving (diSessa et al., 2015). A coordination class consists of a set of “extraction strategies” for recognizin g useful features within a problem context and a “causal net” made up of coordinated knowledge elements activated via recognized features and used to make inferences (diSessa & Wagner, 2005). In this study, we used CCT to characterize students’ flux knowledge to reveal how students learned to use core concepts across a range of physiological scenarios over a semester.

We conducted a qualitative, longitudinal study following two different groups of four students throughout an active-learning undergraduate biology course offered to biology majors. Our goal was to characterize authentic mechanisms of student learning within a natural classroom context at a large, research-intensive university in the United States. We video-recorded the groups working together on all reasoning tasks during each lesson of the Spring 2025 semester. We also collected their in-class work, homewo rk, and exams. These artefacts provided triangulating evidence of flux knowledge development. Using microgenetic learning analysis as an analytical framework (Parnafes & diSessa, 2013), we developed a qualitative coding scheme to document students’ flux 2836 reasoning, metacognition (Schraw & Moshman, 1995; Lobczowski et al., 2021; Stanton et al., 2021; Halmo et al.,

2022) and epistemic framing  (Hammer & Elby, 2005; Scherr & Hammer, 2009; Odden & Russ, 2018). Case

studies of one student from each group (Hazel Group A, Travis Group B) describe interactions between metacognition, epistemic framing, and their developing flux coordination class. Our qualitative analysis identified and characterized all three possible interactions between metacognition, epistemic framing, and core concept knowledge development. The two focal students and their groups differed in their use of metacognitive actions, and they tended to operate under distinct epistemic frames. Sensemaking epistemic frames in which students aim to go beyond presented information to build coherent explanations  could be contrasted with answer-making frames, in which students rely on recall  or authoritative sources to produce satisfactory responses rather than coherent understanding (Odden & Russ, 2018). Differences in epistemic frame and metacognitive actions affected the development of their flux coordination classes, with metacognitive monitoring of gaps in both task and content understanding combined with a sensemaking epistemic frame qualitatively associated with the development of a more robust coordination class. Reciprocally, differences in students’ knowledge of flux influenced their abilities to use metacognitive actions  and sustain a sensemaking epistemic frame when solving flux problems. Hazel  struggled to use previously demonstrated metacognitive actions when tasked with unfamiliar flux problems. Hazel  also struggled to shift into a sensemaking epistemic frame but occasionally did so when their flux knowledge was sufficiently robust. Focal student Travis frequently demonstrated metacognitive monitoring toward both task and content understanding, and this monitoring often coincided with sensemaking. This approach supported his  development of a robust flux coordination class. In several instances, Travis’s flux knowledge also supported his metacognitive monitoring  as he made sense of unfamiliar problem-solving scenarios. Case studies revealed qualitative evidence of interactions between metacognition, epistemic framing, and students’ developing coordination class for the core concept of flux. Our work contributes to a growing body of evidence that suggests scaffolding conceptual resources or metacognition alone may be insufficient for developing core conceptual knowledge that supports broad utility and application without also fostering a sensemaking epistemic frame (e.g., Odden & Russ, 2018). Instructional practices that scaffold this frame emphasize coherent model-building over mere task completion. They also encourage students to draw on authoritative knowledge sources for recall and comprehension, but to engage in generative cognitive work when problem contexts are unfamiliar and/or call for principle-based, mechanistic reasoning.
