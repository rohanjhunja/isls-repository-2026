# From Making Sense to Taking Ownership: Teachers’ Evolving Participation in AI Education Across Disciplines

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study examines how teachers’ participation evolves as they engage in learning about and implementing AI-integrated lessons. Grounded in the Community of Practice framework, this study traces teachers’ movement from peripheral understanding to central contribution within an emerging AI educator community. Findings reveal four forms of participation: disciplinary entry, reframing for disciplinary fit, recognizing the scope and fit of AI practices, and envisioning coherent student learning across courses. These findings show that sustainable AI integration begins with disciplinary anchoring and develops toward ownership and systemic coherence.

The rapid rise of artificial intelligence (AI) has heightened the need for students to develop AI literacy (Holmes et al., 2019; Long & Magerko, 2020). With the integration of AI-based curricula and tools into K-12 classrooms (Ding et al., 2024; Tan et al., 2025; UNESCO, 2021), teachers gain opportunities to cross boundaries between subject areas and technological literacies. AI education research emphasizes embedding AI literacy across disciplines rather than as a stand-alone subject (Holmes et al., 2019; Long & Magerko, 2020; Touretzky et al., 2019), yet this requires teachers to navigate complex challenges as they co-design AI lessons within existing disciplinary frameworks; and existing research focuses primarily on curriculum design and student outcomes, largely overlooking how teachers experience and navigate interdisciplinary shifts (Akkerman & Bakker, 2011; Zhou et al., 2023). To address this gap, this study draws on Community of Practice (CoP; Wenger, 1998), which conceptualizes learning as participation in shared social practice. The CoP framework provides an analytic lens for tracing how teachers negotiate meaning, appropriate shared resources, and ultimately contribute to an emerging AI educator community. This study addresses the following research question: How does teachers’ participation evolve as they integrate AI into disciplinary teaching? Core subject teachers from two public high schools in the United States participated in professional development workshops and then implemented disciplinespecific AI modules in their classrooms. Framed by CoP, findings indicate that AI integration reflects evolving roles and identities: teachers initially recognize AI as “new” or external to their discipline, then reinterpret AI through disciplinary aims, c ollaborate across subjects, and ultimately attend to students’ holistic learning across courses. Our study does not seek to produce a universal model, but rather to add nuance to existing understandings of teacher learning, offering a contextually grounded account that highlights the complexities of cross-curricular AI education and the challenges teachers experience.

## Method

This study adopted a qualitative case study design informed by the CoP framework to explore how teachers’ interpretive processes and meaning-making evolved as they integrated AI into their disciplinary teaching. The study involved two distinct school contexts. The East Coast public high school (Site 1) is a suburban institution serving approximately 1,600 students with a student –teacher ratio of 20:1. In contrast, the West Coast technical high school (Site 2) is an urban, choice-based public institution with an enrollment of approximately 340 students and a 16:1 student-teacher ratio.

Six in-service teachers from English Language Arts (ELA), mathematics (MAT), and history (HIS) (3 – 15 years of teaching experience, TE) participated in the study. To preserve anonymity while maintaining contextual clarity, participants are identified by subject area and site (e.g., HIS-1). The professional development (PD) program followed a multimodal design, including individual and group workshops, discipline-specific and cross-disciplinary sessions, and both synchronous and asynchronous components adapte d to teachers’ schedules. Five teachers fully implemented the discipline-specific AI modules. In ELA, learners use a Markov Chains tool to explore AI text generation; in History, feature extraction and visualization tools support learners  analysis of 1687 grading and mortgage risk; and in Mathematics, learners  examine model weighting using Yelp text data. HIS-1 (3 years TE) was unable to implement in his own classroom due to scheduling constraints but supported implementation in ELA-1 (14 years TE) and MAT-1 (11 years TE) classrooms. Participants had strong disciplinary expertise but limited prior knowledge of AI literacy or its application in K-12 settings. This study employed an interpretive, iterative qualitative design to trace teachers’ participation trajectories across PD, classroom implementation, and reflection phases. Data collection included approximately a total of  15 hours of video/audio-recorded PD sessions, structured implementation teaching logs, classroom recordings from the eastern site only (district policy prohibited in-class recording in the western site). Following implementation, each participant engaged in a 60-minute semi-structured refl ective interview. Analysis was grounded in the CoP framework, moving from the construction of longitudinal analytic case narratives to a crosscase comparative process. This recursive analysis focused on how teachers negotiated meanings and adapted AI curriculum within their specific disciplines, ultimately surfacing four developmentally related forms of participation: (1) approaching AI through disciplinary entry points, (2) reframing activities for alignment, (3) recognizing cross-subject fit, and (4) envisioning coherent student learning across courses. Interpretive credibility was maintained through ongoing peer debriefing and recursive data triangulation, prioritizing the developmental coherence of teacher trajectories over rigid code structures.

Our findings identify four interrelated layers that characterize teachers’ roles evolved as they engaged in discipline-specific AI modules. In this phase, they align unfamiliar AI activities with existing pedagogical frameworks, often relying on PD facilitators for guidance. This stage reflects a dual identity as disciplinary experts and novice AI learners. Second, teachers reframe AI as part of their discipline, integrating it into existing curricula and practice. As PD progressed, greater facilitation and familiarity with StoryQII curriculum modules enabled teachers to interpret AI through disciplinary lenses, seeing it as integral rather than additive. ELA-1 reflected: “I haven’t really thought about AI. One thing that we’re working on with 10th graders...we’ve been trying to figure out ways to remediate students who are behind. So if there are any ways that we could use it to help students, that would be great. And not just for those behind, but those who are doing fine – we want them to go from okay to great. ” Teachers begin to align AI with their pedagogical goals, reframing it as a tool to support inquiry, differentiation, and student growth rather than as an abstract technology. Third, teachers begin to see AI as a cross-disciplinary bridge extending beyond their own subject areas. This shift reflects a broader perspective, as they move from discipline-specific focus to viewing AI as a shared framework for collaboration across subjects such as mathematics, ELA, and history. Through these interactions, teachers develop new understandings of how their expertise connects with others’, fostering interdisciplinary partnerships. During implementation, participation extended beyond individual lesson planning to collective coordination and reflection. Informal interactions (e.g., HIS-1 mentioned hallway conversations) became key spaces for exchanging ideas and aligning instruction. Teachers from Site 1 shared in the group interview: “I give all the credit to [colleagues] because each time we meet in the hallway, we talk for about five minutes – how can we put things together in preparation for the upcoming lessons” “It was a lot of coordination.” “They [teachers] did a lot of work on the ground... The connection they created and how they were talking to the students...there was order and the students were cooperating.” 1689 This reflection captures a shift toward collective practice, where teachers coordinated efforts through ongoing informal communication. Such interactions reflect distributed expertise and reduced disciplinary boundaries. The observed student cooperation indicates that this alignment translated into meaningful classroom outcomes. Finally, teachers reimagine students’ holistic learning across interdisciplinary contexts with AI. At this stage, boundary crossing becomes generative, as teachers collaboratively design learning experiences that transcend disciplines and support cross-domain connections. This shift reflects a broader reconceptualization of both teaching and interdisciplinary learning through AI integration. As teachers from Site 2 explained: “There were some engaging activities like the Markov chain...but I think it would be better served in our computer science class as a type of career.” “For students to realize that data analysis is a career field would have been better served.” Other teachers similarly recognized how disciplines contribute to students’ understanding of AI. For example, HIS-2 (10 years TE) noted increased student motivation when AI tasks connected to math or computer science, while a math teacher emphasized the relevance of linking data and programming to students’ futures. These reflections mark a shift toward a student-centered, career-oriented view of AI as a distributed learning experience. However, teachers also identified structural constraints: scheduling, rigid course pathways, and uneven participation that limited implementation. Although only a few teachers articulated this systemic, studentcentered orientation, these reflections represent the trajectory’s conceptual endpoint, revealing a capacity to envision AI literacy as a distributed, cross-disciplinary practice centered on coherent student learning. At early stages, teachers’ engagement through disciplinary lenses highlights the productive role of disciplinary identity. Rather than constraining integration, disciplinary framing functioned as an interpretive resource, allowing teachers to make sense of AI through familiar pedagogical practices. This finding extends work on disciplinary sensemaking (Putnam & Borko, 2000; Grossman et al., 2001) and teacher adaptation (Ertmer & Ottenbreit-Leftwich, 2010; Kim et al., 2013) by showing that sustainable AI integration begins with anchored interpretation, not abstract interdisciplinarity. This suggests that AI-focused professional learning should deliberately leverage disciplinary expertise and examine how distinct epistemologies shape teachers’ interpretations of AI.

As participation deepened, teachers moved beyond disciplinary anchoring to consider how AI practices fit within and across subjects, ultimately envisioning more coherent student learning trajectories. This extends AI literacy frameworks (Long & Magerko, 2020; Touretzky et al., 2019), which often conceptualize learning within standalone courses. Our findings instead highlight teachers’ emerging systems-level perspective, where AI learning is distributed across disciplines and shaped by multiple epistemic lenses (e.g., ethical reasoning in humanities, modeling in mathematics). This points to the need for frameworks that conceptualize AI literacy as distributed and multi-perspective. Future research should examine how such cross-disciplinary designs support conceptual transfer and the cumulative development of AI literacy over time.

In conclusion, these findings advance a developmental account of becoming an AI educator. AI integration is not simply the adoption of new content, but a progression from disciplinary sensemaking to shared ownership of AI-integrated practice and its coherence across subjects. By showing how teachers negotiate meaning and build shared repertoires within an emerging community of practice, this study underscores that sustainable AI education depends on professional communities that support this progression tow ard agency.
