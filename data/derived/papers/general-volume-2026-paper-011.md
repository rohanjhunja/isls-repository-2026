# Integrating a Conversational Agent into PBS KIDS Media for Interactive STEM Learning at Home

**Conference:** ISLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Interactive media can be effectively enhanced through advancements in artificial intelligence (AI). Through a research–practice partnership with PBS KIDS, we integrated AI into popular PBS KIDS shows to support dialogic interactions that actively engage children in STEM learning. These interactions help ground abstract STEM concepts in socially meaningful experiences. In this work, we present an interactive web app designed to scaffold children’s learning and engagement at home. We discuss design considerations, challenges related to children’s speech recognition, and insights from iterative testing that informed the app’s current stage of development.

Extensive research has shown that interactivity is a major driver of children’s learning and engagement (Ewin et al., 2020; Richert et al., 2011). For example, educational video programs targeted toward young children, such as Blue’s Clues and Dora the Explorer, primarily use pseudo-interactive conversation, where the main character asks the child a question but cannot respond directly. An alternative approach for providing dialogic interactions is the “Wizard of Oz” approach where researchers themselves will adjust responses to children through a “fake” media character (Calvert et al., 2020). Advances in AI now make it possible for tools to support live, dialogic interaction between children and educational content. Early studies suggest that such tools can lead to significant learning gains (Xu et al., 2024), but much of this research has been conducted in highly controlled lab settings, with few being conducted in schools or homes (He et al., 2025). This limits our understanding of how to design or implement interactive, dialogic tools in real-world contexts. Since a majority of children’s interactions with media take place at home, there is a critical need to investigate how AI-integrated tools can support children’s learning and to determine how these tools can be designed to foster learning in naturalistic home settings. As part of an NSF-funded project in partnership with PBS KIDS, we developed a web app that allows children to engage with interactive educational media in home settings. Although the primary goal of the project is to evaluate how children learn from interactive media in comparison to non-interactive media, the design choices for this web app and children’s engagement with them could potentially address design challenges in early childhood. One issue that can arise is with automatic speech recognition (ASR) systems embedded into the web app. Research has found that ASR systems lag in accuracy for children in comparison to adults, due to their divergent speech patterns and higher acoustic variability (Bhardwaj et al., 2022). Past studies found that these systems are satisfactory in understanding children’s speech in lab settings (Xu et al., 2024), where the environment is quiet, and the child is typically the only person speaking. However, this performance is more challenging to maintain in home environments, where varying acoustic conditions and background noise may complicate speech recognition. One study examining the performance of ASR systems in classrooms reported a high overall error rate of 84%, with most errors resulting from words being deleted by the system (Southwell et al., 2022). Additionally, the learning content from PBS KIDS is intended for children between the ages of 4 and 8, and designing technology for this age group can be difficult. Children this young are still developing foundational language, cognitive, and self-regulatory skills (Hirsh-Pasek et al., 2015). They may have limited reading proficiency, shorter attention spans, and difficulty following multi-step instructions, which makes it crucial that the design is developmentally appropriate, as well as beneficial for their STEM learning outcomes. Thus, it is important to consider how to design these conversational systems and apps to adapt to the constraints of children’s everyday settings.

T heoretical framework Learning through conversation is rooted in Vygotsky’s sociocultural theory (1978), which emphasizes that children learn through interaction with more knowledgeable others. Within this framework, we draw on three core Vygotskian principles: (1) learning as a socially mediated process, (2) language as a primary tool for thinking and meaning-making, and (3) the role of guidance in advancing children’s conceptual understanding beyond their current level. Dialogic interaction supports children in constructing meaning and internalizing concepts. In our design, AI enables the media character to take on the role of a “more knowledgeable other” by engaging the child in guided back-and-forth dialogue. The conversational agent (CA) prompts reasoning, encourages elaboration, and provides gentle corrective feedback, using language as the primary medium through which children engage with and refine their understanding. These interactions are embedded in PBS KIDS media that already hold social and emotional meaning for children, helping them ground abstract STEM concepts in their lived realities. Along with integration into a home watching web app, we build on these principles to supplement the social learning taking place naturally in children’s home environments. We adopt a pragmatic approach that draws on dialogic interaction as a mechanism for engaging children, while also emphasizing the role of guidance in shaping and extending conceptual understanding. The CA is therefore designed not only to elicit children’s ideas, but to direct attention toward key STEM concepts through targeted prompts and feedback. We position Vygotsky’s theory as a guiding lens for interaction design rather than a direct operationalization of specific constructs, and note that this reflects an applied interpretation of his work within educational research.

Conversational agent design Interactive versions of two PBS KIDS shows, Elinor Wonders Why and Lyla in the Loop, were developed for the web app. Both shows are science-focused and designed for preschool and early elementary children aged 4-8. Each educational episode lasts approximately 11 minutes and covers topics such as molting, aerodynamics, or step-by-step problem solving. For example, one episode of Lyla in the Loop teaches sequencing and following steps as Lyla bakes cupcakes for her sisters’ birthdays. Children learn to understand and anticipate the correct order of actions, which aligns with core computational thinking skills, including algorithmic thinking and task decomposition.

Using ASR systems, the main character (Elinor or Lyla, depending on the show) can recognize and categorize children’s verbal responses and provide feedback tailored to their input. Each episode includes 10 to 11 conversational moments, in which the character asks questions to gauge understanding or prompt reflection on the concepts introduced. Figure 1 illustrates the dialogic flow of these interactions where: the character introduces a question, waits for the child’s response (or non-response), offers scaffolding or encouragement as needed, and ultimately delivers appropriate feedback and the correct explanation. All the questions and responses provided by the character were developed based on the Next Generation Science Standards (National Research Council, 2013), along with feedback from PBS KIDS’ science learning advisory board. Figure 1 Dialogic Flow of the Conversational Agent I nteractive media web app design For the initial prototype, the web application was developed using Node.js and informed by feedback and observations from prior studies within the broader project. The application was optimized for consistent performance across phones, tablets, and desktop devices on major operating systems to reduce technological barriers. OpenAI’s Whisper model was used for real-time speech-to-text transcription, while intent detection was performed by OpenAI’s GPT-4 to classify children’s responses into predefined categories.

To address research questions concerning children’s interactions and engagement with the CA, video and audio recordings of sessions were collected and securely stored using AWS S3. However, they were used solely for research analysis and were not required for the real-time functioning of the application, as audio input was streamed directly to Whisper for transcription during the interaction. These recordings were crucial for evaluating the accuracy of the ASR system for children, but the recordings could be deleted at any time upon request from participating families.

The prototype underwent several rounds of internal usability testing with children and research team members to refine the interface design, interaction flow, and overall user experience. Designed with families in mind, the interface features a bright and colorful visual scheme, large clickable elements, and simplified navigation. Following deployment to participating families, iterative refinements were made based on user feedback and technical reports. Through these cycles, several key themes have emerged, though we also welcome feedback and collaboration from the ISLS community beyond these: ● Accessibility and reliability: Some families expressed frustration with how the app functioned in their home environments (e.g., excessive bandwidth use or lagging on older devices). To address this, we split recordings into smaller clips that are incrementally uploaded to the database, reducing bandwidth demands and improving reliability across different devices. ● Clarity through visual cues: Families also noted that it was difficult to track how much of the study their child had completed without closely supervising them. In response to this feedback, we added visual indicators such as progress bars and completion counters so parents could easily monitor their children’s progress.

● Transparency and privacy: Families expressed concerns about when the app was “listening” and how recordings would be used. To address these concerns, we added a camera and microphone setup screen before each episode to clearly communicate when recordings occur, their purpose, and how families can request data deletion (Figure 2).

As the ISLS community spans several disciplines and identities, we welcome feedback from anyone who has an interest in or experience with designing learning tools, including other researchers or practitioners. In addition, the insights gathered from this interactive session can inform the ongoing development of our web app and prepare it for future dissemination through the PBS KIDS network. Beyond improving our own tool, we also hope to contribute to knowledge about how to design effective, research-based learning technologies and to advance the field’s understanding of how CAs can support children’s engagement and learning at home.
