# A Systematic Review of Approaches for Building Adolescents’ Geometrical Understanding

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This systematic review aimed to identify prevalent approaches and tools for building geometrical understanding of adolescents. 54 articles from two educational psychology databases were reviewed. Guiding learning theories and pedagogical approaches are mapped to targeted geometry topics and types of interventions. Overall, the learning theories being applied to the design of geometry instruction are scattered. The results show an inclination towards the use of Dynamic Geometry Software (DGS) for a range of geometry topics, due to the many affordances they offer that support geometrical understanding. The Van Hiele Model (VHM) of geometric thinking is found to be the most prevalent framework for building geometrical understanding due to its geometry-specific structure. However, the VHM is not often used to design digital learning environments for geometry. Future research should focus on studying the design and effectiveness of VHM-based learning environments for building geometrical understanding of adolescents.

## Introduction

Geometrical thinking is known to play a n important role in developing adolescents’ educational credent ials, achievements, and occupations, especially in STEM a nd related fields (Wai et al., 2009).  However, geometrical thinking of students is often found to be underdeveloped (Uttal et al., 2013). To address this concern, researchers for many years have studied the design and effectiv eness of interventions that aim to build the spatial abilities of students, but most research in cognitive science is  generally targeted at children in primary grades (Simplicio e t al., 2020). Studies on effective approaches for building spatial and geometric thinking in adolescents are scattered (Jones & Tzekaki, 2016). Due to the abstract and complex nature of middle and high school mathematics, a mix of approaches that support visualization of concept s, develop critical thinking and hands-on activities have been used in the past (Swanson & Williams, 2014). Additionally, given the wide range of topics and concepts that form the middle and high school geometry curriculum arou nd the world, little is known about the theories and approaches that can be adopted to design effective interventions that build adolescents’ understanding of geometry (Sharma, 2019). Hence, it is crucial to identify th e key affordances, features, and theoretical founda tions of approaches that have been used to  build students’ understanding of the various topics in mi ddle and high school geometry. The research question guiding this review was: “Irr espective of the medium used, which effective approaches are prevalent for building geometrical understanding of adolescents?” Purpose The purpose of this systematic  review was to  identify prevalent effective approaches for building geometrical understanding of adolescents (13+ years). It aimed to recognize the pedagogical approaches and  underlying learning theories, mediums, and design of intervent ions found to be effective for building geometrical understanding. The research question guiding this review was: “Irrespective of the medium used, which effective approaches are prevalent for building geometrical understanding of adolescents?”

## Method

A systematic search of literature was conducted acr oss two relevant databases in Educational Psycholog y, PsycInfo and ERIC. Peer-reviewed articles tagged with the subject heading “ geometry” or containing the words “geometr*” or “spatial” in their title were searched.  We included the term “spatial” in our search since spatial ability is a foundational skill for geometry learning and is known to be a target of many interventions at the middle and high school level.  The results were then combined with any peer-reviewed articles tagged with terms associated with the target age group  (high schools, junior high schools, middle schools,  secondary education, adolescen*, teen*) in the subject heading using “AND”. No restriction on publication date was applied while searching in any of the two databases.  372 unique articles were found after searching the two databases. The inclusion criteria were: (a) articles on experiment al studies testing ways of building geometrical und erstanding and (b) articles with adolescents (13+ years) as the target population. Articles with all or some participants at the age of 13 and above were included in the study. Gen erally, 13-year-old children are enrolled in grade eight (OECD, 2019). As such, in cases when  the grade level of the participants was reported but  the age of the participants was not reported in the article, those with all or some participants in grade 8 or higher were included in the review. The exclusion criteria were: (a) articles on studies measuring factors like motivation, engagement, attitudes, etc. but not geometrical understanding, (b) articles discussing students’ approach towards geometry problems, geometry classroom discourses, teachers’ role in geometry classroom, etc. but not discussing  ways of building geometrical understanding of students, and (c) arti cles in languages other than English. It is a five-level, hierarchical framework of thinking levels with increasing levels of complexity (Hiele, 1999). The five levels are visualization, analysis, abstraction, formal deduction, and rigor (Usiskin, 1982). It was applied through different types of approaches (digital and non-digital) and for all geometrical topics but construction and  mensuration, topics that rely more heavily on proc edural knowledge, application of formulae, and algorithmic approach. The second most prevalent approaches were those foc using on visualization (n = 4). Given the abstract nature of the discipline of mathematics, visualizat ion is a key aspect to enhance learning (Elia et al., 2018). It relies heavily on the visual appearance of mathemat ical objects to identify similarities, differences,  properties, and relationships. Specifically, visualization is a fundamental skill required to understand spatial geometry topics (e.g., density of number line) which further inform s higher level topics (e.g., measurement and approx imation). As such, a visualization focused approach was used to build fundamental spatial geometry skills in three articles. It was delivered using different types of interventions including hands-on manipulatives, videos, and DGS. Procedural knowledge forms a large part of mathematical learning and curricula (Rittle-Johnson, 2017). It involves following a series of steps or applying an algorithm to solve a problem. Worked examples help students understand the step-by-step process of solving a problem on a specific topic by reducing w orking memory load. Three articles used a worked examples focused approach through video lessons and printed materials. The topics targeted were mensuration and plane geometry. These  topics need stepwise application of formulae to compute dimensions (e.g., areas, volume) of shapes and solids, that can explain the use of worked examples approach. A semiotic approach involves and relies heavily on the use of symbols, gestures, and signs (Palayukan et al., 2020). It was applied to build understandin g of target topics involving diagrams and coordinat e geometry. These were the topics of construction and functions  that require not only studying and understanding d iagrams but also creating them. Two of three articles that applied a semiotic approach used DGS for the topic of construction and studying functions. All DGS utiliz e a graphical user interface (GUI) with icons and t ools for constructing geometrical shapes and studying their behavior. This also aligns with the semiotic framew ork by Dimmel and Herbst (2015) that discusses the several semiotic systems of geometry diagrams including their type, position, prominence, and attributes. This framework refers to the significance of the several aspects of a geometry diagram like the use of a dot, stroke, their weight, transparency, color pattern, etc. These affordanc es are offered by all DGS, hence their use in the case of articles applying a semiotic approac h. The VHM, use of visualization, worked examples, and semiotic approach were the four theories highly aligned with the goals and demands of the discipline of geometry. The other five theories and  approaches discussed in the next paragraphs are di sciplineagnostic and do not address specific aspects of geometry learning. Constructivism posits that individuals acquire knowledge through their experiences and social discourse (Piaget, 1973). One article applied constructivism to teach plane geometry using DGS. It can be argued  that due to the nature of their interactive, student-centered design, and multi-modal environment, all DGS are fundamentally grounded in constructivism (Vincent, 2002). Additionally, constructivism was applied to two other formats, Virtual Reality (VR) environment and interview-based teaching, both for teaching spatial geometry. Two articles applied the Piagetian theory of adolescent  intellectual development. These were not entirely grounded in constructivism, but only discussed the “formal oper ation” stage of development as identified by Jean P iaget. Hence, they were categorized as being separate from  those coded for constructivism. Formal operation s tage of development betwe en ages 11-14, emphasizes the focus on logical thinking over p erception. Both approaches made use of concrete objects to help students ident ify geometrical properties and relationships.  Cognitive load theory emphasizes reducing working memory load and calls for instructional activities to avoid activities that do not contribute to learning (Paas et al., 2004). It was applied in the context of VR which is known to cause a sensory and mental overload, hinder information processing,  and fatigue (Souchet et al., 2022). It was also ap plied to hands-on manipulatives-based approach. Both articles applied cognitive loa d theory to the topic of spatial geometry. Applying cognitive load theory in the con text of VR can be explained by “ability-as-compensator” hypothesis (Mayer, 2001) especially for low-ability learners as they can gain benefits from vis ual and dynamic representations since they do not have to dedicate cognitive resources for such additional tasks.  Problem-based learning groups students to solve real-world and contextual problems to achieve a learning objective (Allen et al., 2011). It can be used to demonstrate the utilitarian aspects of mathematical topics and how they apply to the  real world. Hence, it was applied in two articles for the topic of 3-D solids and spatial geometry, topics that can be directly associated with the understanding of the spatial nature of the three-dimensional real world. Cooperative learning requires students to work together in pairs or groups in a structured learning activity towards a common goal or learning objective (Dörnyei & Ushioda, 2011). As a task-driven approach and not a concept-driven approach, cooperative learning can be applied to de sign interventions for any target topic. However, i t was also applied in two articles  for teaching plane geometry through video lessons a nd an alternate method comparing student learning individually versus those learning in small groups. Overall, we found a mix of discipline specific (geometry) and discipline-agnostic theories and approaches being applied to d esign interventions to enhance geometry understanding of adolescents.

## Conclusions

In this systematic review, we found that the effective approaches and learning  theories for building geometrical understanding of adolescents are scattered and numerous. The Van Hiele Model (VHM) o f geometric thinking was identified as the most prevalent learning theory applied to creat e effective interventions for building geometrical understanding of adolescents. The VHM is a geometry specific learning theory, def ined specifically to address the requirements and the learning object ives of the discipline. Additionally, the use of Dynamic Geometry Software (DGS)  is the most prevalent approach for building geometr ical understanding of a range of topics. DGS offer unique affordances that support geometrical learning and hence are a popular tool in a geometry classroom. However, we also found that VHM was not applied to design geometrical interventions  in p opular mediums using digital approaches. For example, approaches using video lessons and virtual reality did not apply the VHM in their design. The results identify that discipline-specific approaches were more prevalent in literature thandiscipline agnostic approaches. They could add ress the specific ways of thinking and conceptualiz ation associated with geometry.
