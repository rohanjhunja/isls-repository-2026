# How Thinking Routines Shape Cultures of Thinking: An Exploratory Cross-Case Study in EFL Higher Education

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This exploratory cross-case study examines how thinking routines cultivate a culture of thinking across two university EFL courses —Critical Reading and Movies discussion. Data included students’ written reflections, classroom observations, and focus-group interviews. Thematic analysis results identified four cultural elements: (1) teacher guidance and emotional support, (2) peer interaction and a supportive ethos, (3) structured routines fostering thinking, and (4) confidence and comfort in expression, which pointed to the need for both cognitive scaffolding and cultural mediation in EFL higher education.

In an era of rapid change and uncertainty, thinking remains a core competency for future learning and work. The Future of Jobs Report 2025 (World Economic Forum, 2025) ranks analytical and creative thinking as the top core skills valued by employers. Similarly, the OECD Learning Compass 2030 framework (OECD, 2019) positions thinking—including cognitive, metacognitive, and transformative competencies —as essential for navigating complexity and acting responsibly. Yet while global education increasingly emphasizes teaching for thinking, implementing such pedagogy in Asian English as a foreign language (EFL) classrooms faces contextual challenges. Yuan et al. (2022) identified persistent constraints from exam-oriented cultures, fixed curricula, and students’ passive learning habits. In Taiwan, similar challenges were found in secondary English education (Chang, 2019), where teachers support critical thinking but struggle to integrate it in teaching practice. Among various pedagogies designed to foster thinking, Visible Thinking (Ritchhart et al., 2011) stands out not only as a strategy for guided reasoning but also as a cultural builder that cultivates shared norms of inquiry (Ritchhart, 2015). Developed by Harvard Project Zero, it uses structured thinking routines to make reasoning visible and to enculturate thinking as a collective classroom practice. Each routine consists of a short, repeatable sequence of prompts that target specific thinking moves, such as  explaining, connecting, and considering viewpoints, thereby guiding learners toward purposeful, reflective thinking. Functioning as structured yet flexible tasks, thinking routines scaffold both individual and collaborative reasoning processes. Ritchhart (2015) has proposed eight forces that shape cultures of thinking, while other scholars have linked such cultures to collaboration, class norms, supportive climate, and inclusivity (Alexander, 2020; Mercer & Littleton, 2007). Empirical studies in Asian EFL contexts have applied thinking routines as pedagogical interventions and reported positive impacts on students’ engagement and reasoning. However, they also reveal contextual constraints rooted in exam-driven traditions and hierarchical classroom cultures, which often limit dialogic interaction and reduce students’ confidence in expressing ideas (Dajani, 2016; Khalid & Foo, 2021; Dass, 2017; Manurung et al., 2022). While existing research acknowledges these barriers, it often overlooks how thinking routines function as cultural mediators to foster peer support and equitable participation, thereby enhancing learning in an EFL classroom. This study therefore investigates how thinking routines and classroom interactions jointly shape cultures of thinking in Taiwanese EFL university classrooms.

## Context and method

This qualitative, exploratory cross-case study (Creswell & Poth, 2018) examined how thinking routines foster a culture of thinking across two 16-week Taiwanese EFL university classes (a reading class of 27 students and a movie discussion class of 19). Both courses intentionally embedded thinking routines, such as See– Think– Wonder and Connect –Extend–Challenge, into issue-based discussions to guide interpretation and prompt perspective-taking. Data sources included student reflections, focus group interviews,  classroom observations, and student artifacts. Following Braun and Clarke’s (2022) six-phase framework, data were thematically analyzed using NVivo 14. I first analyzed each case individually to identify key themes, then conducted a cross-case comparison to synthesize recurring patterns. Trustworthiness was established through data triangulation and thick description. Finally, serving as both the instructor and researcher positioned me as an insider. Teacher guidance and emotional support were critical in establishing emotional safety and sustained engagement. Teachers’ responsive questioning, scaffolding, and encouragement helped students overcome initial anxiety toward open-ended discussion and risk-taking in English. Students described the classroom as a safe and trusting space, where ideas were welcomed rather than judged. This affective foundation served as the entry point for deeper intellectual engagement.

Peer interaction and a supportive ethos further reinforced this culture. Through the use of thinking routines and small-group discussions, students experienced mutual recognition, empathy, and respect for diverse viewpoints. Over time, they shifted from merely responding to prompts toward collaboratively building ideas. Such reciprocal participation reflected a move from individual thinking to interthinking (Mercer & Littleton, 2007), where collective reasoning became a shared classroom norm. The affective relationship bond also provided important emotional support.

Structured tasks and repeated use of thinking routines provided a clear cognitive pathway that anchored this process. Routines such as See –Think–Wonder and Circle of Viewpoints guided students to observe closely, interpret meaning, considering multiple viewpoints, and reasoning with evidence. These routines externalized thinking, supported systematic inquiry, and encouraged perspective taking. Over time, thinking routines functioned not only as scaffolds for reasoning but also as cultural tools (Ritchhart, 2015) that brought thinking to the forefront and shaped participation patterns and communicative norms. Confidence and comfort in expression emerged as both a product and a driver of this evolving culture. As discussions became more exploratory and supportive, students’ anxiety about language errors diminished. They reported feeling “more natural,” “not afraid to speak,” and “more able to share real opinions.” Increased fluency, confidence, and willingness to contribute strengthened dialogic participation and sustained deeper interaction, creating a positive feedback loop between affective safety and cognitive engagement. In Taiwanese EFL contexts —where students are often hesitant to speak for fear of mistakes —this finding is particularly meaningful. It highlights the role of thinking routines and a supportive ethos in providing a safe, nonjudgmental environment where language learning and thinking can flourish together. This study explored how thinking routines foster a culture of thinking in Taiwanese EFL university classrooms through an exploratory cross-case design. Four interrelated elements consistently emerged across both classroom contexts: teacher guidance and emotional support, peer interaction and a supportive ethos, structured tasks fostering thinking, and confidence and comfort in expression. Together, they illustrate how a culture of thinking is co-constructed through the interplay of task design, teacher mediation, and peer relationships, enabling students to engage in exploratory, reflective, and collaborative dialogue.
