# Science at Work: Science and Learning among Coffee Roasters

**Conference:** ISLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
For more than a century science education has been guided by a promise that schools will prepare students to use science in everyday life. Yet little research has examined how science is actually used in everyday life. My dissertation takes this gap seriously by studying science and learning as they occur outside of school. Drawing from extended ethnographic fieldwork, I analyze how professionals in the coffee industry use, teach, and learn science in their daily work. Goals of the research My dissertation has three goals: First, to document how science is used outside of laboratories, which have long defined what counts as science. Second, to analyze how learning unfolds through the embodied, material, and social practices that constitute such work. Lastly, to translate those findings into principles for designing science learning environments. Taken together, these goals address a long-standing gap in science education and learning sciences research: the disconnect between the things learned in schools and those used in everyday life. My dissertation argues that reimagining science education-and education more generally-requires renewed attention to the diverse settings in which science and learning occur.

Goals of the research My dissertation has three goals: First, to document how science is used outside of laboratories, which have long defined what counts as science. Second, to analyze how learning unfolds through the embodied, material, and social practices that constitute such work. Lastly, to translate those findings into principles for designing science learning environments. Taken together, these goals address a long-standing gap in science education and learning sciences research: the disconnect between the things learned in schools and those used in everyday life. My dissertation argues that reimagining science education-and education more generally-requires renewed attention to the diverse settings in which science and learning occur. Since the late 1800s science education has aimed to prepare students for life after school (Rudolph, 2023). The most recent of such reforms-the Framework for K-12 Science Education (NRC, 2012)-continues this tradition. My dissertation responds to two issues in this research and design paradigm. First, the forms of science that people use in everyday life are not the forms of science practiced by professional scientists (cf. Aikenhead, 2006; Eisenhart & Finkel, 1998; Irwin, 1995; Wynne, 1995). Despite these differences, how people use science in everyday life has received very little attention, with the majority of "how science happens" being defined by professional scientists (Bang et al., 2012; Warren et al., 2020). The result is that designs for science learning environments are based upon professional scientists' activities because their everyday analogs are underrepresented. The second issue stems from the first: Because everyday settings have received less attention, how learning occurs in these settings is also underrepresented (Nasir et al., 2020).

Stated from another angle, one that is slightly more critical but hopefully generative: One would assume, if K-12 education aims to prepare young people for life after school, that research on their lives after school would be a priority. This has historically not been the case. Instead, the bulk of science education research examines learning that happens in schools, among young people, organized by professional teachers, and measures what these groups do against the activities of scientists. My dissertation takes these issues seriously. It does so by studying people outside of schools and professions typically recognized as scientific. I chose to study people in the coffee industry as representatives of the teaching, learning, and science that happen in everyday life because my background as a barista prior to graduate school sensitized me to the forms of science that occur across the industry. My dissertation develops takes these forms seriously and uses them to expand images of science and learning. My dissertation draws from video-based fieldwork that I conducted at multiple field sites (Becker & Geer, 1957; Emerson et al., 2011; Erikson, 2006). Since 2022 I've conducted over 600 hours of video-based fieldwork, primarily at two Chicago companies, focusing mostly on the practices of coffee roasters. Additional data come from national events, farms, and learning environments I've co-designed. The corpus includes audiovisual recordings ofroasting sessions, including five multi-year apprenticeships, quality control sessions (e.g., tasting coffee), and classes we've taught; fieldnotes and analytic memos; semi-structured interviews; and artifacts such as data logs, graphs, and instructional materials.

I use three analytic approaches to answer three research questions. Briefly before elaborating below: First, I use a deductive coding approach (Saldana, 2021) that borrows the Science and Engineering Practices (SEPs) and Disciplinary Core Ideas (DCis) from the Next Generation Science Standards (NGSS Lead States, 2013) to identify how roasters use recognized scientific practices; second, I conduct i nteraction analysis of audiovisual data (Hall & Stevens, 2015) to examine roasters' practices on their own terms, including the teaching and learning of those practices; lastly, I develop a novel approach to studying learning-as-becoming by using membership categorization analysis (Sacks, 1992; Fitzgerald & Housley,

2015) to trace how apprentices move from novice to recognized expert status through talk-in-interaction.

Together these methods link fine-grained analyses of moment-to-moment activity with coarse-grained representations of developmental trajectories across years.

## Data gathered and preliminary findings

RQJ-How do coffee roasters use science? Across hundreds of video episodes, I detail how roasters' practices align closely with the SEPs, yet also extend beyond them. Roasters, for instance, form hypotheses, select tools, decide on variables, collect data, and revise procedures based on new evidence. Crucially, these investigations integrate the human body as an analytic instrument: roasters smell, taste, touch, listen to, and visually inspect every coffee they work with. RQ2-How do roasters teach and learn their craft? Learning to roast coffee is organized through apprenticeship arrangements in which novices first work shoulder-to-shoulder with experienced roasters before starting independent work (Davey & Stevens, 2024). Early in their apprenticeships, teachers scaffold the learning environment so apprentices coordinate an increasing number of information sources, including the body's senses and workplace instruments. Once apprentices can produce "good-enough" roasts-after only three or four days of training-they are expected to work independently, make mistakes, and reflect on those experiences with colleagues. This brief and intense period of learning complicates fundamental research in the learning sciences that depicts apprenticeships as unfolding over many years. RQ3-How can these findings inform learning design? To translate insights from ethnographic fieldwork into the design of learning environments, I co-designed and co-taught a series of workshops with coffee professionals at Northwestern University. Students designed and carried out experiments to produce the "perfect cup," balancing quantifiable variables (temperature, brew time, extraction) with sensory ones (flavor, aroma, mouthfeel). Preliminary analyses of video (44 hours) and survey data show that students developed alternative conceptions of experimentation and began to view science as something they could do with their own bodies, not just with lab instruments. These workshops demonstrate how designing disciplinary practices based on extended fieldwork in understudied sites of science can reach similar outcomes valued by reforms.

Expected contributions My dissertation documents scientific practices in non-laboratory workplaces. By constructing continuities between everyday and professional practice, my work challenges long-standing hierarchies about who counts as a scientist and what counts as science.

Second, my findings invert an assumption in science and science education: that sensory information is subjective and unreliable (e.g., Varelas et al., 2021). Coffee roasters demonstrate that perception-seeing, tasting, smelling, hearing, and feeling-is not opposed to scientific reasoning but fundamental to it (e.g., Jaber & Hammer, 2016). This part ofmy dissertation has implications for how science educators value bodily experiences and for designing learning environments that integrate the body with more normatively recognized scientific instruments. Third, few learning sciences studies trace development across years in naturally occurring settings. By weaving video data and extended fieldwork together, my dissertation advances an approach for studying learning as it unfolds across extended timescales and sociotechnical systems. Fourth, translating findings from roasteries to designed environments advances design principles for the learning sciences by arguing for designs that: treat the body as a legitimate way of knowing; encourage investigation of the everyday; and connect canonical concepts to the practices through which people make sense of the world.

Lastly, by showing how science exists and flourishes in workplaces, my research offers educators ways to help students see themselves as participants in science and seeks to reconnect disciplinary education with the diverse, lived experiences of people's everyday lives.
