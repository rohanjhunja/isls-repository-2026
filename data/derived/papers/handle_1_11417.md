# Multiple Tiers of Responsiveness in Professional Learning Facilitation: A Call for Praxis in Design-Based Research

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper explores the authors’ facilitation of a week-long teacher professional learning (PL) workshop focused on adapting teachers’ curriculum materials to support students’ epistemic agency (EA). Most literature on supporting students’ EA has focused on the shift of epistemic authority from teacher to students. However, little work has captured what this shift requires of teachers beyond “giving up” of their own epistemic authority. We present findings from a workshop in which our PL facilitation required responsiveness to teachers’ ideas, ideologies, and vulnerabilities. In particular, we showcase two moves of facilitator vulnerability that marked transition points from cycles of deficit talk and resistance, towards teacher-initiated expressions of their pedagogical responsibilities and strategies for supporting students’ EA. We discuss implications for integrating studies of facilitator praxis into design-based research.

## Introduction

Teaching in a way that meets the increasingly sophi sticated demands for complex student learning requires ongoing learning on the part of teachers (Darling-Hammond & Oakes, 2021). The Learning Sciences has increasingly embraced a focus on teachers as learners (e.g., Goldman et al., 2022). However, notably absent from these accounts is examination of the role of a teac her learning pedagogy. In other words, how does the action of teaching or facilitating teacher professional learning impact the version of the designed learning environment for teachers that becomes enacted? And how do various enacted te acher learning pedagogies influence processes of teacher learning? This paper presents a close examination of moments in which three facilitators made their own vulnerability visible, leading to ideological pivot points in teachers’ discourse. Our research and PL design is grounded in scholarship in the field of science education and the learning sciences on re-distributing students’ epistemic agency (EA) in sci ence classrooms (Stroupe et al., 2019). This vision is a radical departure from science instruction in which students engage with science ideas in ways that are largely confirmatory and performative (Windschitl et al., 2008). We view the goal of supporting students’ EA as a crosscutting thread that should be present throug hout a teacher’s science instruction regardless of the specific lessons, units, or curriculum materials used. As su ch, we view teachers as continually selecting, inte rpreting, adapting, and enacting instructional materials to t ransform instructional contexts. We aim to cultivate teachers’ adaptive expertise for instructional design: how th ey leverage their knowledge of the school, policy, and local sociopolitical context in which their students live to craft instructional contexts that empower students as epistemic agents. We do this by explicitly allowing teachers to grapp le with whether, why, and how particular pedagogical practices support students’ EA and the challenges t hat might be associated with enacting them. Our foc us on epistemic agency also assumes that teachers might N OT view current instructional practices commonly ta lked about as “NGSS-aligned pedagogies” as “good” or something they wer e capable of enacting; and thus we intentionally set up space for teachers to surface and grapple with the tensions that emerge when shifting towards these pedagogical practices as well (e.g., Penuel et al., 2023). We view the aforementioned grappling as inherently ideological in that it asks teachers to elicit thei r implicit models about how schooling and science teaching should work. There are multiple, competing ideological models even within the context of “NGSS-aligned” science instruction (Table 1). Our point h ere is not to argue for one vision over another, but instead to highlight how a term like “epistemic agency,” even when well-defined, can imply a broad range of potentially contradictory concrete instantiations in practice depending on the (implicit) assumptions one holds about what schooling, school science, and science knowledge are all about. These varying ideological visions matter because they shape what we might look for, and thus aim to support, as EA. Thus, PL focused on supporting students’ EA is inherently ideological and critical and therefore requires a different kind of facilitator attention and reflection thandoes PL focused on adopting “best practices” or specific teaching techniques or skills. Richards (2023) describes this as responsiveness in PL: “Just as teachers are asked to attend and be responsive to the substance of the ir students’ thinking, PL design and facilitation c an attune to and responsively pursue multiple ways into intended forms of instruction” (p. 490). Moreover, critical ideological responsiveness requires “ critical self-reflection, resisting binaries, and enacting ontolo gical and epistemological plurality” (Carter-Andrews et al., 2019, p. 2) on the part of facilita tors that mirrors that which the PL aims to support in teachers. This is a form of facilitator praxis. Following an emerging tradition of responsiveness a nd facilitator praxis in PL (e.g., Gresalfi & Cobb, 2011; Seltzer, 2024), we present an analysis of two key facilitation moments. The research question guiding our analyses is: How, and how well, did our facilitation of PL discussions allow for grappling with ideological tensions about science and schooling? ## Methods

The study presented here follows a “praxis-infused” design-based research approach. We initially set out to examine how the design of a PL workshop impacted teachers’ (a) expressed ideological stances, and (b)  curricular adaptations aimed to support students’ epistemic agency in whole-class discussions using the typical practices of design-based research: conjecture mapping, multifaceted da ta collection to explore complex claims, and data-informed iteration on designs (Barab & Squire, 2016). However, as we  began working with the data, we recognized the significant role that we as facilita tors played in shaping the ideological tenor of the workshop. Thus, we focused our empirical analysis on our own facilitation. This study context is a partnership with 11 middle and high school science teachers in one dist rict, “Metropolis,” one of two main districts in a small urban communit y in the central Midwest United States.  The data for this study come from a summer workshop hel d in July 2024, the start of our second year workin g with Metropolis teachers. Teaching experience of participating teachers ranged from just finishing a teacher education program to having 30 years of experience with an average of ~12 years of teaching experience. Our PL approach followed a curriculum-linked strategy, situated somewhere between curriculum-based PL (Short & Hirsh, 2022) and PL focused on specific pedagogical strategies or problems of practice. We leveraged teachers’ curriculum materials as tools and resourc es to concretize and experiment with visions for in struction, but focus ed on engaging with the ideological stances that under gird these practices. Entering into Year 2, we assumed both curricular variation and ideological plurality amongst our teacher participants with respect to what it might mean to support students’ EA. We also decided to focus on a Scientists’ Circle discussion as the adaptation that we would ask teachers to make. Our rationale w as that having a similar instructional routine  might help facilitate cross-talk between teachers who were using different curriculum materials. During the summer workshop, we recorded video from two stationary cameras and audio from each small group for the duration of the workshop. We also collected p hotographs of artifacts produced by teacher participants or during whole-group discussions and kept a log of how we revised the agenda each day. To analyze the data with regards to expressed ideologies, we u tilized a computational grounded theory approach (N elson, 2020), leveraging computational tools to augment grounded qualitative  analyses. In our c ase, we used semantic similarities between sentences/phrases of interest to locate similar sentences/phrases throughout the data corpus. Because of our interest in teachers’ deficit talk about students, we first identified utterances or phrases with either asset-oriented or deficit-oriented implications, such as “The more ownership a student has, the harder they tend to work” (asset-oriented) or “But then there are students who I rea lly struggle to get to engage, they just feel totally checked out” (deficit-oriented). We transformed those sentences into embeddings using SBERT se ntence transformers (Reimers, 2019)  and identified semantically related instances across the data corp us. We then visualized where these instances occurred and identified “hotspots”: moments with a high density of either assetor deficit-oriented talk. Using an  informed grounded theory approach (Thornberg, 2012), we analyzed these “hotspots,” drawing on the schooling-and-science ideology elaboration (Table 1) as an initial coding scheme and open-coding segments that did not clearly signal an obvious “named” ideo logy but communicated something about teachers’ stances towards students and schooling. One discussion during the PL workshop emerged as particularly rich in regard to both the overall density of codes and the shifts in patterns of codes during the discussion. This discussion reflected a gradual but sustained shift from teacher talk reflecting neoliberal ideologies of schooling (Table 1, row 1)  to teacher talk reflecting collaborative ideologies of schooling (Table 1, row 2). In addition, a new code, facilitator vulnerability, preceded each new “hotspot” of collective ideology coding. Given our focus on understanding how our facilitation impacted these ideological shifts, we present in detail two episodes containing these facilitator vulnerability codes.

## Findings

An unplanned discussion at the beginning of Day 2 m arked a key transition point in how teachers talked  about their students vs. their own responsibility as teachers. This discussion came about based on our end-of-day debrief about Day 1. In the afternoon of Day 1, we engaged teachers in a community asset-mapping activity. We provided a map and asked teachers to identify areas outside school (a) where students wanted to be; (b) where t hey saw their students thriving; and (c) where they saw learning happening. We then intended to leverage this knowledge of students to think about discussion facilitation.  While teachers were generally engaged in this activ ity, we felt that it did not tap into the depth of student experiences t hat we had hoped, and as such, making the link back to facilitating discussions was challenging. The disco nnect was evident in teachers’ exit ticket reflecti ons from that day. In response, we decided to begin Day 2 by talking about the link more explicitly. Stina (Author 1) facilitated this Day 2 conversation, re-framing the link between the two activities and ask ing teachers to adopt a stance of empathy for students: first, what might make discus sion feel unsafe or risky for students? And then, w hat was it about the spaces identified in the asset mapping activity that may make those spaces safer? Stina intended for this conversation to be brief. However, it lasted over an hour. It was also incredibly challenging to facilitate, with Stina reflecting on  feeling like she was constantly trying to “pull te achers back” from deficit-oriented talk about students. Yet, about halfway through the discussion, these deficit-oriented responses diminished. We highlight two pivotal mome nts of facilitators (Stina, Barbara, and Kevin) showcasing their own vulnerability that immediately preceded the shifts in teacher talk. Moment #1: Barbara apologizing and asking for a “re-do” of her PL facilitation move This moment began with an otherwise non sequitur co mment made by Barbara. In order to understand this moment, it is important to know that Barbara had heard one teacher, Gloria, talking about an ide a in her group during a small group discussion. As part of the small group share out, Barbara had asked Gloria to share her with the whole group, which she did. Jumping forward to the identified moment (facilitator vulnerability moment #1), Barbara apologized for putting Gloria “on the spot,” noting that she especially should not have asked her to share “cold” given that we all, facilitators and teachers, were working through something hard and uncertain. As she described this, Barbara gestured to the board where Stina had taken lots of notes about student uncertainty and vulnerability in discussions. Stina then noted that while asking Gloria to share her id ea seemed innocuous to her at the time, the current discussion was showcasing to her the kind of “culture change” in science educ ation that the NGSS is all about, including small shifts with big impacts that it requires of facilitators. Barbara then “re-did” how she should have asked Gloria to share her idea: by letting her know that she had a really great idea and asking her privately first if she would be willing to share. Gloria interpreted Barbara’s repeated question as genuine and again shared her idea about students being off topic in small groups. In response, several teachers chimed in about strategies they used to address “off topic” talk in  ways that are not punitive but meet students where they are at. In this way, Barbara’s move to “redo” her own facilitation in a way that s he thought would be more sensitive to Gloria’s vulnerability as a learner seemed to encou rage a series of successful shared examples of how teachers supported students. This series was a marked shift from teachers focusi ng primarily on how teaching strategies did not work despite significant effort on their part.

Moment #2: Vera and Stina share emotional challenges around parenting experiences Following immediately from the first moment, Vera d rew a parallel between building routines for studen ts and how she built routines for her 19-year-old son. Her son played baseball, so she described that she and her son had gone to the baseball field “a thousand times.” When  he went by himself for the first time, she was com fortable letting him go because they were both confident he could navigate the space successfully. Stina responded with a similar story about her 5-year-old son. She had dropped him off at summer camp for the first time a few weeks prior: his first time at camp, in a new city, and a new state. Most notably, they did not let parents in–a big change from preschool! She expressed anxiety about how he just “ran through the awning and was gone.” Vera immediately said, “But because you have done things  in the past and set him up to be successful, you’v e walked him through similar things, then he knew, OK, first  I need to do this, and then I’ll do this.” Vera no ted that both their children had had “someone they trusted to gui de them” through previous experiences that helped t hem navigate new territory. Although brief, this connec tion to parenting was significant in two ways: (1) Vera, as a more experienced parent, responded to and reassured  Stina as she shared her anxiety as a more novice parent, creating a reversal of epistemic authority; and (2)  in doing so, Vera articulated a responsibility and  role for the teacher as similar to that as a parent: a “trusted guide” for students through new and un certain territory. A few minutes later, when Uma started slipping into defic it talk about students not paying attention even wi th endless reminders of routines, Vera responded by describing  the importance of collective routines: if everyone knows what the routine is, it’s a team effort to help eac h other remember. Several teachers then shared exam ples of the kinds of procedural routines they used in their classrooms and how they established them. Kevin then jumped in, articulating a limitation of purely procedural routines: students might still feel like they “don’t know what to do” even if “instructions” are clear, especially if the expectations for what counts as an acceptable answer in school sc ience were shifting. He noted that he found himself  as a graduate student doing something that his students had also done: asking if something was “right,” not because they thought there was a definitive correct answer, but because they wanted to be affirmed that they were “on the right track.” Kevin’s reframing of what it meant to “not know” shifted th e uncertainty from the procedural to the intellectual: students might be unsure of what kind of answer or response counts as a right answer, esp ecially when the “game” of schooling has shifted. This led the group to differentiate between procedural and intellectual routines, recognizing that students might need additional types of routines, and teachers might need to establish new kinds of practices, in order to support students in uncertain knowledge-building discussions. In summary, Stina’s vulnerability about parenting led Vera to rearticulation of the power of routines and establishing them as central to a teacher’s responsibility. This led to Vera to push back on another teacher’s (Uma) return to deficit talk, a move th at was successful at guiding the conversation back to productive strategies. This, coupled with Kevin’s critique leading to a differen tiation of intellectual and procedural routines, re sulted in a conclusion to a conversation in which teachers had identified, broadly, and element both within their locus of control and within their felt responsibility as tea chers that they could focus on establishing in orde r to support student discussions that followed a “new vision” for science teaching and learning.

## Discussion

We described in this paper two examples in which instances of PL facilitators expressing vulnerability about their own facilitation were followed by marked differences in teachers’ talk. Specifically, the vulnerability expressions marked a shift away from deficit talk about student s and towards articulations of providing expectatio ns and routines for students as a trusted guide as a key p edagogical responsibility. Convergence around this idea, accomplished by teachers pushing back on each other s’ deficit statements about students as they surfac ed, led to a focus on establishing intellectual routines as a strategy for enacting new science pedagogies. This is not a novel idea in and of itself. We are not claiming that the generation of this conclusion is the contribution here. Instead, we argue that this conc lusion was actually not obvious for teachers; and that making progress towards shifting instructional practice wa s accomplished interactionally, mediated by facilitators' expressions of vulnerability. In light of this, we advocate for the integration of methods into design-based research that have been used to study teacher educator pedag ogies, including self-study (LaBoskey, 2004) and autoethnography. These methods orient teacher educators and teacher learning facilitators around self-improvement, including “reframed thinking and trans formed practice” (ibid, p. 859) in order to underst and the educational processes at play. While we did not exp licitly adopt a such a method in the present study,  we are currently exploring how reflective praxis can be integrated throughout our PL projects (Suárez & Crabtree, 2025). We advocate for the exploration and sharing of thes e and similar methods within the Learning Sciences community as we strive to better and more fully support educators—including ourselves—as learners.
