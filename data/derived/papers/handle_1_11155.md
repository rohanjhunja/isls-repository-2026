# Of Barrios and Favelas: Blending Criticality and Imagination Through Freire-Inspired Utopian Workshops

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
How might we design learning environments that are imaginative enough to go beyond the constraints of reality while sufficiently grounded on the challenges of one's lived experience? This study explores speculative and utopian pedagogies, encouraging students to envision alternative scenarios to current oppressive situations while ensuring a solid understanding of the complexities of inequality and oppression. Central to this research is how educators can integrate criticality and imagination under a speculative civics education paradigm. Drawing inspiration from Brazilian educator Paulo Freire, this study employs a Design-Based Research approach to create a civic imagination workshop, refining it through successive iterations with adolescents in a barrio in California and a favela in Rio de Janeiro. The findings offer robust design principles to guide educators in crafting interventions that blend critical and visionary thinking for marginalized youth.

## Introduction

Solid and resilient democracies rest upon an informed citizenry capable of critiquing societal issues and dreaming about alternative scenarios. Traditional school-based civic and social justice education, however, often falls short of addressing the diverse needs of today's youth, o ften relying on instructionist pedagogies focused o n factual knowledge and standardized assessments and limited to government and electoral issues (Lawy & Biesta, 2006). On one side of the civic education spectrum, approa ches rooted in the Critical Pedagogy movements of t he 1970s reemerge, claiming an education rooted in a structured critique of today's social dilemmas. On the other side of the spectrum, emerging scholarship emphasizes the impor tance of additional skills, literaci es, and dispositions for youth to engage with complex social issues and envi sion potential solutions critically. Speculative and utopian pedagogies, two examples of such new approaches to civic and social justice learning, invite students to elaborate diverse, multiversal scenarios as alternatives to current situations of oppression (Garcia & Mirra, 2023). A central question this study sought to answer is h ow educators working under a speculative civics education paradigm might reconcile criticality and imagination in their programs. How might we design mechanisms and processes in a learning environment that are imaginative enough to go beyond the constraints  of reality while sufficiently grounded on the vicissit udes of one's lived experience? To answer these que stions, this study resorted to the writings of Brazilian educato r Paulo Freire. While often verbal izing that a search for utopia was the ultimate goal of education (Freire, 2014), Freire did not develop the hows of imagination and social dreaming in his pedagogical approach. Instead, Frei re dedicated his theory to helping learners and edu cators develop solid critiques of their limiting situations (i.e., situations of oppression that limit one's capacity of being).

Using the works of Freire as a starting point, this study adopted a Design-based Research Approach (DBR) in which a civic imagination workshop was designed and refined in four consecutive iterations. For thi s task, adolescents were recruited in a barrio of Santa Ana (California, USA) and a favela of Rio de Janeiro (Brazil). The findings are described as design principles aimed a t helping educators develop interventions that blend criticality and imagination for marginalized youth.

## Background

Schools often ask students to reimagine society and  the world they want to live in. But what is imagin ation? The act of imagining involves mentally developing scenario s and representations beyond what is perceived by t he physical senses (Gotlieb et al., 20 18). However, looking at things “as if they could b e otherwise” (Greene, 1993, p. 225) cannot be equated with a romanization of aspects of social  life nor be devoid of solid social critique. This section is focused on what has been written about the tensions and possibilities of criticality and imagination. Denunciation and annunciation Paulo Freire's (1970) pedagogical approach transcen ds traditional unidirectional education to promote a dynamic process where learners actively build knowledge, cultiva te critical awareness, and question hierarchical po wer structures. Freire posits that learning should not be organized around teaching scripts but on supporting students in moving away from a culture of silence – or a lethargic acceptance of one’s conditions – into a culture of participation. This approach prioritizes dialogue, reflection, emp athy, and the active involvement of learners in shaping their comprehension of the world. In Freire 's often poetic language, literacy should move beyo nd reading and writing the word to become the act of reading and writing the world. Although Freire's work focused on providing adult illiterate workers with the tools and means for reading social complexities and oppressive situations, his pedagogy and practice has consistently exhibited pronounced utopian characteristics (Roberts, 2015). While Freire often employed the term utopia in his writings and teachings (for example, see Freire, 2014), he did not envision a world devoid of imperfections or social struggles. As reported by Roberts (2015), Freire often refrained from bein g an idealist but remained a realist thinker, conce rned with a notion of utopia that was not merely confined to wi ld imagination but attainable and worth fighting fo r. In other words, the Freirean utopia lies always within one’s reach. Two complementary elements exist in the core of Freire's search for utopia: denunciação (or "denunciation", in Portuguese) and anunciação (or "annunciation", in Portuguese). Denunciation involves critically analyzing historical and extant socioeconomic condi tions to understand the structures and ideas that h ave led to numerous limiting situations. It means becoming wide awake (Greene, 1993) and acquiring critical consciousness to describe – and relate to – social struggles and denounce injustices. On the ot her hand, Annunciation represents a response to oppression in its various forms: it embodies a solid sense of hope, but, under any condition, suggests passive dreaming. Annunciation  is an active move, crucial in challenging present circumstances, and essential against the pragmatism and complacency that so frequently characterize the school ethos. Together, both concepts work dialectically: denouncing the injustices of today while announcing the within-reach realities of tomorrow. Speculative pedagogies Speculative pedagogies are an expansive set of idea s that transcend curricular boundaries and focus on visionary and future-oriented approaches to teaching and learning. Such pedagogies provide educators and learners with tools and environments to devise alternatives of collecti ve thriving beyond existing horizons guiding policy, research, and practice in the field (Garcia & Mirra, 2023). E xamples of speculative pedagogies involve world-building and collective dreaming, aiming to manifest social justice and equity by challenging established social, economic, and cultural arrangements. Speculative pedagogies take a multiversal approach, encouraging deep interrogation, innovation, and collective action towards other realities that "can and do exist" (Garcia and Mirra, 2023, p.17). Speculative pedagogies have deep roots in social dr eaming and imagination. Garcia and Mirra (2023) posit that speculative pedagogies include an expans ive set of futuristic and visionary methods of teac hing and learning that start with dreaming about liberatory alternatives to capitalism, gender inequality, patriarchy, and other related forms of oppression. Different approaches i nvite learners to consider alternative futures and dystopian worlds. Nooney and Brain (2019), however, offer a critique of speculative practices: pedagogies that rely solely on reimagining futures might incur an “aestheticization of the dystopian” (p. 221). Moreover, these approaches often focus on a designer’s values and views and “rarely demands a confrontation with the inherent narrowness of one’s own experience.” (Nooney & Brain, 2019, p. 222). Consequently, speculative future casting might lead to gadgets, scenarios, and experiences that build alternatives but fail to question structural norms and beliefs. Civic imagination While focusing on contemporary problems might make it hard for learners to see beyond immediate constr aints, pedagogies organized around pure make-believe must provide opportunities for developing s tructural critique. Nevertheless, how does this tension between imagination and criticality play out within civic learning? The education and communication fields present a ha ndful of experiences where imagination and criticality – often separated – are used complementary as avenues for civic learning. Jenkins and colleagues (2020), for instance, suggest a pedagogical approach based on Civic Imagination, “the capacity to imagine alternatives t o current social, political and economic conditions (...) even if those alternatives tap the fantastic” (p. 5). Their approach, highly contingent on popular culture and everyday representations of life, contends that und erstanding that change is possible is necessary for civic engagement and participation. This definition implies that individuals cannot contribute to shifting current societal conditions without imagining what a new society (or an aspect of it) might look like. Similarly, Gutierrez (2008) argues for developing sociocritical literacies, which “privileges and is contingent upon students’ sociohistorical lives” (p. 149). Viable middle points between criticality and imagination within the realm of civic learning is precisely what this study sought to produce.

## Methods

The main goal of this study was to understand what features of a design and imagination-based pedagogy might contribute to civic learning that blends criticalit y and imagination. The study adopted a Design-Based Research paradigm (DBR) (Hoadley, 2002), an interventionist approach concerned with understanding learning phenomena by testing multiple design iterations in real-world conditions. DBR is a research paradigm that u tilizes design to “address theoretical questions about the nature of learning in context” (Gutierrez & Jurow, 2016, p. 5 66). A DBR study often involves multiple iterations, with researchers reporting data collected between such cycles and narrating each design move and rationale (Hoadley, 2002). Ite rations are key methodological features of DBR, all owing researchers to make theoretical and practical claim s about interventions while they are being designed  and implemented. In each cycle, researchers collect dat a, consider contextual requisites, adjust the course, and review methodological elements such as participants’ roles  and stimuli (Campos & Hoadley, 2022). Such realign ments then update the project’s hypotheses, typically expressed in conjecture maps (Sandoval, 2014). A conjecture map serves as a dynamic instrument for  relating intervention designs and research goals. Sandoval (2014) posits that conjecture mapping builds an "argumentative grammar" capable of explaining the particular logics that connect the intervention to its hypothesized result s. As a result, DBR produces usable and explainable knowledge, and generate changes in human and organizational capacity toward design goals. In this study, the iterative nature of DBR was oper ationalized through successive implementations of an educational intervention. During one year, adolescents were invited to participate in a civic imagination workshop, where they were prompted to engage in an array of c ivic imagination tasks and activities, all concerni ng limiting situations (Freire, 1970) identified by themselves. Each new w orkshop fed directly into the findings and conclusions of the previous iteration.

## Research sites and participants

This study was conducted in two research sites cons ecutively: the first two editions of the civic imag ination workshops were held at Santa Ana (CA, USA), and the  third and fourth iterations were conducted at Fave la da Rocinha (Rio de Janeiro, Brazil). The first site, Santa Ana  (CA), has a rich history shaped by diverse socio-economic, cultural, and demographic factors. The 20 17 census (DataUSA, nd) indicates that  approximately 31% of Santa Ana's population lives in low-income and poor neighborhoods, 78% of the local population is composed of Latinos, and 43.4% of residents (approximately 1 44 thousand people) were born outside of the US (na tional average: 13.7%). The second site, Rio de Janeiro (Brazil), is home to expressive inequality, with approximatel y 10% of its population living in favelas (slums). The largest of Rio’s favelas, Rocinha has over 100,000 residents and is larger than 92% of all cities in Brazil. Roc inha has long been known for its poverty and lack o f essential services, including sanitation, healthcare, and education. Over the  years, it has also become known for its high levels of armed violence and drug trafficking, fueled partly by the marginalization and exclusion of residents. This study employed criterion sampling, which invol ves selecting participants based on criteria such a s age or residence (Creswell & Creswell, 2017). All selected adolescents were Santa Ana or Rocinha residents aged 13 to 16 and involved in one or more activities at the partner community center.

## Data collection and analysis

Before each workshop, adolescents were interviewed via Zoom or phone. All interviews lasted between 45  and 60 minutes and consisted of open-ended questions about civic participation, knowledge about local social justice issues, and their interest in such challenges. Exit  interviews assessed changes in views about civic p articipation and knowledge about local civic or social justice issues. All workshop sessions were audio and video recorded. Audio and video from Santa Ana were recorded and transcribed using Zoom transcription services; all audio and video from Rocinha were recorded and transcribed using online professional services spec ific to Brazilian Portuguese. Interview data was initial ly analyzed using topical coding, an inductive approac h in which excerpts are grouped into topics without  a predetermined coding scheme (Richards, 2014). This initial, non-interpretive process was employed  so that similar passages relating to the same subject could be grouped into a coding scheme. A final coding round of the interview corpus was then conducted using this scheme, where short memos were written about each participant. Workshops generated two types of data, each receivi ng a distinct treatment. Discussion data refer to audio and video data of participants during the wor kshops (e.g., discussing a topic or playing a game). Artifact data refers to all artifacts produced by participants du ring the workshops. The initial coding pass include d two concurrent inductive approaches: exploratory topica l coding (i.e., the main topics, standpoints, tensi ons, and decisions of each group) (Richards, 2014) and process coding, a qualitative research method that focuses on how participants engage in activities and events. These codes generated a list of themes, allowing for further thematic analysis. Artifact data was coded using exploratory topical coding to check for additional topics or representations. The workshops Workshops were organized in four consecutive iterat ions. In each iteration, adolescents were divided i nto two groups to discuss, ideate, and engage in civic imagina tion activities. This section detail s four of the many procedures employed in Iteration 4 (Rocinha, Brazil), which embodies lessons learned in prior phases. Iteration 4 was organized in five days, each lasting 2,5 hours. Each day was planned to either level participants' knowledge, spur wild imagination, or ground adolescents in solid structural critique of limiting situations (Figure 1). In essence, cards were utilized to level the playfi eld among participants, promote conversations, and elicit their views about local challenges and possibilities of change. This procedure was based on Freire’s (1970) concept of generative themes, topics or issues that emerge from individuals’ li ved experiences and relate to their limiting situations. Therefore, generative themes m ust be relevant to learners, allowing them to devel op critical consciousness about social issues affecting their lives. During the workshops, adolescents created their own cards and rules for playing, sometimes competitive, sometimes collaborative. Cards included a Theme dec k (with topics such as violence, education, gender inequality, and public transportation), a Focus dec k (with prompts such as "How do you think this issu e happens in affluent zones of Rio?"), and an Imagination deck (with prompts such as "What if you had infinite resources?" and "What if you found a portal to another world in your backyard?"). Such decks allowed adolescents to engage in meaningful dialogues, share views and even moral standpoints about local challenges. For example, adolescents in Rocinha were candid about their ideas of social injustice, voicing how they see the role of the so-called “parallel state” (i.e., organized crime running the favela) in keeping the peace. Most importantly, adolescents would return to the cards at different moments of the workshop whenever a theme needed further discussion. Wicked problems Wicked problems are complex and persistent societal  issues that require collaborative and innovative s olutions because of their interconnected nature (Lönngren & Van Poeck, 2021). Wicked problem diagrams are visual representations that help to analyze comp lex and interconnected problems. They illustrate re lationships between elements, including causes, effects, and stakeholde rs. In this study, participants engaged with  a combination of different types of diagrams to represent the causes, effects, and connections of a given problem: caus al loop diagrams (CLDs), concept maps, and system maps. CLDs show feedback loops and causal links in systems, while concept maps represent associations between ideas without necessarily showing causality. Mapping and dream-mapping Mapping and counter-mapping were adopted in all four iterations. Such t asks included mapping actual features of a given locality in a large piece of paper (e.g., mapping your school or your residence)  and particular socio-economic or environmental challenges (e.g., places with a greater incidence of homelessness). Using ma ps was then countered by dream mapping, a way to reimagine physical places based on the conversations held during the workshops. Maps were employed as epistemic tools aimed at developing critical and imaginative fluencies among participants (Headrick Taylor, 2017). Such use reflects the notion of third spaces: any physical space contains multiple layers of meaning, and be interpreted critically and multi-dimensionally (Gutiérrez, 2008; Soja, 1996). Seeing what you think During the workshops, Generative AI platforms (e.g., Midjourney and Stable Diffusion) were used to give life to the dreams and wishes of youth about their physical space s, affective lives, and lived experiences. In the S eeing What You Think exercise, adolescents were asked to write a prompt about a specific theme previously discussed and relating to their own lived experiences at the favela (e.g., Trash, Clogged Manholes, etc.). Adole scents also wrote a prompt about a dream related to that same t opic (i.e., how they want to see change). After receiving the initial results of their prompts, options would be presented to each individual, who would then propos e changes before a final version could be achieved (Figure 2). Dreams would then be discussed, generating more r eflective conversations and inquiries abou t challenges and possibilities. Most importantly, a dolescents often returned to their initial dreams after engaging in other activi ties, such as card games and wicked diagrams. This dialogical movement ensured that dreamed alternatives were revised based on discussions about lived experiences. Design principles After four iterations, this study found three major  mechanisms and principles associated with how adol escents acquire critical civic imagination and develop their i dentities as civic actors: back-and-forth design, decoding and recoding, and civic storytelling. Back-and-forth design involves intentionally alternating elements of criticality and imagination within tasks and workshops to promote d ouble stimulation among participants, aligning with Vygotsky's (1931/1994) theory of sociocultural cognitive development. The decoding and re-coding processes are rooted in Freire's critical pedagogy, emphasizing h ow participants decode and re-code generative themes to foster critical understanding and social and political eng agement. Civic storytelling is highlighted as a too l to connect personal narratives with societal challenges and empower youth to challenge dominant narratives while redefining their roles as civic actors. These mechanisms and principles collectively transformed adolescents' civic engagement and citizenship perspectives throughout the civic imagination workshops. Back-and-forth design A key finding of this study is that criticality and imagination must be designed as separate elements – or in separate embodiments (i.e., tasks or activities in a designed intervention), following Sandoval’s (2014) conjecture mapping model – but intentionally intertwined throughout the workshops. The sequence of activities that formed iteration 4 – the final iteration in this study – was marked by a new design logic, in which critical ity and imagination elements were designed in a back-and-forth pattern, both within tasks and during the five days of the workshop. Intentionally alternating imagination and criticali ty during a civic learning intervention  promotes concurrent, double stimulation to participants. Double stimulation is a principle rooted in Vygotsky’s (1931/1994) sociocultural cognitive development theory, highlighting the interplay of internal cognitive processes and external social interactions in shaping understanding. This concept underscores learning’s collaborative nature, influenced by both external dynamics and internal cognition, emphasizing context and interaction in cognitive development. In the context of this study, participants w ere presented with an initial stimulus based either  on social critique (e.g., a theme card or a fact about a given social challenge in the community) or imagination (e.g., “ what if…”). Based on the dialogical structure of the workshop, participants engaged in re-signifying or even changing this initial stimulus into something that resonated with  their lived experiences. For example, a map of Favela da Rocinha was employed as a conversational tool through which the youth engaged in dialogues about what happens around their schools instead of merely following th e initial prompt. After this initial reaction, a se cond stimulus was offered to participants. Once again, this stimulus is directed to social critique or wild imagination,  and adolescents would have enough space to add or alter any element they deem needed. This combination of stimuli amplified the scope of each embodiment, conferred richness to civic learning discussions, and helps tie together the crucial elements of social critique and civic imagination. Recoding and decoding The iterative nature of this study revealed two mediating processes (Sandoval, 2014) crucial for attaining critical civic imagination: decoding and re-coding. These categories and their names are rooted  in Paulo Freire’s critical pedagogy (1970), an approach that integrates litera cy, semiotics, popular education, and grassroots et hnographic research. In a Freirean learning environment — often called a cultural circle — “coding” represents how information is initially presented by educators (op erationalized by the numerous embodiments presented to participants), while “decoding” involves learners’ active interpretation and critical understanding of  the content, and “recoding” is the transformative step where lea rners reshape and adapt the information to their  perspectives and experiences. This process, however, cannot work in a vacuum, dev oid of robust knowledge of participants’ contexts, representations, and, most importantly, t he limiting-situations affecting their lives. Said differently, coding and re-coding are highly contingent upon proper coding of such  contextual elements by the researcher during the design of the intervention. In this dissertation, this process was carried out through cumulative initial interviews with participants from all iterations.

Civic storytelling Storytelling conveys information, emotions, or concepts through narratives that may incorporate characters, events, and a plot. By intertwining personal narratives, larger societal forces, and popular culture, civic storytelling prompts individuals to establish a personal connection with civic concerns (Jenkins et al., 2020). Civic storytelling has been described as a group of  approaches to provide young people with tools to express their lived experiences, voice social issue s, and advocate for change. Greene and colleagues (2018) posit that telling stories may contribute to fo stering a sense of community and empowerment and em phasizing critical consciousness, community organizing, and social jus tice. Telling civic stories about limit ing situations has also been described as a form of activism, connecting personal narratives to broader sociopolitical contexts. In the context of this study, youth used stories to  rethink challenges and imagine alternative scenari os (especially in Iteration 4), advocating for changes in state policies and individual behaviors. Through rich narratives

— often portraying characters exercising their agency, surmounting challenges, and acting upon harsh realities —

participants used their historicity and identity to  learn about social issues and develop their agency  and voice. In this process, storytelling functioned as a tool for self-discovery and critical consciousness (Freire, 1970).

## Discussion

Considering that civic education programs are not o ffered equally to rich and poor learners (Kahne & M iddaugh, 2009), civic pedagogies need to be tailored to non-dominant youth and incorporate their own lived expe riences, limiting situations, and modes of expression. This section d iscusses the implications of this study's findings on designing and implementing novel civic education programs, from barrios to favelas, and proposes ways in which such implications might contribute to Freirean practice and scholarship. Freire’s approach aims to move beyond traditional one-way education, encouraging learners to engage in a dynamic process of actively constructing knowledg e, fostering critical consciousness, and challengin g hierarchical power dynamics. This process emphasize s dialogue, reflection, and learners’ active partic ipation in shaping their understanding of the world. During his first encounters with adult workers in Brazil, Freire established his Círculos Culturais (or "Cultural Circles" in Portuguese). Working with such arrangements meant that education happened anywhere, from under a mango tree to a cla ssroom, with learners and teachers facing each othe r and engaging in horizontal dialogues. Most importantly,  Freire created cultural and not educational circle s, honoring residents' alternative epistemologies, local cultur e, and limiting situations (Campos & Anderson, 2021).  But how might one implement cultural circles in today's schools or community centers in the context of civic learning? The results of this work highlight how leveraging c ivic media, such as generative AI tools and student-designed card games, can impart significant results for civic learning among marginalized youth. Several scholars have characterized games as powerful educational tools with the potential to enhance civic learning and proficiency (Kahne et al., 2018). Most importantly, games designed and refined by students can facilitate the creation of affinity spaces (Gee & Hayes, 2011) — or contexts of shared interest mediated by activities familiar to participants — and generate profound understandings of complex social phenomena. Such examples of youth media — coupled with the protean possibilities of image-based generative AI in the context of civic learning workshops — are dialogical in nature, cultural in essence, and humanistic in their aims, thus keeping the essential tenets of the Freirean tradition. A second opportunity for keeping up with Freirean scholarship rests upon finding novel ways to foster the dialectical relationship between denunciation and annunciation. Freire openly opposed transforming his writings into a boxed, universal method. However, this particular — and justified — attitude towards primers and teaching scripts still challenges Freirean thinking, as some  aspects of Freire's philosophy are highly  underdescribed and, therefore, lack field experience and scholarly deba te. This is exactly the case of denunciation and annunciation. Whereas denouncing injustice became the core of the Freirean tradition, educators are not equipped with tools and approaches to foster forms of annunciation that are in line and responsive to Freire's principles. For example, Freire did not develop specific routines or thought processes for reimagining alternatives to oppression (although he was vocal about disliking any form of imagination that is merely intuitive or wholly disconnected from liv ed experiences). This work presented cases where annun ciation and denunciation worked in tandem and roote d in Freirean pillars, where adolescents from non-dominant communities engaged in dialogical activiti es, acquired critical awareness of the limiting situations affecting their lives, recognized their privileges, denounced injustices, and announced within-reach alternatives not just grounded in reality but worth fighting for (Roberts, 2015). Freire’s approach revolves around generative themes that hold significance for the local community. Most importantly, generative themes allow for producing a thread of interconnected motifs that spur dialogue and reflection centered on the relationships between individuals and their existence. In the Freirean literature, working with generative themes involves three main phases. The process begins with an Investigation Stage, whe rein educators engage in informal interactions, interviews, and  participant observation to define a theme for coll ective analysis. Freire contends that defining a significa nt theme within a cultural circle involves a thorou gh co-investigative process. In this study, investigation was carried out cumulatively, through interviews with participants from all four iterations. The themes identified in such interviews  underwent a codification process in the Thematization Stage, resulting in symbolic represen tations such as images or artwork. These encoded representations worked as dialogical seeds, allowin g learners to visualize, introspect, and assess the ir perceptions of the limit ing situations in which they are embedded. Decoding the se themes involves educators and learners uncovering multiple layers of meaning. Freire highlights the potential for new generative themes to emerge during this stage. Finally, learners and educators tran sition from abstract dialogues to tangible empowerm ent actions in the Problematization Stage. Themes are re-codified, encapsulating the circle’s discoveries and critical awareness to promote social and political engagement. In this  study, this third phase was achieved through desig n and imagination activities, where participants recreate d their lived experiences based on a deeper compreh ension of limiting situations affecting their lives.

In essence, decoding and recoding processes – coupled with an intentional back-and-forth design and civic storytelling activities – have the power to liven up the annunciation and den unciation praxis in a Freirean learning environment. Such elements, when carefully  designed for civic learning, may be key in weaving  social critique and imagination altogether. Such categories depend on the initial stimuli presented to participants. For this reason, Freire suggested that generative themes should be structured in an ecological arrangement, from universal social struggles to show such struggles manifest in  society at large to situations specific to a parti cular society during a specific time. Most importantly, generativ e themes should be intentional, open-ended, prone to interpretation, and capture the existential essence of learners’ limiting situations. Understanding such rich and wellestablished Freirean theory might help learning designers develo p civic learning experiences that foster critical decoding of social phenomena and creative, imaginative recoding of such topics.

## Limitations

Limitations include challenges in coding inter-group dialogue from audio or video sources, particu larly in multivoice contexts. The small sample size suits exploratory purposes, but findings should not be extrapolated to broader populations. A single coder executed data analysis; future studies should introduce multiple coders.

## Conclusion

This study described a series of workshops to foster Critical Civic Imagination. As in Freire's poetic language, the workshops encouraged individuals to denounce current soc ial, political, and economic conditions while announcing powerful alternatives, sometimes only sl ightly, often even fantastical. Together, learners and facilitators coded, decoded, and creatively recoded  what is and what could be. This synthesis of imagi nation and criticality is the starting point of a framework for compelling civic learning experienc es that respond to the needs and differences of marginalized youth, from barrios to favelas, within and beyond the Global North.
