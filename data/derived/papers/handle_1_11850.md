# Using Generative AI for Analyzing Student Reflections on Collaborative Virtual Reality Creation

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study designed and examined an approach using ChatGPT for automatic coding of students’ reflection data in a Virtual Reality (VR) collaborative maker activity. We developed a coding scheme focusing on social-collaborative interactions and used few-shot prompting strategies for ChatGPT automatic coding. The results showed strong correspondence between ChatGPT and human coding on all eight codes (Kappa from 0.81 to 0.90), but variations exist on the overall performance of Generative artificial intelligence (GAI) for different codes based on F1 scores. Different explanations are proposed including clear definition of the codes and number of responses in different coding categories. The study contributed to illuminating processes underlying collaborative learning in maker activities, demonstrated the promise of GAI for automatic coding, and suggested implications for research and practice.

## Perspectives on collaboration and reflection

Collaborative learning supported by technology is t aking on increasing importance for promoting studen t development in the changing and complex world as well as educational reforms (Järvelä et al., 2022; Wise et al., 2021). Collaboration is pivotal as it promotes exchange of ideas, inter-subjectivity, co-construction of knowledge and shared regulation (Järvelä et al., 202 2; Stahl et al., 2014). Major efforts and progress h ave been made examining collaborative processes using different a nalysis methods (Puntambekar et al., 2011). With the advent of learning analytics and generative artificial intelligence (G AI), there would be more varied ways to analyze collaboration and discourse data (Shaffer & Ruis, 2 021; Yan et al., 2024). Student collaboration in co mplex environments has been examined using reflection that both assesses and scaffold s student learning (Looi et al., 2013; Yang et al., 2016). Reflection can help students become more metacog nitively aware of their learning process, resolve uncertainties and consider multipl e solutions (Fullana et al., 2016). Many studies ha ve analyzed student reflections for learning processes using ma nual coding (e.g., Cotton, 2001; Tsingos et al., 2014) while automated methods hold the potential to scale up the analysis including those using GAI. Qualitative discourse analysis using generative AI With the increasingly common adoption of technology-enhanced learning and collaboration, labeled or cod ed discourse data is widely used in learning analytics to examine learning and collaboration phenomena (Wise et al., 2021). Discourse data can take many different forms, such as instructor feedback (Osakwe et al., 2022), classroom discussion (Erkens & Janssen, 2008), student essays  and forum discussions (Grag et al., 2024). Researc hers can employ automated tools, humans, or a combination of methods to analyze these data (Shaffer & Ruis, 2021). The advancements in GAI and emerging tools such as Generative Pre-Trained Transformers (GPT) have demonstrated the potentials in automatic qualitative content analysis with increased efficiency and efficacy (Grag et al., 2024). Analyzing collaboration has been a major research t heme in computer supported collaborative learning (CSCL) and harnessing generative AI could provide new possibilities for understanding collaboration. Large language models (LLMs) have supplanted conventional machine learning techniques for numerous text categorization problems (Garrido-Merchan et al., 2023). ChatGPT uses a user-friendly interface to prompt the model for an output, which simplifies the workf low (Garg et al., 2024). By the provision of releva nt information in the prompt, ChatGPT has been shown t o perform more accurately than human coders (Brown, 2020; Garg et al., 2024; Gilardi et al., 2023). GPT-3 fine-tuning approaches have shown more ef fective in educational settings than traditional machine learn ing techniques (Amarasinghe et al., 2023). However, current research also highlights certain drawbacks of gener ative models, such as inconsistent results and chal lenges in providing meaningful prompts (Garg et al., 2024). Similarly, GAI for automatic coding, and developm ent of coding schemes needs to be premised on theoretical basis for unravelling meaningful learning and colla boration processes.

Research gaps and research questions Previous research has examined roles of automatic a nalysis of reflection in writing by using tradition al machine learning (Ullmann, 2019) and GAI (Kim et al., 2024). Specifically, researchers ha ve evaluated the effectiveness of LLMs by using different prompting techniques such as few-shot and zero-shot prompting (Garg et al., 2024), indicating GPT-4 is a promising LLM. However, few existing studies  examined how GAI could be used for coding qualitative data including different approaches of prompting such as few-shot and examining the relations between human and GAI coding (Garg et al., 2024). F ewer explored the analysis of reflection using GAI in collaborative context for informing design decisions. To address this gap, this study aims to evaluate GPT models for automatically coding data obtained from student  reflections on their collaborative learning processes through the use of prompting strategies. Specifically, we i nvestigate collaborative processes students engaged  in when they were doing virtual reality maker activities as shown in their reflection journals. The research questions included: RQ1: What are the common problems and solutions reported in student reflections during a collaborative VR making activity?

RQ2: To what extent are human coding and GPT coding consistent and in what ways?

## Methods

This study compared manually coded data and those a utomatically classified using ChatGPT 4 which has b een shown in previous research as more effective in gen erating better results compared with other models s uch as GPT 3.5 (Garg et al., 2024). To enable ChatGPT 4 to  produce relevant contents, a few-shot prompt strategy was used in this study to provide definition and exampl es of the human-labelled coding scheme to ChatGPT before proceeding to analysis. We then compared ChatGPT ou tput and human manual codes using Cohen’s kappa and F1 score. The F1 score is a comprehensive metric fo r classification performance in that it combines pr ecision, a measure of specificity, and recall, a measure of completeness (Goutte & Gaussier, 2005).

To address the RQs, the reflection data was pre-processed by removing that irrelevant to collaborat ion. Drawing from theoretical constructs of social inter actions in collaboration, we developed a codebook a nd then conducted manual coding based on this codebook. Aft er manual coding, we used few-shot prompt strategy to inform ChatGPT 4 of the characteristics of the reflection texts in each code category and then prompted ChatGPT 4 to code the data. The consistency between human c oding and GAI coding was compa red to evaluate the effectiveness of GAI in analyzing student reflection in a collaborative maker activity learning context.

## Research context and data

The reflection data on students’ collaboration was collected from 150 Grade 7 students in a secondary school in Hong Kong who conducted a digital maker activity on  virtual reality (VR) content creation. Students wo rked in groups of 4 to 5 during an after-school program lasting for three weeks. This learning program was designed to support students in maker activities, specifically in VR content creation,  with the purpose of improving their collaboration and regulation skills. In this VR content creation project, student groups worked on researching and identifying an appropriate topic for their VR stori es, shooting and uploading panoramas, creating and annotating relevant objects, desi gning and connecting different scenes, writing up t he narration and selecting background music (Hu & Ng, In press). During the project, students collaborated both on line and offline using an in-house platform named Collaborative Learning Environment for Virtual Reality content creation (CLEVR) that supported VR content creation while allowing for asynchronous  communication through chats and sticky notes  (Wang et al., 2024).

Student engagement in the collaborative VR content creation activity was valuable but also highly complex, and we asked students to write reflection journals focusing on their collaboration processes. Specifically, we sought to examine their social interaction and r egulation, by asking them to reflect on the challen ges they faced, and the strategies employed to resolve their  difficulties. Students wrote reflection journals r egularly throughout the program, and for this study which ai ms at evaluating automatic AI coding, we focused on  the summative reflection data at the end of program. Two guiding questions on group collaboration were included in this reflection: 1) what collaboration challenges/p roblems the group encountered during the learning p rogram, and 2) what actions the group employed or would have employed to solve the problems. Processing of data for analysis and coding Pre-processing Since we focus on social interaction elements and problem-solving skills in collaboration, inapplicable responses including irrelevant problems mentioned regarding technical issues, gibberish, invalid responses with no problem or solution identified and alike were removed to ensure a valid analysis focusing on collaboration. The processed dataset includes 9 4 records for analyzing collaborative problems (Guidi ng question 1) and 107 records for the solutions (Guiding question 2). There was no word limit for students answering the two reflection questions. The average length of responses is 19.87 words.

Coding In developing the coding schemes, we focused on soc ial interaction and cognitive processes in collabor ation (Hesse et al., 2015; Kreijns et al., 2003). Key elements related to social interaction within and/or amongst groups were identified to investigate factors influencing stude nts’ collaborative learning experience and outcomes. Student responses were coded using two separate codebooks each consisting of four coding categories. The coding scheme for challenges/problems in collaboration was constructed based on key concepts of social interaction and cognitive process (Kreijns et al., 2003). The codin g scheme for solutions to collaboration problems was categorized based on the collaborative problem-solving framework proposed by Hesse et al. (2015) w hich also included both social skills and cognitive skills (Table 2). Using top-down and bottom-up processes, we developed the coding schemes illustrated with definitions and  examples in Tables 1 and 2. In this study, exclusi ve coding was applied, meaning that each student response was coded only with one code. The codebook was then used by two coders who hold master's degrees in education and communications respectively, to code the reflection data. They coded all student responses independently and the inter-rater reliability was calculated in Cohen's Kappa. The responses with different codes were resolved by discussions between the two coders. Prompting This step prompted ChatGPT for the classification t ask with few-shot prompt strategy. Specifically, we started from-indicating the purpose of this conversation, “to analyze the r eflection responses by a group of students regarding collaboration”, and the specific task being “identify a code for  each response”. F or each code in the coding framework (Tables 1&2), the code name and it s definition were fed into ChatGPT, along with five randomly selected text examples manually assigned w ith that code by human coders. These prompts can in form the GAI model of the characteristics of the texts that belong to a specific code (Garg et al., 2024). Classification and evaluation After few-shot prompting, ChatGPT was ready to classify all t he remaining reflection responses based on the codebook. Specifically, the reflection responses were fed to ChatGPT 4 in several batches (about 20 responses in each batch), and ChatGPT output the code for each r esponse. The classification results were then compared with the codes assigned by human coders. F1-score and Cohen’s Kappa were used to measure overal l performance of ChatGPT, the extent to which ChatGPT coding was consistent with human coding.

RQ1: Common problems and solutions in collaborative VR making Conceptual ideas of collaboration for developing the first coding scheme are based on Kreijns et al. (2003). Along these lines, the four codes (Table 1) were classified to capture elements that hinder collaboration due to problems with social dynamics. Bas ed on the data, two major areas of challenges are i dentified: First, social interaction does not naturally happen without designed instruct ion or encouragement, such that channels offered to  support learner’s communication are necessary, however do n ot gua rantee effective social interaction. Second, the importance of the socio-emotional dimension facilitating the cognitive proc ess in collaboration may be easily neglected or even ignored in a CSCL environment. Th e establishment of interpersonal relationships  within a group, more importantly building a sense of communi ty, is emphasized (Garrison, 2009). It is the proce ss as students build trust, impression, a sense of belong ing, which is fundamental to building a team. It is, therefore, significantly impacting their collaborative learnin g experience and outcome if such aspects are underd eveloped. Based on the literature on social interactions in c ollaboration, four indicators were then included to evaluate the mentioned potential obstacles students might or had  encountered during the collaboration of VR-making (Table 1).

## Discussion and implications

This study examined collaborative dynamics underlyi ng challenges and solutions in collaborative VR mak er processes depicted in students’ reflection. We deve loped a coding scheme guided by the theoretical not ions of social interactions characterizing the dy namics of collaboration and tested the corresponden ce between human manual and Generative AI automatic coding. The resu lts showed a strong consistency between human and G AI coding using Kappa scores for all eight codes ident ified (Kappa 0.81-0.90). However, according to the F1 score, the ChatGPT 4 with few-shot prompting strategy can accurately predict some of these codes but not others. These results provide some support for automatic coding but also areas of discrepancies, similar with previous research highlighting drawbacks of GAI models for open-text coding, such as inconsistent results (Brown, 2 020). Nevertheless, results from this study also suggest ways for improving the use of generative AI in codi ng with methodological implications.

Among these codes, “Communication1” and “group cohe sion” have the best performance (Figure 1). The reason may be that these codes have been define d clearly based on the literature compared to other  codes. “Disagreement” and “Communication2” in the “solutio n” part (Figure 2) also had comparable performance to “Communication1” and “Group cohesion”. Besides clear definitions, better performances on these two codes can also be attributed to the fact that they have the l argest number of examples in our data out of all th e eight codes (Table 3). Although few shot strategies were used where exactly the same number (5) of examples were input into ChatGPT for each code, a code with more examples co uld have better coverage of its characteristics by the randomly selected five. Another possible reason for the low perfor mance of ChatGPT for some codes may lie in the complex nature of the codes, such as “contribution” and “problem solving”. These codes can be highly flexible in the context of collaborative learning. Group mem bers can make contributions in many different ways while problem solving can be demonstrated in nuanced form s. For these codes, few-shot approaches with a handful examples may not be sufficient, as pointed out in r ecent literature (Garg et al., 2024). To address th is problem, a more robust prompt strategy such as fine-tuning might need to be adopted, and for this, a large amount of dataset may become necessary. These results also suggest th at researchers should be cautious of using ChatGPT 4 for automated discourse coding in a learning analytics context. This is indicative that for complex, highl y flexible and nuanced codes, a few examples may not be enough  for the model to fully grasp the context needed to  code the text.

## Conclusion and future work

This study has made the first attempt to analyze reflection data using GAI in a collaborative maker activity context. The methods adopted in this study can enrich CSCL research identifying the underlying learning and collaborative processes. This approach can complement the traditional empirical research often relying on manual coding. The coding scheme developed in this study can also prov ide new insights for future researchers to conduct GAI analysis in collaborative learning contexts.

The general consistency and potential of ChatGPT in  automatic coding of qualitative data aligns with current research development and progress (Garg et al., 2024).  Educational implications of AI automatic coding include analyzing students’ reflection responses on  collaboration in a timely manner as they carry out  the VR project to detect problems and solutions, pointing to possibilities to developing dashboards for teachers/ students to reflect on process and adjusting their collaboration. To summarize, this study evaluated the effectivenes s of applying LLM-based GAI such as ChatGPT to analyze student reflection data in a collaborative learning context. We developed a coding scheme grounded on a theoretical framework  based on social dynamics for examining challenges a nd solutions in collaboration respectively. We also explored whether GAI could serve as an alternative means to current text coding in empirical research in collaborative learning contexts. Result s show a strong consistency between human coding and GAI coding using Kappa scores but variation in the over all performance in the GAI coding based on F1 score. Upon reviewing the performance of ChatGPT 4, we found that using a few-shot approach with a limited number of examples may not be adequate for complex codes with  many presentations, as the model might not develop  a comprehensive representation of the code. Accordingly, we recommend using the fe w-shot approach for well-defined codes or those with more and diverse exampl es, while using the fine-tuning training method with sufficient training data for complex and nuanced co des. Future studies include more thorough evaluatio ns on different approaches empowered by GenAI on automati ng the analysis of collaborative learning processes, and using data from different research contexts of VR making to examine roles of GAI in analyzing and illuminating collaboration.
