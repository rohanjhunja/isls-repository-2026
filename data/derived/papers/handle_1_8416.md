# Expanding Teacher Transformative Agency: An Activity-Theoretical Formative Intervention Research in Schoolbased Dialogue Sessions Toward Future Making

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Drawing on cultural–historical activity theory and expansive learning theory, this paper focuses on methodologically reexamining and transforming the linear interventions incorporated in traditional standardized pedagogy and educational research. To achieve this, the present study investigates strategies for generating formative interventions in teacher learning and development in an attempt to engage teachers in collaborative interventions to facilitate expansive learning. Teacher transformative agency can be generated and exercised through collaborative formative interventions. During this process, teachers become responsible activity agents who collaborate to independently reshape their activity systems. This paper presents the findings of a formative intervention study of teacher learning and development in school-based research involving teachers at a municipal elementary school in Japan. The participating teachers’ school-based research can be characterized as expansive learning through which they collaboratively formed complex concepts, giving rise to a new vision of schooling.

## Introduction

In the field of school reform, traditional standard  intervention studies are reduced to linear causali ty, wherein a top-down view regards teachers as entirely passive agents of policies, while policymakers and researchers create a grand design that teachers then apply or revise, resulting in more positive change for students. Conversely, the basic principle of formative intervention studies based on cultural–hi storical activity theory  is that teachers themselves gain the agency to control the intervent ion and take charge of the process. Thus, teachers become change agents. Activity theory studies how agents collaboratively redesign, change, and transform their collective activity systems, which have been culturally and hi storically constructed (Engeström, 1987/2015, 2008,  2016, 2018; Leont’ev, 1978, 1981; Sannino et al., 2009; S annino & Ellis, 2013; Yamazumi, 2021). Here, the fo cus is on triggering and sustaining expansive transformation led and owned by teachers. Moving beyond the limitations of linear interventio n, activity–theoretical formative interventions in teacher learning and development attempt to facilit ate teachers’ expansive learning, as theorized by Yrjö Engeström (1987/2015, 2016)—that is, expanding the object of teachers’ collaborative learning from bel ow to change the broader structure of an entire school as a collective activity system. These collaborative interventions engage teachers in creating their own interventions to independently transform their activity systems (Yamazumi, 2021). Through such interventions, teacher transformative agency can be generated and exercised. In other words, collaborative formative interventions based on acti vity theory enable teachers to become responsible a ctivity agents who collaborate on shared inquiry into desir ed objects, forms, and patterns of practice, result ing in the redesign of schools.

Drawing on the abovementioned frameworks, this paper focuses on methodologically reexamining and transforming the linear interventions incorporated in traditional standardized pedagogy and educational research. To achieve this transformation, this paper investig ates strategies to generate formative interventions  in teacher learning and development in an attempt to engage te achers in collaborative interventions to facilitate expansive learning. Reporting on an activity–theoretical case  study, this paper presents the findings of a forma tive intervention study of teacher learning and developm ent in teachers’ school-based research at Jōetsu Mu nicipal Ōmachi Elementary School, Jōetsu City, Japan, by an alyzing the process involved in the teachers’ expan sive learning in dialogue sessions that combine classroom newsletters and activity reports.

## Formative intervention methodology to expand transformative agency

Today, a form of intervention research based on act ivity theory geared toward people’s expansive learn ing in social practices, work, and organizations is being promoted worldwide. This intervention research has evolved remarkably through a methodological principle calle d formative intervention, which is founded on the a ttitude embedded in concepts such as a “joint venture” (Eng eström, 1987/2015, p. 22) between practitioners and researchers, premised upon practitioners becoming the collective subjects of an evolving new activity system with respect to which they collaborate, analyze, and int ervene in it themselves as agents. In doing so, the ir joint ICLS2022 Proceedings 1093 © ISLS intervention does not follow a predetermined course but instead seeks an expansive transition to “what is not yet there” (Engeström, 2016, p. 9).

Based on the framework of activity theory, which captures collective human creation of activity systems, expansive learning theory is a new theory of learni ng that considers agents’ collective design of acti vities and practical involvement in transformations as their l earning process (Engeström, 1987/2015, 2016). Expan sive learning goes beyond the level of learning to solve a given problem and instead reaches the level of creating the problem or task itself, equivalent to what Gregory Bateson (1972, p. 119) calls “Learning III.” Expansive learning also generates “what is not yet there”—in other wor ds, “novel material forms of collective life, not m erely construction of novel ideas in the minds of the learners” (Engeström, 2016, p. 9). The expansive learning approach to school change th rough formative intervention focuses on methodologically reexamining and transforming linear perspectives of interventions that have been incorporated into traditional standardized pedagogy and educational research. The essential concern with expansive learning in and for the creation of new forms of pedagogical practices in schools is that people involved in schooling can “design and implement their own futures as their prevalent practices show symptoms of crisis” (Engeström, 1991, p. 256). In the area of school reform, the expansiv e learning approach is not based on the idea of “be nevolent reform from above” (p. 257).

Therefore, even in its methodological principles, formative intervention differs fundamentally from the traditional top-down linear intervention that polic ymakers and researchers monopolize. From the viewpo int of formative intervention that facilitates participant s’ expansive learning, new forms of education shoul d be reconceptualized as dialogically negotiated activit ies in which various agents produce new collaborati ve interventions while transforming their activity systems. School-based dialogue sessions as collaborative formative interventions Drawing on the abovementioned theoretical framework and methodology of formative interventions, this section considers school-based research at Jōetsu Municipal Ōmachi Elementary School, Jōetsu City, Japan, by analyzing teachers’ expansive learning process in dialogue sessions that combined classroom newsletters and activity reports. In the 2020 academic year, 33 school-based dialogue  sessions were held at the Ōmachi Elementary School  and attended by all teachers. At the end of each session, the participating teachers wrote individual reflective essays, which all participants read.

It is relevant here to focus on the difference betw een what Mikhail Bakhtin (1975/1981, p. 342) calls “authoritative discourse” and “internally persuasiv e discourse” in order to gain a clear understanding  of the dialogical, responsible, and reflective nature of t he sessions at Ōmachi Elementary School. Bakhtin de scribes authoritative discourse as the “tendency to assimil ate to others’ discourse” (p. 342). In contrast to authoritative discourse, internally persuasive discourse evokes n ew ways of thinking. It allows one to acknowledge one’s innermost feelings, thus bestowing the power of creative self-expression. Having analyzed teachers’ statements, the dialogue sessions at Ōmachi Elementary School can be considered a place for teachers to directly engage with internally persuasive discourse. In other words, dialogue sessions are creative and productive activities tha t encourage teachers to acknowledge others’ interna lly persuasive discourse. Engaging in these interactive relationships awakens “new and independent words” within shared educational practice. This invites us to reveal “ever newer ways to mean” (Bakhtin, 1975/1981, p. 346). Hence, the dialogue sessions at Ōmachi Elementary School resemble the so-called indigenous thinking that arises from below through the teachers themsel ves. This involves their everyday practice of educa ting and thorough reexamination and requestioning of the col lective life of collaboration between teachers and children. In other words, this is tightly interwoven with the concept of “one’s own word,” as Bakhtin states. The aim is to generate a common ground and language. As one teach er stated, the dialogue sessions inspire indigenous, sensuous, and wild cognition, thoughts, and express ions rooted in the everyday lives of teachers’ work  with children. In connection with this, they are heading  toward the creation of new common images of childr en and schools. Here, where school-based research is consi dered to be the model of a collective activity syst em (Engeström, 1987/2015, p. 63) for making a school, the dialogue sessions seem to act as instruments me diating internally persuasive discourse among the participants. This research activity in the third semester can be  considered to be linked to the creation of a grand design for the school in the new academic year, the resetting of educational and priority goals, the way school evaluations, and the organizing the curriculum. (Jōetsu Municipal Ōmachi Elementary School Research Promotion Department, “The Proposal for the 29th School-based Dialogue Session [Revised and Annotated],” January 7, 2021) The shared concept (words) that the teachers formed through the dialogue sessions at Ōmachi Elementary School is not among those “stable, well-defined and neutral concepts” (Engeström et al., 2005, p. 48) with which only the “standard cognitive theories of concept fo rmation” have been preoccupied. Rather, it belongs among “unstable and contradictory complex concepts” (p. 48) that teachers seek and spin out as expansive concepts that are “not yet there.” In this sense, these “complex concepts” give the subject the power of creative expression by awakening “new and independent words” (Bakhtin, 1975/1981, p. 345), and they are invited to reveal “ever newer ways to mean” (p. 346). Engeström and his colleagues (2005) have stated that complex concepts are formed during creation. This represents the externalization and generation of culturally new concepts. Mr. Eisuke Takahashi (2020, p. 19), head-teacher of  research at Ōmachi Elementary School in 2020, understood the teachers’ dialogue sessions in schoo l-based research that combined classroom newsletter s and activity reports as opportunities to collectively l isten to “children’s ways of living and episodes fr om ‘activity with children.’” In other words, the dialogue session is a performance of “anchoring down,” based on “experiential and descriptive language” or “cases, feelings, and ethnography,” as referenced by Engeström (2004, pp. 98–99). In this anchoring down, teachers ask “how” questions (Engeström, 2016, pp. 95–96). Moreover, by asking “who, what, when” questions, teachers also perform “anchoring sideways.” However, the dialogue sessions at Ōmachi Elementary School were not limited to anchoring down and anchoring sideways; they also aimed at collaborative concept formation, as described above. This means that they also strive toward “anchoring up.” Therefore, these are dialogue sessions in which teachers asked “why” questions about the origins of the problem and contradictions in educational practice, as well as “where to” questions about the future of the entire school activity system and the network of activity systems. Engeström (2004) s tates that when these different types of questions act as “second stimuli” (instruments as mediating artifacts), as Lev Vygotsky (1930/1999) emphasized in his “ double stimulation” principle, in workplace meetings, the following is important: “[T]he key is in complement arity, interplay, and transitions among the different types of questions” (Engeström, 2004, p. 101). Thus, expansive learning in which practitioners the mselves design and transform their own activity systems can be characterized as the “construction a nd use of dynamic multi-level conceptual instrument alities” (Engeström et al., 2005, p. 59). In doing so, two-way movements, namely bottom-up and top-down, fill the gaps between situational “what” and technical “how” conc eptualizations and spatial–systemic “why” and historical– visionary “where to” conceptualizations. At that point, the agent uses multi-level questions as mediating artifacts and second stimuli, whereby they themselves invent, use, and mediate, thus controlling their actions from outside and redefining the circumstances. When they actively intervene in the situation, transformative agency emerges, triggering intentional transformative actions (Engeström, 2016, pp. 224–225). In other words, the “Session on School Making” was an attempt to collaborate and collectively perform epistemological work in which teachers tackle “where to” questions through the invention a nd use of mediating artifacts. This work explores a nd assembles a shared concept that expresses and direc ts the intended vision of the child and of the scho ol. ## Conclusions

Teachers’ school-based research at the Ōmachi Eleme ntary School can be characterized as expansive lear ning that fostered the collaborative formation of concepts and future making toward a new vision of the school. Such concept formation and future making does not occur in the dimension related to the method or technique  of discrete teaching, but rather in the dimension of a sking “why” and “where to” questions pertaining to an entire school as a collective activity system and the future of the school. School-based research helps formulate “where to” concepts such as “making together,” which is a useful living instrument (mediating artifact) that teachers can use to develop a vision for their school’s future activity system and redesign its present activity system. Modeling the school’s future strongly suggests the adoption of a school learning activity based on mutual aid and care as an alternative to schooling linked to the creation of a post-capitalist degrowth society. With this alternative, the use value of school learning is geared toward people living side by side and creating joy, common good, and well-being for everyone. To achieve this,  the school is perceived as working on rediscoverin g and expanding sharing and as a place where learning is concrete, unique, and useful to each person.
