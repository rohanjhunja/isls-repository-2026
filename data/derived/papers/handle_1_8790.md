# Chin Youths’ Epistemic Agency in an Afterschool STEM Learning Environment

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
We implemented an afterschool STEM program for Chin youth to engage them in critical science literacy while using their full linguistic repertoires. For the present study, we conducted a grounded theory analysis of 24 interviews from this project to reveal how translanguaging supported epistemic agency in this space. Our findings from four youths’ narratives suggest that the youth integrated translanguaging as an extension of their everyday discursive practices to foster shared epistemic agency among their peers. In addition, we identified structures from their school science classrooms that inhibited translanguaging and stunted their epistemic agency.

## Introduction

Our research has explored the way in which multilingual refugee youth engage in critical science literacy through translanguaging in informal learning programs (Ryu & Daniel, 2020). Translanguaging is the fluid and flexible use of full linguistic repertoires that integrate m ultiple named languages to achieve specific communi cative purposes (Blackledge & Creese, 2014). In the presen t work, we contrast Chin youths’ experiences in our community-based after school setting and school sci ence classes to explore whether and how translangua ging fosters shared epistemic agency in STEM learning.

We draw upon Miller et al. (2018) to operationalize epistemic agency as “students being positioned with, perceiving, and acting on, opportunities to shape the knowledge building work in their classroom community” (p. 1058). Furthermore, we draw on the idea that shared epistemic agency arises “during collaborative activities aimed at the creation of shared knowledge objects” (Damşa et al., 2010, p. 146). Specifically, learners exer cise shared epistemic agency through collaboratively identifying gaps in knowledge, naming problems, and generating ideas. With these constructs in mind, we asked, How does translanguaging support Chin refugee youth s’ epistemic agency in STEM learning environments?

## Context and methods

We conducted our afterschool program for high schoo l-aged youth in collaboration with a non-profit Bur mese Organization in a historically White neighborhood on the outskirts of a major city where Chin people established an ethnic enclave (see Ryu & Daniel, 2020). We conducted and analyzed 24 total interviews with 14 youth who regularly attended the STEM program to understand t heir relationship with science learning in our prog ram, in school, and in their daily lives. For this paper, we draw on four youths’ narratives: Nyunt, Kevin, Joshua, and Da Zin. Although we identified epistemic agency throughout the data set, the excerpts here were particularly powerful and salient.

We used constructivist grounded theory methods (Cha rmaz, 2014) to center the youths’ voices in the analytical claims. After transcribing the interview s verbatim, the first author engaged in an iterativ e qualitative analytic journey. After multiple rounds of initial and focused coding, initial codes such as “translan guaging to help others,” and “naming who has authority over English” coalesced to relate to themes of agency, positioning, and power. To better articulate these themes, we visited the literature in science education, sociocultural theories, and refugee studies. The second author drew on fiel d notes and earlier analysis of the data set to pro vide ethnographic insights into the construction of our findings.

## Findings

Our analysis revealed that translanguaging is an essential practice of the Chin youths’ sensemaking and daily life. Thus, the youth in our program exercised shared epi stemic agency through translanguaging. However, sch ool science classrooms marginalized their linguistic assets which restricted opportunities to exercise epistemic agency. The youth in our STEM program translanguaged every day. Across the 14 regular attendees, they spoke a total of 13 named languages and dialects. In the interviews, they shared that it was “fun”, “special ”, “comfortable”, and “normal” to mix languages with t heir classmates, friends, and family. For instance,  Nyunt learned the Mizo language growing up, Burmese in sc hool, English throughout resettling, and Matu after  she arrived in the U.S. because her parents wanted to p reserve their ethnic heritage language. Nyunt shared that she ICLS2022 Proceedings 2208 © ISLS and her friend Rosie frequently translanguaged in the STEM program: “When she talk in Matu, I just reply to her in Burmese. We do that all the time.” The youth readily translanguaged because it was historically, socially, and politically embedded in their lives.

In our STEM program, the youth exercised their shared epistemic agency by organically generating ideas to make sense of terminology through translanguaging. The Chin youth faced a unique linguistic conundr um in science: There were no direct translations for many Western scientific jargon among the languages they spoke. In Joshua’s group, the group mates took turns sharing their conceptions of terms after they did some researching. He said, “We did by group, we split like 2 or 3… The vocabulary, the word, we tried to find what does that mean and after that we explained to other people, like presentation.” Kevin, one of Joshua’s group mates, was persistent in getting their small group to translanguage, even though none of them were entirely confident speaking each others’ primary language. Kevin elaborated, “Since he [another peer] don’t speak English, I just talking to him, ‘Tell me in Falam,’ and then [if] the things that he say is kind of easy, then I make him to say it [in English]. Then if I think it is kind of hard to say it, I kind of translate i t [from Falam to English].” Through translanguaging, the youth positioned one another as collaborators in scientific sensemaking, expanding the shared epistemic agency among them.

Contrary to the possibilities realized in our STEM program, the dominant assumptions and practices in school science classrooms constrained the youths’ o pportunities to translanguage. Subsequently, they w ere positioned with less epistemic agency relative to themselves in our STEM program and to their American peers in school. Kevin, who worked hard to build linguistic bridges between his peers in the STEM program, brought to light exclusionary behaviors by his school peers: “There’s people who just don’t interest in making friend with whoever is not American… If the teacher told them to work with non-English people, they will never talk to them, even if they sit together. They will not even stare each other.” Another youth, Da Zin, who also felt excluded by American students, said she felt “embarrassed” and “angry” because they would not let her help. The American students’ behavior and expectations dominated the c lassroom norms such that everyone else was position ed as “not English” to the detriment of their participation in school science.

## Discussion

These Chin youths’ epistemic agency is contingent o n positioning and opportunities with respect to translanguaging. Whenever the Chin youth congregated together, translanguaging was the norm, and it fo stered their epistemic agency in science learning. In their school classes, the monolingual English-speaking Americans determined the norms which rendered their translang uaging resources ineffective. Although it could not  be represented here, the shared epistemic agency reflected in these youths’ narratives will be further corroborated by video data in future analyses. We urge researchers and practitioners to embrace heterogeneity to equitably expand epistemic agency in science learning.
