# Leveraging Eye Gaze Data for Metacognition and Perceptual Scaffold in Visual Problems

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
An eye gaze display is a dynamic representation of a person's eye movement. Gaze displays capture problem solving processes due to the substantial coupling between a person’s eye movement, attention, and cognition. This has prompted the design of instructional approaches that assist learners in information processing and make expert processes observable. We consider a novel approach of using such displays to generate problems, where learners engage with another person's problem-solving process through questions that require them to interpret a gaze display within the context of the underlying problem. We analyze the verbalizations of a dyad solving such tasks to study the potential of such problems to elicit metacognition and offer perceptual scaffolding during problem solving.

## Introduction

Eye gaze displays are videos that illustrate the ey e movements of individuals as they perform a given task. In visual tasks, these displays depict a person's eye movement and reveal their attention to different ob jects as they solve a problem (Rayner, 1992). These displays can encode problem solving processes (Thomas & Lleras, 2007) which when decoded by a learner leads to deeper lea rning. This premise led to the design of eye moveme nt modeling examples (EMME), which have been shown to enhance learning in diverse tasks, including proble msolving and understanding multimedia content (Xie et al., 2020). A recent proposal suggests the use of gaze displays  to generate a new class of problems where learners can reflect on the problem-solving process and outcomes of a model (e.g., some one solving scientific graphs or radiology charts) by viewing the model's g aze data (John & Mitra, 2023). We hypothesized the pedagogical benefits of such tasks to be two-fold.  First, to nudge learners to be metacognitive about themselves and others as problem solvers. Second, to scaffold basic perceptu al processes and enable learners to notice intercon nected concepts that underlie the visually salient elements of a pr oblem.  In this study, we aim to show how such tasks, also called eye gaze interpretation (EGI) tasks can  achieve these benefits. We analyzed the verbalizations of two learners attempting to solve EGI tasks for a  simple and complex statistical reasoning problem.  Through the qualitative analysis of dyad verbalizations, we aim to address the following research questions:

1. How do learners interpret the problem solving process of another person from eye gaze displays?

2. Can EGI tasks catalyze metacognition about themselves or others as problem solvers?

3. Can EGI tasks scaffold problem solving by drawing attention to salient elements and noticing interrelated

domain concepts?

## Method

EGI tasks are made with the selection of eye gaze displays of successful and unsuccessful models prioritizing for the interpretability of the gaze display and the amount of task-relevant knowledge necessary for their interpretation (John & Mitra, 2023). Selecting the best gaze displ ays from a pool of 7 participants, EGI tasks were c reated for two base tasks, which were statistical reasoning pr oblems (details in John, 2023).   The simple base task had objective, unambiguous answers, such as interpretation of basic box plots or histograms, while the complex base task required extensive reasoning and had a two par t answer. The EGI tasks (EGI1, EGI2) presented pair s of successful and unsuccessful gaze models for each an swer. The complex base task, requiring a two-part answer, included two such pairs of eye gaze displays (EGI2a, EGI2b). The learners solved the EGI task as dyads and the recorded verbal interactions were transcribed verbatim for qualitative analysis. To analyze the dyad's att empt at interpreting the eye gaze displays (meta-task), their verbalizations were coded into two cat egories: "observations" when they took note of spec ific segments of the gaze display, and "interpretations" when they inferred the problem-solving approach or mental state of the model beyond what’s directly observable from the gaze dis play. The observations were further coded based on their relevance to forming interpretations and interpreta tions were coded as correct and incorrect based on the gaze display interpretations validated previously from m odels' think-alouds. The interpretative utterances of metacognitive nature were coded in terms of the metaco gnitive knowledge of oneself (MK-self), of the model (MK-model) and of problem solvers or problem solving processes in general (MK-universal) (Falvell, 1979).

In EGI1, the dyad struggled to form correct inter pretations of their observations prior to attempting the base task, and afterwards d irectly went on to form correct interpretations. Fo r EGI2a, the dyad failed to solve the complex base task. However, the gaze displays scaffolded them by highlighting important objects and the act of interpreting successful gaze  models made the dyad notice conceptual connections between salient objects. Interestingly, for both EGI tasks, the dyads tend to be metacognitive about themselves in the earlier phase and then metacognitive about the models’ problem-solving processes in the latter phases. ## Discussion

Learners intuitively identified relevant gaze segme nts and constructed interpretations about models’ p rocesses. Construction of interpretations were found to scaff old problem solving and nudged learners to draw fro m their metacognitive knowledge about themselves and the mo del in the context of the base problem. Their role as a scaffold was evident in the complex base problem wi th the gaze display guiding learner attention to re levant objects and the act of interpretation helping learners notice interrelated concepts underlying those objects. We also found that learners were more productive wi th EGI tasks when these tasks followed an attempt of the base task. Hence, future iterations shall explicitly instruct learners to attempt the base ta sk before the EGI task. Future work shall aim to identify the exact mechanisms through which EGI tasks scaffold problem solving.
