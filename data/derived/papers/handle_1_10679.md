# Illustrating the Interplay of Behavioral Patterns and Clinical Competencies in a Virtual Patient Simulation using Epistemic Network Analysis

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In this study, we used Epistemic Network Analysis (ENA) to illustrate how nursing students’ behavioral patterns related to their practice of clinical judgment, person-centered care, and professionalism competencies in a virtual patient simulation, Elsevier’s Shadow Health Digital Clinical Experiences™ (DCE). We obtained transcripts (dialog and click data) from two undergraduate nursing students who completed one Contact Tracing assignment and two Focused Exam assignments using the DCE. We coded each learning event based on three behavioral interaction types and six cognitive and psychosocial competencies recommended by nursing regulatory bodies. Findings revealed that the two students made strong connections to clinical competencies through questioning the patient in the Contact Tracing assignment versus answering system prompts for patient assessment and care planning in the Focused Exam assignments. This study unpacks how simulation-based learning facilitates competency-based education for nursing students’ practice readiness, offering valuable insights for future analytics and design.

## Introduction and relevant background

The goal of nursing education is to equip students with knowledge, skills, and attitudes essential for  providing optimal and safe care to diverse patients. Recently, frameworks such as the National Council of State Boards of Nursing Clinical Judgment Measurement Model (NCJMM,  Dickison et al., 2020) and The Essentials: Core Competencies for Professional Nursing Education (Am erican Association of Colleges of Nursing (AACN) et  al.,

2021) have guided the discussion on practice-ready nurses. Although evidence for competency-based evaluations

is growing, research examining student behaviors in simulations and their relationship with clinical competencies outlined in NCJMM and AACN is scarce (Cole, 2023).

Researchers have extensively used Epistemic Network  Analysis (ENA) (Shaffer, 2018) to trace connection patterns between various constructs in m yriad healthcare and health profession s education contexts (e.g. Shah et al., 2022). Notably, this study build s off the work of Wang and colleagues (2023) who an alyzed connections between clinical competencies in a sing le-user virtual patient simulation, Elsevier’s Shadow Health Digital Clinical Experiences™ (DCE). In doing so, they illustrated how multimodal  transcripts logged in the DCE can illustrate students’ emerging practice read iness in diverse patient scenarios. However, Wang a nd colleagues (2023) exclusively examined connections between clinical competencies. We argue that tracin g connections between clinical competencies and t he behavioral means to enact them will provide a better understanding of student learning in nursing simula tions. Thus, in this study, we employ ENA to model student behaviors in the DCE for two assignment types (Cont act Tracing and Focused Exam). Particul arly, we illustrate

(a) how two undergraduate students used specific beh aviors afforded by the DCE to practice a set of cli nical

competencies reflective of clinical judgment, person-centered care, and professionalism (AACN, 2021; NCJMM, 2020), and (b) whether these connections between be havioral patterns and clinical competencies differe d based on assignment type.

## Participants

We conducted this study on two nursing students (Rose and Roshni, pseudonyms) from the same cohort at a U.S. university. We selected these two students because they both had completed Focused Exam assignments in Gerontology and Mental Health in Fall 2022 and a Co ntact Tracing assignment in Community Health in Spr ing

2023. Wang and colleagues (2023) provide more details on each of these assignments.

Shadow Health Digital Clinical Experiences™ Typically, in a DCE assignment, and across scenarios from different content areas, learners are guided to perform physical examinations and interview patients to gat her information about their reasons for seeking care, m edical histories, and sociocultural backgrounds. Learners engage in an open clinical discussion with a virtua l patient using a text and a speech-to-text chat feature. Their inputs such as questions a nd statements trigger high-fidelity responses by the virtual patient. Learners document findings and interpretations of their assessment in a simulated electronic health record (EHR) utilizing multiple c hoice response options and open text formatting. DC E assignments use multiple interaction modalities to meet s pecific learning outcomes, with each assignment typ e serving a distinct instructional purpose. For instance, the Contact Tracing assignment focuses on helping students discuss sensitive topics such as HIV diagnosis. The DCE guides students in carrying out contact tracing calls and educating patients to alleviate their concerns. In contrast, the purpose of the Focused Exam centers o n helping students learn how to gather comprehensive patient data to make evi dence-based decisions. In this assignment, students are prompted to synthesize assessment find ings as well as create and communicate a care plan that is customized to patient needs for palliative care, chronic disease, or restorative care. We coded nursing student behaviors such as QUESTIONS, EXAM ACTIONS, and ANSWERS based on an automated labeling system in the DCE. Table 1 includes descriptions and examples of these codes.

Additionally, we coded log traces for the following  clinical competencies: ANALYZING CUES (AC), DIAGNOSIS AND PRIORITIZING HYPOTHESES (DPH), GENERATING SOLUTIONS (GS), CARE-MANAGEMENT AND COORDINATION (CMC), THERAPEUTIC COMMUNICATION (TC), and SUBJECTIVE ASSESSMENT (SA). Accounting for nuances involved in affect-intensive complex constructs and domain-specific jargon and terms, we coded TC, SA, and CMC manually under social moderation with p erfect agreements. Utterances were coded for AC, DP H, and GS using automated coding. A full codebook containing thorough definitions, examples, and coding decisions is provided by Wang and colleagues (2023). We omitt ed clinical competencies with weak or redundant interpretative power concerning this study.

To represent the interplay of behavioral codes with  clinical competencies, we applied ENA with a “masking function”, coercing the network only to il lustrate connections between clinical competencies and student behaviors as well as within student behavio rs, rather than within clinical competencies as done by Wang and colleges (2023). Thus, in the ENA model, nodes represent behavioral types in the DCE and clinical competencies; edge thickness represents the connections between them. To capture recent temporal context within each assignment, we specified a window length of fi ve utterances; we derived this from a qualitative understanding of the data. We defined the smallest unit of analysis as students within each scenario engaging in learning activities (n = 6, Contact Tracing, n = 28, Focused Exam). Tha t is, we considered the two students at a finer level of granularity. Then, we performed a dimensionality reduction using “means rotation” (Bowman et al.,

2021) to maximize the statistical power and interpr etability of differences between the Contact Tracing and

Focused Exam assignments. Through means rotation, w e calculated “ENA scores” for each unit, summary statistics of their individual connection patterns. Mean ENA scores were then calculated for each assignment. In our quantitative analysis, we found that student s behaved and enacted clinical competencies in alignment with each assignment’s instructional purp oses. Specifically, QUESTIONS drove more connections to nursing competencies in Contact Tracing (Figure 1a), while choosing ANSWERS drove more connections to nursing competencies in the Focused Exam (Figure 1b). The connections reflected in Figures 1a & 1b sug gest that the two assignments enabled students to practi ce various clinical competencies characterizing cli nical judgment, person-centered care, and professionalism. However, we can  better understand the contrast between student learning strategies in each assignment type by analyzing their difference plot (Figure 1c). Comparing the two assignment types, we performed a Mann-Whitney U-test on unit ENA scores (r =.40, p =.002**, U = 405). Figure 1c illustrates th is significant network contrast. We can see that students’ QUESTIONS connected more strongly with TC, CMC, and SA in Contact Tracing. These findings are unsurprising. Therapeutic communication is essential for nursing practice; students are encouraged to embrace empath y throughout the nursing process, especially in conte xts where promoting patient and community well-being is paramount. And, by design, the Contact Tracing assi gnment provides a greater opportunity for practicin g subjective data collection. This occurs through gui ding students to collect sensitive patient informat ion during subjective assessment and care management tasks. Students learn how to create a safe environment while questioning patients and discussing decisions about  their care. In contrast, in the Focused Exam, students’ ANSWERS connected with CMC, AC, DPH, and GS more strongly. The Focused Exam concludes with a care plan activity, in which students must recall insights fr om the assessment phase, applying clinical judgment  to indicate patients’ signs and symptoms, diagnose, plan short-term goals, and communicate recommended interventions.

## Discussion

In this preliminary study, we demonstrated how the interplay between student behaviors and clinical competencies reveals useful practice patterns in Elsevier’s Shadow Health® Digital Clinical Experiences™ (DCE). In the future, we plan to include more student data to dive deeper  into the behavior-cognition connections, and by extension, scale our investigation on the synergy between the learning design and analytics of students engaging in virtual simulations for cultivating practice readiness (Macfadyen  et al., 2020). Expanding upon this investigation will also allow us to obtain a more generalizable result  through our comparisons. Additionally, we will col laborate with instructors who use the DCE to derive actionable insights and co-design analytics for enhancing their clinical instruction with virtual simulations. In summary, this case study addresses a relevant gap in the nursing education literature on facilitating competency-based education using simulations, contributes to the growing body of ENA applications in health profession education, and provides promising directions for analytics and practice.
