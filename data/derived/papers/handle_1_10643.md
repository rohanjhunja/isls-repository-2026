# The Dual Benefit of Learning by Constructing Models of Complex Systems: Promoting Understanding of Electric Circuits and Model Construction Practices

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper presents a study that explores students’ learning of electric circuits in physics through constructing computational models of complex systems using the Much.Matter.in.Motion (MMM) platform. The study employed a quasi-experimental, pretest-intervention-posttest-control comparison-group design with 33 eighth-grade students using MMM compared to 23 students who followed a traditional curriculum using textbooks. Both groups had six 1.5-hour sessions and answered identical pre-and posttest questionnaires. Log files while using MMM were collected. Results show: Learning by constructing models with MMM better supports science concepts and systems-level understanding than those who followed the traditional teaching approach; In more advanced construction activities, students tend to construct fewer models but make more comprehensive changes within each model compared to earlier activities. This is the first study that analyzed the development of model construction practices through MMM log files.

Objectives Electricity serves as the primary form of energy transmission, and its comprehension carri es significant importance. In educational settings, the topic of e lectricity, particularly the flow of electric curre nt, presents a challenge for students across different age groups (Reiner et al., 2000). Students express several alternative mental models when attempting to explain the behavior of an electric current in a closed electric circuit. When instruction emphasizes the macro-level aspects of the circuit, thes e alternative mental models become resistant to cha nge (Cohen et al., 1983; Reiner, et al., 2000). Moreover, students encounter difficulties in conceptualizing the electric circuit as a system wherein the emergence of electr ic current arises from the interaction between its constituent elements (i.e., the flow of electrons and their int eraction with atoms within the wire). Even after re ceiving extensive instruction, students struggle to grasp f undamental concepts related  to the topic of  electric circuit s (Cohen et al., 1983; Eylon & Ganiel, 1990; Sengupta &  Wilensky, 2009).  Few studies addressed this issue by introducing one approach to learning about electric  circuits that presents electric circuits as a complex system. This approach addresses the particle-level behavior of the circuit and relates it to the aggregate level of the system. "Complex systems" refers to a cognitive framework (Bar-Yam, 1997) that describes the mechanisms underlying diverse phenomena that encompass the collective beh avior of numerous interacting entities  (Barabasi & Bonabeau, 2003; Turchin, 2003). These studies  used computer simulations that allowed students to explore prebuilt models of electric circuits (Frederiksen et al., 1999; Sengupta & Wilensky, 2009). Computer simulations are common tools for modeling the structure and dyn amics of complex systems and can show how macro-level patterns are generated from micro-level behaviors and interactions. However, these studies did not allow students to actually construct a micro-level model and observe the emergent system at the macro-level. To the best of our knowledge, no study has examined the affordances of constructing micro-level computational models of electric circuits, and this is the first gap that this paper addresses. In our study, we provided students with a modeling tool, the Much.Matter.in,Motion (MMM) platform (Levy, Saba, Hel-Or, & Langbeheim, 2018) for constructi ng computational models of complex systems in the domains of chemistry and physics. This platform allows students to build  computational model s of an electric circuit by drawing the macro-level elements, such as wires  and electric fields, and coding the micro-level entities, such as electrons and atoms.  The second gap pertains to advancing model construction practice s that accompany acquiring knowledge about electric circuits. Several studies have demonstrated the notable advantages of engagin g in learning th rough constructing computational models, as it facilitates comprehension of both sys tems and conceptual aspects (e.g., Saba et al., 2023). Nevertheless, these aforementioned studies did not explore how model construction practice s can be cultivated throughout the process of learning. Our study is the first that utilizes students' log files (generated when students construct a model) to delve into the progression of students' model construction through various activities. This study focuses on learning  about electric circuits through the lens of complex systems by constructing computational models. Its objective is to examine the impact of model con struction with MMM on the advancement of systems comprehension (at the mi cro-and macro-levels, as well as the connection between them) and the conceptual understanding of electric currents. Additionally, it delves deeply into the development of model construction practice during the learning process, recognizing that this fundamental practice holds the potential to enhance both systems comprehension and conceptual understanding of electric circuits.

## Theoretical framework

Construction of models of complex systems A scientific model is a representation of a phenomenon initially formulated with a specific objective in m ind (Gilbert et al., 2000). The process of constructing a model involves simplifying the phenomenon of interest based on its intended future application or purpose and serving as a means of explanation (Gobert & Buckley, 2000). In this study, we have chosen to employ the agent-based modeling approach (ABM) for the construction of models related to complex systems, which is grounded in complexity theory (Ba r-Yam, 2003). The ABM approach encompasses the repre sentation of systems through their constituent entities, assigning them with specific behaviors and facilitating interactions between them. By running the simulation, these entities can act and interact. Consequently, an emergent collective pattern may arise bottom-up. I have chosen to adopt this perspective in the current study due to its generativity in both science and in facilitating students' comprehension of the interrelation between micro and macro levels (Wilensky & Resnick, 1999; Levy & Wilensky, 2009). When learners pay close attention to the micro level of a system, they can better understand the entities, their actions, and interactions. This allows them to observe local patterns and relate th em to the collective macro-level regularities. Research has shown that using ABM  in learning is particularly effective in promoting conceptual learning and systems understanding. For example, in chemistry (Levy & Wilensky, 2009) and in physics (Sengupta & Wilensky, 2009), learners have benefited from this app roach. Numerous platforms support students’ construction of models of complex systems in science, such as using DeltaTick (Wilkerson-Jerde et al., 2015) and EvoBuild (Wagh & Wilensky, 2018) to learn about specific topics in the domain of biology. In this study, we used MMM (Levy, Saba, Hel-Or, & Langbeheim, 2018), which enables students to construct  computational models of complex systems in chemistry and physics (Saba et al., 2021).

Learning electricity by constructing computational models Studies on  electric current learning indicated that using dyna mic visual representations of electrical concepts promoted students' conceptual understanding of electricity (Sengupta & Wilensky, 2009; Frederiksen et al., 1999). Most studies utilized platforms focusing  on macro-level circuit representations (e.g., Zacharia, 2007; Roll et al., 2018; Kapici et al., 2022). These simulations are designed to function as vi rtual experiments, allowing students to manipulate circuit components like lightbulbs and "measure" the current and voltage. As an example, In the study conducted by Roll et al.  (2018), PhET Circuit Construction Kit (http://phet.colorado.edu/en/simulation/circuit-construction-kit-dc) was used as a tool for learning about electric circuits. The simulation mainly focuses on the construction of macro-level circuit components while also providing the o ption to observe the flow of electrons within the wire that results from connect ing all the circuit components. The representa tion of electrons serves as feedback that indicates the correct closure of the electric circuit. Therefore, the electric circuit i s not regarded as a complex system. The "Spark" platform was created by Beheshti et al. (2017) as an exhibit for science museums that allows children to construct an electr ic circuit and run a simulation alongside it. The s imulation displays the movement of electrons along the wires, which helps students understand the connection between the macro-level of the circuit and the micro-level of the electrons' flow. Beheshti et al. (2017) found that this connection improved students' conceptual understanding of electric circuits. However, it should be noted that the construction practice only occurs at the macro-level, similar to the PhET simulation. Although Spark is based on the complex systems approach, its construction process does not afford to construct models at the micro-level and observe the system at the aggregate level.  In our study, students use the ABM platform MMM to construct computational models of electric circuits.

The Much.Matter.in.Motion platform MMM (Levy, Saba, Hel-Or, & Langbeheim, 2018) is an ABM platform for constructing computational  models of a variety of complex systems in physics and chemistry (Figure 1). It was developed using NetLogo (Wilensky, 1999), and designed for the standard science curriculum in middle school. A model of an electric conductor is comprised of lines that represent macro-level boundaries and arrows that depict environmental fields, with micro-level entities portrayed as balls. To draw macro-level objects such as walls and fields, users can select the paintbrush tool located on the left side of MMM and create brushstrokes that are automatically transformed into arrows that indi cate the field's direction. Additionally, users can  specify the strength of the electric field. The model entities are represented as balls, which can be placed by cl icking them into position via the control buttons on the left s ide of the interface (see Figure 1). The balls can represent atoms and/or electrons, and each type of entity is consid ered a population with its own computational struct ure. For instance, in a model of an electric conductor, the atoms are coded as one population while the electrons are coded as another population. Once th e entities have been defined, users can press the "play" button to observe the emergent phenomena of the electric current. The dynamic behavior of the model is based on Drude's (1900) model, where the atoms (Figure 1; brown balls) act as stationary obstacles that impede the motion of free electrons (Figure 2; red balls) that are driven by the electric field.

## Method

The study is a quasi-experimental, pretest-intervention-posttest-control comparison group design. Fifty-six eighth-grade students (22 females, 34 m ales) from a regular urban school in Israel participated in the study. 33 students’ learning by constructing models is compar ed with 23 students’ learning with a normative curr iculum. As part of their science class, two groups of stude nts were taught the topic of electric circuits usin g different approaches to learning. The experimental group worked in pairs on their computers using MMM and the "Moving with Electrons" learning unit. This unit was specifically designed to meet the requirements of the National Science and Technology Curriculum for Elementary and Junior High Schools (2015) which focuses on the macro level of phenomena with minimal references to  micro-level mechanisms. "Moving with Electrons" learning unit emphasizes the relationship between phenomena obser ved at the macro level and the micro-level mechanism. It presents several phenomena related to electric current as co mplex systems and uses  MMM to construct computational models of these complex systems. The comparison group, on the other hand, used a nor mative science curriculum that involved lectures, experime nts, discussions, and textbooks. The normative appr oach to the topic of electric circuits is presented at the macro-level with minimal reference to the micro-level. It is based mainly on connecting several components of the elec trical circuit to explore related phenomena. Each group participated in six 1.5-hour lessons.

Data sources Both groups answered two identical pre-and post-test questionnaires. It consists of 13 items  and tests for conceptual knowledge and systems-level understanding related to the science phenomena targeted in the activities. Log files were collected from 17 computers throughout the learning process (six lessons). These files were produced by MMM and stored on the students’ computers. The files were collected at the end of each lesson.

## Results

Conceptual understanding Repeated Measure ANOVA was conducted to compare groups regarding the advancement in students’ conceptual understanding of Electric currents. Results showed a significant time effect (F(1,54)=116, p<0.01) from pre-to post-test. A significant interaction was revealed between time and group (F(1,54)=6.67, p<0.05). In the pretest, the groups were mostly similar (comparison: M= 0.46, SD= 0.21; experimental: M= 0.45, SD = 0.15). In the posttest, both groups displayed learning, however, a higher sc ore was obtained by the experimental group (comparison: M= 0.63, SD= 0.21; experimental: M= 0.72, SD = 0.13). Systems-level understanding The questionnaire items were divided into three systems components: micro, macro, and relating micro to macro. We used the coded answers to test the systems-level understanding and to compare the scores between the experimental and comparison groups. A repeated measure ANOVA shows a significant time eff ect (micro: F(1,54)=10.71, p<0.05, macro: F(1,54)=56.96, p<0.01, micro/macro: F(1,54)=26.49, p<0.01) from pre-to posttest. The interaction between time and group shows the superi or learning of the experimental group  only at the micro-level reasoning regarding the systems (F(1,54)=8.03, p<0.01). The analysis of systems-level understanding revealed that students in the experimental group si gnificantly enhanced their systems-level understanding of electric circuits compared with the comparison group. The student s worked mainly in pairs. Activity 1 was completed in Lesson 4, after exploring a model of electrons (with no atoms) inside a wire and investigating the effect of introducing an electric field on the electrons’ flow. During class room discussion, studen ts explored how different types of conductors can be distinguished at the micro-level. They realized that the electrons are the same in all conductors and that the difference between conductors is in the atoms within the material.  In Activity 1, the teacher guided the students to use MMM to compare models of two types of wires: a wire containing only electrons and a second wire contain ing both electrons and atoms. Running the simulation, s tudents could observe t he mechanism by which electrons change directi on when approaching an atom, thus slowing their mot ion, or creating resistance. They could also see how increasing the density and size of the atoms or increasing the resistance redu ces the intensity of the  emergent electric current. Activity 2 was completed in Lesson 5 and was unguided. Lesson 5 began with a class discussion that focused on the factors that may affect the current strength of the electric wires. The teacher then asked students to construct comput ational models to explore the effect of a wire’s th ickness on the electric current in a closed circuit (Activity 2). Running the simulation, students could observe that in a wider wire, electrons have more space to move through, or more path options, which enables increased electron motion, and a larger electric current.

Comparison of earlier and later modeling activities We analyzed the log files created during the construction of the mo dels for 10 (out of 17) pairs of students  who completed all the constructed computational models in  the two activities (Figure 2). All pairs began their construction process by drawing two wires, the electric field, and its direction, and then added electrons and atoms. We analyzed each model by examining changes students made to their previous model to create the  current one. Students’ actions were extracted in the log files. The stream of actions was segmented by model runs so that these actions were transformed into a list of changes that students performed in the model concerning the previous run. Bottom-up analysis of student’s log files of the two activities revealed ten types of changes that caused the models to be different, such as the number of electrons, number of atoms, and electrons’ headings. These changes are the column heads in Figure 2. The rows indicate the different runs for each pair of students. The gray blocks indicate a model change from the previous model. The first m odel is not shown because it has no previous model.  From Figure 2 one can analyze for each pair of students how many models were constructed in both activities and for each model what changes were conducted. In Activity 1 students constructed several versions of  the model by applying one or two changes at a time before runnin g the model. In Activity 2 the students constructed a smaller number of models and made several changes at a time before running their models. The average number of models is 6 (2.6) for Activity 1 and 3 (0.85) for Activity  2. The average number of changes per run in Activi ty 1 is 1 (0.29) and in Activity 2 is 2 (1.13). The more frequent changes were made to “Number of Electrons” and “Number of Atoms ” in both activities. The change of “Electric field strength ” was more frequent only in Activity 1. However, in Activity 2, an increase was shown in using a visual measurement tool while exploring the model (a yellow sensor that counts the number of electrons passing through it). One is the gradual way in which the students revised their models. Second is the ease with which students revised their models so they could experiment with a variety of models i n each activity. Third is the way that the platform supported the students in externalizing their thinking and running it as a dynamic model so that they could see the results of implementing their thinking in code. In the first model of Activity 1, the students drew two wires (light blue in Figure 3) without considering the width of the wires  and did not attempt to equate the wire widths. They  added an electric field and set its direction to be the same in both wires (blue arrows). They scattered the same number of electrons (red  balls) in both wires and added atoms (green balls) of a larger size to the second wire only. They made several changes that focused mainly on trying to draw two wires of the same width, changing the electric field strength and the number of electrons. In the final model, they added a counter to only one wire. In Activity 2, the students drew two wires with different widths and scattered a different number of electrons (red balls) and atoms (larger green balls) in the two wires. They constructed three additional models that mainly differed from the first by decreasing the number of electrons and atoms, changing the electric field  strength, and drawing two counters for each wire.  In both activities, students were gradually revising their models by changing one or two components of their model. In addition, we can see the ease of revising their models. In both activities, the students kept trying to draw wires that met the research question they were exploring (Activity  1: wires of the same width; Activity 2: wires of different widths). However, Figure 3 shows that in both activities, they did not maintain an equal number of electrons and atoms in both wires.

Finally, the platform provides students with visual measurem ent tools, such as an electron flow counter (using the “Draw counter” button) to observe the number of electrons passing through.  This tool helps student s explore the emergent current by measuring the elect rons that traverse through the counter over a certain time (yellow sensors in Figure 3). This measurement tool supported their exploration while running the constructed models. In Activity 2, students improved their use of this feature and drew counters in different places in the two wires compared with Activity 1, where they drew only one counter for one wire, which could not help them compare the numbers of electrons in the two wires.

To summarize, our results from analyzing the proces s of learning while constructing computational models reveal cultivation in students’ construction  practice from constructing more models that differ  by fewer changes in the earlier activity to constructing fewer models with more changes in the later activity. ## Scholarly significance

Electricity is viewed as a challenging topic in physics among students of all ages (Reiner et al., 2000). One of the reasons for this is introducing electric circuits at the macro-level without discussing the micro-level (Eylon & Ganiel, 1990; Sengupta & Wilensky, 2009). However, understanding the behavior of atoms and electrons a t the micro-level is crucial to comprehend macro-level variables such as current. This study focuses on learning about the larger balls are atoms,the smaller balls are electrons, the arrows, and the light blue background color are the field vectors; the conductors themselves have darker blue outlines; for a given conductor, increasing the number of atoms increases the resistance, and increasing the number of electrons increases their flow or the electric current. electric circuits using the agent-based modeling approach, emphasizing both micro and  macro-levels. The study results indicate that adopting this approach signif icantly contributes to facilitating both conceptual  learning and systems-level understanding  in physics (Brady et al., 2015; Sengupta & Wilensky, 2009). The micro-level concepts are particularly well-learned through a complexity approach that separate s and combines the micro and macro-levels in a system, as used in this research. Thus, the learning outcomes provide evidence that constructing models through ABM enhances students' conceptual le arning and helps in cultivating their micro-level understanding of systems (Sengupta & Wilensky, 2009; Samon & Levy, 2017; van Mil et al., 2013). Findings also demonstrate that exploring the micro-level promotes more sophisticated interpretations o f the behavior of electric circuits at the aggregate level. Furthermo re, using the ABM approach did not harm the macro-level reasoning of students, indicat ing that students can learn all three topics-the macro-level, the micro-level explanation, and modeling-within the time constraints of a typical school setting. This study focused on the model construction practic es of students during two activities that took place in different lessons. These activities ranged from highly guided to more open, allowing us to observe the gradual development of model construction practices. Results showed that students at a later stage of lea rning, in a more open activity, were more focused and constructed fewer models but made more changes per model. This indicates a shift in their view of scientific models, from se eing them as an illustration of phenomena to viewing them as a tool for explanation (Gobert & Buckley, 2000). Moreover, this shift was reflected in their approach to testing out different ideas to improve or compare with their in itial model, as opposed to just perfecting it. I also included an example of one pair of students who significantly i mproved their model construction practices and conc eptual understanding of electric circuits. Their model construction practices were characteriz ed by gradual revision of the models and ease in experimenting with different models. In addition, MMM assisted the students in expressing their thoughts and testing them as a dynamic model, allowing them to observe the outcomes of implementing their thoughts in code. Finally, students’ use of visual measurement at later stages indicates that at early stages, students deal with more conceptual issues, such as stabilizi ng a successful model, while later,  they explore a variety of conceptual structures in their models, such as inve stigating quantitative aspects of the conceptual relationships they were discovering in the process. These findings show that constructing models of complex systems in science has a dual benefit. It not only facilitates conceptual learnin g and systems-level understanding of the target topic, but it also promotes the practice of constructing m odels as a general scientific skill that can be tra nsferred and applied in various fields. These findings highlight  the importance of engaging in model construction a s a means of improving both subject-specific and general scientific knowledge. While this case study is promising, it is important  to explore and generalize these findings to a larg er sample of students. However, we must note that the sample size was small due to the COVID-19 pandemic, which prevented us from collecting data properly. In futu re work, I plan to conduct the study with a larger sample size, allowing me to explore the interrelationship between advancing model construction practices and developing a deep conceptual understanding of science topics on a larger scale. Moreover, it should be noted that the evaluation of the development of model construction practices in this study was based on only two activities, which presents another limitation of this study. To gain a deeper understanding of the learning process and how mod el construction practices can be improved, analyzing log files from all activities in future w ork is recommended. Finally, in order to enhance the generalizability of the findings, it is imperative to replicate the employment of the MMM learning approach in diverse settings and across varied subject matters.
