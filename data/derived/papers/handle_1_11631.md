# Game-Based Co-Design to Support Youth Moves to Elsewhere in Formal Learning Spaces

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This poster reports on a co-design research partnership between a sixth grade English teacher and a learning scientist to create a table-top roleplaying game (TTRPG) for a U.S. classroom. The goals of the codesign were to support critical engagement around power structures in ways that supported “moves to elsewhere” (Davis et al., 2020) for youth players. Findings discuss how game design choices such as word limits and the removal of the teacher as the narrator envisioned making space for youth to engage in processes of collaborative and consequential decision-making.

Purpose Educational scholars and learning scientists alike have lauded the alignment between gaming structures  and effective learning designs. Fishman & Hayward (2022) explore how gaming principles align with effective teaching practices. Barab et al. (2010) examine how games act as productive contexts for ap plying disciplinary learning. However, other scholars argue that gaming in classr ooms can often be used merely to “veil [garbage tasks] in glitz, glamour, and an additional simulat ed reality focused on inspiring student engagement at the cost of deeper meaning making” (York et al., 2022, p. 45). Many commonly used gami fication techniques in the classroom merely add competitive elements to reinforce particular route behaviors. Thus, it becomes important to support teachers with principles of game-based learning informed by critical theories of learning that attend to disrupting normative educational structures (Garcia, 2017a; Storm, 2023)  and students’ consequential meaning-making (Beach, 1999; Gutiérrez, 2020, Author1 & Colleague,  2023; Author3, 2024). This paper attends to ways that co-design partnerships can engage with gaming in ways theoretically aligned with critical and consequential educational goals. In this study, we are concerned with table-top roleplaying games (TTRPGs), analog collaborative games involving the creation of playa ble characters which players use to tell stories go verned by a system of central mechanics.

## Theoretical framework

Games both reflect cultural practices and engage so ciopolitical systems of power (Garcia, 2017b; Kim e t al., 2023). We are interested in how game-based learning candisrupt normative participation structures in classrooms, as opposed to gamification which enforces normative  structures  (York et al., 2022). Consequential learning moments allow youth to “repurpose tools and reorgan ize the participation structures” (Gutiérrez et al., 2020, p.

79) of learning activities in ways that are meaning ful for their own everyday practices. We see this a s connected

to speculative acts of self-determination, called “moves to elsewhere” (Davis et al., 2020), which are defined as “intellectual and social instances of reclaiming and reimagining the activity of learning” (p. 2).

## Methods

This study reports on a co-design research partnership (Mawasi et al., 2023; Penuel et al., 2022)  between a sixth grade English teacher and an educational researcher to create a table-top roleplaying game (see Table 1). This codesign was situated within a virtual practitioner i nquiry group (Cochran-Smith & Lytle, 2009)  focused on the implementation of TTRPGs in library and classroom s paces. Author1 and Author3, educational researchers  with expertise in gaming and literacy, recruited three p ractitioners from across New York State (a libraria n, a library volunteer, and a classroom teacher) who were using tabletop roleplaying games in their teaching contexts to join the inquiry group for the 2023-2024 school year to support development and expansion of their programs’ support of critical storytelling practices. As part of t he practitioner inquiry, we collected the following  data sources: 1) recordings of quarterly virtual discussions; 2) res ponses to post-discussion reflection surveys from inquiry members; and 3) semi-structured interviews. Author1 and Author3 also conducted visits to each s ite to observe youth programming and support inquiry members with program goals implementation, collecting 4) notes from co-design meetings with practitioners; 5) written arti facts (i.e. lesson plans, roleplaying designs); and  6) field notes from roleplaying sessions with youth. The authors engaged in an iterative memoing process and  thematic analysis of how co-design decisions supported goals relating to youth “moves to elsewhere.” This poster provides a preliminary analysis.

Game description This classroom TTRPG was designed to support youth engagement with Jeffrey McDaniel’s poem “The Quiet World,” which presents a dystopian world where the government limits the amount of words that citizens can use per day. This poem was planned as an introductory t ext to a unit about the American Revolution because  it addressed themes of authoritarianism.  In the game Author2 and Author1 designed  intended to be played before reading the poem, players create fantasy characters who are cursed wi th a magical spell that stops them from talking after they use fifty words a day. The players are divided into small groups and use a system of randomized dice mechanics to collaboratively imagine ways to circumvent obstacles on their way to confront the wizard who cast the curse. The game incentivizes players to us e more words to take more mechanically impactful ac tions, though players can also choose to use their words to build relationships with other players or characters.

## Findings

Author2 was interested in how games could be uniquely designed to accomplish multi-layered objectives relating to textual interpretation, historical meaning-making, and social-emotional learning. Author1 was interested in how the game could support criticality around power  structures in ways that were meaningful for youth participants. Here we discuss choices that Author2 and Author1 made in the game design to intentionall y open space for youth’s speculative moves to elsewhere (Davis et al., 2020) in formal classroom spaces. Moving toward resistance and rebellion In order to make sense of the poem “The Quiet World,” readers h ave to interpret how the characters feel about having their everyday actions curtailed by an outsi de force. During the co-design process, Author2 and Author1 discussed how games could simulate certain pow er imbalances while avoiding drawing from traumatic  political contexts. Though they originally considered several situations where students had to escape from being captured on a pirate ship or spaceship, the idea of students simulating capture was rejected by the school’s administration. The team pivoted to framing the game with a powerful wizard making a unilateral decision to put a word limit on a magical kingdom, and the fictional residents of t he town were framed as experiencing legislation wit hout representation. This allowed space for youth to ima gine what it might be like to experience authoritar ian power structures and engage in fictional resistance while avoiding the potential for traumatic connections with real events or administrative/parental censorship.

Moving toward democratic decision-making In addition to interpretive meaning-making goals, Author2 cared about supporting students with democratic ways of interacting. She was interested in how games cou ld help students practice creative decision-making and collaborative (instead of competitive) interactions. Based on this goal, Author2 decided to create wha t is known as a “Game Master-less” game, or a game without a facilitator or a na rrator. Instead of Author2 structuring the activity and telling students what to do at each point of the game, Author1 and Author2 worked to create scaffolds like a turn order and guiding questions to help students navigate decisions themselves as they worked through different scenes of the game collaboratively. This intentional relinquishment of teacher control was i ntended to allow youth to “reclaim and reimagine” what this ac tivity meant to them (Davis et al., 2020, p. 2), al lowing structural space for negotiations around intellectual and social authority (Langer-Osuna et al., 2020). Moving toward dialogue as a valuable currency The game design limited youth to fifty words or les s during gameplay. This mechanic turns character di alogue into a valuable currency for decision-making. As youth encounter story obstacles, the priorities of their character become very clear as they make visible decisions ab out what actions are important enough to spend word s on. This not only emphasizes the relational importance of language (a theme in the poem) but also relates to youth agency and decision-making about what narratives they will collectively create.

## Conclusions

We show here how Author1 and Author2 used game-based learning structures to create space for youth  “moves to elsewhere” (Davis et al., 2020), a concept drawn  from after-school learning spaces that emphasizes “breaking away… from boundaries born of schooling” (p. 17). TTRPGs made for classroom spaces, which invite navigation of academic and everyday practices (Gutiérrez et al., 2020) and allow for coauthored negotiation around structures of storytelling (Author3, 2024), align well with Da vis et al.’s (2020) call for “increasingly flexible models of design” to support youth self-determination. We invite educators and researchers to further explore the possibilities of transformational learning through the creation of TTRPGs for formal learning spaces.
