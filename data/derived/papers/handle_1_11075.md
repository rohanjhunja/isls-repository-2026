# Exploring Gender Differences on the Impact of Initial Early Coding Motivation and Prior Knowledge of Computational Thinking

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
The relationship between motivation and computational thinking (CT) performance yields mixed findings in existing research on elementary-aged students. This study explored potential relationships among Latinx, upper elementary learners to consider opportunities for broadening participation. Findings revealed that motivation neither influences their CT performance nor does CT skills affect motivation reciprocally. Similarly having higher initial motivation does not influence changes in performance, but having above-average prior CT knowledge influences changes in motivation among Latino students.

## Introduction

In computer science education, a participation gap among Latinx students arises notably at the seconda ry level, even more so among Latinas, leading to subsequent disparities in higher education and workforce representation. Despite students' chosen career paths, computer science has evolved into an indispe nsable interdisciplinary skill crucial for full participation in society. Further studies on the relationship between early coding mo tivation and computational thinking (CT) performance among eleme ntary students are needed, which have predominantly focused on each aspect independently (e.g., Liu et al., 2022). This exploration seeks to enhance our understanding of motivational factors that shape computational thinking (CT) development, influencing approaches to students' engagement in computer science (CS) learning opportunities.

## Methods

We explore the relationship between early coding mo tivation and CT performance among Latinx upper elementary students (i.e., 3rd and 4th grade). The students received a year-long computational thinking curriculum that consists of unplugged and code-block based activities that align with a structured inquiry approach. Students took all pre-tests before starting the curriculum and a post-test at the end of the school year, regardless if t hey completed the curriculum. This analysis comprised s olely of students  who completed all pre and post-tests, forming a sample size of 124. Within this group, 58% were female students, and 36% were designated as English language learners (ELL).

The Elementary Student Coding Attitude Survey (ESCAS) i s used to measure motivation and incorporates measures for confidence, interest, uti lity, social influence, and perception of coders. The measure was modified to consider the construct of attainment and added items for social influence, interest, and perception of coders that are reflective of the cultural value s of our Latinx students and critical computing per spectives that can influence motivation.  With the new items, the measure  maintained reliability (alpha=.9419). The mea sure consists of a 6 point Likert scale and 37 items, and a total composite score was used to represent the  level of motivation for the student. The assessment of CT relied on the Assessment of Computing for Elementary Students (ACES), gauging proficiency in sequences and loops. This assessment utilizes a combination of code blocks and word problems to measure students' comprehension.

The analysis comprises of four ordinary least squar es (OLS) regression models employing clustered errors by the teacher. The first two models explore  the direction of the relationship between motivati on and CT using post test scores. All models consists of  control variables of gender (male=1), English langu age fluency of the student (English language learner =1), teacher (seven classrooms), and grade (fourth=1) to accommo date the curriculum progression differences among grades as third-grade classrooms completed only half of the curriculum. The last two models also explore the di rection of the relationship between motivation and CT but focus on the extent to which having an above-average or below-average initial level informs changes over time and is moderated by gender.

## Results

Table 1 displays the outcomes of distinct Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) models. The initial model investigates if post motivation levels influence post CT performanc e, while the subsequent model examines the reverse relationship. Despite the insignificant relationship between these variables (p >.05) acros s both models, the first model indicates motivation can account for a larger  variance in CT performance (21%) with markedly low er standard errors (0.02) compared to the reversed model. The third and fourth models highlight the influence of pretest scores on subsequent changes and  both depict an insignificant association between mo tivation and CT in either direction. However, the fourth model reveals  that male students with high prior knowledge exhib it significantly higher motivation (p <.01) than their male counterparts with low prior knowledge and female peers, regardless of their prior knowledge levels.

## Discussion

Prior literature reveals mixed findings as to when motivation and learning influence each other for this age group, and such is the case in computer science education (Ojeda-Ramirez et al., 2022). Contrary to strong predictiv e relationships, this study unv eils a limited mutual predictability between motiva tion and computational performance. However, it signifies an avenue for fu rther investigation into moderating and mediating r oles. At the same time, we begin to see a gendered differenc e in the extent to w hich having prior knowledge of coding concepts can greatly inform motivation among male students. This suggests that entering a learning space with a level of prior knowledge can inform a positive deve lopment of motivation among Latinos. Additionally, we see that the stronger models involve the direction wher e motivation influences CT. This study is limited a s there are clear confounding variables that can explain the in significant relationship as suggested in model one,  with classroom and designation as English Language Learner as significant predictors of CT performance.
