# Exploring How Rural Teachers’ Place Identity Relates to Place-Based Curriculum Implementation

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Implementing place-based education (PBE) requires teachers to access place resources as pedagogical tools. In this qualitative study, we focus on how rural teachers (n = 17) drew upon their place experiences to define their (dis)connections to place to access resources for a PBE unit. Results suggest that how teachers described their teaching place experiences was linked to whether they viewed instructional resources as affordances or constraints to PBE implementation.

## Introduction

Place-based education (PBE) “emphasize[s] the connection between a learning process and the physical place i n which teachers and students are located” (Yemini et  al., 2021, p.1). PBE employs places as pedagogical  tools to provide authentic experiences that contextualize student le arning. With PBE, students identify problems within places they inhabit and learn how to improve these places. With learning content rooted in local place, PBE is a compelling approach to support science learning for  environmental sustainability tailored to students’  daily lives. Rural environments, and rural teachers, are often r omanticized as holding rich ecological and communit y ties, seemingly pre-made for creating meaningful PBE experiences (Avery, 2013). However, working as a teacher at a rural school does not mean automatic  access to the rural place to teach PBE meaningfully (Azano, 2 011). While there is extensive research on the ways in which PBE benefits students, there is little exploration into how teachers, particularly rural teachers undertake PBE within their local place (Yemini et al., 2021). Major issue addressed Gruenewald (2003) proposed a sociological dimension of place that considers the ways in which individuals place meaning upon spaces they routinely inhabit. “Place” is a complex mental construct comprised of experiences individuals use to define themselves and others. As  individuals mentally construct place, they formulate feelings of inclusion or exclusion within those places. Feel ings of connection to place can foster  “place identity,” or the notion that place can become integral to one’s self concept (Peng et al., 2020). In this current work, place identity refers to the extent to which a teacher defines themselves as connected or disconnected to the rural school and/or rural community. That level of connection to place may impact how teachers access place-based resources, which exist as physical (e.g., buildings, materials), social (e.g., relationships), knowledge (e.g., content, pedagogical), and cultural (e.g., rurality) (Rivera Maulucci et a l., 2015). Resources also exist at different contex tual layers of community, school, and individual. For example, if the teachers view themselves as connected to the place, they may perceive resources at multiple levels that are accessible to their teaching. However, if they see themselves as external, or disconnected to the place, then they may view these resources as constraints (Bennion et al., 2022). Methodological approach This exploratory qualitative study focuses on the w ays rural middle school science teachers implemente d a PBE unit on energy-efficient building design. There were 17 sixth through ninth grade rural scien ce teachers who volunteered to participate in this study. The teach ers ranged in experience from one to 21 years of cl assroom science teaching. Prior to teaching the unit, all t eachers participated in a fa ce-to-face or virtual workshop taught by the project team. Data collection for each teach er included lesson plans, weekly reflection surveys  about unit implementation, and a post-unit 60-minute semi-structured interview. The research question was: How does rural middle school science teacher place identity impact  their perceptions of resources to teach a place-based energy literacy unit? All teacher data was used for qualitative content analysis. The individu al teacher was the unit of analysis. Team members wrote detailed memos for eac h teacher to capture their place identity, and reso urce perceptions. The final themes were the result of a consensus-based process (Harry et al., 2005).

## Major findings

Three themes emerged. In the first theme, four teac hers identified as place disconnected and all resources served as constraints that prevented unit implementation. They discussed disconnect between themselves and their school communities. In the second theme, s even teachers identified both moments of connection s, and disconnections from their rural schools. These teachers viewed their community and school resources as constantly shifti ng between affordance and constraint, as they shifted between connection and disconnection. Resource affordances included supportive administrators and engaged stud ents; resource constraints included the small size of their communities with insufficient resources. Finally, six rural teachers defined themselves as connected within their place and all resources at all levels were affordances. These teachers’ connections were so tightly wound with in their community, they did not see themselves as separate from place or from PBE. These teachers spoke in terms of “we” to indicate themselves, their students, their schools, and the community.

## Discussion and implications

Limited research in rural education has examined th e importance of characteristics, affordances, and c hallenges faced by rural teachers (Azano, 201 1). Effective PBE in rural schools involves more tha n teaching in a rural school; it requires viewing oneself as connected to place. This can become integral to self-concept, so that This connection is a place identity). The teachers described experiences in which they placed themselves as outsiders (theme one) or on a continuum of insiders (theme two and three). Furthermore, these experiences appeared to be linked to whether they viewed differ ent levels of resources as constraints or affordanc es to PBE. Those who conceived themselves as disconnected viewed the constraints as insurmountable (theme 1), while those who perceived themselves as connected by being “rural t eachers” resolved school level constraints (theme 2). Finally, those who identified themselves as fully embedded in their ru ral contexts viewed all resource levels as affordances (theme 3). Literature on place identity suggests that person-place bonds can be fostered through both socially and physically based attachments (Scannell & Gifford, 2010). Supporting teachers in resource exploration for PBE, may support viewing resources as affordanc es, and provide a foundation for place connection. PBE curricular tools should address guided explorations  prior to unit implementation so that teachers can try out different resources within their rural place. Ultimately, fostering stronger connections between educators, schools, and communities can unlock the full potential of PBE for student learning.
