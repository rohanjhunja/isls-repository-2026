# An Integrated Approach to Examining How Science Teachers Build Expertise in Field Geology

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study examines the development of secondary science teachers’ expertise in geology as they observe sedimentary rocks in the field. We describe how their knowledge-building was shaped over time by the learning tasks, social and material tools, and physical space. The analysis revealed shifts in the teachers’ complex mechanistic reasoning resulting from affordances in instruction and the physical setting. The results document the development of expertise in field geology as a multifaceted process.

Scientific observation as a multidimensional process The development of expertise is multidimensional and includes  knowledge-based perceptual patterns (Reimann & Markauskaite, 2018), along with tool use, languag e practices, and identity work (Cicourel, 1999; Carr, 2010; Braden, 2022). Scientific observation is used pervasively in reform-based science instruction as learners generate data and evidence  to support knowledge building. Yet, learners experience challenges connecting observation with relevant disciplinary knowledge (Trumbull et al., 2 005) across academic levels from elementary through university-level (Balliet et al., 2015; Ford, 2005). Furthermore, few studies have examined the development of observational expertise from a multidimensional perspective that integrates knowledge with learning tasks, social and material tools, and physical space.

Building from prior work (Barth-Cohen & Braden, 2021), w e draw from coordination class theory (Levrini & diSessa, 2008)  to conceptualize the development of expertise in field geology. Within this theory, extractions are “a perceptual component, which is, essentially, all possible observations relevant to determining a concept’s parameters” (diSessa et al. 2015, p. 19),  (e.g. color, size, and texture of a rock) while inferences are forms of knowledge that “provide a sense of relevan ce for extractions” (pp. 36 –37) (e.g. knowledge about the depositional environment, such as a desert or deep ocean). In a field geology  context, learners can experience varying amounts of success or challenges with gener ating extractions and connecting those extractions to inferences, before they can apply a concept successfully across new contexts (e.g. sedimentary formations deposited over time across many formations) (Barth-Cohen & Braden, 2021). Given the nuanced dependency of expertise on knowledge structures (Reimann & Markauskaite, 2018), we may expect learners making extractions to fixate on features that are easier to notice (e. g., the color or existence of a fossil) instead of considering larger perceptual patterns across a physical, spatial, or temporal region.  O ur context is a highly designed learning environment that includes physical geologic formations, pedagogical scaffolds  (Reiser, 2004), and inquiryoriented tasks that engage learners in scientific practices collaboratively (e.g., Osborne, 2014), all of which impact scientific observation. We address the following: (RQ1) In what ways did teachers use extractions in kno wledge building? (RQ2) In what ways did the learning environment afford and/or constrain teachers’ knowledge-building?

## Data collection and analysis

The data for this analysis draw s from a six-day field experience in Southern Utah, USA in which secondary science teachers observed sedimentary structures throughout each day. Participant recruitment prioritized teachers who were teaching Earth Science out-of-field (without formal training in geology) and teac hers with limited access to high-quality professional development. The field guide included scaffolds aimed at moving the teachers from more structured observations to more open-ended observations while helping them construct scientifically accurate explanations. Additionally, much of the instruction leveraged small group work and discussion to engage teachers in the scientific practices of knowledge building (e.g., N RC, 2012). The culminating assignment was to generate a stratigraphic column depicting the geologic histo ry and paleo-environments that contributed to the region’s formation. Data collected consisted of audio and video recordings from teachers wearing GoPros or audio recorders, and teacher-produced artifacts (field notes, nightly reflections, and stratigraphic column posters).

Using qualitative thematic analysis of point-of-view video data, we compare teachers’ knowledge building at the same site, on their first day at the site (Wednesda y-Day 4) to when they revisited the site to prepare a final stratigraphic column (Friday-Day 6). The analysis process began with reviewing field notes and video data, then we generated transcripts, wrote narratives, and engaged in open-ended coding that then transitioned to more structured coding for extractions and inferences.

Knowledge building on days 4 and 6 as shaped by the learning environment We found shifts in teachers’ reasoning across the two site visits. On the first site visit, participants primarily used one piece of evidence (extraction) at a time as the  defining feature of a unit with minimal synthesis and limited consideration of underlying mechanisms. One teacher refers to the units as “dark stuff” “white stuff, a nd “shell stuff.”  The teacher may have been recognizing each unit as having one distinct and in dependent extraction, or possibly, they viewed these as the most important or obvious features. There were also longer conversations that involved a prominent extraction about the existence or lack th ereof, of shells as the defining extraction when identifying a  unit that may have been formed by either a freshwater or marine environment. Across these representative moments in time, inferences about th e type of depositional environment are generated based on single extractions without synthesis, thereby contributing to limited consideration of an underlying mechanism. During this visit, the teachers’ knowledge building was shaped by the learning envir onment, via the learning tasks, the social and material tools available to them, and the physical space in which they engaged in these tasks. Specifically, we found that the learning tasks (identifying four distinct geologic units  as supported by a graphic organizer t-chart) inspired teachers to engage in binary thinking. Similarly, the social tools, including “talk moves” along with the physical environment, productively constrained the knowledge building and afforded opportunities for more nuanced reasoning.

On the second visit, teachers’ extractions and inferences were more coor dinated across time, formation layers, and physical scales, revealing more complex and mechanistic reasoning. When discussing the causes of an increase in energy that formed the depositional environment, two extractions are mentioned  (Michael: Much smaller grain size // Elliot: The shells) that support two possible explanations for why there were changes in water level (“OK Because you went from lakes to rivers. -Which says water went down. // Water went down...Or ground got pushed up a little bit and then [hmmm] sea-Level caught up to that little.”)  During this visit, the teachers’ knowledge-building was similarly shaped by the learning environment, but in different ways that earlier. The learning task  guided the teachers to  build a coherent story of how and why the landscape  had changed over geologic time  in the summative assessment  of a stratigraphic column. The social tools  were different as the teachers worked without an instructor and relied on  their own intuitive ways of fluidly collaborating. They also walked to a higher vantage point that allowed them to make sense of the bigger picture in new ways, which points to a supportive change in how they interacted with the physical space. On day 4, the teachers may have missed this higher vantage point because the instructor-imposed pedagogical constraints on the physical space, while those constraints were absent on day 6. All of these affordances of the learning environment on Day 6 were made possible because of when this day occurred, given t he teacher’s familiarity with the geological unit, their peers, and reasoning approaches within field geology.  Thus, both the learning environment and experiences were critical in supporting the development of their mechanistic reasoning.

## Discussion and conclusion

Documenting the multifaceted process of knowledge b uilding as shaped by the learning environment, incl uding the learning tasks, social and material tools, and physical space is important for gaining a more comp rehensive understanding of the complexity involved in scienti fic observation and in the development of expertise. Earlier scholarship on scientific observation tended  to emphasize knowledge (Chinn & Malhotra, 2002) and simplify the complexity of the process (e.g. Balliet et al., 2015). However, the development of expertise is a multifaceted process that involves the development of t ool use, language practices, and identity work (Braden, 2022; Cicourel, 1999; Carr, 2010;), reflected in how  secondary science teachers buil d expertise with scientific observation in field geology. Our results highlight the importance of integrating intellectual approaches to gain a more comprehensive picture of knowledge building in field learning env ironments (Ayotte-Beaudet, et al., 2024; Stroupe & Carlone 2022).
