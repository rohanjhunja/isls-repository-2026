# Co-design and Co-Creation of Knowledge in Collaborative Teacher Teams

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper examines collaborative knowledge creation in teams, through analyzing the ways a team of academic teachers worked collaboratively on curricular innovation over the period of an academic year. The focus is on the creation of shared knowledge artifacts (syllabus, assignments, assessment, etc.), which serve both as focus of their collaborative co-design process and as mediating tools for innovating their teaching practices. The paper proposes a conceptual framework entailing epistemic, interactional and objectual dimensions, and spans interactions, resources, and outcomes being created or being instrumental in the process. The qualitative analyses of the team’s dialogues, products, and reflections show that knowledge creation in teams is constituted through a combination of processes, dimensions and mechanisms, which materialize intonew knowledge or novel practices for individual and collective benefits.

## Introduction

Mobilizing and  creating new knowledge by teams of professionals is increasi ngly relevant in the current the landscape of academic professions, suffused with  a quickly evolving body of knowledge and versatile digital technologies. This creates a need for constant rene wal of practices, which, in turn, require collective knowledge and action. C ollaboration in teams  have the potential to  create environment s that facilitate individual and collective knowledge and competence development, as well as  provide solutions or advance practice. Creating knowledge in teams implies team members acting jointly to generate new ideas and inputs of an epistemic nature and materialize them into artifacts or practices of instrumental value in the teams’ work (Damşa, 2014). Teamwork has been studied in several contexts, including the workplace, organisations, and education. A large body of work has focused on team functioning and performance, with an emphasis on team effectiveness, team composition and functioning, or factors influencing team functioning and decision making (e.g., Salas et al., 2017). A more recent wave of research inspired by sociocul tural, sociomaterial, and sociology of knowledge led to studies of collaborative work that focus es on social interaction in teams (e.g., Nerland & Damşa, 2019). Such studies expose the way teams capital ize, at a micro-level, on intellectual, social, and digital-material resources available in their environment. The latter studies touch upon the way knowledge is involved in team activities, but the focus is not necessarily on the ways teams engages in creating new ideas, solutions, and artefacts. This paper examines collaborative knowledge creation in teams, by combining conceptual models and an empirical examination of teamwork to provide the ory-grounded interpretations of how knowledge creation unfolds. It does so by analyzing  the ways knowledge is created by a team of academic teachers, who worked collaboratively on curricular innovation over the period of an academic year. The focus is on the creation of shared knowledge artifacts (syllabus, assignments, assessment forms, etc.), which serve both as focus of their epistemic inquiry and as mediating tools for improving and  innovating their teaching. The paper proposes a conceptual framework for knowledge creation in teams and applies it to interpreting knowledge co-creation work (i.e., team dialogues and products, as well as the teachers’ reflections regarding the team and their collaborative efforts). Conceptualizations of knowledge creation in teams Research concerned with learning and knowledge work  has, in the past decade s, undergone a major epistemological shift that emphas ises knowledge as being  created, as opposed to simply being absorbed in a mechanistic manner. The knowledge creation metaphor (Paavola & Hakkarainen, 2005) explicitly addresses the creation of knowledge in collaboration, in small gr oups of professionals/learners, and the material ization of created knowledge into concrete output. Collaborative knowledge creation is a specific type of learning or work; it is intentional in nature and directed toward delivering a knowledge output (i.e., an intellectual product, service, or technology). The core notions are that participation in (social) activities benefits cognitive processes, and it strongly emphasizes the aspect of collaboration around shared objects of activity. The knowledge building model (Bereiter, 2002) highlights learning through work with new ideas, which models experts’ work and usually takes place as problem-solving. Knowledge is considered to consist of conceptual art ifacts that can be systematically produced and developed  through collective discourse. Du Chatenier and colleagues (2009) emphasize an epistemological distinction between two kinds of knowledge, tacit and explicit and pose that the aggregation level at which the collaborative knowledge creation proce sses converge is essential. In externalizing and sharing, individuals verbalize and share their knowledge with other professionals,  resulting in distributed knowledge. Interpreting and analyzing involves absorb ing and analyzing what one hears and interpret s; decentralized knowledge is the result. In negotiating and revising, professionals gather and order different interpretat ions and build shared understanding and meaning. While combining and creating, professionals accumulate and create new ideas, resulting in co-created knowledge (e.g., an action plan, new technologies). The models highlight mechanisms at the core of the knowledge creation process and allow us to identify (a) activities central to teams’ collaborative work, (b) principles for team productivity, and (c) ways to enhance knowledge creation in teams. Dimensions and mechanisms of knowledge creation in teams Three dimensions within which processes of knowledge are generic and cut across the conceptual models are used to explain the mechanism of knowledge creation. An epistemic dimension emphasize the  cultural, social, or digital-material context, and knowledge is something ‘real’, materialized, objectified, and subjected to consensus and that k nowledge processes, such as  teamwork, are have linkages with structures of collective action and knowledge (Nerland & Damsa, 2019). In teachers’ work, knowledge work is expressed mostly through epistemic resources available in local communities, with the exception, perhaps, of general policy docu ments. The development of knowledge is often based on face-to-face interaction and individual efforts. The objectual dimension emerges as k nowledge w ork and learning are  mediated by symbolic and material objects. Objects are carriers of knowledge and ‘capture exper iences, ideas, thoughts and goals’ (Markauskaite & Goodyear, 2017, p. 200), aiding the  creation of  new meaning. Knowledge objects or artifacts embody the type of activity they mediate. The most general include material art ifacts (e.g., a pen), intangible artifacts (e.g., software or reports), and processes (e.g., manufacturing processes). Artifacts can be instrumental (i.e., used to create other artefacts), historical (e.g., embody knowledge created in time), and  can be the outcome of knowledge work (e.g., can be shared, articulated, or further developed). The complexity of this construct lies in its dynamic position in relation to the interactional process. Finally, the c ollaborative work on knowledge objects require s (productive) interaction (Damşa, 2014), an interactional dimension. Within a sociocultural framework, personal knowledge  becomes external ised in various forms —actions, artifacts, or language—and is  then incorporated into  communication with other individuals. Through this iterative, intersubjective process, knowledge is exchanged, adjusted, and elab orated, which requires intersubjectivity. Searching for common background and mutual ‘mindreading’ involves coordinating with others on common goals and interests. Creating common ground for engagement  happens through shared understanding and prepares for the final sta ge, joint activity/knowledge construction. Collaborative k nowledge creation is realised through interaction and mediation, among which communication with others is most important.

Empirical illustration: Curriculum innovation in teacher education The context of this study was  a department for teacher education in a large Scandinavian university. The study examined an orchestrated effort by a group of academic teachers to renew the curriculum in a teacher education part-time program. Subject to renewal was a compulsory course in pedagogy, taught through a flipped-classroom approach. The participants were seven academic teachers, ranging in age from 26 to 60 years old, with teaching experience at the university level ranging from one semester to 20 years. All had a background in pedagogical studies or discipline didactics; five of them held academic positions, while two held teaching-only appointments. The teacher team organized co-design meetings at regular intervals of two months over the course of an academic year. The meetings usually lasted four hours each. Prior to each meeting, materials (i.e., slides, notes, a readings list, and reflections) were prepared by team  members. The designed curriculum was implemented gradually and self-reflection on experiences with teaching were collected after each teaching sesssion. Data and analytical framework The illustrative analyses in this paper are present ed in the form of a vignette. This vignette was created based on the entire corpus of data that included observations of team meetings (audio recordings and field notes), collected team products (i.e., course materials), reflections on teaching sessions, and interviews with the six participa nts. The audio recordings were transcribed verbatim and anonymized. The selection of  data from the  corpus was performed by identifying relevant episodes of interaction, corresponding t o relatively bounded sequences of speech or encounters in group discussions (Linell, 2009). The episodes indicate the general thematic o rientation of the discussion. The first level of analysis involved generating a descriptive account of the content of the selected data and yielded information about  explicit dialogue  linked tonew knowledge, concrete actions towards developing shared art ifacts, and explicit reflections by team members.  In the  second level  of analysis, the descriptive account served as a stepping-stone for interpretations made possible by the thematic dimensions. The analytical framework used for interpretation builds  on notions elaborated in this paper, which highlight essential mechanisms and dimensions of the knowledge co-construction process: inter action, object-orientedness, and embeddedness in epistemic cultures of the profession. Types of actions in knowledge creation processes (Damşa,

2014) enabled interpretations of the joint knowledge work in the team.

Finally, the team members’ overall reflections on the team  and knowledge work make s a case for the way knowledge creation processes capital izes on epistemic, interactional, and objectual dimensions. These knowledge and resources distributed within the grou p anchored the knowledge work in a rich pool of dom ainspecific resources  and were teased out, ana lyzed, externalized, selected, combined, and elaborated on through dialogue and productive interaction, which the team engaged in deliberately. In this way, meaning was made collectively of both existing and  emerging knowledge. The knowledge objects developed are viewed as ways to ‘freeze’ the knowledge created by the team. However, they also help to synthesize the produced knowledge and are a means to keep the collaborative process focused on the goals and its outcomes. Bridging models and dimensions in enacted knowledge creation in teams This paper clarified conceptualizations and mechanism through which prof essionals work together to generate knowledge. Dialogical interaction during an academic teacher team’s revealed not only interactional elements, but the synthesis/integrative elements by Du Chatenier and colleagues, i.e., externalizing, sharing, and negotiating. The team ’s reflections allowed to qualify the nature of this intersubjective practice  as deliberate, with team members seeking actively the exchange of knowledge. The framework features of knowledge co-creation identified by Damşa (2014) are also illustrated here. The epistemic dimension and associated processes are represented through the embeddedness in the knowledge culture of the teache r education domain and the enactment of knowledge construction at the local level (Nerland & Damşa, 2019). This aligns with the notion that creating new lear ning designs builds on theoretical and practical kn owledge from these knowledge domains, practices and cultures. The local enactment is through the team ’s knowledge creation work, resulting in a new curriculum structure for seminars and the formulation of assignments. The objectual dimension is reflected in the way knowledge objects and artifacts are materialized, aligned with sociocultural and sociomaterial notions. The purposeful way these art ifacts give direction to and material ize the team’s work provide evidence of how knowledge co-construction is shaped by and into these objects. The knowledge co-creation identified in this study have impact on the context in which they emerge and at different levels  of the collaborative co-design process. At team  level, knowledge creation generates fuel for new activities in new ways, with teachers learning both from the knowledge creation process, through producing and implementing the knowledge objects, and through reflection. At individual level, the processes and outcomes of this knowledge creation effort lead to the activation of knowledge in a shared environment and the advancement of individual knowledge and practice, unattainable through individual efforts.
