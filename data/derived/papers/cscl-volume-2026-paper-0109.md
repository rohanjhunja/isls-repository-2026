# “He’s Gonna Say It’s Trash”: Students’ Emotional Experiences with AI Feedback in Collaborative Scientific Inquiry

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study explores how 5th grade students playing a scientific inquiry game interact with (and react to) AI-generated feedback presented by in-game agent characters. We explore how AI conversational agents impact student inquiry and emotional engagement as students build socioscientific arguments. Results indicate that while students initially take up agent feedback to improve their argumentation, prolonged cycles of revision lead to frustration and negative reflections as students struggle to utilize AI feedback.

Across classrooms, many educators are exploring how to integrate the latest advances in generative AI. Early evidence highlights that AI feedback could enhance student engagement and motivation in learning games (Mehrvarz et al., 2025) and that AI conversational agents can support students in monitoring and reflecting on their learning (Järvelä et al., 2025). However, a critical aspect of this integration lies in understanding how interaction with AI agents affects students’ social and emotional wellbeing. Emotions play a central role in shaping how and what students learn (Jaber & Hammer, 2016), so it is vital to understand how AI agents impact learners’ affective experiences. This study explores the influence of AI feedback for student argumentation on 5th grade learners’ emotional experiences during game-based scientific inquiry. Receiving feedback is an emotion-laden process in which students may perceive critical feedback as a threat to their skills and identities. Trust and relationship-building are viewed as core components of how students respond to and internalize feedback (Molloy et al., 2019). This highlights the importance of understanding how students respond to feedback that is offered from a non-human source, such as generative AI, that lacks su ch social abilities. Our poster will explore how students react to AI feedback on their writing during a collaborative, playful learning activity, and how these emotional reactions appear to influence students’ engagement with scientific inquiry. Design This study analyzes a pilot implementation of SciStory: Pollinators, a 2D visual novel-style game designed to engage middle-school students in open-ended exploration of a socioscientific dilemma: What should a community do with an empty plot of land? As students visit different locations, converse with multiple characters, and take notes in their in-game notebooks, they work in pairs to construct a persuasive argument for the mayor, advocating to convert the empty lot into either a community garden or a parking structure. Through these interactions, students learn about the relationship between pollinators and people and the role of community food systems. The game integrates AI agents embedded within multiple narrative characters to support scientific inquiry (see Figure 1). Content Knowledge Agents provide information about pollinators and human-environment interactions. Argument Feedback Agents offer feedback on students’ draft arguments, prompting students to strengthen claims and incorporate evidence. Finally, the Argument Evaluation Agent reviews students’ final arguments and determines whether they are persuasive enough to influence the community. All agents were powered by a locally hosted Llama-3.1-8B model using a retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) pipeline grounded in a curated science knowledge base and response templates co-designed with educators. Student input is sorted (question, evaluation, or inappropriate language), and then agents use tailored prompts with gameplay information and argument history dynamically filled in to generate responses.

Frustration was a key emotion that students commonly expressed when receiving AI feedback, particularly 1) when they struggled to use game tools to compose an argument to share with the agent, and 2) when the agent was perceived as harsh or repetitive in t heir judgments and suggestions. The first source of frustration appeared to stem from difficulties with spelling and typing as well as some technical issues, which slowed down student progress. The second source of frustration compounded on top of the firs t, because after drafting arguments to share with the Mayor agent, students would often receive feedback that their argument was either too vague or simplistic. This feedback was often accurate but sometimes included hallucinated suggestions for irrelevant evidence, which amplified student confusion. Initially, many groups responded to feedback by speaking with more characters and gathering new notes to improve arguments. However, when groups revised their arguments and again received feedback that they were not yet persuasive enough, some dyads reacted to the feedback with harsh assessments (e.g., “Elliot, you’re useless” in response to repetitive advice given by an argument feedback agent) or exasperated exclamations such as, “I don’t know how to get freaking evidence. I’m gonna crash out on you, do you understand that?” Some students attempted to resolve their frustration by communicating it, such as when one group said to Riley, “I found out [the Mayor] is so mean.” However, the agent was unable to respon d helpfully to this input and instead continued to offer argumentation advice. Students also began tonegatively reflect on feedback before it was even received, with one group guessing, “He’s gonna say it’s trash” while waiting for the Mayor’s response. Overall, these initial results, which we will explore further in our poster, highlight ways in which AI agent feedback can lead to frustration and disengagement when agents are unable to meaningfully support the emotional layers of receiving and responding to feedback. The significant presence of student frustration in our data suggests that the design of AI conversational agents must take into account the emotional layers of receiving feedback in the delivery of critiques on student writing. This supports recent studies  that have highlighted the importance of pedagogical agents providing emotional feedback to support student motivation and reduce confusion (Lang et al., 2022). The goal is not to eliminate frustration, but rather to design agents that productively respond to and scaffold the regulation of challenging emotions, while providing feedback that is perceived as supportive rather than harsh or overwhelming. However, much is left to learn about how to weave together emotional and cognitive feedback when presenting students with AI-generated guidance, as well as learners’ overall perceptions of receiving such feedback from AI tools versus human educators (Le et al., 2025).
