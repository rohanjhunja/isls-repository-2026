# Improving Pre-K–12 Students’ Executive Functions Using Digital Games: A Systematic Review

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Executive functions (EFs) are critical cognitive skills that influence learning, decision-making, and problem-solving. This systematic review examines the impact of digital games on improving EF skills in Pre-K–12 settings by following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. Contributions from diverse fields, including psychology, education, and technology, reflect the interdisciplinary interest in this area. Based on the analysis of game design integrations, this systematic review organizes game design elements into two categories: high-impact, where games are purposefully designed to improve EF skills, and low-impact, where games are not intentionally designed to target EFs. The findings reveal trends in game design elements, such as adaptive challenges and feedback systems, that contribute to EFs improvements. This study highlights digital games as promising tools for enhancing EFs. Findings underscore the need for more rigorous designs and future research on higher-order EFs.

## Introduction

Executive functions (EFs) are a group of top-down mental processes that help with focus and attention (Diamond, 2013). These skills become necessary when relying o n automatic or instinctual responses is inadequate or insufficient to address the demands of the task (Bu rgess & Simons, 2005; Diamond, 2013; Espy, 2004; Mi ller & Cohen, 2001). EFs involve thinking before acting, addressing unforeseen problems, resisting temptat ions, and maintaining concentration (Diamond, 2013). EF skills are essential for regulating higher-level cognitive processes such as sequencing, planning, decision-making, reasoning, and problem-solving (Cristofori et al., 2019; Miyake et al., 2000). EFs also promote academic success, p hysical and mental well-being, and overall cognitive, social, and psychological growth (Diamond, 2013). According  to the World Economic Forum’s report, analytical thinking, creative thinking, cognitive flexibility,  and attention are among the top ten core skills de sired by employers in 2023 (Zahidi, 2023).

According to Soussini et al. (2022), EFs have been defined in various ways, depending on the theoretical point of view and the age range or population studied. For example, the unitary perspective (Friedman & Miyake, 2004; Garon et al., 2008) stresses common processes  among me asures of EFs. All sub-facets of EFs are linked together and operate as a single, unified cognitive ability rather than as independent processes. For example, when a student solves a math problem by using working memory to hold numbers, inhibition to block distractions, and cognitive flexibility to switch strategies, thereby showing how these abilities function together. The modular or componential view (Diamond, 2013) emphasizes dissoc iable EF processes and distinct developmental patte rns for each process. For example, a child uses their w orking memory to remember the steps n eeded to complete a puzzle but struggles to control anger when the puzzle is taken away with weak inhibition. Despite these different models conceptualizing EFs, there is a broad consensus among researchers that EFs comprise three facets (Miyake et al. 2000; Souissi et al, 2022): inhibition, or attentional control  in other words at the level of attention (i.e., not attending to irrelevant features), updating or working memory (i.e., keeping track of multiple events), and cognitive flexibility or task shifting (i.e., switching from one task to another). These three facets are the foundation for developing more complex, higher-order EFs, such as reasoning, problem-solving, and planning (Diamond, 2013).

Several initiatives have demonstrated the potential  of high-quality early education to support EF skills development, indicating that EFs can be trainable (David et al., 2017; Diamond & Ling, 2020) except in cases where a child has neurological impairments. Through cultivating EFs from a young age, individua ls can be prepared for the multifaceted demands of society.

Digital games are efficient tools for fostering cognitive skills (Plass et al., 2015). Most individuals begin playing games at a young age. According to a report by the Entertainment Software Association’s (2023), 26% of American video game players are under the age of 18. Research shows that well-designed digital games can enhance EFs, underscoring their potential as a tool  for cognitive development (Homer et al., 20 18; Parong et al., 2017; Plass et al., 2019). Well-designed games integrate interactive problem-solving, clear goals, adaptive challenges, player control, ongoing feedback, uncer tainty, and sensory stimuli to enhance engagement a nd learning (Shute & Ke, 2012).  Therefore, the purpose of this study is to synthesi ze the literature's findings and explore how digital games impact Pre-K–12 children’s EF skills. Current study In this review, we examine studies in the literature that highlight various approaches through which digital games or tools enhance EFs across diverse populations. Th ere are several systematic reviews and meta-analyses that have examined the effects of serious games, exergames, and video games on attention, EFs, and overall cognitive skills (Wiley et al., 2023; Abd-Alrazaq et al., 2022; Parthasarathy et al., 2023). These studies explore the impact of digital game genres, gamification, and game-based learning approaches on learning and cognitive performance. Additionally, these studies often focus on specific groups, such as youth or individuals with cognitive impairments (Eng et al., 2023; Li et al., 2022). Several studie s also emphasized supervised methods,  such as rubrics or functional Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) techniq ues, to assess the cognitive benefits of gaming interventions (Huang & Cheng, 2022). The most relev ant study to the current systematic review was cond ucted by Choi et al. (2020), in which they examined exergaming effects on EFs in children for pediatric purposes. To our knowledge, there is no systematic review in the literature that focused on the effects of digit al games in promoting EFs within Pre-K–12 educational settings. In this systematic review, we aim to address this question. Specifically, we focus on the design consideratio ns of digital games and how they support the development of EFs in this study. The following research questions guide our systematic review: RQ 1) What are the trends (e.g., target audience, sub-skills being targeted, game genres) in using digita l games to improve EFs?

RQ 2) What are the effects of digital games on EFs?

RQ 3) What are the common game design considerations in digital games to improve EFs? By addressing these questions, this systematic revi ew aims to expand the growing body of literature on effective educational game interventions and to inf orm the design of future digital games for promotin g EFs. In the following section, we discuss the methodology we used to identify the relevant studies.

Studies were rated as high (greater than 70%), medium (40 – 70%), or low (lower than  40%) quality. A second reviewer validated the ratin gs and confirmed that all studies met the minimum quality threshold of 40%. As a result, all 49 studies were included. ## Results

RQ 1: What are the trends in using digital games to improve EFs? The included studies comprised journal articles (n = 39), conference proceedings (n = 5), and dissertations (n = 5). Most studies were conducted in the United States (n = 13), followed by Brazil (n = 4), Argentina (n = 3), Spain (n = 2), Canada (n = 2), and Australia (n = 2). One study each came from Germany, China, and the United Kingdom. This distribution demonstrates global interest and highlights the int ernational scope of  research on digital games for EFs.

Reviewed studies investigate the sub-facets of EFs working memory (n = 19), followed by inhibition (n = 14), and cognitive flexibility (n = 4). Visuospatial working memory (VSWM) was addressed in only one study (n = 1). However, most studies investigated multiple sub-facets of EFs that reflect interconnected and hierarchical nature of executive function components. A notable number of studies combined working memory, inhibition, and cognitive flexibility (n = 10), while others focused on inhibition and cognitive flexibility (n = 7) or inhibition and working memory (n = 7). Higher-order EFs, such as problem-solving and planning, were explicitly addressed in a smaller subset of studies (n = 7). This pattern shows a balanced research focus on foundational EFs and their integration with higher-order cognitive processes in targeted interventions. RQ 2) What are the effects of digital games on EFs?

As mentioned above, the majority of the included studies investigating the effects of digital games on EFs reported positive outcomes (n = 31), with some mixed results (n = 14) and a few showing no effect s (n = 4). Although further speculation on these results is beyond the scope of this systematic review, meta-analyses may offer further insights. We now turn to these studies and present our analysis of the effects we reported. Games desi gned with adaptive mechanics, and targeted cognitive tasks often demonstrated imp rovements in EFs components such as working memory, task-shifting, and selective attention. For example, stu dies using puzzle and strategy games (Plass, 2019; St. Clair-Thompson, 2010) revealed that incorporating dynamic  and engaging elements could enhance cognitive flexibility and adaptability. Sim ilarly, brain training games strengthen working mem ory, inhibition, and problem-solving through repetitive cognitive challenges tailored to participants’ abilities (Ramsay, 2015). Games tailored to individual differences in cognitive abilities, such as the Reflex™ platform, were found to have promising results by addressing visuospatial working memory and anxiety (e.g., Li, 2020). However, not all EF domains were equally impacted in these studi es; while some games effectively improve task-switching or memory (Le, 2 023; Plass, 2019), others fail to address skills su ch as inhibition (Le, 2023). This finding underscores the importance of alignment between game design and targeted EFs outcomes. The diversity in game genre and methodologies contributes to variations in observed effects. Brain training games, such as City Builder, which integrated working memory training with game mechanics, often succeeded in improving motivation and engagement but showed comparable cognitive gains to non-gamified versions (Boendermaker, 2018). Moreover, while some studies demonstrated significan t improvements in emotional intelligence and sociab ility alongside EFs enhancements, as seen with Pokémon GO (Hsieh, 2019), others highlighted limited effects i n specific areas such as cognitive flexibility (St Clair-Thompson, 2010). These findings suggest that while digital games hold promise for supporting EFs, their effectiveness dep ends on the EFs sub-facets they target, game design preferences, and the context in which they are implemented. Moreover, the transferability of EFs into real-world settings remains a key area of interest in re viewed studies. Results in this area are varying across studies. For example, Goldin et al. (2014) demonstrated a far transfer of EFs to academic domains. Elementary sch ool students showed significant improvements in lan guage and math performance, which highlights the potentia l for EFs-focused digital game interventions to impact real-world educational outcomes. Some studies found promising results regarding the far transfer of working memory capacity. Ramani et al. (2020) reported sustained improvements in working memory several weeks after training. Results show the potential for digital game interve ntions to foster lasting cognitive skills. Similarl y, Johann and Karbach (2019) found long-term transfer effects of game-based EFs training. Game-based inhibition training led to lasting improvements in reading speed, and cogni tive flexibility training resulted in sustained gains in reading comprehension. On the other hand, some studies find  contradicting results. Flynn and Richert (2017) ca nnot confirm whether the observed short-term change in EFs can transfer to other contexts o ver time. Even though studies found limited evidence of far transfer effe cts for some su b-facets of EFs, researchers suggested that the results might be task-specific and should be addressed in future research (Dörrenbächer et al., 2014). RQ 3) What are the common game design considerations  in digital games that can improve EFs?

Reviewed studies analyzed game design integration i nto the improvement EFs at two levels: high and low. Most studies demonstrated high integration (n = 27), where games were explicitly designed or sign ificantly modified to enhance EFs. For example, Berg et al. (2021) aimed to develop and test eFun designed to measure and improve EFs in primary school children. eFun used engaging game elements such as interactive sto ries, fun characters, and adaptive difficulty levels. The results showed that these game elem ents improved task engagement and supported the development of EFs. Similarly, Ober e t al. (2021) investigated patterns of engagement and impact on inhibition in a custom-made digital game named Gwakkamole. Gwakkamole  has game elements such as Go/No-go tasks, adaptive difficulty, and delayed response  mechanics. Results showed students improved inhibition skills based on reaction time and accura cy when playing Gwakkamole. High-level game design integration studies also used targeted mechanics such as adaptive difficulty or incentive systems. For instance, Li

(2020) aimed to examine how adaptive difficulty in the Reflex™ game-based platform influenced elementary

students’ math fact mastery and EFs. Reflex™ adjusted the difficulty of math problems based on e ach student’s performance. The results showed that adaptive difficulty improved students’ math skills and executive functions, especially for students with strong visuospatial wo rking memory. However, Pawar (2020) found contrasti ng results. The researchers used a custom-designed game called All You Can E.T. to explore task-shifting skills in three different adaptive conditions. Researchers ad justed the difficulty level of the game in adaptive condit ions. However, there is no significant difference found i n EFs between the treatment and control groups. Mor eover, incentive systems are a commonly used game design e lement in high-level integration games  (Castellar et al., 2015; Alzubi et al., 2018; Weijer-Bergsma et al., 2016; Ramos et al., 2019). Various forms of incentives, such as rewards (Berg et al., 2020), bonus points (Rosas et  al., 2019), and stars (Tam, 2023), are frequently employed across studies. Incentive systems are shown to be effective in working memory (Berg et al., 2020). Add itionally, narration is another game design element used and shown to be effective in high-integration games like BrainWare Safari (Sittiprapaporn, 2019) and Memory Booster (St. Clair‐Thompson et al., 2010). Several studies fell under the low integration cate gory (n = 21). The studies in this category included games that were pre-existing (not specifically designed to target EFs) or focused primarily on other skills, with EFs improvement as a secondary or indirect outcome.  For example, Lorant and Lieury (2014) investigated  the effects of different types of digital games on  the development of cognitive skills and academic achiev ement in elementary school students. The recreational games used in this study, such as Kawashima’s Brain Training, Big Brain Academy  and New Super Mario Bros, lacked specialized design elements targeting EFs development. Results showed no statistically significant improve ments in EFs or academic achievement in these games. In another study, Monteiro et al. (2021) introduced an  Attentional Skills Training (AST)  program for elementary school students that focused on enhancing attention al and EFs. The study included games named Attention Bird Alert, Climb It, and Twins, which targeted visual processing, working memory,  decision-making, and inhibitory control. The results showed a 40 –50% improvement in attentional skills on standardiz ed visual decision-making tasks. However, there were no significant changes in mindfulness self-reports or parent-reported EFs.

## Discussion

EFs have been recognized as critical for cognitive and academic development (Miyake et al., 2000; Diam ond, 2013). This review identified 49 studies in the literature that used digital games to improve EFs, including working memory, inhibition, and cognitive flexibility. The first key point of discussion is  the diversity of research fields, EF sub-facets, and target audiences represented in the reviewed studies. Researchers from fields such as psychology, neuropsychology, education, and compute r science contributed to this body of work, spannin g various global contexts. These studies often targeted specific EF subdomains, with participants ranging from PreK to high school. Elementary school students comprise d the largest group, reflecting the emphasis on ear ly cognitive development.

Additionally, the interconnected and hierarchical n ature of EFs resulted in some inconsistency in outcomes and focus areas. For example, some studies  specifically targeted working memory or inhibition, while others focused on different sub-facets such as t he cognitive flexibility of EFs. This variability h ighlights the complexity of EFs as a construct and the challenges  in designing interventions that comprehensively ad dress its multiple dimensions.

Despite these challenges, the findings reveal both opportunities and limitations in using digital games to foster transferable EFs. While some studies demonstrate promising far transfer effects, others highlight variability and task-specific outcomes. This inconsistency suggests that the design and context of interventions play a crucial role in determining their effectiveness.  Notably, games with high levels of integration EFs consistently demonstrated positive outcomes, particularly in enhancing cognitive flexibility, working memory, and inhibition simultaneously. These games used complex, adaptive challenges that required players to engage in susta ined mental effort and strategic thinking. Plass et al. (2019) found that digital games designed with adaptive difficulty adjustments effectively improved task-shifting skills across various age groups. Similarl y, Homer et al. (2019) reported that emotionally engaging game designs activated adolescents’ EFs and led to significant improvements in cognitive flexibility. Well-crafted interventions can lead to significant promi se for improving EFs. However, studies reporting mixed or no effects suggest the n eed for further investigation into factors such as game design, duration of interventions, and contextual variables that may influence outcomes (Diamond & Ling, 2020). The findings highlight the critical role of intenti onal and targeted design in digital games aimed at enhancing EFs. High-integration games, which incorporate features such as adaptive difficulty, incentive systems, and engaging narratives, demonstrate significant po tential for improving specific EF skills. However, the variability in outcomes across studies underscores the complexity of designing universally effective interventions. Mixed results suggest that contextual factors, such  as the implementation of game mechanics, the durat ion of gameplay, and individual differences among particip ants, significantly influence the efficacy of these interventions. In contrast, low-integration games often lack the specialized mechan ics needed to directly target EFs, resulting in limited or secondary improvements. The se findings suggest that while general-purpose games may support some cognitive skills, their impact on EFs is inconsistent and less pronounced. Moving for ward, it is essential to explore how game mechanics can be o ptimized to address the nuanced demands of EFs, ens uring that interventions are both engaging and effective.  Additionally, further research should investigate how contextual variables, such as classroom integration and player motivation, interact with game design to influence EF development. Future research will explore how sp ecific game design elements impact EFs to provide c learer insights into their effectiveness. This focus will guide researchers in developing more targeted and effective games for enhancing EFs across various contexts.

## Conclusion

EFs are critical for cognitive skills that influenc e planning, problem-solving, and decision-making in our daily lives. This study reviewed the existing literature to assess the potential of digital games to enhance  EFs. Digital games have shown promise as  tools for strengthening EFs and have demonstrated evidence of transfer to real-world outcomes. However, this review highlights a gap in comprehensive studies specifically designed to improve the long-term impact of game-based interventions on EFs. Addressing this gap cou ld open new pathways for researchers to design targeted, evidence-based digital games that support the development of  these essential cognitive skills.
