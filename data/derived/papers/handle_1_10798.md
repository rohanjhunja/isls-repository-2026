# “You Perform Civility Here”: Analyzing Discourses Surrounding Technology in Black Panther: Wakanda Forever

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper invites us as researchers and educators to consider discourses surrounding technology that are perpetuated by popular culture as a way to both understand, resist, and reimagine their impacts on curricula, teaching practices, and policy. Drawing from speculative fiction as a medium for reimagining our pasts, presents, and futures, we conducted discourse analysis on “Black Panther: Wakanda Forever” to unearth narratives about emerging technologies related to nondominant communities’ relationships to these technologies and the ways they both experience and resist domination. In our findings, we present two prominent discourses that emerged from our analysis: technology as a dangerous weapon, and technology as challenging cultural traditions. We then discuss how providing learners opportunities to examine popular culture can be centered in STEAM education, supporting their engagement with speculating possible futures where their cultural wealth and histories enhance and intertwine with STEAM knowledge, practices, and tools.

## Introduction

The goal of this paper is to illustrate the educati ve power of popular culture and how it risks reinfo rcing specific narratives–particularly surrounding technology –that if left unexamined can reinforce systems of op pression. Campos and Blikstein (2023) noted that an analysis of how narratives about technology are communicated reveals the ways in which individuals receive, negotiate, a nd replicate discourses. These discourses, defined by Gee

(1990) as language-in-use that can influence social and cultural practices, shapes our perceptions of technology’s

definitions and purposes. This paper invites resear chers and educators to interrogate discourses surro unding technology perpetuated by popular culture as a way to understand, resist, and reimagine their impacts on STEAM education. Drawing from speculative fiction as a me dium for reimagining our pasts, presents, and futur es, we focused our analysis on “Black Panther: Wakanda For ever” due to its popularity in the box office, as w ell as its use throughout STEAM outreach efforts for Black youth and girls (e.g., Gaskins, 2022).

## Background

The instructional potential of popular films and television as visual texts in K-12 education is notable. As Morrell

(2015) discusses, these media forms, endorsed by th e National Council of Teachers of English and Inter national

Reading Association Standard s, enable students to apply visual literacies to in terpret and deconstruct texts, including those in STEM fields. Popular culture, pa rticularly when examined through the lens of specul ative aesthetics, holds significant educational potential  in STEAM education. More specifically, it allows l earners to critically engage with and reinterpret dominant nar ratives about science and technology, while also em powering them to incorporate their racial identities and com munity cultural wealth —experiential knowledge a nd cultural practices from home and community (Yosso, 2005)—as enriching elements in the learning process. In the context of CS education, for example, pedagogies like restorying through design  have allowed youth to speculatively deconstruct and reconstruct dominant narratives about technology through analyzing advertisements, comics, and science fiction about technology (Shaw et al., 2023). Furthermore, Ojeda-Ramirez et al. (2023) have been conceptualizing an asset-based pedagogy for STEM + Arts curricular content, one that is infused with the aesthetic and speculative fiction movement of Latinofuturism as a way to counter the deficit perspective often l inked to students of color and recognize the strengths of their communities. Aesthetic movements like Latinofuturism  (Merla-Watson, 2019) and its inspiration Afrofuturism (Womack, 2013) use speculative fiction to envision new futures, particularly ones that dream and think  beyond the present social and technological realities by centering Latine and African aesthetics of various media. Within the context of STEAM, the use of Afrofuturism has r eimagined STEM for Black urban learners by transfor ming race from a barrier into a tool for challenging nor ms and leading in scientific advancements (McGee & White, 2021). We argue for engaging learners in examining pop culture that contains dominant discourses about technology and its articulation with community cultural wealth, as well as contesting discourses about oppression, have the potential of being used in  STEM classrooms. Specifically, speculative aesthetics within STEM education not only promote scientific exploration but can also catalyze a reimagining of racial and ethnic identities, thereby allowing youth to bring their community cultural wealth to the analysis of popular culture (Yosso, 2005).

In this paper, we applied discourse analysis to the  Marvel film “Black Panther: Wakanda Forever”, in order to illustrate what narratives could be dissec ted and analyzed by teachers and students across ST EAM education. This film, with a runtime of 2 hours and  41 minutes (161 minutes total), garnered a remarka ble worldwide box office revenue of $859,208,836. The f ilm's status as the sixth-highest-grossing film of 2022 worldwide not only highlights its popularity but also grants the narratives it communicates with significant cultural importance. When contextualized within the STEM education landscape, the film's success and representation of Wakanda as “an African nation rich in diverse Black  people and culture, resources, and technology is i n stark contrast to the stereotypical and oftentimes negative portrayal of African countries as being barbaric, stuck in the past, lacking in resources, capital and infrastructure to sustain economic viability, and thus, technology deficient” (Harrington, Klassen, & Rankin,  2022, p. 5). Through examining the discourses in th e film surrounding technology, we hope to illustrate ways of speaking/ listening and/or reading/writing about technology t hat can reveal particular socially recognized identities, a s well as the narratives  that surround and/or are perpetuated by those social groups.

## Methods

Discourse analysis in the learning sciences Discourse analysis, a methodology that examines lan guage at use in the world to do things (Gee, 2010),  has previously been used in the context of the learning  sciences. For instance,  Campos and Blikstein (202 3) studied the discourses of talks given by technology leaders as a way to understand their impact in education practice and politics by engaging in critical discourse analysis  (CDA) of their talks as an “invitation for learnin g scientists to challenge such assumptions and propose new ideas” b ased on such discourses (p. 186). Discourses repres ent conceptual frameworks that become evident through t he interplay of knowledge, language, and communicat ion (Campos & Blikstein, 2023; Fairclough, 1992; Gee, 2010). While discourses emerge from sociocultural activities, they also have a socially constructive role, affecting behaviors and molding how people view the world, according to Fairclough (1992). Viewed from this angle, disco urses act as discreet vehicles for "master narrativ es" (Brown & Gilligan, 1992), which are frameworks of knowledge that transmit norms, practices, and identities that become culturally dominant, prevalent and widely endorsed by society. Data sources and analysis We examined the film “Black Panther: Wakanda Foreve r”, which was released in November 2022 as the sequ el to the 2018 movie “Black Panther”. Our primary obje ctive in this study was to illuminate the film’s un derlying discourses by generating semiotic descriptions of h ow messages, whether intentional or unintentional, were constructed and conveyed. We chose this film becaus e as a continuation of Black Panther, it has commun icated narratives that reimagine Black people’s relationsh ips to technology through Afr ofuturistic aesthetics; it also introduced youth to narratives and themes like Indi genous-futurism from Central America, migration and colonialism, and women leaders in science and techn ology. In our examination of the film, we conscious ly adopted a critic al viewpoint, basing ourselves on the principles se t forth by Fairclough (1992) and Gee (2010) within the tradition of critical discourse analysis.

Initially, we utilized an inductive approach to pin point significant "Big D" discourses, which represe nt ideologies and beliefs expressed through communication, encompassing language, identity, and social practices, as outlined by Gee (2010) and Campos & Blikstein (2 023). Both researchers independently coded segments  of the film, yet we also engaged in collaborative disc ussions to analyze specific scenes collectively. In  these discussions, we openly recognized our roles as lear ning scientists and candidly addressed our inherent  biases. Moreover, we discussed the connection between our i dentities and those of the film's characters, and recognized our involvement with popular culture beyond our res earch, thus embracing and critically examining our positionalities. In line with the tradition of CDA, we intentio nally conducted our analysis from a critical standpoint, but we ensured that our conclusions rem ained firmly grounded in the data, and that the mas ter narratives found could be clearly exemplified by specific scenes of the film.

## Results

Our analysis of the technology discourses in “Black  Panther: Wakanda Forever” revealed two prominent narratives: technology as a potential dangerous weapon, and technology as challenging cultural traditions. Technology as a potential dangerous weapon One of the dominant narratives communicated throughout the film was that emerging technologies risk being used as dangerous weapons that can pose a serious threat to people, whether from the perspective of those in power or from the perspective of marginal ized communities. For example, during an early scen e in the film, Queen Ramonda attends a hearing by the United Nation, whe re the Secretary of States for both the United Stat es and France chastise the Queen for Wakanda’s preservation of vibranium, a nearly indestructible metal that can absorb, story, and release kinetic energy. In their argumen ts, Wakanda “broke their promise” with the other na tions to share their resources and paint vibranium in the ha nds of the Wakandans as a threat to international s ecurity. As stated by the Secretary of State of France: “..France has information that states vibranium has limitless potential as a renewable energy source. And yet Queen Ramonda has still declined to  open access to the material through international trade, or any of Wakanda’s philanthro pic ventures. We also have information that vibranium can be used to make weapons of mass destruction. It is a substance that does not trigger any known metal detector. It could be s muggled. It is a direct threat to the global security environment and the nonproliferation regime.” (Coogler, 2022, 00:07:25) In her statement, the Secretary of State of France intentionally chose words that would instill fear among the other delegates about Wakanda having sole possession of v ibranium, such as the possibility of it being “smug gled” or able to pass through metal detectors without being triggered. Her use of the phrase “weapons of mass destruction” triggers memories and feelings around September 11 and fears of terrorism, which proved to be ironic given that earlier French mercenaries had attacked one of Waka nda’s outreach centers in an attempt to steal vibra nium. Exposing the Secretary’s lies, Queen Ramonda noted that the true danger was the “dangerous potential o f some of the people in this room” (Coogler, 2022, 00:07:2 5). In terms of discourses, Queen Ramonda char acterized the French and U. S. delegates as dishonest and threate ning, stating that they “perform civility” yet whis per in their halls and military facilities, “The King is dead. The Black Panther is gone. They have lost their protector. Now is our time to strike” (Coogler, 2022, 00:08:50). This exchange be tween the Queen and the other delegates reflects long-standing histories of global and violent imperialism and colonization of African and Indigenous nations and peoples for their resources, which invites viewers to interrogate narratives about emerging technologies and their relation to power.

Technology as challenging cultural traditions We found that the film frequently explores the tension between technological advancement and cultural tradition, a theme that manifests frequently. This discourse i s particularly evident in the portrayal of Wakandan technologies, which, while progressive, often clash with the nation's deeply-rooted traditions. For instance, the introduction of new laser-based weapons, juxtaposed with the traditional Vibr anium spears used by the Dora Milaje, Wakanda's elite group of warriors and special forces, exemplifies this conflict.This thematic tension is not just a background element but a central plot point that resonates through various characters and scenes, underlining the struggle between preserving heritage and embrac ing modernity. The following key dialogue from  an early combat scene vividly illustrates this conflict, as General Okoye confronts Aneka over her choice of we apon, symbolizing the broader debate between tradition and innovation within Wakandan society. In the scene set at approximately nine minutes into the film, General Okoye, a figure deeply rooted in tradition, questions Aneka, a member of the Dora Milaje, about her choice of weapon: “Okoye: Aneka, where is your spear?

Aneka: Shuri gave me these to try. You know, I like them better. Okoye: Our foremothers gave us the spear because it is precise, elegant, and deadly. It will not change under my watch.

Aneka: Yes, General.” (Coogler, 2022, 00:09:52) This exchange highlights the resistance to change w ithin Wakandan leadership and the generational divi de in attitudes towards technological advancements. The r eluctance of Okoye to accept the new weapons offere d by Shuri underscores a key narrative tension  in "Black Panther: Wakanda Forever." It reflects a broader theme of cultural preservation versus technological advancem ent, a debate that resonates among the main charact ers. This scene, along with another where Shuri prioritizes weapon development over traditional biotechnological research on the 'Heart-shaped herb', signifies a shift in priorities among Wakanda's younger generation. These initial scenes set the stage for a recurring motif in the film: th e struggle to balance the allure of technological i nnovation with the importance of maintaining cultural heritage. Th is raises critical questions about the impact of mo dernization in traditional societies and the potential loss of cultural identity in the face of rapid technological progress.

## Discussion

In our study, we observed the recurring themes of t echnology as a potentially dangerous weapon and technology as challenging cultural traditions in "Black Panther 2: Wakanda Forever." Although such narratives are not unique to speculative genres, they diverge from the premises of Afrofuturism. Alondra Nelson (2002) highlights how the digital divide has often been linked to racial and economic inequalities, portraying blackness as inhe rently opposed to technological progress. However, Afrofut urism and Latino futurism counter this narrative by positioning people of color at the forefront of tec hnological innovation. They are portrayed not merel y as consumers or victims of technology's dangerous pote ntial, but as creators. Such films provide learners  with the opportunity to reflect on their engagement with tradi tion and speculate on futures where cultural wealth  and traditions enhance and intertwine with STEM practic es. This encourages potential learners to consider how their cultural backgrounds and artistic expressions can be integrated and advanced through the technological aesthetics presented in these films.

Drawing inspiration from Campos & Blikstein (2023),  we propose that learning scientists critically examine the narratives about technology promoted by  popular culture. Our work converses with Vakil and McKinney De Royston's (2022) portrayal of youth as technology philosophers, which foregrounds "youth learning to decode and dismantle tech’s power dynamics through artistic, moral, and humanistic inquiry" (p. 336). We aim to spark dialogue on how STEAM education can also c enter the practice of analyzing popular culture nar ratives, focusing on the roles of both teachers and learners in critiquing technology's artistic representations. This approach will help understand, challenge, and reshape popular culture’s influence on STEAM education. In future analyses, there is a need for applying an intersectional analysis of these discourses given the various identities of characters and their culture’s unique histories with these systemic oppressions.
