# Frequency Transition Network Analysis (FTNA)

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper introduces Frequency-based Transition Network Analysis (FTNA), a novel method to model transitions between learning states. FTNA provides a framework for analyzing the relational dynamics, enabling researchers to uncover dominant events and notable patterns, compare different processes, and gain deeper insights into how events unfold over time. Moreover, FTNA’s capability to use statistical techniques like bootstrapping and permutation tests allows for rigorous validation of the observed interactions to establish the reliability of the identified patterns and transitions. This helps ensure that the findings are not just artifacts of the data but reflect genuine interaction dynamics. This paper presents FTNA’s theoretical grounding, compares it to existing methods with a large-scale simulation study across 10,000 datasets.

## Introduction

Transition networks (TN) were proposed to model the relational and temporal dynamics within interacting systems (Nicolis et al., 2005; Zou et al., 2019). Yet, they were only brought into education recently by the introduction of Transition Network Analysis (TNA) (Saqr, López-Pernas, Törmänen, et al., 2025). In a nutshell, TNs capture how states or events follow each other as they unfold in time. In doing so, they map the relations, temporalities, as well as the generating mechanism that shaped the process, e.g., self-regulation. Several types of TNs exist in the literature. For instance, TNA is a Markovian-based TN where the transitions between states depen d only on the current state without considering the historical co ntext or prior states (i.e., a memoryl ess process) (Zou et al., 2019). To address these scenarios, we propose an alterna tive approach: Frequency-based Transition Network Analysis (FTNA), which captures the observed freque ncies of transitions, rather than estimated probabi lities (Nicolis et al., 2005; Zou et al., 2019). FTNA does not impose Mar kovian constraints, nor does it require larger datasets to accurately capture the Markovian proces s. This makes FTNA less susceptible to statistical instability in processes that do not adhere to the Markovian as sumption (Nicolis et al., 2005; Zou et al., 2019). Similar to TNA, FTNA is suitable for modeling temporal process es where events are influenced by prior interaction s, e.g., in social or cognitive processes. While FTNA is use ful for a wide array of scenarios, it is particularly useful for small datasets where certain transitions may be rare and TNA would inflate their estimates. Although TN as a concept has been harnessed in other fields, they have yet to receive attention in learning sciences despite its potential to help researchers capture the dynamics of the learning process (Zou et al., 2019). This may be due to the lack of tools supporting their implementation. In this paper, we introduce FTNA as a novel framework for analyzing TNs along with a comprehens ive software toolset for implementing the method, including a tna R package (López-Pernas et al., 2024)  and a web applicati on that enables all the functions of FTNA along with tutorials, datasets, and examples. The toolset of FTNA offers rich visualization possibilities and network measures like centralities to identify key events or transitions. Furthermore, FTNA offers sta tistical inference techniques that bring rigor and robustness to the estimation process, which is not only novel but is also hardly seen in similar methods. Namely, we introduc e bootstrapping for model validation, permutation f or comparison across groups, and case dropping for verifying the stability of centrality measures.

## Background

Educators have a range of methodologies that have b een developed to capture the breadth of the collabo rative learning process. Interaction analysis, as noted by Jordan & Henderson (1995), provides a foundational approach to examining fine-grained interactions, focusing on conversational turns, negotiation, and feedback, capturing the micro-level dynamics of collaborative meaning-making. Social Network Analysis (SNA), which maps social structures within collaborative networks, offers insi ghts into the roles  and influences of participants through metrics like centrality, highlighting the distribution of interactions and the presence of influential nodes in CSCL environments (Saqr & López -Pernas, 2021). Epistemic Network Analysis (ENA) is  another popular CSCL method, allowing researchers to capture cognitive and epistemic linkages by mapping co-occurrences of elements like skills or knowledge areas across time windows,  offering a view of how these elements interact wit hin the learning process (Elmoazen et al., 2022). Ordered Network Analysis (ONA) is a variant of ENA that accounts for the direction in which connections are made. Similarly, Process Mining (PM) can capture the temporal transitions between learning events (Helske et al., 2024).

Despite the value of these methods, they each have some limitations. For example, while rich in detail, interaction analysis is labor-intensive and difficult to scale for larger dataset s, limiting its application to broader CSCL studies. SNA’s focus on sta tic structures restricts its ability to capture tem porally dynamic learning processes, an essential feature of collaborative in teractions. ENA and ONA cannot model directed trans itions between states, making it difficult to fully capture the sequential dependencies within evolving processes  (Helske et al., 2024). Although PM captures the temporal aspects of tran sitions, it does not provide the capabilities of network analysis, relational features, or their measures. These constraints highlight the need for a method that can integrate temporal dynamics, directed t ransitions, and structural features, such as FTNA. Furthermore, the aforementioned models have limited model validation techniques that offer a statistical quantification of each part of the model or comparison across models. FTNA mode ls these dynamic relations and introduces modern ro bust methods for validating each edge in the network and  comparison with statistical significance and effec t sizes (Saqr, López-Pernas, Törmänen, et al., 2025).

FTNA: An introduction, advantages, and theoretical foundations The FTNA method starts by capturing interactions and transforming them into events (see Figure 1). Nex t, transition rates are computed by counting the number of transitions between learning events, and FTNA networks are generated. FTNA models the transitio ns between learning as a directed weighted network from the transitioning event to the transitioned-to event represented as G=(V, E, W), where nodes (V) represent a learning event (e.g., argue, discuss, or disagree) or broader concepts like dialogue moves and emotional reactions. Edges (E) capture the transitions from one activity to anothe r, indicating the direction of each transition. Weights (W) represent the frequency with which one activity leads to the next, providing insight into how often each transition occurs. After developing FTNA graphs, several valid ation techniques (e.g., permutation, bootstrapping)  can be applied to the model to examine the significance of  the transitions. Context data can be incorporated to build comparison models to explore the differences among groups or settings. Finally, the network properties  of the model can be utilized to identify major transition patterns and other network measurements, such as the centrality of specific learning events (Saqr, López-Pernas, & Tikka, 2025). ## Outline of the FTNA Method

The core mathematical concept of FTNA revolves arou nd counting transitions between states in a sequence of events. Given a set of states \(S = \{s_1, s_2, \ldots, s_n\} \) and sequence data \(X = \{X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_m\} \), each sequence \(X_i = (x_{i1}, x_{i2}, \ldots, x_{iT_i}) \) consists of states from \(S \) where \(T_i \) is the length of the sequence. The goal is to construct a transition frequency matrix \(F \) where the entry \(F_ij \) is the transition frequency from state \(s_i\) to \(s_j\). In simple terms, this matrix captures how frequently one state follows another. For each sequence \(X_i \) in \(\mathcal{X} \), we consider each pair of consecutive states \((x_{it}, x_{i(t+ 1)}) \), for \(t = 1, \ldots T_i - 1 \) and count the occurrences of unique pairs. In oth er words, \[F_{ij} = \sum_{k=1}^{m} \sum_{t=1}^{T_k-1} \mathbb{I}(x_{kt} = s_i \text{ and } x_{k(t+1)} = s_j)\] where \(\mathbb{I}(\cdot) \) is the indicator function. This formulation allows FTNA to construct networks where nodes represent states and directed edges rep resent transitions, with edge weights reflecting th e frequency or relative importance of transitions.

The novelty and strength of FTNA  stem from its statistical rigor and the capabilitie s to capture the relational dynamics of the learning process. In mos t network and PM research, validation and statistic al significance of models are rarely discussed (Zabolotna et al., 2023). FTNA is particularly nota ble for its robust statistical validation techniques, especially boots trapping and permutation methods. The integration o f bootstrapping into FTNA allows researchers to asses s the robustness and reproducibility of FTNA models  by offering a quantifiable measure of the significance  of each transition in the network (p-value) (Epskamp et al., 2018; van Borkulo et al., 2022). Insignificant or s purious edges are thus eliminated, leaving only edg es that are more representative of the underlying process. In d oing so, researchers can identify weak parts of the  model that may not be reliable. A robust, validated model enab les a cr edible data-driven theory building to help assess, validate, or refine existing hypotheses and build o r advance future ones. Furthermore, when comparing learning processes across different conditions, traditional methods rely on comparisons of frequencies or using  visual inspection of process maps or network models (e.g.,  ENA) (Elmoazen et al., 2022). However, without rob ust statistical significance that pinpoints the actual differences, it is hard to build meaningful conclus ions. FTNA addresses these limitations by employing a robust permutation-based approach that allows researchers to estimate the statistical significance of each transition in the network (van Borkulo et al., 2022). In doing so, FTNA helps make informed decisions and build sound conclusions  about, for instance, how high achievers differ fro m low achievers in their learning process. Lastly, centra lity measures have proven valuable for describing n odes, identifying key events, or ranking the nodes accord ing to their roles in the modeled process. Yet, alm ost all the centrality estimation methods offer no way of confi rming these. FTNA offers case dropping for verifyin g centrality stability (Epskamp et al., 2018).

The theoretical foundation of FTNA draws on the com plex dynamic system theory in which learning is understood as a complex system of multiple interact ing components (Saqr et al., 2024). It also builds on Winne & Perry's (2000) framework, which identifies learning as a sequence of occurrences, contingencies, and repeated patterns that form tactics and strategies. These oc currences and contingencies create patterns of inte raction over time. For example, one participant might ask questions (occurrence), and the other group members might respond with clarifications or elaborations (contingency) (Winne et al., 2010). FTNA tracks these interactions by mapping how each occurrence leads to a contingency and uses these transitions to build the transition network. The analysis of the transition identifies typical patterns of behavior in CSCL dialogues, helping to understand how collaboration evolves and how learning is facilitated through int eraction. Figure 2 shows an example of the FTNA net work of a single single group who was part of collaborative  project-based learning as described in detail in (Saqr, LópezPernas, Törmänen, et al., 2025), and depicts two distinct comm unities, i.e., groups of nodes that have stronger connections with each other than with other nodes i n the network. The first community (green nodes) fo cuses on the problem-solving aspect of collaboration, which involves processes, such as synthesizing available information, monitoring progress, or sharing information to find solutions. The strongest transitions within this community are from monitoring to exploring (n=24) and from explor ing to synthesis (n=25). In contrast, the second co mmunity (blue nodes) emphasizes the socio-emotional side of collaboration with students seeki ng cohesion and mutual understanding or engaging in co-regulation. The strongest transitions in this commu nity occur between understanding and co-regulation (n=33), as well as between emotion and understanding (n=20). While the two methods are not supposed to render si milar results given their inherent methodological underpinning, the simulation aims at understanding the strength, differences, and similarities between  methods and where they are expected to diverge. The number of sequence spells per dataset varied between 300 and 2,000 to assess the impact of sample  size on edge weight estimation. Sequence lengths we re set between 15 and 100 states per row to account for both short and long t emporal patterns. Furthermore, a random number of m issing values was generated in each row in each dataset to  account for the fact that many educational dataset s have a different window size (session), with datasets cont aining between 0 and 20 missing states per sequence, which helps offer an assessment of the robustness of esti mation under incomplete data conditions. To evaluat e the structural alignment and quantify the differences between the TNA model and the FTNA mode l across multiple dimensions, we computed four primary categories of metrics: weight deviation metrics, correlation metr ics, similarity measures, and dissimilarity measures using the compare function of tna package with minmax scaling to ensure both networks are compared on the same scale. Weight deviation metrics indicated how much edge we ights differed between the TNA and FTNA models. The Mean Absolute Difference was 0.101 (SD = 0.040), suggesting a low average difference with some variation. The Median Absolute Difference was slightly low er at 0.079, indicating that deviations remained re latively small for at least half of the transitions. The Root Mean Squ ared (RMS) Difference was 0.135, showing that large r edge weight deviations were relatively small. The Coeffi cient of Variation (CV) Ra tio was 0.884 (SD = 0.083), indicating some deviations, but their relative magnitude remained fairly consistent across the network. Correlation measures revealed a strong relationship. The Pearso n correlation was 0.906 (SD = 0.033), indicating a highly linear relationship between edge weights in both model s, showing similar weight trends. The full results of the simulation study can be accessed in the supplementary material available at (Saqr, Misiejuk, et al., 2025).

## Discussion

FTNA builds on established graph theoretical underpinning, learning theories, and previous methods, and extends them by integrating modern statistical inference methods that offer a robustness layer. Unlike traditional methods, FTNA employs bootstrapping a nd permutation tests to validate significant transi tions or significant differences among groups to ensure a reliable and accurate representation of learning dynamics. This statistical rigor supports data-driven theory building, allowing researchers to val idate, refine, or develop new hypotheses. Furthermo re, FTNA addresses the subjectivity and misinterpretation inherent in visual approaches to comparing across groups, e.g., in ENA. Using permutation to quantify differe nces in learning processes, FTNA provi des clear metrics and statistical significance for comparing high and low achievers, for example. FTNA offers an advantage in analyzing complex processes with any data size, while TNA —based on Markov models—  is more suitable for prediction and modeling complex behavior. However,  it requires larger datasets, making it less flexible for handling intricate dependencies. Furthermore, Markov models assume the process has no memory and only depends on the previous state (Helske et al., 2024). These distinctions make each method appropriate for different contexts, depending on researcher choices and contextual variables.

This manuscript is the first introduction of FTNA to the learning sciences community. While we did our best to introduce FTNA, many features were not desc ribed in this paper due to space constraints, such as pattern mining, node and edge centralities, and community finding. Furthermore, the method has a development plan that includes more statistical inference methods, a long itudinal network extension, and the inclusion of ot her techniques, such as simulations. Furthermore, our focus was on the methodological and empirical aspects. For the readers to apply the methods, we prepared an R pack age (López-Pernas et al., 2024), tutorials (López-Pernas et al., 2025; Saqr, López-Pernas, & Tikka, 2025), vignettes with case studies, and sample data that help readers apply the methods. Furthermore, we have created a web application (https://sonsoleslp.shinyapps.io/tna-app) that allows users to apply FTNA with no coding skills..  Like any other method, FTNA is limited in certain s cenarios. Large networks with many nodes are particularly noisy and challenging to analyze. Also, FTNA —while it has statistical inference methods— its predictive power is limited and not as rich as Markov-based TN. Furthermore, FTNA does not allow clustering of the process data, and therefore, one cannot rely on it to address the het erogeneity. However, in this particular case, researchers are advised to cluster the FTNA input data using techniques such as sequence clustering, which would give similar results.
