## Introduction

This paper reports findings from the first of a five-year research-practice partnership (RPP) that examines how the curriculum and practices of a STEAM Lab embedded in a culturally situated, community-based youth development program—Downtown Boxing Gym (DBG) —supports the STEM engagement of the Black youth they serve. In Year 1 o ur interdisciplinary team of researchers, practitioners, graduate students, and youth researchers (YR) used mixed methods grounded in community-based participatory research (CBPR) (Israel et al.,

1998) to answer two questions: (1) How does DBG support students’ interest and participation in their STEAM

Lab?; and (2) How is the STEAM Lab curriculum structured and what lab practices are used to create opportunities for student engagement with STEM content?

The U.S. has struggled for decades to keep pace with STEM innovations in European and Asian countries (Jamrisko & Lu, 2020). This may be due to a shortag e of qualified U.S. workers due to the systematic e xclusion of underrepresented minority individuals from postsecondary STEM training programs (e.g., Hughes & Rob erts,

2019) and to barriers in economically marginalized,  high-minority serving primary and secondary schools such

as lack of  hands-on STEM resources, limited advanced STEM classes, and teach er underqualification and bias (e.g., Olszewski-Kubilius et al., 2017). Such barriers play an espec ially important role for students of color who may perceive STEM fields as available only to white, middle-class males (e.g., Cherng et al., 2022). To circumvent school-based systemic barriers, the U.S. Department of Education (DoEd) (2016) called for a re-envisioning of STEM preparation  to include informal learning programs. However, des pite decades of research supporting the utility of informal learning activities in improving STEM outcomes (Young et al., 2017), questions remain about how such spaces can effectively broaden STEM participation of Black and Latinx youth. Some of these questions exist because most research on informal STEM programming is conducted on explicitly STEM-focused programs (e.g., science  or robotics clubs) that likely draw students who are already interested in or feel efficacious about STEM. As a result, it is not clear how informal learning spaces might foster STEM interests in students who do not self-select into STEM programs. In addition, questions remain about how informal STEM spaces can be structured to create safe and inclusive spaces for culturally diverse youth (e.g., Martin, 2019). Our RPP project addresses  these questions by exploring DBG’s approach to promoting interest and participation in their STEAM Lab, which has a 98% voluntary participation rate. In Year 1 of the project the lab was equipped with makerspace resources and overseen  by a full-time instructor (Coach R) who provided programming five days a week. Although all DBG youth are eligible and encouraged to participate in  the lab, attendance is optional. STEAM Lab programming is based on a curriculum collaborati vely developed by the STEAM Lab instructor and youth. The curriculum explores me aningful, ambitious, multi-week themes utilizing project-based learning that exposes students to STEM method s. In keeping with DBG’s student-centered approach, weekly topics are voted on by youth, resulting in a curriculum that is based in youth voice and interests. Theoretical basis The project is grounded in two theoretical framewor ks: situated learning and the inductive model of in terest and identity development. The situated learning perspective (e.g., Greeno & MMAP, 1998) suggests learning is a joint accomplishment between an individual and setting re sources (Hand & Gresalfi, 2015). From this perspect ive, STEM preparation and persistence is shaped by the i nteraction between “repertoires of practice” (Gutié rrez & Rogoff, 2003, p. 22) learners bring into each setti ng and the affordances of settings that enable yout h to engage in new STEM practices and explore new self-perceptions. This perspective is supplemented by Re nninger’s

(2009) inductive model of interest and identity dev elopment, which purports that learners’ beliefs abo ut

disciplinary content (e.g., science) can constrain or promote their interests in those content areas (Han nover & Kessels, 2004) and that environments can support learners’ revisions of their self-perceptions. Methodological approach Our RPP is grounded in CBPR, a collaborative approach found ed in the belief that scholarship is more socially impactful when community members (i.e., practitioners and youth) are involved. In CBPR, community members

(1) are equitably involved in the research, (2) eng age in shared decision-making, and (3) have mutual ownership

of products of the research (Holkup et al., 2004). Drawing on these concepts, our project was jointly conceptualized and executed by our team of DBG practitioners and YR, researchers, and graduate assistants. Data sources In Year 1, a range of mixed-methods data, including attendance, survey, video, and interviews, were collected. This paper will focus on findings from the student and staff interviews. Interviews with 25 current DBG youth were conducted by trained DBG YR in Spring 2024. In line with the participatory methods of the project, DBG staff initiated the decision to have YR conduct the interviews, believing youth participants would be less forthcoming to unfamiliar and culturally dissimilar project researchers. To support this data collection, the four YR participated in an intensive three-day mentoring program that covered research ethics, qualitative research methods, and interviewing skills and practice. The PI and graduate students provided weekly group and individual supervision to  the YR throughout the three months they were conducting the interviews so they could receive feedback on their research skills. Student interviewees were purposively selected to re present three levels of participation (7 non-participating, 8 intermittently participating, and 10 consistently participating) based on Fall 2023 S TEAM Lab attendance data. These interviewees were equally distributed across three age ranges (8 elementary, 8 middle, and 9 high school) and matched the gender composition of DBG youth (7 females, 18 males). Interviews focused on:

(1) why interviewees choose or choose not to partic ipate in the STEAM Lab; (2) th eir most and least preferred

aspects of the STEAM Lab; and (3) factors that would cause them to participate more or less. Interviews were also conducted in Spring 2024 by th e PI with 5 DBG staff members who had insights into student engagement. Staff interviews gathered data on: (1) intentions of STEAM Lab programming; (2) processes behind lab curriculum and practice development and refinement; (3) factors they believe affect youth participation in the lab; (4) methods they use to encourage participation in the lab; and (5) how the lab reinforces DBG’s broader and culturally situated goals. All intervie ws were thematically ana lyzed by a team of university researchers and graduate students following Braun &  Clarke’s (2006) six stage inductive thematic analysis approach, in consultation with the YR and DBG staff. After independently reviewing several transcripts, the team collaboratively developed staff and student coding manuals. All interviews were coded by at least two team members who came to consensus on final coding; throughout t he process coders were cognizant of emergent codes to add to the code book.

## Major findings

RQ1: How does DBG support youth interest and participation in their STEAM Lab? Results from staff interviews revealed a variety of  actions DBG staff take to support youth interest  and participation in their STEAM Lab, even amongst students who may not initially be drawn to STEM opportunities. Some of these efforts involved advertising STEAM Lab topics to students to garner excitement for participation. For example, as one staff member noted, “ The [STEAM Lab] coordinator spends our drop-in hour hanging out by the front, walking around outside at the courts...talking about what they're going to be doing [in the STEAM Lab],...[and asking] who wants to come.”  Other staff members also talked to students about what is occurring in the STEAM Lab so students could decide if they want to participate. To supplement staff efforts to garner student parti cipation, staff members also rel ied on peer influence to support student interest in the STEAM Lab. As a staff member shared, “it's almost like [th e STEAM Lab coordinator] has these little minions of 20 kids [w ho]...go out and talk about [the STEAM Lab] and then it just trickles out and all of a sudden everybody's really  excited.” At times, it seem ed leveraging peers to encourage participation was unintentional, however staff also described intentionally using peers to draw in nonparticipating students: “I knew these four kids are super into th is [STEAM Lab activity]. I'm going to go talk with them about it [while] these other four kids are standing aroun d. They're hearing that the five of us are really e xcited. Now there's nine of us that are really excited about this.” These efforts seem ed to achieve their intended aims. Students described feeling curious about what occurs in the STEAM Lab, suggesting they were aware that interesting things happen in the space that they want to be a part of. As one student said, “I wanna see what’s is new a bout [the STEAM Lab]”; another noted “sometimes I just [want to] see what experiment the y would do.” Because of this curiosity, students de scribed being drawn into the space, which staff members als o described being aware of: “[The STEAM Lab] is kin d of elusive. It's off to the side, but the doors are open so you can see cool things going on there. Even when the doors are closed, it's like, ‘I wonder what's going on back there.’ ‘I wonder what they're doing today’.” Staff also described offering opportunities for stu dents to explore the STEAM Lab without committing to it: “We would do a whole drop-in week where it'd be like everybody come in...my g oal is to get everybody to try the STEAM Lab once this week just for 30 minutes.” This staff member stated that, perhaps because of that and the other recruitment efforts, "It feels like e very time I go into the STEAM Lab, there's new faces who are signed up and it feels like when I'm hearing kids talk, they're talking about being in this STEAM Lab.” Beyond staff efforts to advertise and garner excitement for the STEAM Lab, both staff and students also described how relationships support youth interest and engagement  in the Lab. As one staff member described, “Coach R’s personality lends itself so well to...th e STEAM Lab...[her] excitement...[makes kids] super into her and what she's doing.” This idea was echoed by several students, who noted their connection with the STEAM Lab instructor was key to their participation. One student shared, “I go ther e mainly to see Coach R.” Another student reinforced this, saying “I’m pretty sure most everybody goes the STEAM ‘cause Coach R is there.” RQ2: How is the STEAM Lab curriculum structured and what lab practices are used to create opportunities for youth engagement with STEM content? Student and staff interviews also revealed curricul ar and pedagogical characteristics that created aff ordances for student engagement in the STEAM Lab. Perhaps most i mportant to staff was that the STEAM Lab curriculum is based on student voice. As one staff member describ ed “it's very much student voice led. The students have a tinkering moment where they're like, oh, it would b e so cool to learn about this.” Based on that stude nt interest, the STEAM Lab coordinator then iteratively designs the next curriculum unit: the girls were specifically really interested in th e chemistry of makeup...So [we brought in a] young black female engineer who made her own lip gl oss line...who's teaching girls how to make lip gloss...But then it's like, okay, let's think of… how to keep it going...the students were really interested in… designing their own logos to go on the lip gloss bottles. So then now we're talking about computer science...And then it’s just, okay, now we need to learn the financial math aspect of how would we market this...and so then just the ball gets rolling down the hill. Staff members also described STEAM Lab practices as based in power sharing and co-learning/coworking, which they believed were crucial affordanc es for youth engagement with STEM content. One staf f member stated: “a lot of times [in the STEAM Lab]...the adult is le arning alongside with the students, which I think is really powerful...It’s not just adults...g iving you this amazing knowledge and you should lea rn about it and receive it. It's like let's tinker and figu re this out together, which I don't think our kids experience a lot and I think is pretty profound to see an adult also be le arning and then joyful about learning together.” Because of this co-learning, other staff members described how the STE AM Lab normalizes not knowing and wondering as essential aspects of STEM learning, which in turn allows students to play with STEM concepts rather than feeling as though they should already know them.

In addition, both students and staff highlighted th e atmosphere within the STEAM Lab as particularly facilitative of student engagement with STEM content. For the students, this atmosphere was primarily described as a safe, non-judgmental space where they were free to make mista kes. As one student noted: “ [The STEAM Lab] tries to make everybody feel welcome to speak their opinion. Like nobody there shut down about anything...Like it's a very respectful space and it  just a fun safe place for you to talk abou t whatever.” Another student confirmed this feeling, noting that “ it's just a great environment. It's like somewhere you want to...place yourself in.” Although students noted their peers play a role in establishing this atmosphere, they largely attributed the establishment of this safe atmosphere to the ST EAM Lab instructor, who they described as instrumen tal to making the STEAM Lab a “calming,” “loving,” and “caring” space. Staff members as well highlighted the importance of  the STEAM Lab being non-competitive and nonevaluative in encouraging student engagement with S TEM content. One staff member shared, for example, that “Because there's no tests or quizzes attached to it, there's no assignments, there's no homework...the re's no team to make or not make,” students of all ages and with  varying efficacy about and exposure to STEM conten t feel a sense of belonging in the space. This, staff member s suggested, is an instrumental aspect of the STEAM Lab’s design, which ensures the lab is accessible to all students. Another staff member captured this idea: when a student is performing four grade levels behi nd...there are some [things that are]...not going to be fun …until we get you caught up....[In] the STEAM Lab, we don't have to do all that...[when] they walk through the STEAM Lab, they're not [four] grade levels behind. They're just figuring out how an engine works alongside everybody else...So, it's kind of like a monkey climbing off your back of ‘I just get to be a student in here, a true student of life’.

## Conclusions and practical implications

Results of Year 1 data provide specific and tangibl e recommendations for how informal learning environ ments can promote interest and participation in STEM spaces for youth who may not self-select into informal STEM programs as well as for how such spaces can be structured to create affordances for youth engagement with STEM content. Research has consistently confirmed the essential role relationships play in fostering youth engagement in out-of-school-time (OST) programs (e.g., Price et al., 2019; Roth & Brooks-Gunn, 2016); researchers have also found that, especially for youth who are underrepresented in STEM fields, relationships rather than career interests motivate their involvement in STEM activities (Lyon et al., 2012). However, less work has examined the role of relationships in underserved youths’ involvement in  informal STEM programming. Findings from Year 1, therefore, provide empirical evidence for how these relationships promote interest in informal STEM spaces and highlight how OST professionals can leverage those relationships to encourage STEM interests and participation. Also in alignment with our findings, research supports the importance of peers in fostering STEM career interests and involvement (e.g., Robnett & Leaper, 2012). However, little existing literature focuse s on the role of peers in informal STEM space  participation or provides specific examples of how organizations can leverage peers to encourage curiosity about STEM activities.  Our findings address these gaps, both endorsing that peer influence spurs interest amongst students who may not initially be drawn to STEM opportunities and providing examples of how DBG staff leverage peer influence to encourage student participation. In doing so, findings demonstrate how peers can challenge learners’ const raining beliefs about disciplinary content (Hannover & Kessels, 2004) to create opportunities to enter spaces to revise STEM self-perceptions (Renninger, 2009). Finally, results also highlight contextual affordances that enable youth engagement with STEM content in DBG’s OST space (Hand & Gresalfi, 2015). Within the STEAM Lab, student voice plays a central role, guiding initial lab topics and how those topics are iteratively buil t upon. Though much has been written about incorporating student voice into classroom practice s (e.g., Cook-Sather, 2020), less research has been published about the role of student voice in STEM spaces, esp ecially in informal settings. However, based on our  Year 1 results, creating opportunities for youth to guide the curriculum not only encourages their desire to engage with STEM learning opportunities but also enables them t o understand how disciplinary content is connected in the real world. To support STEM educators in following youth voice, results also support the positive benefits of colearning/co-working, which aligns with research on the pedagogy  of joint activity (Vossoughi et al., 2021). By embracing a collaborative approach, STEM educators are freer to follow student voice, as they need not be experts in all curriculum content. Simultaneously, when STEM educators embrace a co-learning approach, they can model curiosity and normalize not knowing (Friere, 1998) as essential aspects of scientific and learning processes.
