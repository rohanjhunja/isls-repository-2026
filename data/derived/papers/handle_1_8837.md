# A Tale of Two Teacher Trajectories: Analysing Change in Teachers’ Beliefs on Technology Integration During the COVID-19 Pandemic

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Existing research on teachers’ beliefs remains majorly focused on cognitive aspects, while lesser attention has been paid to affect and context. In the present study, we use the lens of the cognitive-affective model of conceptual change, to understand how teachers’ beliefs about technology integration changed when they were compelled to move to online teaching as a result of the COVID-19 pandemic. We present findings from a multiple-case study of two secondary school teachers in Kerala, India. Contrary to expectations, the teacher who had low training and low self-efficacy showed stronger belief change towards effective integration of technology, compared to a teacher who was skilled in using technology for physical classroom activities. Motivation, context and situational resources played a crucial role in understanding the difference. The findings have implications for learning sciences researchers working on effective integration of technology-focused solutions in the classroom and in designing TPD programs.

## Introduction

Aligned to global trends, India is striving to tran sform the nation into a digitally empowered society  and knowledge economy. It is envisioned that technology  will play an important role in achieving this goal  by improving educational processes and outcomes (NEP, 2020). However, even when policies favour the integration of technology in education, its effectiveness depends on teachers’ beliefs about teaching, learning and technology, and how they include it in their actual practice (Ertmer, 2005). Even if first-order barriers to technology integration such as access, training, and support were removed, teachers would not automatically use technology effectively unless their beliefs aligned with a constructivist pedagogy (Ertmer, 1999), thus the consequences of b elief congruence or incongruence become important. When t eachers are required to implement practices that ar e different from the beliefs they hold about teaching and learning, it may adversely affect their satisfaction and wellbeing and they may implement the practices in an ineffective manner (Buehl & Beck, 2015). Research on how and when teachers change their beliefs is vast and diverse (Pajares, 1992; Ertmer, 2005; Park & Ertmer, 2007; Taimalu & Luik, 2019). While r esearch has largely focused on the cognitive aspect s of teacher belief change, (Fives & Buehl, 2012), recen t studies have recognised the role that affective (Watt & Richardson, 2014; Gill & Hardin, 2014) and contextual factors (Tschannen-Moran et al., 2014) play in teachers’ beliefs. The affective factors include motivation, emotion, goal orientation etc. and contextual facto rs include social, cultural, political, historical and school contexts teachers experience during their careers a nd have been reported to be inseparable from the teachers’ belie fs and actions (Levin, 2015). One contextual factor  that may play an important role in influencing a change in teachers’ beliefs is an emergency situation such as the COVID19 pandemic. In the Indian context, there are a few studies that  give evidence for the relationship between affecti ve and contextual factors on teachers' beliefs about t echnology integration (Bandyopadhyay, 2013; Chand, et al., 2020; Sharma & Srivastava, 2020; Kundu, et al., 2020). However in-depth studies exploring the role of affective and contextual factors in the process of change in teachers' beliefs about technology integration are lacking. The present study aims to address this research gap and contribute to further expanding the belief change model. We chose the educational context of the state of Kerala in India to understand how teachers’ beliefs about technology integration changed when they were compelled to move to online teaching as a result of the COVID-19 pandemic, and the role played by affective and contextual factors. Our research question for this study is “How do teachers’ beliefs about technology integration change and what role do affective and contextual factors play?”

## Theoretical framework

Teachers' beliefs about technology integration According to Park & Ertmer (2007), teachers' belief s about technology integration comprise three components: pedagogical beliefs about teaching and learning, self-efficacy beliefs for using technology, and beliefs about the ICLS2022 Proceedings 535 © ISLS value of the use of technology. These three components were found to be the main predictors of teachers' use of technology for pedagogical purposes (Taimalu & Luik, 2019). Ertmer (2005) considered pedagogical beliefs as the  teachers’ educational beliefs about teaching and learning. Teachers with more traditional beliefs tend to implement more teacher-centred uses of technology and do not see technology as important and useful for teaching and learning, whereas teachers with more constructivist beliefs tend to implement more student-centred uses of technology and are seen as innovators (Taimalu & Luik, 2019; Ertmer et al., 2015). Bandura (1997) defined perceived self-efficacy as “beliefs about one’s capabilities to organise and execute the courses of action required to produce given attainments”. Self-efficacy is formed based on four major sources of information: enactive mast ery experiences, vicarious experiences of observing  others succeed through their efforts, verbal persuasion and physiological and affective states (Bandura, 1997). Teachers' perception of the value of using technology for teaching and learning is an important predictor of their future use of technology in their class. Researchers found that teachers will use technology only when they see value in using technology for specific educational purposes (Ertmer, 2005). Cognitive-affective model of conceptual change (CAMCC) Although conceptual change models are developed pri marily to explain knowledge acquisition, they could  be effective in studying changes in beliefs as well (Gill et al., 2004; Posner et al., 1982). Consequently, we adopted the Cognitive-Affective Model of Conceptual Change (CAMCC) as a potentially appropriate model for our analysis, which integrates cognitive frameworks of belief change with motivational and affective facto rs (Gill, 2003). The CAMCC model proposes two routes of belie f change resulting in either assimilation or accommodation and identifies systematic processing as the mediator of conceptual change. The belief ch ange process begins with the presentation of a reform message to the teacher. If the message causes dissonance to the existing beliefs of teachers (Implicate self? Yes), they appraise the situation as stressful (Stress appraisal). It is important to note here that teachers’ appraisal of the reform message happens automatically before characteristics of the message (intelligibility, plausibility, fruitfulness) are seriously considered. Even though dissonance is a prerequisite, belief change is unlikely to occur unless the teachers perceive sufficient motivation and ability. Kubanyiova (2012) in their integrated model of Language Teacher Conceptual Change (LTCC), which is a modified version of CAMCC, proposed to combine motivation and ability components into a single component called ‘ Reality check appraisal ’. During the reality check appraisal, if a teacher feels that his or her internal and/or exter nal resources are sufficient, may appraise the situ ation as challenging (Challenge appraisal)  and adopt an immediate goal to approach the refo rm message (Approach intention), which eventually leads to its ‘systematic processing’. The CAMCC proposes that only this systematic route can lead to a teacher's belief change. Howeve r, systematic processing does not guarantee a chang e unless the teacher yields to the message (Yielding?).

On the other hand, if a teacher does not see him or herself implicated by the reform message (Implicate self? No), will therefore not experience stress, discomfort or dissatisfaction. This may be either because he or she believes that they are already using technology in their classroom effectively (Positive appraisal) or because he or she sees the reform message as neutral and therefore dismisses the reform input as irrelevant (Benign appraisal). The CAMCC proposes that those who make such apprais als will lead to a ‘ heuristic processing’ rather than systematic processing. Heuristic processing further leads to a decision choice (Yielding?). If the teachers yielded or accepted the message, he or she will lead to a state of Assimilation or Superficial belief change. Similarly, even if the teacher may have entered the systematic rout e of belief change (Implicates self? Yes; and experienced dissonance emotions), there is a chance that he or she may revert to a ‘heuristic processing’. During the ‘Reality check appraisal’, if the teacher feels his or her internal and/or external resources are insufficient, the teacher will perceive the message as a threat (Threat appraisal) and adopt an immediate goal of avoiding the threat (Avoidance intention). From this point, the teacher follows the same path discussed in the heuristic processing.

## Educational context

The present study is conducted in the state of Kerala in southwest India, which is known for its high literacy rate. Kerala has implemented various innovative programme s to support teaching and learning through technolo gy such as VICTERS (Versatile ICT Enabled Resource for  Students) and Samagra eResource Portal. VICTERS allows students and teachers to access quality educ ation from experts through its TV and YouTube chann els. Samagra eResource Portal is a repository of digital  resources on various subjects from grades 1 to 12.  These initiatives have strong support from the government but are driven primarily by teachers, who contribute learning resources such as videos, animations, audios, stimu lations and interactive content which can be access ed by students. Teachers also contribute unit plans for various topics in addition to micro-planning that can be accessed ICLS2022 Proceedings 536 © ISLS by any other teacher for preparing their instructio n. The Samagra portal has active discussion forums,  in which teachers can communicate with each other and clarify their doubts. After the closure of schools due to the pandemic in  March 2020, the Kerala government decided to continue school education through online media. The  General Education Department initiated an interim arrangement for regular classes for students of gra des 1-12, called ‘First Bell’. The classes under th e First Bell programme were streamed live through VICTERS educat ional channel. The unaided private schools mainly utilised tools like Google Meet for delivering clas ses. All schools extensively used the WhatsApp tool  for communicating with students and teachers, sharing materials and providing feedback.

## Participants and data collection

Our broader study has been designed for 15 teacher participants from different types of schools (gover nment, government aided-private, and unaided-private) in Kerala. This study began in mid-2020, a few months after the pandemic and lockdown in India. The Institutional R eview Board (IRB) approval was obtained from our institution. Teachers from schools near the home di strict of the first author of this study were conta cted. Data collection includes interviews, observations of online teaching and teacher reflections. The study is ongoing and so far we have interviewed 6 teachers.

The present study used a multiple-case design and f ollowed a theoretical replication logic (Yin, 2018). As per the ‘maximum heterogeneity sampling’ strateg y (Patton, 2015), we purposefully selected two teac hers, who showed clear evidence of the role of affective and contextual factors in the process of belief change. The researcher conducted semi-structured interviews with the participants. The interview protocol was designed in such a way to elicit the changes in tea cher beliefs about technology integration (i.e. ped agogical beliefs, self-efficacy beliefs for using technology, and beliefs about the value of the use of technol ogy) and the cognitive, affective and contextual factors influen cing this change. We also tried to understand teach ers’ perceptions about the use of instructional technology in future. Interviews were conducted over the mobile phone in the native language Malayalam and took approximately 1 hour. Follow up clarifications were sought via text and audio messages. All interviews were audiotaped for further analysis purposes. Personally identifia ble data were anonymized immediately after data collection. Sample interview questions included: ● On a typical day, how do you utilize technology in your classroom? Briefly describe any two such instances from your classroom one year before and the present day. ● How competent do you perceive yourself to select and use various technologies to support teaching and learning?

● What might be your response if I had asked you the same question one year before? Provide some instances for supporting your statement. What might be the reason for this change?

## Data analysis

The recorded interviews were transcribed into Malayalam text. The transcribed text is translated into English for further analysis. The CAMCC framework is used as a ‘coding frame’ for structuring transcribed data (Schreier, 2012). We have used a concept-driven way (deductively) and tried to find evidence from the data that corresponds to the related aspects of the framework.

Additionally, some of the constructs were operation alized as follows. ‘Reality check appraisal’ encompasses self-efficacy beliefs as well as other aspects, such as actual cognitive ability, subject-matter knowledge, educational context, supportive colleagues, students’ expectations, resources and time. ‘Yielding’ to the reform message is influenced by a variety of fa ctors, such as whether the processing was biased, t eachers’ existing conceptions, and characteristics of how the message is perceived (Gill, 2003).

## Findings

Case 1: Teacher Ms. D Ms. D is a higher secondary (Grades 11-12) school teacher in an unaided private school. She teaches Accountancy and Business. She is on a contract and her job is not permanent. Her school is situated in rural areas of the Thrissur district of Kerala. At the beginning of the lockdown, she had two years of teaching experience at the school level and four years of teaching experience at the colleg e level prior to that. She has MPhil and BEd and is  qualified State Eligibility Test (SET). She has not received any training on ICT and her exposure to educational technology before the COVID-19 pandemic was limited to WhatsApp for social networking. Ms. D and her colleagues started using the Google Meet platform to conduct online classes. It was the first time Ms. D and most of the other teachers in her school were using this platform. Teachers sat together in the school and learned the features of the platform from each other using trial and error methods. They conducted  mock classes among themself to understand the possible c hallenges and errors that may arise during the live  classes. They also used WhatsApp groups to share new ideas a nd information about the technology, and practices for online classes. In addition, teachers used Google c lassroom for sharing materials and worksheets for f ormative assessment. The ICT teacher in the school helped Ms. D to install the Google classroom app on her mobile phone and taught her basic features of the platform. Ms. D also took help from her husband and children to understand how to write content and upload it on the platform.

Ms. D used WhatsApp groups for communication, sharing educational materials, off-the-class discussion space for students to clarify their doubts, conducting formative assessments using quizzes and thought-provoking questions, and conducting summative assessments in which question papers and answer sheets are communicated. WhatsApp is also used as a way to encourage student s by putting students' artefacts as WhatsApp status. In addition, Ms. D used WhatsApp to communicate with her students’ families also. Analysing Ms. D’s change in beliefs about technology integration The compelling situation when Ms. D’s school instructed all the teachers to seek various digital alter natives for their classes can be considered as the reform message, i.e. ‘rearranging the teaching-learning process by utilising available digital resources to help students to con tinue their education during the difficult time of COVID-19 pandemic’ (see Figure 1). The message of this refor m is strong because of multiple reasons. Ms. D’s ab ility to implement this message had consequences for her continuing to keep her job. Also, she was aware of the need for students to continue their education even during the lockdown. “When the school was closed……. students were anxiou s about their future career. Because the secondary level is the stage they need to make a career choice. As teachers we are also concerned about our students and their better career. I think these online classes can reduce these anxieties.” However, implementing this reform message was a dif ficult process for Ms. D because she had no exposur e to teaching online and the message challenges her conv entional instructional practices. Ms. D considered this message as a dissonance to her existing beliefs abo ut the teaching-learning process and her self is at  stake (implicates self).

“We can understand things by looking at the faces of children in a normal class..further explanation is required or not. But there are limits to such things in online classes.” “Studious children will learn in all mediums. Normal classroom learning is a must if you want to bring children who are backward in education to a forefront of the class.” Hence she is likely to appraise the situation as stressful, (Stress Appraisal) and feel corresponding anxiety at first. Those who make such a stress appraisal will show th e necessary motivation to process the reform messag e systematically. Here motivation will be decided by the characteristics of the person such as Knowledge and Selfefficacy beliefs for using technology. Despite Ms. D’s six years of teaching experience and strong command of subject knowledge, her self-efficacy beliefs for us ing technology were low initially. She had not rece ived any training in integrating technology into the teachin g process. She expressed this unfamiliarity and lac k of opportunity multiple times.

“I am not fully confident. I have not studied computers yet. I use my family's help to manage technology. I am still at an LKG [lower kindergarten] level in technology.” In Ms. D’s case, contextual factors played an impor tant role to affect her motivation. She shared that  her colleagues and friends helped her a lot in preparin g to shift to an online medium and learn new techno logies to facilitate the online teaching process. There was a lso a high expectation from the side of school admi nistrators, parents and students to continue schooling using digital technologies. Thus, Ms. D was motivated to approach the reform message, despite her weak self-efficacy and ability. This strong Motivation made her perceive the reform message as a challenge (Challenge Appraisal) and processes the message systematically. She had spent a considerable amount of time discussing with colleag ues, friends and family members the possibility of moving into an online teaching-learning process. She took their help in learning the affordances of new tools required for conducting online classes and preparing materials. She made efforts to prepare students for online learning. In the case of Ms. D, since her school was a private unaided school and her posting is on a contractual basis, she had to prove that she is capable of teaching in an online medium. Otherwise, it may affect her job also. All these compelling situations created a performance goal orientation in her. Since she has strong beliefs about the value of the use of technology and goal orientation that supports the reform message, she Yields to, or accepts, the reform message.

## Discussion

The study conducted among secondary school teachers in the state of Kerala, India has shown a differen t range of changes in teacher beliefs about technology integration. We have found that teachers have undergone different path of beliefs changes in accordance with CAMCC. These changes can be attributed to a number of contextual and motivational factors of teachers.

In the case of Ms. D, the self-efficacy beliefs for using technology and ability were found to be very low. In spite of the weak and insufficient characteristi cs of the teacher, Ms. D processed the reform messa ge systematically. The situational resource (supportive others) is found to be a crucial factor in the pr ocess. Ms. D and colleagues formed WhatsApp groups to discuss th eir concerns and problems faced during technology integration, share their experience of successfully  using technology tools, and deepen their knowledge  and expertise in both content and technology use. The idea behind this communication through WhatsApp was with a hope that everyone will benefit from their issues  and expertise. Since the teachers used WhatsApp gr oups as their primary mode of interaction, they can also be  considered virtual communities of practice (Wenger, 2008; Dubé et al., 2005). One more factor is Ms. D’s empa thy toward her students. She was very concerned abo ut students' lack of digital resources. She was availa ble to students even during weekends, through WhatA pp and mobile calls for solving doubts and sharing their concerns. She was aware of the role online learning played during the pandemic in shaping students' education and the ir future careers. For Ms. D, technology was more t han a transactional medium. She used it in a broader way by involving other stakeholders in her students’ le arning process and building community.

Mr. S had strong self-efficacy beliefs for using technology and sufficient ability. In some contexts, his beliefs as well as his practices were aligned and strongly supported student-centred learning. For example, he was successful in implementing active learning in face to face classes. However, his pedagogical beliefs on technology integration were found to be traditional, so he used technology mostly for communication and operational tasks. He did not show any instance of using technology for learner participation. Nor did he make the effort to exploit the affordances of the new technology medium. While he had good training and experience using technology and support from the school in terms of access to digital resources, he was unable to integrate student-centric learning practices into the new medium. In fact, despite rep eated probing, he showed an inability and unwilling ness to conceptualize student-centred practices in the online medium. This case study highlights the importance of factors influencing effective technology integration, going beyond training on how to use technology. We found that factors such as support from colleagues and co ncern for students’ future and a sense of community playe d a major role. At the same time, our findings may seem counterintuitive, where the trained teacher did not change his beliefs as much as the teacher who had low training for technology use due to the presence of the affective and contextual factors. However, the training prior to the pandemic did not provide for the unique situation we are in now. Hence, the training may have made Teacher 2 more rigid in their use of technology. The findings  from this study contribute to expanding the unders tanding about the belief change process. Specifically, this study illustrates the importance of favourable contextual factors in the effective integration of technology. The findings have implications to learning sciences researchers working on effective integration of technology-focused solutions in the classroom and in designing TPD programs. Even though we have valuable findings from the stud y, one limitation is that we do not have measurements of teachers’ beliefs prior to the pandemic, except via retrospective reflections of teachers. We have not yet measured teachers' actual practice in the digital medium and it will be a part of future work. ICLS2022 Proceedings 541 © ISLS
