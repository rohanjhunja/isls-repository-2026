# How do Students Build Community Knowledge for Sustainability through Collaborative Discourse?

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Student questioning is key to fostering critical thinking, yet opportunities for student-directed inquiry are often limited in K-12 classrooms. This paper examines how students engage in collective inquiry on sustainability issues through a collaborative learning program based on Knowledge Building. Held biannually at a tertiary institution, the program brings together students from different schools to advance community knowledge on sustainability. Students identify and explore their own sustainability problems in this interest-based design. Data were collected from two iterations of the program: 35 primary school students (Grade 5) in October 2023 and 25 secondary school students (Grade 9) in June 2024. The primary data source was student discussion notes posted on an online forum, which were analyzed to identify questions that deepened inquiry. Results show that students' questions, especially “wonderment” and “explanation-seeking” questions, played a central role in driving learning and advancing their collective understanding of sustainability.

## Introduction

Student questioning plays a critical role in enhanc ing learning outcomes (Chin & Osborne, 2008). Not o nly do student-generated questions provide valuable insights into their comprehension, but they also encourage deeper exploration, guide educational experiences, and fac ilitate knowledge construction. However, research s uggests that traditional classroom settings often restrict opportunities for  students to ask their own questions, as teachers typically control the flow of content and inquiry, limiting student-driven learning (Ritchhart et al., 2011). Knowledge Building (KB), a pedagogical approach  that emphasizes students’ genuine inquiries as they seek to understand their world, has emerged as a powerful method for fostering deeper learning (Scardamalia, 2002). KB encourages interest-based learning through real-world problems, allowing students to take ownership  of their learning by engaging in collective discourse, theorizing, analyzing information, and integrating ideas (Chen et al., 2017). This paper explores student engagement in a collaborative out-of-school learning program, the Knowledge Building Design Studio, which focuses on sustainability issues and the collective advancement of knowledge. We examine how students’ questions in their discourse deepen discussions and enhance community understanding.

## Literature review

Student-generated questions are essential for enhancing educational outcomes by fostering active knowledge construction, promoting meaningful discourse, and encouraging self-regulation. When students generate their own questions, they transit from passive recipients to active constructors of knowledge, making connection s between prior understanding and new concepts (Osborne & Wit trock, 1985). However, in typical classroom setting s, students often lack opportunities to guide their own inquiry. Research shows that learning environments supported by Knowledge Building provide a space for student i deas to thrive, emphasizing the importance of deep, meaningful questions that drive collaborative inqui ry, rather than seeking simple answers (Zhang et al., 2007). Knowledge Building places students at the heart of the learning process, encouraging engagement with real ideas and authentic problems (Scardamalia, 2002). In such  environments, student-generated questions initiate and propel collaborative inquiry, allowing s tudents to explore topics that genuinely interest t hem. To facilitate this student-driven discourse, online platforms like Knowledge Forum are used to scaffold engagement (Scardamalia, 2002). Within these platforms, students contribute ideas, questions, and explanations, building on one another’s contributions to deepen collective inquiry.

To understand how student ideas develop and improve  through online knowledge-building discussions, researchers have employed thread-level analysis of student discourse, focusing on ho w questioning advances collective knowledge (Lai & Law, 2013). For example, Lin and Chan (2018) categorized connected notes i nto discussion threads, analyzing them across various e pistemic levels —from knowledge sharing to the more advanced stages of knowledge construction and building. A common approach in discourse analysis is to classify student questions by type (fact-seeking v ersus explanation-seeking) and the depth and coherence of their responses (Resendes, 2014). An emerging category, " wonderment questions," has gained attention for ref lecting students' curiosity and openness tonew perspective s, often without seeking in-depth explanations (Zhang et al., 2018).

While Knowledge Building frameworks support the con tinuous enhancement of student ideas (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2003), it is essential to assess whether student-driven discourse truly advances community knowledge. Previous studies have shown a positive link between student-generated questions and idea improvement. For example, Lai and Law (2013) found that students who generated more explanatory questi ons tended to construct more advanced knowledge, sugges ting that higher-level questions promote deeper understanding. Similarly, Khanlari et al. (2017) obser ved that student-generated questions led to longer inquiry threads and more productive discussions, particular ly in science learning contexts. These studies high light the significant role of student-generated questions in fostering learning and Knowledge B uilding, especially within collaborative discourse. However, despite the prove n benefits, traditional classroom environments ofte n restrict student-driven inquiry, and there is limited research on authentic student-generated inquiry and translation of such practices into classroom settings.  This study seeks to address this gap by investigati ng how students negotiate meaning and advance knowledge within a collaborative, interest-based design which can adapted in practice. The central research question guiding this study is: “H ow do students build community knowledge for sustai nability through collaborative discourse?” By exploring this question, the study a ims to understand idea improvement in student discourse and the role of student questions in advancing community knowledge collaboratively.

## Method and analysis

This study involved 35 primary school students (Gra de 5) and 25 secondary school students (Grade 9) fr om Singapore, who participated in a short (2 to 3-day) research program called the Knowledge Building Design Studio (KBDS). Table 1 outlines the program's  design, which featured a three-phase Knowledge Building process focused on sustainability. The first phase, Idea Generation, encouraged students to explore environmental issues, identify areas of interest, and formulate hypotheses in small groups, sharing ideas both in person and online. The second phase, Idea Connection and Improvement, supported students in conducting research, gather ing data, interacting with peer ideas, and building prototype s to test and refine their designs. The final phase, Rise Above, enabled students to showcase their solutions, excha nge feedback, and reflect on improvements for futur e development.

Throughout the program, students were encouraged to  document their findings and engage in discussions on the Knowledge Forum, an online platform that facilitated public access  to ideas for further exploration. To support the knowledge-building process, a whole-class debrief was held at the end of each day, wher e a researcher facilitated discussions using customized scaffolds to help students share t heir ideas across groups and identify gaps or new insights that could refine their soluti ons. The dataset for this study consisted of 259 online notes posted on the Knowledge Forum, where students had made individual contributions or built upon existin g notes to deepen the topic or issue at hand. The online forum allowed quick identification of  “build-on” responses by showing links between another note (visually represented by an arrow on the platform). The student notes were first sorted into two categories: questions (usually prompting for a response) and explanations (usually sharing of information). All questions were then coded using the framework (Zhang et al., 2018) of question types shown in Table 2.

## Findings and discussion

The results revealed interesting patterns in student qu estioning during their online discussions. Figure 1  presents the results of coding student questions from their collaborative interactions, focusing on the two pha ses where question-and-explanation exchanges were particularly rich. Initially, there was a s trong presence of fact-seeking questions during the idea generation phase, indicat ing that students were focused on gathering concret e, factual information about sustainability issues. This likely reflects the need for foundational knowledge at the start of the process to inform their understanding of the proble ms. However, as the program progressed into the ide a connection and improvement phase, fact-seeking questions diminished, suggesting that stude nts had established a shared base of factual knowledge and were beginni ng to connect these facts in more sophisticated way s. In contrast, wonderment and explanation-seeking questions persisted across both phases. This highlights a curiositydriven approach, where students continued to ask br oad, open-ended questions about how their ideas worked, while also asking more focused, e xplanation-seeking questions to deepen their understanding of the issues. Notably, a distinct difference emerged between the student groups. Younger students (Grade 5) exhibited a higher frequency of wonderment questions, suggesting that their questioning approach may have fostered more innovative thinking and creative solutions. In contrast, Grade 9 students, who asked fewer wonderment questions, appeared to shift towards more explanation-seeking questions, reflecting a deeper focus on und erstanding the underlying causes and processes of sustainability issues as their discussion progressed.
