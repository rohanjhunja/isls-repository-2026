# Equity-Logics for Designing New Activity Systems Toward Rightful Familial Presence in Middle School STEM

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Minoritized parents have historically been sidelined in parent-school engagement opportunities. Research-practice-partnerships (RPP) have been one way to bring together stakeholders across communities in collective sense-making. We explore the design and enactment of a new activity system where minoritized parents, their middle school children and science teacher and researchers, engaged in the FamJam RPP, to co-design and enact engineering curriculum that centers community and seeds rightful familial presence in middle school STEM. Guided by frameworks of rightful presence, RPPs for learning at the boundaries, and cultural historical activity theory, the findings highlight new possibilities for family engagement through considering the underlying logics of equity. These include spatial and temporal equity, solidarity for equitable collaboration, collective responsibility for justice-oriented STEM, and fostering STEM for thriving and democratic citizenry. The study explores how these equity-focused logics candisrupt and transform systemic injustices in educational practices through new modes of relationalities and role/boundary mediation.

## Introduction

One of the macro-structural roots of inequities in educational oppor tunity for youth of Color in STEM is in how parents and families are valued in school settings. While the impact of familial involvement in school on children’s learning and overall well-being has been well documented (Boonk et al., 2018), most models of parental involvement are rooted in white and middle-class power structures that reproduce inequities along racial and class lines (Paredes-Schribner & Fernandez, 2017). Forms of familial engagement that matter in children’s learning diverge from school-driven modes, obscuring how parents’ social and cultural contexts and funds  of knowledge influence their interactions with schools and teach ers (Fernández & López, 2017). Thus, white middle-class parents have been able to exert significant influen ce on the schooling of their children and by extens ion, also shape the learning experiences of other children, w hile parents of minoritized students have lacked su ch opportunities to be equally involved in shaping educational experiences. Yet it is insufficient, even if it is imperative, to only make visible and acknowledg e the community cultural wealth parents possess  in models of familial engagement. Thus, the purpose  of this study is to explore family engagement in STEM education as a contested equity terrain with specific attention to minoritized families and communities. We draw upon equity logics, which recognize (or not) “the pervasiveness of pe rsisting institutional racism, historic and current exclusio nary institutional practices, a nd disparities” (Jayakumar, 2012, p. 16). Such logics have the potential to challenge  and transform systemic inequities present in educa tional practices.

Our work is situated within FamJam, a critical and participatory co-design project, involving families, youth, teachers and community members, focusing on familial engagement in middle school STEM for sustainable communities.  T he co-design of new activity systems is proposed to  contest neoliberal logics and promote rightful familial presence in STEM, aiming to challenge and dismantle the persistent racial and classbased oppressions embedded in STEM classrooms and s chooling. A non-negotiable consideration for FamJamis the salience of broader social, economic and politi cal systems of inequity that will always manifest l ocally in STEM teaching and learning contexts. FamJam is grounded in an effort to collaboratively reimagine and transform entrenched and normalized structural, systemic inju stices. We seek to simultaneously transform local c lassroom power dynamics as well as the broader societal powe r dynamics in which class rooms are ineluctably nested. Inherent in this orientation is the direct focus on  salient identity markers to F amJam – including race, class, immigration status, multiple languages and intergen erational dynamics – and how they inform the equity logics that undergird FamJam structures. The questions guiding our study are:

1. What equity logics  that undergird FAM JAM co-design activities? With these equity logics, w hat

entrenched power-dynamics are made visible and contested and what is left unconte sted, as seen in: a) the r oles & responsibilities of parents, youth, teachers, researchers?  b) t he d irectionality of flow of exchange of ideas and actions?  And c) how power is enacted/disrupted, by whom/what, and towards what ends?

2. How do these equity logics inform familial engagement activity systems that support STEM for thriving

or not?

Familial engagement in STEM The Rightful Presence for justice-oriented teaching and learning framework (Calabrese Barton & Tan, 2020) argues for equity beyond inclusion toward establishing the rightful presence of minoritized stakeholders. Minoritized families often have limited rights as g uests and are expected to conform to dominant practices. Rightful presence challenges these restrictions, ai ming to reveal what it means to be fully human and actively present in educational spaces. This involves collective political struggles between those with more power (e.g., school personnel) and those with  less (e.g., minoritized students and families), mak ing both injustice and social change in the here-and-now visible in educational practice. To ide ntify and understand practices and structures that contribute to rightful presence, the framework includes three  interconnected tenets: 1) the right to reauthor rights, which is integral to what it means to learn; 2) making visible the intersections and justice/injustice in the present while orienting towards new social futures; and 3) amplifying the sociopolitical processes inherent in teaching: promoting culture of disruption toward s justice, where modes of power/authority are colle ctively called into question.

We also draw from Farrell and colleagues’ (2022) fr amework of research-practice partnerships with a focus on equity logic. Specifically, we examine the  intentional design underlying logics-beliefs and practices-of equity represented in parent-teacher-researcher co-design meetings. These equity logics guide our powe rmediated critical analysis because familial engagement in STEM, like all social activities, is shaped by “taken for granted assumptions, beliefs, norms, and practices”  (Ishimaru & Galloway, 2021, p. 471) that shape dec isions, actions, and interactions regarding families. At the same time, families’ interactions either reinforce or challenge these logics. We emphasize how these logics may recognize, challenge, and transform systemic inequities present in STEM-related educational practices.  Baquedano-López et al. (2013) challenged the field to think c ritically about how neoliberal equity logics “often miss the multiple ways nondominant parents participate in th eir children’s education because th ey do not correspond to normative understandings of  parental involvement in schools” (p. 149). Specifically, we focus in on the intentional design of boundary infrastructure, with attention to boundary practices through parent-teacher-researcher co-design meetings. Thus, within the design of FamJam’s RPP boundary infrastructure, we utilize cultural hi storical activity theory (CHAT) (Engestrom et al, 1999) to make sense of the new modes of activity that may em erge and their relationships to particular tools, d ivision of labor, involving which stakeholders in the communit y, toward what kinds of desired outcomes.  We examine FamJam’s RPP activities through a lens of rightful presence, focusing on what happens, who participate s, how ideas flow, and where tensions arise. This approach  helps uncover the equity logics embedded in the sy stem— revealed through design features, roles, responsibilities, and the directional flow of ideas and actions—ultimately shaping the kinds of outcomes achieved.

## Methods

We draw upon a critical justice and participatory d esign-based research approach with researchers, teachers, families, and youth in three urban school districts. Our wo rk is rooted in an assets-based stance that centers how people’s lived lives and community cultural wealth and wisdom yield powerful forms of cultural knowledge/practice relevant to learning and engagin g in STEM. We seek to accentuate students’ and fami lies’ ingenuity to leverage their assets for social change making in transformative and future-oriented ways. Figure 1 illustrates the FamJam Design Cycle ’s primary components: 1) FamJam Co-exploring and codesign centering on familial capital and community cultural wealth for STEM learning, 2) Classroom Enactments, and 3) Reflective Dialogues. We aim to integrate new modes of rightful familial presence into focal STEM units, with the goal of fostering student learning and well-being. Three focal STEM units guided this study: Engineering for Sustainable Communities, Stress!, and Climate Justice. Data generation and analysis Data generation occurred collaboratively with partners and in the context of FamJam activity structures, and were similar in form across sites. Data sources included: RPP sessions (Researcher fieldnotes, Video, audio, and photo records of meetings, Collection of artifacts produced), Classroom enactments (Researcher fieldnotes, video/audio records of classroom enactments, teacher-produced artifacts, such as adapted lesson plans, r eflection notes, and student work, including activity sheets, sketch-ups, images of projects in various stages of develo pment, assessments, and daily or periodic exit tickets. We also generated images of all class produced work during class sessions (e.g., white board notes) and which hung o n class walls (e.g., list of sustainability  ideas). Last we conducted before/during/after design cycle intervie ws with students, teachers and family members. Stud ent interviews focused on their reflections on the work  they produced, their participation and engagement, descriptions of interactions/support youth received  from peers, educators, and community members, reso urces used, knowledge and practices (STEM knowledge and practice, funds of knowledge), and youth introduced topics. Teacher interviews focused  on their sense-making of on-going questions, concerns, and feel of the enactmen ts, including a focus on their understandings of family engagement in the RPP and focal unit. Lastly family interviews were conducted in parent/family and youth teams and  focused on their collective experiences in FamJam as well as the meaning of the student work produced for themselves and their families. This study took an abductive analytic approach, whi ch refers to “an inferential creative process of producing new hypotheses and theories based on surp rising research evidence” (Timmermans & Tavory, 201 2, p.170). Our approach to abductive analysis sits wit hin an historicized and future-oriented design-based participatory orientation where we give witness to and learn with youth and community.  Abduction involves an iterative process of moving back and forth between context-specific observations of such puzzling data to explanations that accounts for those particular obs ervations against a background of multiple existing  theories (and in this case a subset of literature involving justice-oriented approaches to familial engagement in STEM, see Calabrese Barton, Tan & Sedawi, accepted); and through systematic methodological analysis of  creatively inferencing and double-checking these inferences with more data.  Abductive analysis is, by design, a more complex and intuitive process as compared to traditional inductive research such as grounded theory. We began by engaging in dialogue with participating  teachers, families and students to identify any moments any team member (youth, families, teachers and/or researchers) thought was interesting, challe nging, and potentially offering insight (however tacit) in to what familial engagement is and can be. We began  simply with the question, “What is happening here?” (Charmaz, 2006). We then began coding fieldnotes, transcripts, and teacher/student artifacts from sessions identified by researchers, teachers, and families as important/powerful for familial engagement. This coding focused on equity conceptualizations, analyzing the logics driving pr oblem identification, engagement design, and outcomes. Us ing equity logics frameworks from research-practice partnerships (Farrell et al., 2022), we categorized logics, d istinguishing those aligned with dominant neolibera l approaches from those promoting justice-oriented stances. As codes emerged, we reflected on questions such as: How do these logics reshape our understanding of familial engagement? What new perspectives or gaps arise? What tensions challenge equity conceptions? These d iscussions informed further engagement with the justice oriented literature on familial/community engagement in STEM as we sought to further codify our initial insights against this literature and generating new analytic questions to pick at the edges of meaning of these insights. We then engaged a third coding phase with new guiding questions: Where, when, and how does engagement occ ur? Who learns, what is learned, and through which activities? How is STEM reshaped? Whose voices are legitimized, and to what end? Where does responsibility and powe r reside? How flexible are participants' roles? Usi ng these questions, we developed themes that we  further explored in FamJam activities: relational (temporal/spatial resonances, role fluidity), ethical (intersectional representation), and sociopolitical (solidarity, STEM for justice). We further analyzed these stances to uncover their underlying logics and their application in daily pr actices, structures, and activity systems.

These vig nettes were selected because they offer variability  in moments selected by our RPPs and illustrate a diversity of ways in which familial eng agement can take shape in transformative ways. Some teachers took on childcare roles during RPP meetings where young siblings attended. Ms. H, a Black woman veteran teacher in her sixties,  was extremely animated and got down on all fours to  crawl around with toddler siblings. Ms. H’s entire being was ani mated – she spoke in toddler-winning tones, modulating her inflections, making exaggerated facial expressions,  sang songs and moved in big sweeping movements to catch and hold the young child’s attention. She pulled out markers and huge drawing pads and engaged toddler siblings in tracing their feet and hands as an interactive g ame, while the parent s were building a paper circuit with their middle schooler. Families also used their mother tongues, such as Spanish, Farsi, Vietnamese and Akan, in their STEM tasks. Teachers marveled at the back and forth of rapid dialogues amidst a flurry of hand gestures and parent-child STEM collaboration in process. Teacher Ms. T watche d as her 7th grade student Andy and his mother conversed quietly in rapid Vietnamese (Andy’s mother was very  soft spoken, and Andy is by nature very boisterous  in the classroom, but he spoke softly to his mother during the RPP) w hile building an electric art card together.  Andy explained to Ms. T in English that they have a pet cat that his mother loves and she wanted to make a card with the cat as a design and for both eyes to light up, which led to Andy discussing with his mother, the kind of circuit (parallel versus simple) that they would need to then build. While Andy’s mother had never created a paper circuit before, her expertise could be seen by the more skillful way, compared to her son, in which she handled the fragile copper tape and her insistence to him that they lay down the copper tape slowly to avoid wrinkles and thus, breaks in the circuit. This was apparent to Ms. T  from her observation of the unfolding interactions between Andy and his mother, without understanding Vietnamese.

We see here an expansion of roles and responsibilities for both the teacher and the parents. In fact, there was a switching of traditional roles where teachers  became the c hild caretaker for younger siblings and parents became the co-learner and collaborator of their middle schooler i n STEM-focused hands-on activities. For example. Ms. H became the parental figure for the t oddlers, and Andy’s mother became his collaborator and teacher, when she showed him a better way of laying do wn copper tape without wrinkles, thereby trapping a ir bubbles and breaking the continuous circuit.

Within the FamJam RPP activity system, teachers and parents engaged in “role mediation” (Tzou, 2019), enacting new boundary practices  that disrupted previously settled parental and teacher roles. The new ways in which teachers and parents are positioned and experienced in action became salient and necessary to the collective allied political struggles for familial rightful presence in middle school STEM. Teachers and parents were able to encounter difference at the RPP boundary, for example, in the switching of their traditional roles and it is a feature of the FamJam RPP’s “boundary infrastructure” (Bowker & Star, 199 9) that facilitated the ability to respond to this difference. In stepping into her new toddler care-taker role, Ms. H pulled materials (washable markers, paper, a toy) to create a new activity system (within the larger FamJam RPP activity system) salient to her skills and knowledge about young children from being an aunt who took care of many nieces and nephews. There was also a redistribution of labor mirrored in the switching of parent-teacher roles. Here, we also see tenet 1 of the rightful presence framework at work: Ms. H and parents engaged in their right to reauthor rights with their traditional role change.

Through engaging in new activities of difference, a  more expansive flow of ideas and understanding flowed bidirectionally between teachers and parents. We see evidence of a deeper, and emergent, evidence-based understandings of teacher-parents’, bi-directionally, commitment to their middle school students’ STEM learning and overall well-being as a child in a familial unit. Beyond articulating a belief that “my child’s teacher cares for him” or “My child’s parent is interested in his STE M learning”, both te achers and parents experienced in embodied actions, the commitment of the other salie nt adult in their middle schoolers’ lives, to their  STEM learning. When Xavier’s grandmother said to Ms. H, Xavier’s 7th grade science teacher, “I need to know what is going on in his learning, what he is doing!”, she was then able to experience herself what Xavier did in Ms. H’s class, when he taught her how to create an electric art card for his great grandmother. While Ms. H hovered with smiles and encouragement to grandma, she also joked with Xavier, “let’s see you teach, now you know how hard it is!” and all three of them laughed at Ms. H’s comment. ## Discussion

Attending to equity logics that advance minoritized  families’ rightful presence and support their well being is necessary for disrupting the neoliberal ideology of  STEM and STEM education. Our findings suggest the following equity logics model as a guide for design ing and enacting rightful familial presence activit y systems. This model includes four interconnecting equity log ics: 1) Spatial and Temporal Resonances; 2) Solidar ity towards Equity Collaboration and Coordination for the Here-and-Now and towards Desired Futures; 3) Collective Responsibility for family engaged justice-oriented STEM; and 4) STEM for Thriving, Recognitio n and Critical Democratic Citizenry (see Figure 4).

Figure 4 Equity Logics in Family Engagement in STEM Towards Thriving These equity logics attune to the disruption of dominant power relations with respect to race, social class, language and immigration status, as well as to the formation  of new activity systems characterized by new ethical modes of relationalities and participation, intergenerati onality, and structural and role remediation. They provide a framework for how to conceptualize equity with respect to supporting children and families thriving within/across communities and with/in STEM. They also offer direction for the design of justice-oriented approaches to familial engagement in STEM. Taken together, these logics point towards complex articulations of how interventions may account for policy levers and systems redesign that move beyond indivi duals to reconstructing systems for collective thriving. The y also reveal a new collective educational imaginary for justice-oriented familial engagement that disrupts White-dominant school-centric approaches and legitimizes families’ community cultural wealth/capital, including sustaining forms of prese nce, and fost ers movement between families and schools, especially when such have historically been marginalized within STEM learning. The spatial and temporal resonances logic explores familial engagement dynamics, addressing the where, when, and how of these engagements. This logic invo kes spatial and temporal resonances by bridging gap s between families’ lives as defined by historicized injustices and resilience, with justice-oriented STEM education goals. It recognizes the multiplicity of familial networking as multidirectional movement, multi-location, and multiple forms  of familial presence. As shown in the vignettes, fo stering cultu rally responsive connections between families and schools involves multi-directional interactions among diverse participants, including educators, researchers, and extended family members, to connect schools, community resources, and curr icula. The second vignette, for example, illustrates multi ple forms of familial presence  – embodied, onto-epistemic, agentic, and intergenerational. Families engage d not only through bodily presence in FamJam sessions but also as onto-epistemic presence, acknowledging the c ultural, sociohistorical, and linguistic capital. T hey acted as codesigners in their children’s STEM education, fostering active engagement in engineering designs at home, in the community, and in school settings.  When families co-created electric art cards in the first vignette, t hose cards served as the families’ signature science artifacts (Calabrese Barton, Tan, & Rivet, 2008), carrying their presence to particular family members who were the intended recipients. The solidarity logic  explores whose voices are welcomed and legitimized in familial engagement in STEM. Solidarity is crucial for equitable educational futu res, involving relational, communal, and collective approaches. This entails building trust, mutual respect, and shared power am ong families, educators, and communities (Hong et al., 2022). In the first vigne tte, mutual relationships are built by recognizing family expertise, such as students taught their parents to build a light-up circuit. This shows how Ms. H became a trusted caregiver, allowing parents to focus on activities with their middle schooler s. The second vignette highlights a caring praxis, inviting students to explore differe nt ways of knowing and being in STEM, integrating t hese into designs that address families concerns.

The collective responsibility logic explores the roles that people play in familial engagement, and where responsibilities and power lie. This logic focuses on structural remediation by disrupting deficit-oriented views of families and communities and enacting agency for making structural changes for equitable relations (Tzou et al., 2019). For example, FamJam was designed to legitimize rightful familial presence in STEM, with families, researchers, and teachers sharing responsibilities for structural change, working together as allies to incubate new understandings of how STEM-relevant expertise resides in everyday life, and wh at that could look like in the classroom. This activity, and other FamJam co-designed, shared activity structures that make pres ent families onto-epistemologically, agentically and intergenerationa lly, served as both anchors and launching points fo r youths’ in classroom engineering for sustainable communities projects. The STEM for community thriving logic examines who is learning, what they are learning, a nd how the learning process unfolds. This logic redefines and shapes STEM learning as a means of social transformation and community thriving.  The vignettes demonstrate how working collaboratively with families’ members supporting healthier communities and an engaged citizenry.  This logic positions minoritized communities as key stakeholders and decision-makers in STEM, making their learning experiences m eaningful for  broader social change (Ballard et al., 2023).

The vignettes  illustrate moments of successful disruption of historically entrenched teacher-as-morepowered dynamic, through the new ways of embodied, collective activities. These activities were the processes by which new power dynamics were seeded between tea cher-parents as sociopolitical allies. New ties of relationality were networked and solidified, as tea chers and parents who had not met in such a collabo rative activity system before, were able to begin witnessing, and enacting care, love and joy, through engaging in STEM activities and necessary familial responsibilities – caring for other children in the family, being a son, daughter, a grandson, to one’s parental figure that is ever pre sent and ever relevant to a middle schooler. Teachers were able to appreciate the care the care and love their stud ents have for their families, and not just the fami lies’ love for them. Ms. T was particularly touched by how gentle Andy was with his soft-spoken mother.  A new teacher-familial unit, where teachers have genuinely caring interactions with their students’ family members, was a consequential outcome of the FamJam RPP activity system. These logics, although presented in four components for clarity, work in concert and are entangled with one another. These integral connections also add to  the utility of the logics to help us identify star ting points and necessary connections and expansions of which stakeholder’s roles, kinds of activities, and where and when they might take place. The logics therefore can serve as a “grid” to both design and “power” rightful familial STEM presence as well as to identify and diagnose potential or missing structures, stakeholders and activities important for seeding and solidifying rightful familial STEM presence.

## Implications and conclusions

This study examine d equity logics in familial engagement in STEM educat ion, emphasizing the importance of recognizing and integrating familie s’ cultural capital into school structures to support learning and thriving. Building new activity systems, grounded in new equity logics, allows families to reclaim sustained engagement in their children’s STEM education. Policies should  explicitly support diverse forms of family presenc e— embodied, agentic, intergenerational —and outcomes that go beyond individual gains to fos tering comm unity wellbeing. Viewing rightful familial engagement as a structural issue, not a deficit of families, requires collective efforts to co-create inclusive structures. By embracing shared responsibility and solidarity, we can uplift familial brilliance and foster spaces for enduring agency and thriving for youth, families, and communities. For this work to go forward, teachers and school administrators n eed to be key stakeholders at the table with families and researchers to co-design for new activity systems that would foster and sustain new relationalities and collaborative, STEM-and-family centered activities  across formal classrooms and informal spaces that work in concert to disrupt entrenched power dynamics. These activity systems need to reflect the differen t, salient identities that stakeholders simultaneously embody, that of a parental figure, a son or daughter, a sibling, a teacher, an ally and a co-learner of STEM and of families’ wisdom and experiences.
