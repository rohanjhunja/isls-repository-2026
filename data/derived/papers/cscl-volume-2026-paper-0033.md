# Small Arguments, Big Ideas: Fostering Children’s Collaborative Reasoning with LLM Agents

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Collaborative reasoning is at the core of critical thinking, problem solving, and effective communication, with growing attention being devoted to developing pedagogical and technological supports that enhance it. While many existing conversational agents (CAs) for education emphasize comprehension or factual recall during reading, the use of advanced reasoning behaviors, such as persuasion and inquiry, may further foster children’s engagement in collaborative reasoning. Based on recent advancements in large language models (LLMs), we created CAs that engage children in collaborative reasoning through argumentation during book-reading activities, by operationalizing three dialogue types from Walton’s framework of dialogue and argumentation: information-seeking, persuasion, and inquiry. We conducted a four-day exploratory study with 18 8-year-olds at a literacy-focused summer camp. Our findings revealed that while asking questions can support children’s collaborative reasoning, incorporating even small amounts of persuasion and inquiry elicits significantly more reasoning behaviors, especially explanations, clarifications, and reflections, from the children.

Collaborative reasoning is foundational to learning, enabling individuals to jointly develop their knowledge and understanding (Mercer, 1995) through exchanging information, resolving conflicts, and constructing shared understanding (Walton, 1998; Walton & Krabbe, 1995). It is well studied in learning science for fostering deeper engagement, critical thinking, and meaningful learning experiences (Cui & Teo, 2021; Mercer, 1995). Dialogic reading (DR) provides a rich environment for collaborative reasoning in early education, through interactive and reflective reading practices between children and adults (Pillinger & Vardy, 2022). It helps children develop critical socio-emotional skills (Fu et al., 2022; Pillinger & Vardy, 2022; Sethi & Jain, 2024; Whitehurst et al., 1994), which are strongly associated with healthy development and success both within and beyond the classroom (Newman & Dusenbury, 2015; Slovák & Fitzpatrick, 2015). With the advancement of Large Language Models (LLMs), emerging research identifies new and promising opportunities for Conversational Agents (CAs) in engaging children in productive DR activities (Chen et al., 2025; He et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2022; Xu & Warschauer, 2020; Zhang et al., 2022). However, existing reading CAs primarily rely on interactions driven by information-seeking questions (e.g., Zhang et al., 2022 and He et al., 2025) and scripted task-oriented responses (e.g., Hu et al., 2024), leaving a critical gap in leveraging the full spectrum of collaborative reasoning behaviors, such as posing challenges, offering rebuttals, seeking rationales, and providing explanations (Gillies, 2016; Tegos & Demetriadis, 2017). This study examines the impact of CAs with different dialogue types, specifically information-seeking, persuasion, and inquiry, on engaging children in collaborative reasoning during DR. These dialogue types are informed by the Waltonian framework of dialogue and argumentation (Walton, 1998; Walton & Krabbe, 1995) and are implemented by offering different kinds of responses (i.e., information-seeking, persuasion, and inquiry response types) that each embodies its distinguishing characteristics. This theoretical framework, originating in philosophy and formal argumentation, has been successfully applied to pedagogical agents (Norman et al., 2003; Sklar & Azhar, 2012) and, more recently, to classroom discourse analyses (Foster et al., 2022; Rapanta & Christodoulou, 2022). We conducted a four-day exploratory study involving 18 8-year-old children in a literacyfocused summer camp to interact with four CA prototypes: three that prioritize each response type separately, and a fourth that more freely mixes all thre e types based on the conversational flow. Our initial analyses, leveraging multiple existing behavior coding frameworks implemented via human-validated automated pipelines and qualitative analyses, show that CA prototypes met design goals and that SOTA LLMs candeliver argumentation224 based reasoning moves. Persuasion and inquiry responses elicited deeper reasoning behaviors, including questionasking, explanations, and metacognitive reflection. Notably, the mixed condition, despite being predominantly information-seeking with some offering of persuasion (8.2%) and inquiry (9.5%) responses, led to significantly more student justifications and explicit reasoning.

Our main contributions are 1) an initial exploration of the effects of different Waltoniandialogue types on children’s collaborative reasoning behavior during DR; 2) CAs implemented using two distinct architectures that offer information-seeking, persuasion, inquiry, and mixed dialogue types; and 3) a demonstration of how LLMs can be effectively used for conversational coding analyses.

## Related work

Story-reading conversational agents DR emphasizes the co-construction of knowledge through meaningful, reciprocal dialogue between readers, fostering critical thinking and active engagement, especially for socio-emotional learning (Wegerif, 2007). Xu and Warschauer (2019) provide an early exploration of the area, demonstrating the promising effects of CAs in engaging children with DR activities through the use of open-ended questions, praise, and explanations. There is emerging work that utilizes LLMs in building CAs for DR. Zhang et al. (2022) introduced StoryBuddy, which enhanced the experience by leveraging a fine-tuned LLM-based CA to dynamically generate questions that were more engaging, logically connected, and aligned with educational objectives. More recently, StoryPal by He et al. (2025) operationalized two DR frameworks (i.e., PEER and CROWD) through prompt engineering, enabling LLMs to generate framework-informed questions, dynamic scaffolding, and contextual feedback. While information-seeking is an important and effective DR practice, it remains unclear how CAs incorporating more advanced collaborative reasoning behaviors would impact children. This work offers an early exploration of the impacts of CAs that prioritize behaviors such as information-seeking, persuasion, and inquiry, as well as offering a natural mixture of all, in supporting collaborative reasoning for children in DR activities. Collaborative learning and the Waltonian framework Walton’s framework of dialogue and argumentation (Walton, 1998; Walton & Krabbe, 1995) provides a normative theory for understanding collaborative reasoning. It classifies dialogic argumentation into seven dialogue types —persuasion, negotiation, inquiry, deliberation, information-seeking, eristics, and mixed —each defined by its initial conditions, goals, and behavioral rules, which can be further refined into subtypes (Walton & Krabbe, 1995). In educational contexts, information-seeking, persuasion, and inquiry dialogues are particularly relevant, aligning with information exchange, where learners clarify and share knowledge; conflict resolution, where individuals negotiate competing perspectives to reach consensus; and co-construction of meaning, where individuals synthesize ideas to build shared understanding (Foster et al., 2022; Rapanta & Christodoulou, 2022). Walton’s framework has practical applications in the design of CAs and classroom interaction analyses, helping categorize teacher-student and peer argumentation in STEM education to foster critical thinking (Foster et al., 2022; Rapanta & Christodoulou, 2022). It guides systems that engage learners in structured argumentation, promoting deeper engagement and reasoning skills (Garrison et al., 1999; Reed, 2021; Sklar & Azhar, 2012). Despite the significant body of work, theory-based argumentation-driven CAs remain underexplored, as highlighted by Castagna et al. (2024). Our work aims to address this gap by creating CAs that operationalize Walton’s framework of dialogue and argumentation, chosen for its generalizability compared to DR-specific frameworks, such as PEER and CROWD (Pillinger & Vardy, 2022), and its flexibility for casual conversation over other formal argumentation theories, like Toulmin’s method (Toulmin, 2003). CA design and implementation We developed four CA prototypes operationalizing Walton’s framework of dialogue and argumentation by offering information-seeking, persuasion, inquiry, and a natural mixture of all three response types. The first three CA prototypes were designed as controlled implementations of the three dialogue types that allow us to gain an initial understanding of their strengths and limitations in influencing collaborative reasoning during DR. Using more sophisticated techniques, namely ReAct (Yao et al., 2023), we bu ilt the fourth prototype to explore how effectively SOTA LLMs can engage children in collaborative reasoning, under the Waltonian framework. Agent prototyping The CAs are designed to engage children in DR activities by incorporating the story context  (i.e., reading materials), an initial discussion question (e.g., How can one person’s hope and actions inspire a whole community 225 to work together for change?), and an agent’s internal stance  that guides the agent’s own reasoning (e.g., One person’s hope and actions inspire a whole community when they combine genuine passion, perseverance, and an inclusive approach that respects and uplifts others), which could be automatically generated or authored by educators (1). The user interface, shown in Figure 1, resembled a standard chat environment similar to ChatGPT, with the discussion question and a progress tracker displayed at the top to remind students of the question and their progress through the conversation. The two-stage generation approach is illustrated using the example conversation shown. To accommodate participants’ diverse literacy levels, the interface includes speech-to-text for child input and text-to-speech for the agent’s responses. Figure 1 System Overview with Interface, Sample Transcript, and Generated Responses Generation of information-seeking, persuasion, and inquiry responses To highlight the characteristics of each dialogue type, we adopted a two-stage generation approach common among CA designs, which decomposes the response generation process into a response generator and a dialogue manager (Ramesh et al., 2017). This approach allows for the separation of concerns and precise control over when each response type is offered. We adopted the zero-shot prompting approach for the response generator by providing descriptions of the characteristics of each response type based on Walton’s framework (2). An iterative refinement process was employed to adjust the descriptions in the prompt, ensuring the generated responses were more closely aligned with the expected response type. Each CA prototype is then associated with a simple rule-based dialogue manager that prioritizes the target response type. Similar to prior work, such as ContextQ (Dietz Smith et al., 2024), the dialogue managers assess the appropriateness of the generated response, as judged by an LLM. When the response is deem ed inappropriate for the conversational context, the manager defaults to LLM-based free response generation for that turn.

Generation of a mixture of the three response types While the straightforward two-stage generation approach helps deliver and amplify the characteristics of each dialogue type suitable for a controlled experiment, it may undermine the natural flow of humandialogue, thus lacking resemblance to authentic collaborative reasoning. Therefore, we introduced a fourth CA prototype that aims to include a mixture of the three response types while maintaining the natural flow of conversation. We adopted the ReAct prompting technique (Yao et al., 2023), which is centered on the think-then-act process that demonstrates effective dialogue reasoning ability. The ReAct-based agent implements the following reasoning pattern. At the start of each turn, the agent first formulates its current agenda, which specifies the immediate conversational goal (e.g., introduction itself, collaborating on a question, or completing an unfinished subdialogue). The obligation component tracks rules or prior commitments that constrain how the agent should act with respect to the agenda. The agent also engages in an internal thought-critique-revise process, reasoning about how best to fulfill its agenda, given its obligations. Finally, the agent determines the most appropriate dialogue move from the theory and generates a response. As a form of working memory, we also allow the agent to create comments, which are tracked throughout the conversation. 226 Technical evaluation We used a manipulation check inspired by Govers et al. We randomly sampled five agent responses from each of the eight conversations (approximately one-third of all agen t responses) and applied the zero-shot generation prompts to produce multiple alternative responses. ## Research method

We conducted an exploratory human-subject study using the CA prototypes to explore two research questions: RQ1. How to build CAs that can engage children in collaborative reasoning? RQ2. What are the strengths and limitations of different dialogue types for supporting collaborative reasoning during DR activities?

## Study design

The study consists of four conditions, each corresponding to a CA prototype that provides a target dialogue type: information-seeking, persuasion, inquiry, and mixed. Because student attendance varied across days, the study condition was randomly assigned to participants for each day’s session. The randomization was designed to ensure that each participant was exposed to each condition at most once, and the conditions were balanced across days, gender groups, and reading materials to the greatest extent possible. In addition to the CA conditions, each day also included a child-child baseline condition in which two participants were randomly selected (without repetition) to discuss the same question with one another. Each day, a new book was introduced to the participants. The research team created the discussion questions for each book in consultation with the summer camp organizers to ensure relevance to the camp curriculum and age-appropriateness. We used ChatGPT to generate the agent’s internal stances for each question based on the teacher’s curriculum learning objectives of the book.

## Participant

A total of 18 students (10 males, 8 females), all aged 8, participated in the study. Most participants identified as Black or African American and native English speakers. Students exhibited varying levels of prior experience with digital technology, inclu ding using laptops, but reported minimal familiarity with LLMs such as ChatGPT. 227 Informed assent was obtained prior to the study, and the study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the academic institute.

## Study procedure

The study was conducted over four consecutive days at a literacy-focused summer camp. Prior to the study, one of the authors led an introductory session designed to familiarize participants with the concepts of LLMs and CAs. Participants were invited to have a free-interaction session with the CA prototypes, and feedback from the session was incorporated into the interface.

Each study day, participants took part in a 30-minute study session, following a guided reading session of a new book led by teaching assistants from the summer camp. The study session includes four stages:

1. Pre-study test-Participants were asked to answer a pre-defined discussion question about the book.

2. Collaborative reasoning-Participants are invited to discuss the same question with one of the CA

prototypes. To ensure sufficient interaction, each conversation was required to be at least 15 turns long— a threshold derived from observations during the introductory session.

3. Post-study test-Participants were asked to answer the same discussion question after CA interactions.

4. Usability questionnaire-Participants completed a short Likert-scale usability questionnaire about their

interaction with the CA.

We collected pre-and post-study tests using an open-ended questionnaire. Full conversation transcripts and internal system logs were recorded, including response start and end times, dialogue manager decisions, and the model’s internal reasoning traces for the ReAct-based CA. We used the Chatbot Usability Questionnaire (CUQ) (Holmes et al., 2019), a 16-item Likert-scale questionnaire developed for evaluating chatbot usability, to assess participants’ perceptions of each CA prototype. The child-child baseline condition was video-recorded.

## Data analysis

Preprocessing and screening In total, we collected 53 child-agent dialogues. One author manually inspected and corrected all participants’ utterances due to imperfect speech-to-text performance in natural classroom settings. Following the manual screening process, we removed 11 child-agent dialogues that were either fewer than three turns or largely off topic, resulting in 42 valid conversations for analysis. Additionally, 29 completed usability questionnaires and 26 matched pairs of pre-and post-study tests were collected. For the c hild-child baseline condition, two out of four sessions were retrained for analysis. The session on day one was excluded due to poor audio quality, and the session for day four was excluded because one participant had not completed the assigned reading pri or to the Coding of verbal interaction For agent utterances, we developed a lightweight classifier for information-seeking, persuasion, and inquiry, trained on 480 manually coded agent responses from the system’s technical evaluation phase. We utilized SentenceTransformers (Reimers & Gurevych, 2019), specifically the all-mpnet-base-v2 model provided by the library, to obtain high-dimensional sentence embeddings. We then trained a logistic regression classifier from Scikit-learn (Pedregosa et al., 2011) to predict the corresponding response type (i.e., information-seeking, persuasion, or inquiry). We evaluated the classifier through five-fold cross-validation, stratified by response type to maintain class distribution, achieving a mean accuracy of 83.1% and a weighted F1 score of 82.9%. For child utterances, we adopted two established coding schemes of collaborative reasoning behaviors in learning to obtain comprehensive perspectives on the impact of different dialogue types. The first scheme, developed by van Boxtel et al. (2000), contains 11 categories of communicative functions for collaborative reasoning: arguments, evaluations, questions, requests, proposals, confirmations, rejections, repeats, orders, and off-task utterances (van Boxtel et al., 2000). The second scheme, developed by Rathod and Murthy (2022), contains nine categories with more detailed perspectives regarding reasoning-oriented moves: clarify, support, question posing, recognize, propose, defend, reject, evidence, and causal talk (Rathod & Murthy, 2022). To develop automated classifiers for these two coding schemes, we sampled four conversation transcripts from the screened corpus, one from each CA condition. Two authors independently annotated all children’s utterances at the sentence level using both cod ing schemes. A Cohen’s Kappa of 𝜅𝜅 ≈ 0.70 was reached for each 228 scheme, indicating substantial agreement. Discrepancies were jointly resolved, yielding a dataset of 168 labeled samples (84 per coding scheme).

We implemented a zero-shot LLM classification pipeline for the two coding schemes, as the previous embedding-based approach yielded poor results. Two prompts were designed following a consistent template: 1) a high-level task description, 2) the coding manual, 3) special notes to clarify ambiguous distinctions, 4) formatting instructions, and 5) the full conversation history ending with the target child’s utterance. We iteratively refined the prompts, focusing on the special notes section, to reduce confus ion between similar codes. Finally, using GPT-5 (i.e., gpt-5-2025-08-07), the two zero-shot prompts achieved an accuracy of 80.5% (weighted F1 of 80.4%) for the van Boxtel et al. (2000) scheme and an accuracy of 77.8% (weighted F1 of 76.9%) for the Rathod and Murthy (2022) scheme.

230 Mixed CA  Participants in the mixed CA condition exhibited the most Argument and Clarify moves, indicating explicit reasoning and repair to externalize and share their internal thoughts. The condition’s CUQ score was similar to that of the persuasion condition, and human inspection revealed that the responses typically followed a three-part structure: 1) acknowledgment of the child’s contribution, 2) introduction or extension of ideas by the agent, and 3) a question to further engage the child. This structure is outlined in the agent’s prompt. The dialogue managers generally produced the intended response types, with the mixed agent naturally blending strategies. Our analysis suggests two design principles for improving conversational naturalness and engagement: 1) Adaptive and Contextual Blending of Dialogue Strategies. The mixed condition, while predominantly information-seeking, incorporated persuasion (8.2%) and inquiry (9.5%) responses, prompting significantly more Argument and Clarify moves from children than the persuasion or inquiry agent s. Persuasion and inquiry strategies were most effective when applied selectively and in response to conversational context, rather than through fixed or uniform patterns. 2) Keep Responses Concise and Interactive. Persuasion and inquiry agents often produced long, lecture-like turns associated with lower usability and fewer reasoning-oriented child moves. Breaking content into micro-turns—short prompts, comprehension checks, and quick follow-ups—helps maintain conversational rhythm and engagement (e,g., DAPIE by Lee et al., 2023).

## Limitations and future work

This study has several limitations, and the results should be considered preliminary. 1) the sample was small (i.e., 18 participants) and drawn from a single summer camp; variable attendance led to uneven exposure across conditions, and only one human baseline per day limited peer comparisons; 2) the child-child baseline condition included only male participants due to practical constraints; and 3) the pre-and post-responses were brief because of time limitations in the natural classroom environment. Moreo ver, the current implementation of the three Waltoniandialogue types represents a simplified interpretation of the theory, realized through turn-level response types rather than episode-level sub-dialogues. Future work will expand the population sample and ensure even exposure across CA conditions using within-subject study designs. We also seek to address the limitations of the current CA prototypes and develop more advanced realizations of the Waltoniandialogue types through adaptive dialogue policies that detect debatable moments, dynamically mix dialogue types, and control response length via micro-planning; gathering and incorporating stakeholder feedback and pedagogical best practices. Finally, we also plan to leverage additional measures (e.g., delayed post-tests, transfer tasks, discourse-based reasoning rubrics, and engagement measures) to deepen our understanding of the underlying mechanisms linking collaborative reasoning with learning processes and outcomes. This work examined how four CAs (i.e., information-seeking, persuasion, inquiry, and mixed) can support children’s collaborative reasoning during DR activities. The information-seeking agent was rated highest for usability and engagement, while the persuasion and inquiry agents elicited broader reasoning behaviors but often produced longer, less interactive turns. The mixed agent, blending multiple strategies, prompted the most reasoning-oriented moves from children, suggesting the benefits of adaptive and context-sensitive strategy use. Overall, the findings indicate that conversational agents should balance structure with flexibility and maintain concise, interactive exchanges to better foster children’s reasoning and engagement. However, given the small sample and simplified dialogue models, these results should be viewed as preliminary but point toward promising directions for designing more natural and pedagogically effective child-agent interactions.
