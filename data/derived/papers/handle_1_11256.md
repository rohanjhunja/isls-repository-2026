# From the Dominance of Authorities to Residents’ Inclusion to Decision-Making Processes

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study aims to investigate the collaboration of civil society and municipal administration in the systematic change process of a neighborhood. Five community workshops were conducted in the City of Espoo, Finland. The method of community workshop provided a structure for examining a complex urban situation and facilitated collective problem-solving. The methodology of cultural-historical activity theory (CHAT) enabled to identify the expansive learning actions and follow the transformation of the object of activity during the workshop process. The study shows that the expansive learning actions helped the residents and civil servants collectively resolve contradictions. This in turn enabled them to see the developmental potential of their activities and take actions to transform them. This study reveals a learning process in the context of urbandevelopment by addressing contradictions as valuable resources for learning and development.

## Introduction

There is a growing interest to include residents in developing urban areas and engage them in learning within their communities. Consequently, appropriate methods to foster the residents’ democratic participation in and learning about urbandevelopment are needed. Recognizing the capacity of communities is important  for acknowledging that people can lead social change in their neighbo rhoods and communities. (Gutiérrez et al., 2016; Gutiérrez & Jurow, 2016; Jurow et al., 2016; Meléndez, 2021).

In this study, an urbandistrict was being redeveloped, and  residents’ opinions about the future of their neighborhood were needed. In Finland, open decision-making practices by promoting spontaneous local activities and the place-based knowledge of the residents and communities  in accordance with the Local Government Act

(2015) are enhanced.  This aims to increase opportunities for different g roups to participate equally. However,

including residents in collaboration activities and decision-making processes in urbandevelopment was challenging for the city governance. To ensure that all the different voices in residential areas are heard, we need to learn more about how to bring about change when,  for example, culturally diverse communities collab orate with municipalities.

## Theoretical framework

The theoretical framework of the study was based on CHAT, which provides a methodology for studying practical transformation efforts and expansive learning in a human activity. Engeström’s (1987/2015) theory of e xpansive learning describes the sequence of actions, the reasons for these sequences, and the mechanisms that generate the transitions from one action to the next. Contradictions are driving forces in learning, because they turn the object of activity into a motive. They are central sources of change and development.  The object of activity that derives from CHAT (Leont’ev, 1978) is relevant for changing dysfunctional activities and practices. The methodology of expansive learning facilitates human learning throu gh the analysis of systemic contradictions. In this  process, tools serve as a means to mediate the activity to transform the joint object of activity  (Engeström et al., 2014; Sannino et al., 2016).  Changing the object means  that participants reframe what they are collectively wor king toward, which can transform their actions and the system. This type of learning process leads to the development of participants’ learning, from individual actions to a collective activity system. Expansive learning is a process of analyzing, experimenting with, and reconceptualizing the object of activity through solving the structural contradictions that emerge between the practices (Engeström, 1987/2015). A new expanding object emerges when learners constr uct a concept for their collective activity and rea lize it in practice (Rantavuori et al., 2016). A change in an activity takes place through a succession of expansive learning actions (Engeström & Sannino, 2010). The process begins with questioning the aspects of the activity or practice. By analyzing the situation, participants can engage in modeling new ideas and models, which in turn offers a solution to a problem. Examining and implementing the new models enables them to understand the dynamics and possible extensions of the situation, after which they can reflect on the process of expansive learning and consolidate the new model in practice. After this dialectical process, people can overcome the contradictions and tensions between the opposing forces, integrate the new knowledge into people’s lives and everyday actions, and distribute it into collective practices (Engeström, 2020; Sannino & Engeström, 2018). Expansive learning actions form an expansive learning cycle. Expansive learning is a movement between consequential here-and-now actions and long-term envisioning (Engeström et al., 2013). The close interaction of future-oriented, activity-level envisioning and consequential, action-level decision-making helps connect the goals of the actions to th e object of the collective activity.

Contradictions are defined as historically accumulating structural tensions within and between opposing forces in an activity and their environment (Engest röm, 1987/2015; Il’enkov, 1977). For identifying contradictions, the framework of the types of discursive manifestations of contradictions is useful (Engeström & Sannino, 2011). In this study dilemmas are in focus. They refer to aspects of socially shared beliefs in dail y thinking that give rise to dilemmatic thinking among individuals. Contrary themes of discourse usually consist of issues that make participants argue and over which they mull in their lives and practices (Billig et a l., 1988). Dilemmas are usually manifested when evaluations are incompatible and typically reproduced than resolved, often through denial or reformulation.

## Community workshops and participants

Five successive workshops, based on the method of C hange Laboratory (Virkkunen & Newnham, 2013), each lasting two and a half hours, were held in the spri ng 2015 at two-week intervals, with an experimental implementation period of two months between the fourth and fifth workshops. The appropriate set of instruments, the structure of the collaboration, and opportunities for multivoiced reflective dialogue were highlighted. The aim of the workshops was to analyze the tensions and contradictions in the neighborhood, envision future development models, plan implementation measures, and sketch fu ture developmental steps.  The intervention focused on establishing a shared object for the development in the neighborhood and enhancing collaboration between  civil society and cross-sectorial municipality.

Four researcher-interventionists, including the author, acted as facilitators in th e participants’ groups, while the main interventionist was in charge of leading the worksh ops. The researcher-interventionists elicited and maintained a collective learning process that included carefully designed stimulating activities, organizations of the working groups, and guidance. The workshop p articipants were divided into four groups in each s ession. The groups comprised different individuals each time.

Community workshops connect ed actors and learning sites across different sectors and levels such as urban, non-governmental and grassroot level to collaborate and  combine their resources to resolve the contradictions and enact their activities (see Sannino, 2020). Altogether 49 participants from  various residents’ assemblies, non-governmental associations, and administrative sectors attended the community workshops to cocreate the redevelopment of their neighborhood. They were of all ages, from different backgrounds, and included both immigrants and people from the majority population, and women and men. Data and analysis The discourse data were collected through group discussions in five video-and audio-recorded workshop sessions that yielded 22 hours of recordings  which were verbatim transcribed. To understand how the c ommunity workshops helped different stakeholders collaborate when facing changes in an urban neighborhood, I focused on episodes that revealed the residents’ need to parti cipate in the decision-making processes and the public sector’s need to enhance collaboration among the urban actors. The analysis involved coding linguistic cues for discursive manifestations of the contradictions and expansive learning actions in natural interactions, connecting them to the contextual purpose. The findings I share here are carefully selected, g rounded instances from the analysis. My inquiry proceeded through the following steps.

First, I identified the contradictions through their explicit manifestations in the participants’ discussions, according to the systematic conceptual framework of  contradictions, with their linguistic cues connect ed to the contextual purpose, to analyze the sequences of cha nge in the neighborhood activities (Engeström & San nino, 2011). In this study, a dilemma was indicated by the expli cit linguistic cues “on the one hand… on the other hand”, or constant occurrences of the words “yes, b ut” in the conversations. Second, I traced the expansive learning actions of questioning, analyzing, modelling, examining, im plementing, reflecting, and consolidating in the data (Engeström & Sannino, 2010). Third, I wrote the findings as a case-led narrative. The participants in the narrative include Emma, resident and development manager in the municipality, Keith, counselor in local mental health association, Louise, social worker in local parish, and Philip, project leader in municipality.

## Findings

This study investigated  the learning processes that emerged when civil soci ety and a municipal administration collaborated to co-design local practices and solutions in a changing neighborhood. Residents’ participation in the decision-making processes and collaboration with the public sector was in a focus. The neighborhood offers safety walks regularly to i ts residents. They are joint walking tours by civil servants and residents with the aim of evaluating t he neighborhood’s quality in terms of safety and he alth risks and informing urban planners of the results. In the second workshop, a resident’s experience concerning the safety walk was presented to participants. The resident felt that the municipality had not made any improvements  toneighborhood according to the feedback from safety walks. This inspired a lively discussion and helped to recognize the problematic situation.

The participants started to analyze the municipality’s ability to use its executive power in the development of the urban areas as their object of the activity. They felt that the municipality had not taken the  feedback of the safety walks into consideration. On the one hand, residents are invited to state their opinions and to make proposals for the development of their neighborhood, and on t he other hand, the funding for the implementation o f the proposals is missing. The contradiction here manifested difficult choices in the municipality, and it was marked a dilemma (Dilemma #1). The dilemma with its linguistic cues can be constructed from this description by Philip: The safety walks have been very successful. Approxi mately 200 residents have attended them. Besides residents, there were representatives from the police and housing companies, politicians, and civil servants from various administrative sectors. Nevertheless, there has not been a budget for implementing the improvement prop osals from the residents. That is a problem. In addition, the advertising of the safety walks has been poor, and the reporting of the results has been slow.

After the participants had realized that the munici pality would not implement the residents’ improveme nt proposals, they concentrated on transferring the decisional power to the residents  as the ir new object of the activity. They criticized the separate budgets of the departments in the muni cipality. They used tools to solve the dilemma. First, the civil servants modeled incentive-based budgeting as a tool to transfer the decision-making power to residents to take action in influencing their neighborhood. An incentive-based budgeting model provides individual units with incentives to achieve a posit ive financial performance. This model, which is bas ed on aligning funding resources with activity levels, was not supported by all. Second, the participants modeled a digital platform and new electronic tools for connecting pe ople to collective urban planning.  These tools were not accepted by all. Third, the participants modeled a cross-sectorial and multiprofessional regional developmen t group consisting of residents, community associations, and civil servants as a means to steer resident-based development in the city.

In the third workshop, they examined the mandate of the regional development group by explicating that it should have the decisional power, responsibilities,  and resources to lead the value-based discussions of residents’ initiatives across the borders of the ad ministrative sectors.  Louise indicated this by saying: “There should be the resources, rules, and the cross-sectorial collaboration to support the dialog.” Kei th continued the discussion and claimed: “If the residents express an idea or a proposal, there should be a group that elaborates on the residents’ initiatives.” In this way, the active residents could access municipal financial resources in the future. In the fourth workshop, concerning the modeling of the regional development group and its responsibilities, Philip asked: “We might alsoneed  a thorough discussion of the values. Does the valu e basis emerge from the members of the regional development group, or shall we [the participants in the workshop] define the goals now?” This utterance produced another dilemma (Dilemma #2) showing the hesitancy of the participants with the obscure power relations and decision-making processes. On the one hand, the residents ha ve the opportunity to participate in decision-making, but on the other hand, the long-term goals are missing. However, the utterance included the word we, which indicates a shift to working collectively. The solution, for the time being, to both dilemmas materialized during the conversation by modeling the regional development group to include resident members in order to allocate power, resources, and responsibilities among the urban actors. Later, the regional development group would plan a model of participatory budgeting as a tool for enabling residents to make decisions concerning the implementation of residents’ proposals. The participants still raised the criticism that the residents wer e often asked their opinions, but they did not lead  anywhere. However, Emma stated that the residents could make the rules  in the participatory budgeting process, and Philip ’s earlier hesitance disappeared: Emma:  The critical point, for example, in the participatory b udgeting process is the choosing of the winning proposals.  The residents should participate in the selection of the winning proposals. It has to be a common choice. It could be a digital voting process. It could be wise to spend t he money periodically, so that the residents could attend in the next round.

Facilitator:  Can everyone participate in the voting? What is the practice? Emma:  Of course. We can make the rules  [a regional development group including resident members], for example, the voting phase could be twice a year. Philip: The most important thing is that the residents should c reate the goals of the participatory budgeting process.

Emma committed to bringing the idea of participatory budgeting to the municipality’s cross-sectorial development group. Participatory budgeting would enable residen ts to decide and implement their welfare proposals in the neighborhood. In 2016 –17, the participatory budgeting process was implemented  in the neighborhood. The residents were involved in planning, monitoring, and evaluating the process.

## Discussion

The two dilemmas concerned obscure power relations;  the municipality did not target money to the resid ents’ improvement proposals and had no intention to give the residents the opportunity to decide on them. The dilemmas repeatedly highlighted the responsi bilities of the municipality, its capacity to coord inate activities, and its collaborating practices. This enabled deliberation, triggered the expansive learning actions to emerge and helped the participants to find a shared object of activity.

During their learning process, the participants cha nged the object of using the executive power in the municipality intonew object of transferring the de cisional power to the residents with the help of ex pansive learning actions. The residents succeeded in increasing their opportunity to participate by bringing together the ir future-oriented activity-level envisioning of having power and resources wit h the action-level decisions of including residents in the regional development gro up and planning a participatory budgeting process. The establishment of the cross-sectorial regional development group, including residents, can be considered a solution for the dilemmas for the time being. The idea of participatory budgeting paved the way for detailed opportunities to make goals and rules, and it represented a powerful tool in the future to sustain the residents’ collective learning and transform their activity. In this vein, the residents’ strengthened their agency. The methodological steps of the expansive learning actions helped the participants move toward a horizon that could contain many future opportunities for them. Expansive learning was strongly connected to the object of activity. The participants developed tool s to solve the contradictions and pursue the joint object of the activity. In this study, the cross-sectorial regional development group can be conside red a key tool, since it increased residents’ decision-making power, connected them to the collaborat ion with civil servants in the city, and enhanced their participation and agency in urbandevelopment. By seeing communities as sites of learning, the study contributes to the research on the learning processes of civil society and municipal administration. The study enables new understanding of how learning  can take place in informal settings. Working and learning in  a changing neighborhood required recognizing suita ble practices and challenging situations for learning. Community’s practices can be used for powerful lear ning (see Gutiérrez & Jurow, 2016). The methods and tools based on CHAT helped collectively  transform the potential of contradictions into valuable resources for learning  and development (see Engeström et al., 202 2). This opened new possibilities for the redevelopment of the residents’ neighborhoo d and promoted building a more equitable social world, conceptualizing change as coming from  within the practices of the community and connecti ng it to learning goals (see  Curnow & Jurow, 2021; Meléndez, 2021). Working on the contradictions improved the residents’ and civil servants’ collective learning and triggered a change in urban activities.
