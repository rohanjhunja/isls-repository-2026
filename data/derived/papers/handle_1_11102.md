# Building Digital Competence: Personal and Contextual Factors in Teachers' Professional Learning

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Teachers are working within an ever-changing environment where digital transformation requires continuous updating of their digital competence. To support them in developing their digital competence, SELFIEforTEACHERS (SfT) proposes a self-reflection process guiding them to self-assess their digital competence and engage in professional learning practices. The present study comprised 13 case studies from six European countries and explored various use cases of SfT through qualitative analysis. This paper discusses findings in relation to the conditions that can support or hinder teachers in engaging in a self-reflection process using SfT for identifying their learning needs, based on which they design their professional learning paths to further develop their digital competence. These findings can contribute to a better understanding of the opportunities and limitations of the SfT self-reflection process, as a meaningful professional learning process for teachers that can support their agency to further develop their digital competence.

## Introduction

The teaching profession requires educators equipped wit h the necessary competences to respond to rapidly changing demands for teaching and learning. There has been an increasing need to find ways to support teachers’ professional development to harness benefits and opportunities, as well as d eal with challenges that digital technologies present to education.  More learner-centered pedagogical approaches are emerging to add ress teachers’ professional learning, with emphasis on teachers taking an active role and developing their agency as reflective professionals (Boeskens et al., 2020). SELFIEforTEACHERS (SfT)1 is a tool developed by the European Commission that adopts self-reflection as a professional learning approach to support teachers’ agency to develop their digital competence, by engaging them in self-assessing their digital competence and prompting them to design their professional learning paths  (Economou, 2023). The present study adopts a qualit ative approach to explore the opportunities and limitations of self-reflection using SfT as a professional learning process to support teachers’ agency to further develop their digital competence.  The findings from the application of the SfT in the professional development of teachers in six European countries, can inform us about the applicability and limits of such an approach with teachers in different areas of Europe, representing different levels of various socio-economic development as measured by the Digital Economy and Society Index (DESI) and different types of educational systems, ranging from centralized to de-centralized models of educational governance.

## Theoretical framework

Educators’ digital competence Recent efforts to provide a common definition of the term ‘digital competence’ at European level led to defining it as “the safe, critical, and sensible use of digital  technologies to learn at work and to participate i n society, as well as to interact with them” (European Commission, 2018, p.9). Based on this definition as well as the definition of ‘competence’ as a combination of knowledge, skills and attitudes (E uropean Commission, 2006), educators’ digital competence was described in the European Framework for the Digital  Competence of Educators (DigCompEdu) as a construct which comprises six areas with 22 competences (Redecker, 2017). Using the DigCompEdu conceptual framework as a benchmark for self-assessing teachers’ digital competence, SfT aims to respond to the need for effective professional development to foster digitally competent primary  and secondary teachers across Europe (European Commission, 2020).

Reflection, self-regulation and agency to change SfT is an integral part of a comprehensive professional  learning process based on self-reflection, and it aims to encourage teachers’ agency through active engagement in their professional development. Reflection, perceived as a personal process that candeepen one’s understanding of self and lead to significant discoveries or insights (Desjarlais & Smith, 2011), can trigger  self-assessing one’s capacity to improve. It can  support teachers in becoming self-regulated learners who have agency to design and pursue their  own professional learning paths. Frazier, Schwartz and Metcalfe (2021)  have proposed a dynamic model of self-regulation, in which envisioning the directions self-development may take (possible selves)  and applying metacognitive strategies to achieve that (metacognition), can lead to self-efficacy and agency  (agency), and can produce outcomes (behavior) that can fulfill some of the possibilities envisioned at the beginning. The SfT tool can support teachers in identifying their digital competence but also set  future goals by benchmarking themselves according t o the SfT levels. It also suggests strategies on how to proceed achieving the se goals. SfT  has been designed to be at the core of  a professional learning process where teachers identify their learning needs based on the feedback they receive from the tool, and then proceed to design their professional learning paths and professional learning practices. It aims to involve teachers in self-reflection at three stages: reflect on their profes sional practice and self-assess their digital competence guided by the SfT tool; reflect on their results and the tool feedback to plan their professional learning paths; and reflect on the whole process and their progress (Economou, 2023). As critical self-reflection can lead to engaging in strategies for continuing p ersonal, professional and career development (for e xample, setting goals) (Cheng et al., 2015), teachers are encouraged to design their own professional learning paths to further develop their digital competence.

Teachers’ agency in SfT is approached from the perspective of teachers’ critical self-assessment of their digital competence and interest to design their pro fessional learning paths for further development. I t extends to their professional practice and how they apply their new skills in their work environment. In line with Priestley et al.’s (2015) ecological approach, teachers’ agency to self-develop is  considered as  not merely dependent on teachers as individuals but also on their socio-cultural environment. In this respect, self-reflection may also be achieved in a collaborative setting  and supported by peers. However, t he nature of  teacher agency and how teachers’ agency can be supported in professional l earning and work  environments is still not well-understood (Günal-Aggül et al., 2023). Furthermore, while reflection is often discussed as  a powerful driver for teacher learning, and digital tools like video and synchronous or asynchronous communic ation tools have been used to support reflective dialogues on teachers’ practice  (Robertson, Hall & Brock, 2023),  there are no reports  to our knowledge on the potential of self-reflection tools, like the SfT, to support teachers’ agency to develop their digital competence. Thus the work described in this paper can  contribute to understanding the affordances, but also the challenges, of how digital self-reflection tools, like the SfT, could support teachers in developing their agency for professional learning.  As such, this study also has implications for professional development and practice.

## Research questions

The goal of this multi-case qualitati ve study was to explore how self-reflection, using SfT, can support professional agency and build teachers’ digital com petence, from both a “process” and an “outcome-oriented” viewpoint. Analyzing the process includes key factors, both enablers and barriers, for the effective use of SfT for self-reflection in  support of building teachers’ digital competence. Key factors influencing teacher s’ use of technologies, as drivers or barriers or both, can be approached from different perspectives such as teacher characteristics, school factors and systemic factors (Law et al., 2008), or extrinsic and intrinsic factors (Ertmer, 1999). The current study examined influencing factors from the perspective of the tea chers’ specific experience (i.e., the tool and the process), personal characteristics, and institutional and systemic factors. Analysis of the outcomes includes teachers’ active engagement in designing t heir learning paths and use of digital technologies in their professional practice.  Therefore, in this paper, we are addressing the following research questions.

• RQ1: Can a self-reflection tool (SfT) support an agentic approach to teachers’ development of their

digital competence? If yes, how and to what extent?

• RQ2: What are the main conditions, enablers and barriers,  for the effective use of self-reflection using

SfT to support teachers’ development of their digital competence?

## Research design

The study was designed as an instrumental, multiple  case study (Stake, 2013), with the data corpus con sisting of 13 case studies. The case studies represented a variety of use cases  designed and implemented in six European countries – Cyprus (CY), Finland (FI), Greece (EL), Ireland (IE), Spain (ES), and The Netherlands (NL) – such as: teachers from different schools  registering for a professional development course o ffered by a university on fostering digital competence through the SfT self-reflection tool; a school leadership team using the SfT to support teachers’ digital competence and instructional practice; an in-service teacher taking the initiative to integrate SfT into her school to empower the digital competence o f her colleagues; a  Ministry of Education Inspector investigating the SfT tool’s potential to support teachers’ development of digital competence; a cluster of schools using the SfT to support their teachers’ digital competence; or in-service teachers, who are also graduate students, engaging in self-reflection as part of their teacher certification requirements (see Table 1). ## Participants

The study adopted a purposive sampling approach, wi th 116 participants having completed the SfT. The main actors of the case studies were pre-or in-service teachers, while school leadership teams, te acher professional development institutions, and representatives of educational authorities we re secondary actors. The recruitment of teachers was based o n convenience sampling (Etikan et al., 2016), prima rily through existing networks and collaborations between local universities and schoo ls, as well as through an open call. In particular, most of the teachers participating in the study were self-selected and already highly motivated to improve their digital competence. Informed consents were signed by all participants and ethical guidelines in qualitative research were followed.

## Data collection

Two semi-structured interview protocols were developed to collect data  in all participating c ountries: The first was used with individual teachers or teacher groups, and the second with school leaders and a policy maker. Questions in each of the instruments sought to unde rstand how the SfT self-reflection process interacted with participants’ agency, self-efficacy, motivation, and plans of action, as well as the personal, insti tutional, and systemic aspects that could support or hinder teach ers in taking up the feedback provided by the SfT t ool. Additionally, data were collected for triangulation from complementary sour ces such as teachers’ presentations, teachers’ collaborative discussions, field notes, t eacher action plans and professional learning desig ns. The aggregated anonymized data collected in the SfT tool were also available to complement qualitative data with quantitative data on participants’ self-assessment of their digital competence based on their responses to the SfT.

## Data analysis

Data were transcribed verbatim and coded using the ATLAS.ti qualitative analysis software, using a detailed codebook. The coding scheme had six major categorie s with several sub-codes. Intercoder agreement was above satisfactory (Cohen’s k>0.7). The six major coding categories identified were: (1) Perception of SfT, (2) Teacher Attributes, (3) Process of improvement, (4) Using S fT for professional growth, (5) Professional learni ng design, and (6) Broader Technology use. These categories were used to investigate the two research questions (see Table 2).

## Findings

The analysis of the data indicates that there are c ommon patterns repeated across the 13 case studies and the different use cases, which can help us respond to the two research questions addressed in this paper. What follows is an overview of the main findings per research question. Teachers’ agency (RQ1) The analysis of the data indicated that SfT contributed to the development of teachers’ awareness, supporting their understanding of  educators’ digital competence as a complex construct comprised  of multiple dimensions and proficiency levels. E.g., “Well, it was quite surprising for me… The fact tha t there were levels, about which I had no idea previously… This made it more visible…  Well, it helped me understand where I need to improve or to learn something more. It was really g ood that the tool had  these areas, which clustered the questions and answers…”  (In-service language arts teacher, Spain) This realization was also informed by teachers’ reflection regarding three temporal perspectives: (a) prior relations with technology  (past), (b) current digital competence (present), and (c) intentions to act in order to develop their digital profile (future). Teachers reported that while they were responding to the SfT, they were continuously reflecting on their digital practices. E.g., “It helps you understand what level you are at, wher e you are lagging behind, what different methodologies you can follow, what areas need improvement, what you are good at.  I think of it as a personal test about where you are educationally at school.”  (In-service, secondary school STEM teacher, Greece) The SfT self-reflection also helped teachers assess their present digital competence, which, in turn, contributed to their commitment towards their professional growth. E.g., “[SfT provided me] a baseline of my current competen ce level in digital technologies, which will allow me to plan the path for developing my di gital competence and learning how to use these tools in instruction.” (In-service elementary school teacher, Cyprus) Moreover, most teachers expressed their willingness to undertake various actions to develop their digital competence, even though this was sometimes expressed as an intent instead of as a concrete professional learning design plan. E.g., “From a practical point of view, I would like to go back and check my resources, to fi nd out how I could make (my lessons) more interactive. My driving question will be how can my students learn better? What can make them become mo re interested in and eventually learn better? So, yes… I wil l make my lessons more interactive and much more in teresting for my students.” (In-service elementary school teacher, Cyprus) Responding to the first research question “Can a self-reflection tool (SfT) support an agentic approach to teachers’ development of their digital competence? If yes, how and to what extent?” the study results show that SfT provides a context that  can support teachers go through a self-reflection process to self-assess their digital competence, while at the same time considering their professional growth and digital practice. Teachers expressed their intent to take action upon their self-reflection results to develop their digital competence; however, teachers also expressed the need for additional support to conceptualize and pursue concrete professional learning plans. Key factors for the effective use of self-reflection using SfT (RQ2) The analysis of the data also shed light on multiple personal, institutional, and systemic factors, as well as to the specific SfT experience, serving as barriers or enablers of teachers’ agency to develop their digital competence. From a personal perspective, in most cases, participants were highly motivated to take part in this selfreflection exercise with the aim to improve their digital competence. Nonetheless, they could not specify strategies on how to do it, if used on their own  outside a supportive context. Teachers also referred to issues such as their workload in conjunction with their available time to follow-up on the feedback provided by the SfT. E.g., “Because, if I am on my own in my daily routine, I will simply forget it quickly, and soon, I’ll return back to what I used to do with technology…” (Elementary school teacher, Cyprus) In relation to the SfT experience perspective, most of the teachers recognized a potent self-reflection tool in SfT that can help them benchmark their digital competence and acquire self-awareness. E.g., “Well, it was very interesting to reflect as a teacher about what you are actually doing or not, as well as about how many things you could do… […] Wel l, I was thinking about what I cando better, how I can integrate (digital technologies) more effectively in my courses.”  (In-service elementary school teacher, Cyprus) However, even though they found the automated feedback report very useful, many of them expressed the need for receiving more specific guidance and suggestions on how to move to the next level, asking for related resources, activities, and digital tools.

“Personally, I would like to have something more pr actical. For example, here (my feedback), it says: ‘collaborate with your students to use technology-supported activities’. I would also like to have an example. What type of activities could we adopt to achieve this goal? Or there could be a website, some proposals… […] I would like to h ave something more concrete and more practical regarding how to use it in my lessons!” (In-service elementary school teacher, Cyprus) Systemic and institutional factors were also pointed out as important contributors (or deterrents), such as the support from school authorities in realizing actions in relation to the feedback provided by the  SfT. Teachers also referred to the available school infrastructure, access to technology and ICT-related pedagogical resources as the main contextual aspects affecting their digital agency. E.g. "In our staff meetings, we have had a monthly pedag ogical time at the beginning, where sometimes there have been these presentations, for example, our development workshops have been introduced there. It has been a place where ev en occasionally there has b een a specific program introduced, like 'Hey, this works like this, give it a try’.”  (School leader, elementary school, Finland) “We need, probably, to think more about getting value for investment in  digital infrastructure, such as reliable broadband and accessible devices. And we need to be better at removing those practical barriers that might hinder teachers' abil ity to actually put into practice the feedback.” (School leader, elementary school, Ireland) Teachers discussed interdependencies between personal, insti tutional, and systemic factors. They saw value in receiving mentoring for designing learning plans and in the creation of communities of practice within their school in relation to the SfT self-reflection. Some felt they needed scaffolding to help them identify their learning needs based on their results and plan their professional learning paths. Even though they felt that the tool provided them a rich experience to understand the spectrum of what their digital competence entails, many of them expected the tool to be embedded in professional development programs in which they would be guided in a structured way on how to design and implement their professional learning paths. Another key factor that came up was their need to work collaboratively, to identify common needs and plan common learning activities. Even though SfT proposes a professional learning process to follow, a more structured approach would provide additional support. E.g., “Schools can encourage reflection on teachers' digital competence as part of their organisational culture. This can be achieved through training, the  creation of spaces for exchange and recognition of individual efforts to improve digita l competence.” (In-service vocational school teacher, Spain) Thus, responding to the question “What are the main conditions, enablers and barriers, for the effective use of self-reflection using SfT to support teachers’ developme nt of their digital competence? " it seems that the affordances of SfT are intertwined with teachers’ p ersonal characteristics as well as with the socio-technical environment in which the tool is situated in. A strong need was expressed to have a structured proc ess where the SfT is offered (such as a professional development program), as well as create communities of practice allowing teachers to share and discuss their reflections, learning designs and results.

## Discussion

The results of this multiple case study indicate th at using SfT as a self-reflection tool, which provide s feedback on teachers’ digital competence, has the potential to contribute to their agency to change, through creating self-awareness and promoting self-assessment of one’s digital competence. Nonetheless, our findings also point to the need to situate such self-reflection efforts in a broader context, in which t eachers can easily identify and solicit support to help them develop and realize specific professional learning design plans. These findings also confirm reports in the literature regarding the mult iple factors (i.e., personal, institutional, systemic) and their  complex interactions which may affect teachers’ digital competence (e.g., Lucas et  al., 2021). On the other hand, our findings are aligned with discussions about the multi-dimensionality of teacher agency (Pyhältö, et al., 2015) and the need to support teachers in envisioning paths for self-development, identifying strategies and seeking additional support to achieve such goals (Frazier, Schwartz & Metcalfe, 2021). Overall, t he results presented in this paper  have implications that relate to the design of teacher professional development activities:

• Building teachers’ digital competence is a complex process and it involves the development of teacher

agency and self-efficacy.

• To support the development of teachers’ digital com petence, a teacher-at-the-center perspective (as

a learner) and a transformative learning approach (Mezirow, 1997) is required; in this endeavor, selfreflection is of central importance in becoming sel f-aware and in planning learning designs for self-improvement; however, even though self-reflection is required, it is not sufficient.

• The development of teachers’ digital competence is contextually, relationally and personally bound;

one needs to understand these aspects well to be able to situate reflective tools like SfT in effective conditions that can support teachers’ professional learning and development.

In conclusion, the professionalization of teaching increasingly requires that teachers act  as agents of change. The findings from this multi-case study can contribute to the literature, as they off er a theoretically-informed yet pragmatically-bound understanding of the conditions in which teachers’ agency to self-improve can flourish through self-reflection.
