# Museum Educators' Knowledge Brokering With Boundary Objects

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Museum educators often use objects as part of education programs for visiting school groups. We examined how museum educators leveraged boundary objects and forms of talk to as part of knowledge brokering between the bounded communities of palaeontology and school groups. Findings show how educators broker with talk and objects, and how brokering can fail.

## Introduction and background

Commonly, natural history museums are institutions built around collections of objects such as authentic artifacts, replicas, and exhibits (Paris, 2002). Museums and t heir objects can also facilitate boundary crossing between social worlds, such as between scientific disciplines and the general public. At such boundaries, obje cts can act as boundary objects, which are useful to the inhabi tants of multiple social worlds, but in different w ays (Star, 2010). To facilitate boundary crossing museum educa tors often act as knowledge brokers that translate and communicate specialized knowledge between bounded communities of disciplinary experts and the public (Meyer, 2010). Our interest lies in how museum educators le verage boundary objects and different forms of talk  in knowledge brokering processes within education programs for school groups visiting a palaeontology museum. Studies have examined how museum visitors such as families (Zimmerman et al., 2015) or school groups (Achiam et al., 2016) interact with museum objects,  but rarely from the perspective of museum educator s who often facilitate such interactions. Boundary objects are “plastic enough to adapt to local needs and the constraints of the several parties employing them, yet robust e nough to maintain a common identity across sites” (Star & Griesemer, 1989, p. 393). For instance, a fossil ca n serve paleontologists as an object of study, the public as a general interest, or school groups visiting a museum to learn about the Earth's history. Museum educators often reside at these boundaries and use boundary objects  to broker across worlds. Knowledge brokering alway s involves making decisions about what to include and what to leave out and what to expand or contract, mediating the experiences of participants’ contact with borde ring worlds (Crowley & Jacobs, 2002). We explored t he following question: How do museum educators facilit ate knowledge brokering using different forms of ta lk in conjunction with boundary objects?

## Context and methods of data collection and analysis

We observed and video-recorded three museum educato rs lead four educational programs for three groups of middle school students visiting a paleontology muse um. Each 60-minute program took place within dedica ted learning spaces (separate from the public galleries) or outdoors. Each program had a different format including a readers-theatre, a trivia-style game, fossil casting, and an outdoor walking tour. All the educators had a universitylevel science background and were trained by the museum to facilitate the programs.

Using literature and our data we developed three categories of objects and three forms of talk. The object categories include Authentic, Disciplinary, and Educational objects (Hampp & Schwan, 2015). Authentic objects were original objects such as fossils, rock samples, and geological features (e.g., rivers), but exclu ded replicas (e.g., fossil casts) or images. Disciplinary object s were those used in disciplinary work and they cou ld be specialized (e.g., dental plaster) or common (e.g.,  toothbrush for fossil excavation). Educational obj ects were typically human made for the purpose of explaining or showing information, such as images, diagrams, a nd replicas or simplified/abstracted versions of other objects. We categorized objects based on how they were used in the observed contexts, even if they were relevant to multiple categories. The educator talk categories include perceptual, co nceptual, and connecting talk (Allen, 2002). Perceptual talk relied on sensory perceptions to in teract with, observe, or describe objects, such as when an educator showed and described a fossil's features. Conceptual talk involved the educator using informa tion or observations of an object to encourage students to make inferences and develop causal explanations about objects and concepts. This was often posed as questions in forms such as, "Knowing X about a dinosaur, what do  you think will happen when in Y scenario?". Connecting talk invoked prior knowledge and experiences beyond  a specific interaction, such as what students might know from school, media, or other parts of the museum.

## Findings

The recordings were transcribed and using the categories of talk and objects, we coded a total of 201 utterances and 83 unique objects across all four programs (Ostrowdun & Kim, 2022). We observed the educators use multiple ICLS2022 Proceedings 1838 © ISLS combinations of object types and forms of talk with varied success in brokering. For instance, in the walking tour, the educator successfully used all three forms of talk in relation to a petrified tree stump (authentic object). They invited students to observe the tree stump (percept ual talk); described correlations between the objec t and scientific knowledge of climate, vegetation, and geological history (conceptual talk); and asked students to recall the meaning of rings on the stump (connecting talk).

In another episode, an educator used posters (educa tional objects) of a Tyrannosaurus Rex skeleton to explain how social worlds are dynamic and evolve, b ut not always in congruent ways. One poster showed the skeleton in a vertical stance, while the other showed a horizontal stance. The educator explained how advances in disciplinary understanding found that the horizontal stance was no longer considered accurate, but portrayals of Tyrannosaurus Rex in popular media were inconsisten t and often still used the horizonal stance. This i nstance showed how new discoveries and evolving knowledge requires educators to broker and re-broker knowledge and understanding of objects.

The usefulness of an object in brokering has limits and brokering can "fail" when the limits are exceeded. For instance, the educators often referenced a geol ogical timescale (educational object) to relate chr onological time periods (e.g., Cretaceous, Devonian) with rock layers and fossils. In one program, plastic rings were stacked chronologically with each ring showing dinosaur gly phs, and students answered related trivia questions (connecting talk). The stacked rings coarsely showed when dinosaurs came into existence relative to ea ch other and time but provided no other details (e.g., climate, environment). So, when the educator referenced the timescale and asked students whether mammoths and Tyrannosaurus Rex coexisted, the object failed as a brokering device because its scope of usefulness was exceeded. Stude nts could not answer the question because they coul d not glean the necessary information from the object and  did not have sufficient background knowledge (e.g., mammoths lived during the ice age, which was too cold for Tyrannosaurus Rex).

## Conclusion

Using boundary objects and forms of talk in concert can be a powerful way for museum educators to broker across worlds. However, we observed brokering was most suc cessful, and could bridge students more deeply to disciplinary knowledge, when educators used all three forms of talk in conjunction with boundary objects. Heavy reliance on any one form of talk limits brokering opportunities. For example, connecting talk can promote factual recall and perceptual talk can address observed obj ect features, but on their own each have limited co nceptual depth. As well, successful brokering relied on the educators working within an object's scope of usefulness and dynamically adjusting their talk. Brokering is especially challenging in museum programs where educators often know little about students’ prior knowledge and have limited contact time (Tran, 2008).
