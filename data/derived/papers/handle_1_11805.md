# Emotional Dynamics in Asynchronous Math Discussions: Analyzing the Impact of Negative Emotions on Learning Outcomes

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study investigates the role of negative emotions in asynchronous online math discussions and their impact on learning. Using data from 402 students and 3,084 discussion posts, the study employs sentiment analysis and machine learning to categorize emotions. Findings reveal that low levels of negative emotions can enhance learning by fostering active engagement, while higher levels correlate with lower performance. Topic modeling and sequential pattern mining show that students with low negative emotions engage more productively, focusing on both 'Help-seeking behaviors' and 'Understanding.' In contrast, high negative emotions lead to shorter interactions and focusing primarily on 'Help-seeking behaviors.' Sequential analysis indicates that low negative emotions often transition toneutral or positive states, supporting continued engagement, while high negative emotions result in repetitive negative sequences and disengagement. These findings underscore the importance of understanding negative emotions and providing appropriate support in asynchronous learning environments to foster engagement and improve outcomes.

## Introduction

Asynchronous Online Discussion (AOD) enriches interactions between learners and instructors, aiding knowledge construction on specific topics (Lee & Recker, 2022). It offers flexibility by overcoming time and loc ation constraints (Meyer, 2003), enhances academic performance (Darabi et al., 2013), and fosters social presence and engagement (Xie & Ke, 2011). AOD also provides valuable insights into learners’ behaviors and patterns through forum post analysis (Moreno-Marcos et al., 2018). One method for understanding these interactions is sentiment analysis, which examines learners' emotions by iden tifying specific negative or positive emotions expr essed in their posts (Ahmad et al., 2022). Given the signifi cant impact of emotional states o n the learning process, identifying these emotions is crucial (Zhou & Ye, 2 023). However, limited attention has been given to multilayered analyses of how negative emotions influence students' learning in AOD settings. Negative emotions in learning environments often stem from perceptions of failure, anticipated setbacks, or critical feedback, manifesting as feelings such as sadness, anger, or anxiety (Pekrun, 2006). These  emotions can undermine students' motivation and engagement w ith learning strategies, ultimately hindering their  learning outcomes. Notably, AOD settings may elicit more frequent nega tive emotions compared to synchronous discussions (Peterson et al., 2018). Such emotions are often regarded as factors requiring immediate management or intervention to sustain effective learning (Li & Yu, 2020), as research consistently associates negative emotions with lower academic achievement.  For instance, studies show that students experienci ng negative emotions like boredom or anxiety often demonstrate lower learning outcomes (Tzafilkou et al., 2021). Despite these challenges, negative emotions do not always have detrimental effects on learning outcomes (Kim et al., 2021). For example, Cheng et al. (2020) found that in virtual, game-based learning environments, negative emotions can actually enhance long-term retention more effectively than positive emoti ons. Similarly, students who felt frustration and confusion while l earning algebra online achieved better learning out comes compared to those who did not feel these emotions (Liu et al., 2013). This aligns with neuroscientific  findings, which suggest that negative emotions can improve th e processing and retention of related  information (Mickley & Kensinger, 2008). Given research showing that negative emotions can both aid and hinder learning in different contexts, this study aims to investigate how negative emoti ons are expressed and how they impact learning outcomes, particularly by examining the nuanced and evolving nature of these emotions within AOD settings. To effectively capture and analyze emotional expres sions and how they affect students' learning within AOD, it is crucial to employ methods that involve m ultiple layers of analysis (Chen et al., 2021). How ever, analyzing emotions through traditional manual coding or qualitative methods requires an intensive amount of time and is impractical given the extensive volume of di alogue in AOD. As a result, educational researchers  are increasingly turning to machine learning techniques  to automate emotion detection and provide timely a nalyses for educators and practitioners. For  example, Baker et al. (2012) proposed a model that detects emotions by analyzing learner interactions using log data, and Wen et al. (2014) developed an approach for emotion detection in forum posts by designing automatic emotion detec tors that use sentiment lexicons to classify emotio ns. These methods enable more scalable and systematic monitoring of emotional trends in learning environments. Combining emotion detection with computational tech niques like topic modeling and pattern mining provides deeper insights into the context and dynamics of emotional expressions in AOD. Topic modeling, often using the Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA) algorithm, identifies and tracks underlying topics, revealing learners’ interests, confusion points, and interaction patter ns (Zankadi et al., 2023). For example, Ramesh et al. (2014) analyzed MOOC discussion topics to uncover structur al relationships and suggest strategies to improve learning outcomes. Similarly, Xing et al. (2024) used topic modeling to explore engagement differences between core and peripheral students, highlighting specific needs. B uilding on these studies, this research categorizes  discussions by themes to examine how negative emotions relate t o specific topics and whether certain subjects elicit more negative responses, offering targeted insights for improving AOD environments. Sequential pattern mining is effective for analyzin g behavioral trends and participation patterns rela ted to emotional expressions (Huang et al., 2024). Sequential pattern mining can identify the order in which emotions are expressed and how these sequences vary between different groups of learners. For instance, Huang e t al.

(2024) utilized sequential pattern mining to identify sequences of emotional transitions among distinct interaction

groups within MOOC forums, providing insights into group-specific emotional dynamics that can guide tailored educational interventions. These computational techniques are not only efficient but also provide a dy namic lens for understanding how emotions, particularly negati ve ones, evolve over time  and how they are connected to learners’ behaviors and learning outcomes in AOD environments. While computational techniques offer new ways to an alyze emotions in AOD settings, there remains a gap in understanding how negative emotions manifest and impact learning. The dynamic nature of these emotions and their evolving influence on engagement and outcomes require further study. This paper addresses this gap by applying topic modeling and pattern mining to explore how negative emotions in AOD relate to students’ learning outcomes, discussion topics, and communication patt erns. In this context, negative emotions refer to emotions identified by an affective computing tool in discus sion forum posts within an AOD designed for a high schoollevel math course. This study addresses four research questions:  (RQ1). How donegative emotions manifest in asynchronous online math discussions? (RQ2). How donegative emotions influence students' learning outcomes in asynchronous online math discussions?  (RQ3). How do topics vary in asynchronous online math di scussions based on the level of negative emotion expressed?  (RQ4). How do patterns of emotional expression vary in asynchronous online math discussions based on the level of negative emotion expressed?

## Methods

Data description and cleaning We analyzed data from Math Nation, a widely used asynchronous math online learning platform supporting over a million K-12 students and educators across the United States.  A key feature, the “Student Wall,” serves as a collaborative Q&A board where students post math-related questions from schoolwork, homework, or qui zzes. Each question initiates a thread where tutors and p eers offer scaffolded responses, guiding students t oward the correct answers and fostering a supportive learning  community. The dataset included end-of-course performance scores and 6,744 discussion posts related to the Algebra I course. To prepare the dataset for analysis, we first removed empty posts, ensuring that only meaningful content was retained. Next, we excluded four students whose post counts were extreme outliers—either excessively high or low—based on interquartile range. This  helped to confirm that the remaining students' post  counts fell within a reasonable range, mitigating the risk of system errors that might have duplicated posts. As a result, we obtained a refined dataset containing 3,084 posts. First, this study employed a large language mo del (LLM) based on the DistilRoBERTa arc hitecture (Hartmann, 2022), fine-tuned specifically for emotion classification tasks. The pre-trained model categorizes emotions into seven classes: joy, surprise, neutrality, sadness, fear, anger, and disgust. To enhance its accuracy and reliabilit y, the model was trained on six distinct datasets  that contain emotional labels from Twitter, Reddit, and self-reported student texts. The emotional labels for th e social media datasets are annotated based on Ekman’s six univers al emotions, plus a neutral category, as defined wi thin the framework of the psychological affective semantic space (Demszky et al., 2020). In addition, an opinion-based model was utilized for emotion detection, pro viding a simpler and interpretable approach. This model calculates senti ment scores using the formula p/(p+n), where p represents positive opinion words and n represents negative ones. The opinion-based model categorizes emotions into three classes: negative, neutral, and positive. Posts wit h a score greater than 0.5 are classified as positi ve, those below 0.5 as negative, and a score of 0.5 is assigned to posts without opinion words (Hu & Liu, 2004). Known  for its lower susceptibility to bias (Huang et al., 2019), this model serves as both a reliable method for emotion detection and a reference point to validate the LLM’s output. Both the LLM-based and opinion-based models were initially used to cross-validate classifications, minimizing biases from relying on a single model. We calculated each student's negative emotion post rate by dividing posts with negative emotions by their total posts. Negative emotions in digital learning environments are deeply influenced by the context of the environment and the tasks involved (Wortha et al., 2019). Therefore, based on the distribution of our dataset, students were categorized into four distinct emotio nal groups: Nonegative (negative ratio = 0), Low Negative (negative ratio < 0.2), Mid Negative (0.2 ≤ negative ratio < 0.4), and High Negative (negative ratio ≥ 0.4). Topic modeling To address RQ3, we applied topic modeling to analyz e students' negative posts. The initial step involv ed text preprocessing to obtain clean and effective posts, followed by employing topic modeling techniques to derive the corresponding negative post topics. For text preprocessing, we removed symbols such as parentheses, semicolons, and dashes. We then tokenized the text and eliminat ed stopwords and other irrelevant words (Rivera-Bergollo et al., 2022). Subsequently, we performed lemmatizatio n to normalize word forms using part-of-speech tagging, ensuring consistent formatting for words with the same content. Figure 1 Data Analysis Process.

After preprocessing the text, we used LDA for topic modeling, chosen for its ability to autom atically identify latent topics within large collections of text data. LDA is advantageous because it does not require prior annotations or predefined categories, making it sui table for exploratory analysis of unstructured data. The topic modeling was conducted at the post level, allowing each post to be associated with a specific topic. The optimal number of topics (9) was determined using a topic c oherence metric (shown in Table 2). This metric calculates the degree of semantic similarity between the high-scoring words in a topic by assessing how frequentl y these words co-occur in the corpus; higher coherence scores indicate topics that are more interpretable and meaningful to humans. Finally, two researchers manually coded the derived topics to assign them real-world meanings. Sequential analysis To investigate differences in emotional expression patterns across groups with varying levels of negative emotions, we conducted a sequential analysis of students' pos ts in asynchronous online mathematics discussions, focusing on emotional pattern mining. Based on the proportio n of posts expressing negative emotions, each stude nt was classified into one of four emotional groups: None gative, Low Negative, Mid Negative, and High Negati ve. Frequent subsequence mining, a data mining techniqu e, was used to discover frequently occurring patter ns in sequential data. Using PrefixSpan (Kang et al., 202 0), we identified the highest-weighted frequent subsequences for each of the four groups, where weight is define d as the ratio of observed frequency to statistical ly expected frequency, and ranked them in descending order. Thi s indicates that the identified sequences represent  the most frequent and consistent emotional patterns within e ach group. For each group, we identified and examin ed common patterns of emotional expression from the em otional sequences of students' temporally ordered p osts, focusing on the seven most frequent patterns to assess typical interaction sequences. This analysis captured shifts in emotional tone across posts, revealing changes in emotional expression. Additionally, we analyzed the average length of these patterns for each group to explore potential differences in the breadth of discussion threads.

The results from both models matched 70.30% of the time, indicating significantly higher accuracy than random detection of each emotion. The LLM-based model classified emotions into seven distinct  categories, with sadness, fear, anger, and disgust representing the primary negative emotions examined  in this study. The proportions of these negative e motions were 4.90%, 2.69%, 1.04%, and 0% of the total posts, respectively, re sulting in 266 posts (8.63%) classified as negative. In addition, 117 students (approximately 29.1%) posted at leas t one message containing negative emotions. In comparison, the simple opinion-based model categorized posts into three emotion la bels: positive, neutral, and negative. It detected 361 negative pos ts (11.71% of the total posts),  with approximately 38.3% of students posting at least one message containing negative emotions. The simple opinion-based model demonstrated a tendency to overclassify posts as negative. To ensure a more precise evaluation of whether a post genuinely exhibits negative emotions, we opted to use the LLM-based model. This decision also aligned with our goal of identifying specific types of negative emotions and  exploring their individual impacts on learning. Consequently,  the LLM-based model's emotion detection results were selected for further analysis.

## Discussion

Students’ emotional experiences when learning with technologies are diverse and have been investigated  using several frameworks (D’Mello, 2013). Most previous studies have revealed that the way emotions impact learning and achievement is closely related to  their valence. More specifically, positive emotions  are positively, and negative emotions are negatively related to the learning process and learning outcomes (e.g., Pekrun et al., 2002; Pekrun & Linnenbrink-Garcia, 2012). In this study, we explored how n egative emotions are manifested in asynchronous online math discussions and their rela tionship with students' learning outcomes, discussion topics, and emotional expression patterns. In this context,  approximately 10% of the total posts contained neg ative emotions, and about 30% of the students made at least one post expressing negative emotions. According to many previous studies, such expressions might be associa ted with negative learning outcomes, which highligh ts the importance of understanding how negative emotions manifest. It is important to note that in the context of this  study, the reason for the relatively high frequenc y of negative posts among some students might not necessarily be due to strong negative emotions about the learning content itself. Rather, it could be due to the nature of asynchronous discussion posts, where students ask questions and explore topics they do not fully understand. Co nsequently, emotions like fear or sadness might nat urally emerge when students are faced with concepts they struggle to grasp. As shown in the analysis (see Table 4), most of the negative emotions were categorized as "Sadne ss" or "Fear," while intense negative emotions like  "Anger" were relatively infrequent, and extreme negative em otions like "Disgust" did not appear at all. Theref ore, in this study, we assume that students who made posts with a low proportion of negative emotions are likely experiencing some negative feelings when faced with challenging concepts, but overall, they are attempting to under stand the content through predominantly neutral or positive posts. Conversely, students with a relatively high proportion of negative emotions and having relatively strong nega tive emotions, such as anger, might consistently st ruggle to understand mathematical concepts, remain unable to reach a neutral or positive emotion al state, and eventually stop participating in the discussion forum.

When comparing the learning outcomes, there was no significant difference in learning outcomes when simply comparing whether students posted with negat ive emotions. However, distinguishing between stude nts based on the proportion of negative emotions did re veal significant differences. Students who posted n egative emotions at a low rate (Low Negative Emotion Group)  performed better than all other groups, including those whonever posted negative emotions. This suggests t hat expressing a small amount of nega tive emotion might indicate active engagement in seeking understanding, where momentary negative expressions occur as part of the learning process. Ultimately, these students appear ed to learn mathematical concepts more actively thr ough asynchronous discussions. For example, students in the Low Negative Emotion Group had an average of 29.12 posts, significantly higher than other groups. Therefore, these small amounts of negative emotions might activate the discussion and facilitate students’ understanding of the learning content. On the other hand, as the rate of negative emotions  increased, there was a marked decline in learning outcomes. The Middle Negative Emotion Group showed no significant difference in learning outcomes compared to the Nonegative Emotion Group. However, the High  Negative Emotion Group had the lowest learning outcomes among all groups. Notably, this group exhi bited a higher proportion of fear and anger, which are more intense negative emotions that candisrupt learning. Given that their average number of posts  was only 1.96, it can be inferred that these students likely expressed negative emotions without resolution and either did not return to participate further in the asynchronous discussi ons or failed to achieve sufficient understanding o f the mathematical concepts. Therefore, if students post with negative  emotions, especially those expressing fear or anger, and do not make further posts or continue to  express similar emotions, instructors should inter vene and provide additional support. This also suggests that it is not simply the presence of negative emotions but also the specific type of negative emotion that plays a crucial role in learning outcomes. This pattern is also evident when analyzing the top ics that students posted about (see Table 6). Helpseeking behaviors appeared nearly twice as frequently in the High Negative Emotion Group compared to the other groups. This aligns with the above interpretation, suggesting that although students in this group frequently asked questions or sought help when they did  not understand something, they made minimal subsequ ent efforts to comprehend or confirm learning content through disc ussions involving "Understanding and confirmation" or "Math concept discussions." Moreover, this group showed very low percentages of "Positi ve social interactions" (1.82%) and "Clarification and platform features" (also 1.82%). In other words, students in this group had fewer positive social interactions with peers or mentors in the asynchronous discussion and engaged less in discussions about platform features (e.g., how to write equatio ns) in the digital learning environment. On the oth er hand, in the Low Negative Emotion Group, "Help-seeking behaviors" accounted for 22.74%, which was about half that of the High Negative Emotion Group, but they exhibited  higher rates of topics directly related to understanding the content, such as "Understanding and confirmation," "Math concept discussions," and "Clarification and platform features." This indicates that more productive asynchronous di scussion patterns can be observed when students experience relatively a small amount of negative emotions. Sequential pattern mining provided further insights  into the differences in emotional expression acros s the four groups. For students in the Nonegative and Low Negative Emotion Groups, the most frequent pa tterns consisted mainly of neutral posts, with occ asional transitions to positive emotions like "joy. " The Low Negative group exhibited the broadest range of pattern lengt hs (2-7 posts), suggesting that these students participat ed in longer, more sustained discussions, which likely co ntributed to their higher learning outcomes. In the  Mid and High Negative Emotion Groups, shorter sequences wer e observed, with most emotional transitions involvi ng negative emotions such as "sadness" or "fear." Nota bly, the High Negative Group's sequences often ende d with negative emotions, indicating that these students did not experience emotional resolution through the discussions, which may have negatively impacted their motivation  to continue participating. The sequential analysis  also revealed that in the Low Negative Group, negative e motions like "sadness" were often follo wed by neutral emotions, suggesting a resolution process that may have helped these students re-engage with the learning content. This finding emphasizes the importance of guiding s tudents through emotional transitions in learning environments, particularly asynchronous settings, where timely feedback is not always available. The findings underscore the importance of understanding students' emotional expressions in AOD. While negative emotions can indicate engagement and motiv ation, they must be effectively managed to prevent disengagement. For students with a high ratio of negative posts, timely instructor intervention m ay help them transition from negative emotions to more productiv e emotional states. It is important to clarify that  we do not argue for encouraging negative emotions in the online discussion learning environment. Instead, we acknowledge that the occurrence of negative emotions is a natur al phenomenon, and we aim to provide insights into how instructors can respond when such emotions arise. B y understanding these emotional dynamics, educators  can better sup port students through targeted interventions that t ransform negative emotions into learning opportunities, ultimately fostering a more inclusive and effective learning environment.
