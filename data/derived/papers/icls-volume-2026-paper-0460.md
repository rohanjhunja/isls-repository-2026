# Computational Qualitative Analysis of Elementary Students’ Understanding of Data and Data Science

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study used a computational qualitative approach to examine how a data unit designed around elementary students’ lived, emotional, and embodied experiences impacted their understanding of data. Pre/post-test responses were analyzed using semantic similarity and qualitative open coding to connect measurable patterns with interpretive nuances. Results showed that students expanded their understanding of data and recognized its role in daily life. The qualitative interoperation also identified specific design features for further video analysis.

Data is increasingly manifested in daily life, which highlighted the need for individuals to develop skills in understanding and working with data to address real-world problems (Jiang et al., 2022). Currently, these skills are primarily addressed in mathematics classes, often using datasets devoid of students’ lives (Adisa et al., 2023). This disconnect can prevent students from seeing data as relevant (Matuk et al, 2022). Although more studies have incorporated personally relevant data (Zhou et al., 2024; Lee et al., 2021), still less is known about the impact of such connections on students’ understanding of data  and data science in general. To address this, this study designs a five-day unit built around students’ lived, emotional, and embodied experiences to develop their understanding of data, and examines how participating in this unit shifts students’ understanding of data and data science. This study asks: How did students’ understanding of data and data science shift  after participating in a data unit designed around lived, emotional, and embodied experiences?

## Theoretical perspectives

This study is grounded in three strands of literature: embodied cognition (Nathan, 2021), emotion as a resource for exploring data from data feminism (D'Ignazio & Klein, 2020),  and lived experience from the humanistic approach to data science (Lee et al., 2021). Embodied cognition holds that human abstract thinking is deeply intertwined with our physical bodies and sensorimotor interactions with the physical world (Kersting et al., 2021). Thus, representing data through body shapes and movements  and manipulating physical data objects utilize the body as a semiotic tool to support students in understanding data  (Nathan, 2021). Data feminism challenges the traditional view of data as objective and detached to emphasize emotion as central to data sensemaking (D'Ignazio & Klein, 2020).  When students physically embody data points/phenomena, they develop strong emotional connections that make abstract concepts more relatable (Desportes et al., 2022). The humanistic approach to data science values students’ direct experiences in data-related activities and leverages lived knowledge in shaping how they work with and understand data (Lee et al., 2021). Together, this study forms an integrated, multifaceted view of embodied, emotional, and lived experiences in students’ engagement with  data to support a broaden understanding of data in everyday life (Fagerlund et al., 2024) and foundational data skills (Erickson et al., 2019). Design Within large design-based research on the humanistic approach to elementary data science, this study involved eighteen fourth graders from a full-inclusion classroom in a Midwestern charter school. Students participated in five 45-minute sessions designed to connect data to their lived, emotional, and embodied experiences. The unit topic was checked with the teacher to ensure it met students’ interests/excitement. Day 1 introduced students to data science by involving them in searching for data in their classroom, comparing reading data to reading novels, and drawing creative graphs using self-collected conversation data. These activities centered students’ lived experiences by connecting to familiar data sources. On Day 2 and 3, students explored a dataset from a local animal shelter. They embodied data points by acting as animals in an adoption event and worked as data scientists to sort data cards by different criteria to make decisions about medical care priorities. These activities were designed to foster emotional connections with the dataset through embodiment and drew  on students’ prior experience with animals. Day 4 was planned to introduce the same data in the Common Online Data Analysis Platform (CODAP; The Concord Consortium, n.d.), but an unexpected technology restriction at the school prevented students from accessing the dataset. Instead, they explored a publicly available dataset in CODAP to learn the platform. On Day 5, students collected evidence from CODAP graphs about the animal shelter dataset to debate whether animal age affects adoption readiness. 2809

## Data and data analysis

This analysis examines students’ responses to an open-ended question assessing shifts in  their understanding of data and data science on the pre-and post-tests administered on Day 1 and Day 5. Sixteen of the eighteen consented students completed both the pre-and post-tests. A computational grounded theory approach (Nelson,

2020) was used to leverage computational tools to support qualitative analysis (Krist et al., 2025). First, semantic

similarity was utilized to measure how closely each student’s response was to a target explanation.  This target explanation was developed from a student who only completed the post-test; thus, their response was excluded from the analysis. T his response was selected because it reflected the study’s learning objectives, “ Data science is studying, collecting, averaging, and graphing data. People use data in their life because technically everything you do involves collecting, processing, or reacting to data.” One additional sentence, “Data comes in different forms, such as words and numbers” was added to emphasize that data includes more than numbers. Students’ preand post-test responses were transformed into embeddings using SBERT sentence transformers (all-MiniLM-L6v2; Reimers, 2019) and compared to the target explanation by calculating cosine similarity score, which represents how semantically close each response is to the target explanation.  A Shapiro-Wilk test showed that scores were not normally distributed (W=0.88, p <.05); thus, a Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used to analyze the learning gains. Because SBERT sentence transformers may not fully capture nuances in young children’s language, this computational analysis was complemented with iterative qualitative coding  (Saldaña, 2021). The qualitative point for identifying emerging ideas by grounding the coding in instances likely  to have richer/comprehensive ideas. Then, the codes were applied across all responses and counted to quantify the brea dth of ideas expressed by each student using a paired t-test. Finally, students’ responses were reviewed qualitatively to capture how they talked about these ideas.

## Result

The Wilcoxon signed-rank test on semantic similarity scores showed that students' responses on the post-test became more closely aligned with the target response (W = 4.0, p =.0015). Consistently, the paired t-test on the number of data ideas in students’ responses increased from the pre-test (M=1.25, SD=1.29)  to the post-test (M=2.62, SD=1.31), t(15) = 4.57, p <.001. These results suggested that, after the implementation, students’ understanding became more closely aligned with the learning objectives and demonstrated a broader understanding of data-related ideas. Qualitative analysis and review of the coding showed that, on the post-test, students expanded their understanding beyond seeing data only as numbers. Many recognized that data can also include words and information, which is likely influenced by the activity of comparing reading data to reading books, an activity students enjoyed daily, and identifying data in the classroom. These activities leveraged students’ lived experiences of reading books, interacting with classroom materials to make the data more relatable and familiar. Additionally, on the pre-test, students’ perception of data function was mostly limited to school-related and the pre-test examples, such as tests, science, and field trips. On the post-test, they noted that data exists in their daily lives and informs actions. For example, one student wrote, “People need to use data for things like getting food from the store.” This shift was likely related to using data from students’ peer-conversation and a local animal shelter, which prompted them to consider how data could reflect life and helped inform life decisions. Lastly, on the pre-test, only one student mentioned data graphs, but on the post-test, many mentioned data practices, including creating data representation s, analyzing data, and organizing data variables. For example, “data science has to do with data since numerical variables, categorical variables, numbers, and so much more” and “I think data science is a type of science where you find data about really anything and you make graphs and tables” These shifts were likely supported by the curriculum design in which students sorted data using variables, created and analyzed data graphs in CODAP.

Pre-and post-test results showed that participating in a data unit designed around students’ lived, emotional, and embodied experience expanded their understanding of data to move beyond viewing data only as number s in school context to recognizing its presence in everyday life  in different forms. These changes were evident quantitatively through increased semantic similarity scores and code counts, and qualitative ly through students’ more nuanced explanations.
