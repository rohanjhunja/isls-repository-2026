# Examining the Complex Webs of Supports That Sustain Classroom Practices

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Reform of educational systems is difficult, with changes often fading or diverting in unproductive ways. To disrupt this pattern and understand why unwanted practices persist and new ones distort, we need to consider how new and old practices are sustained at multiple levels. I discuss the framing and methodological findings from a larger study that investigated practices and supports within an undergraduate course for elementary education majors and discuss how these can inform educational change.

## Introduction

Reform efforts in education are often ineffective, either not creating lasting change (Payne, 2008) or losing sight of the key constructs behind them (Dancy et al., 20 16). A focus on “best practices” or “Evidence-Based Instructional Practices” reduces the problem of reform to a simple practice change without addressing the supports needed to sustain that change within the complex, contextualized, sociocultural structure that maintains classroom practices (Payne, 2008). In order to make lasting c hanges, we need to consider how the complex, multil eveled system sustains patterns of classroom practices. Fo r example, looking at teacher development framed wi th process-product logic cannot explain why change eff orts fail (Opfer & Pedder, 2011). Reform efforts mu st consider what supports sustain classroom practices.

The research question I address in this poster paper is: how can systematic examination of patterns and the supports that sustain them inform educational change efforts? Within a larger study (see Zeller, 2022; Zeller, Under Review), I utilized the MoRe Institutional Fr amework (Anderson & Colyvas, 2021) to look within t he classroom for patterns of practice and across multi ple system levels for supports that institutionaliz e these practices. The MoRe Framework offers flexibility for researchers to investigate supports that sustain patterns at a specific level without excluding the influence of the other levels. MoRe Institutional Framework Institutional theory offers a framework for considering how patterns of practice are supported and sustained within classrooms. The MoRe Institutional Framework starts with Jepperson’s (1991) definition of institutions as “social patterns” that are sustained through “self-activating social processes” that continually reproduce institutionalized patterns (pp. 144-145). The self-activating social processes manifest in various modes and at multiple  levels of social systems (Anderson & Colyvas, 2021). Focusing on these modes of reproduction (MoRs) offers a view into why some patterns of classroom practices are sustained while others fade within the complex system of schools. It is important to not only look at what reproduce s desired patterns, but to also consider patterns t hat have been institutionalized and may stand in the way of desired reforms (Anderson & Colyvas, 2021). Focusing beyond idealized practices illuminates how the MoRs  for multiple patterns of practice coexist and inte ract. Looking across continua of patterns that exist with in classroom practice allows researchers to capture  complex interplay between old and new practices and break down walls that stand in the way of effective reforms. MoRs take many forms. Anderson and Colyvas (2021) identify categories of MoRs endemic to education systems including policy, stakeholder interests, id entities, and roles. MoRs are distinct from the pat terns they reproduce and can act at many levels within a compl ex system (Anderson, 2017). When looking at an undergraduate course, MoRs may act at levels ranging from individual to global. The sustaining force of a MoR can act at a completely different level than the pattern of practice manifesting at the focal level of a study. The MoRe Institutional Framework provides a roadma p that helps navigate the intricacies of practices and the supports that sustain them. The investigation of both new and old practices creates a unique snapshot that captures change processes within a single timespan. The discovery of the MoRs that sustain patterns of practice creates the opportunity to leverage change efforts where they are most effective at creating lasting change.

## Methods

This work examines the methodological implications of a larger study that leveraged case study and act ion research to deeply investigate practices within a science content course for undergraduate elementary education majors. In this paper, I explore how methods based on the MoRe Institutional Framework serve such an analysis.

The study investigated a physics and earth sciences course for undergraduate students over the Fall 2020 semester. It is part of the requirements for the university’s elementary education major, which focuses on teaching ICLS2022 Proceedings 1842 © ISLS in urban environments. The university is a large, R1 institution in the Midwestern United States and a minorityserving institution. The course team consisted of two co-instructors and two teaching assistants (TAs). The author was the physics instructor, while the other instructor was an earth science professor with many years of experience with the course. The course had 72 students and was held synchronously online, with three lab sections led by the TAs. Data collected includes course materials, field notes, and course team member reflections and interviews. The data collected was initially sorted into either practices or supports for initial analysis. Data analysis utilized iterative coding rounds that created an in-depth account of how patterns manifested across the semester and supports (see Zeller, 2022). I coded the supports data using Anderson and Colyvas’ (2021) categories of MoR. Once the initial practices and supports coding was complete, micro-cycles of iteration occurred as sup ports suggested practices and practices suggested possible supports. The iterative process resulted in final descriptions of patterns of practice and self-activating supports.

## Findings and Implications

In the larger study, I investigated the practices a nd supports of the continua of science representati on (Zeller,

2022) and student vs teacher centering practices. In this poster, I discuss the utility of my framing and methods to

inform change efforts that came out of this wider work. Examining patterns of classroom-system practice and the supports that sustain them inform educational change by illuminating the breadth and depth of how practices manifest within the classroom as well as how these practices are supported via multiple levels beyond the classroom. Within the examination of sciencerepresentation practices, a variety of patterns wer e observed. An overall science representation patte rn carried across the course as a whole, but variations were found within the different structures of the course, the different disciplines covered, and the assessments as the semester progressed. Supports for these patterns included takenfor-granted, global-level disciplinary norms about what introductory courses cover and the content ava ilable to teach. Supports also included the individual-level roles of the instructional team and university-leve l shared stakeholder interests in the students as future teachers. The detail that resulted from the systematic investigation of science representation practices and corresponding supports created descriptions with both depth and breadth of what sustains practices and led to implications that informed changes made to the course. This shows that the application of these methods for other practices holds similar potential for informing and guiding change efforts. The systematic examination of patterns and support s also led to the emergence of theory-rich patterns that created deeper understanding and showed opport unity for future work. Within the examination of te acherand student-centered instructional practices, a pattern of students building more positive relationships with science emerged that was being supported by the student-cen tering practices observed. This informed how the pa tterns the study focused on were in turn supporting patterns in shifts of students’ science identities. This illuminated an additional level of findings and implications about  student-centered practices. A separate pattern rel ated to the learning of the teaching assistants also emerged fr om the larger study. This emergent pattern pointed towards a potential new line of study of teaching assistant learning that focuses on learning through participation in course design. These emergent patterns further show the ut ility of systematic examination of patterns of prac tice and supports using the MoRe Institutional Framework.
