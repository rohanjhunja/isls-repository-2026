# Developing Postgraduates’ Feedback Literacy through Multiple-sourced Feedback Practices in an L2 Writing Class

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study explored a multi-sourced (peer, AI, teacher) feedback model in enhancing student feedback literacy. Participants were 18 postgraduates registered in an L2 business English writing course. Findings revealed the complementary roles of different sources: peer review developed evaluative judgment, AI addressed linguistic accuracy, and teacher feedback targeted content and logic. The model extends beyond single or doubled sourced feedback practice and has pedagogical implications on instructional design for L2 writing classes.

As a core component of second language (L2) writing instruction, feedback has consistently remained a central topic of interest for researchers (Carless & Boud, 2018; Hyland & Hyland, 2019; Yu et al., 2023). Scholars argue that by developing feedback literacy, students can better understand the value of feedback, make informed judgments, regulate affective responses and take actions, thereby enhancing their ability to use feedback for improvement (Han & Xu, 2020; Molloy et al., 2020). Despite the growing re cognition of its significance, developing student feedback literacy in L2 writing remains at an exploratory stage and empirical evidence is still limited, especially in the Era of AI.

Traditional English writing instruction often relies on teacher feedback, which influences student feedback literacy through cognitive, behavioral, and affective dimensions. With a paradigm shift to student-centered learning, more peer feedback practices are implemented in writing instruction. By evaluating peers’ writing, students not only deepen their understanding of writing standards, but also improve their ability to critically appreciate feedback, self-regulate and manage their own feedback processes (Zhang & Gao, 2025). More recent research has shown that AI-generated feedback has emerged as a strong plus to the sources of feedback. Compared to teacher or peer feedback, AI feedback offers students more accurate information about student learning performance (Lu et al., 2023) and enhance learners’ acceptance of feedback (Banihashem et al., 2024). However, empirical research on the role of AI-generated feedback in developing students’ feedback literacy remains limited. In particular, few studies have systematically explored how teacher, peer, and AI feedback can be integrated to form a coherent multi-sourced feedback system that enhances students’ feedback literacy. To address this gap, the present study investigated a tri-sourced feedback within a structured instructional framework, aiming to reveal how their complementary strengths contribute to the development of students’ interpretive, applicative, and affective feedback capacities. This integrative approach not only contributes  to a more nuanced understanding of feedback literacy development but also carries significant pedagogical implications for the integrated and effective use of multi-sourced feedback in L2 writing classrooms.

## Research design

This study mainly focuse d on how the multi-sourced feedback in English writing promotes learners ’ feedback literacy and improvement on writing. Specifically, it include d two research questions: (1) What are the characteristics of peer, AI and teacher feedback in postgraduate English writing  class? (2) How does the multisourced feedback practices (peer, AI and teacher) improve postgraduates’ feedback literacy? The participants of the study were 18 Year-1 postgraduates majored in applied linguistics at a Chinese Mainland university. In a course titled “ Research and Practice of Business English Writing”, multi-sourced feedback practices were designed and implemented throughout the course. Specifically, s tudents were systematically engaged in a series of feedback activities involving giving, receiving, interpreting, and acting upon feedback from multiple sources, including peers, AI tools (e.g., ChatGPT), and the instructor. For peer feedback, a technological platform called Peerceptiv was used to facilitate the peer feedback giving and receiving processes. For AI feedback, ChatGPT was utilized to generate comments for students ’ writing improvement. The teacher’s feedback was conducted after peers’ and AI’s feedback. The interfaces of feedback process involving peers (Peerceptiv), AI (ChatGPT) and teacher (Revision mode of Word) are shown in Figures 1, 2 & 3 respectively. Students were also required to write reflective journals when they submit ted each of the revised version. In 782 reflective journals, students articulate d their experiences, feelings, understanding and actions interacting  with different types of feedback and compared the merits and demerits of different types of feedback in their writing. Throughout the course, three tasks were designed, following a circle of “assigning the writing task → students submitting Draft 1→conducting peer review by Peerceptiv→ composing revised Draft 2 and reflective journal → AI generated feedback to the revised Draft 2→ composing further revised Draft 3 and reflective journal → teacher ’s feedback →  finishing final Draft 4 and reflection ”. Each task /circle lasted four weeks. The data included records of feedback activities, drafts of writing, and reflective journals at various stages. ## Preliminary findings

This poster only summarized a few themes pertaining to student feedback literacy in the dataset.

(1) Characteristics of peer, AI and teacher feedback. Peer feedback, conducted via Peerceptiv, was

highly structured and accessible due to the provided rubrics. Students found it manageable to score and evaluate based on criteria. However, the tendency to rely on the rubric also limited its depth. Most students offered minimal written comments, and those that were provided were often general, focusing on surface-level issues like grammar. In contrast, AI-generated feedback was comprehensive at the linguistic level. It produced a fully revised version of the text, primarily offering substitutions for vocabulary and sentence structures. It paid little attention to higher-order concerns such as the appropriateness of content and argumentative coherence.  The teacher’s feedback shifted decisively to content and logic, as compared to the previous surface errors such as grammar. For example, in the recommendation letter writing, the teacher pinpointed inadequacies in content, logical flaws, and structural weaknesses. This strategic focus guided students toward a deeper understanding of the writing task.

(2) Role of peers, AI and teacher feedback in promoting student feedback literacy. The integration

of multi-sourced feedback played a distinct and complementary role in developing postgraduates ’ feedback literacy. The act of providing peer feedback within the structured Peerceptiv system was crucial for helping students make sense of  assessment criteria, thereby building their evaluative judgment. Repeatedly  managing affective responses to diverse peer comments fostered greater emotional resilience and a more objective view of critique. AI feedback empowered students in the practical use of feedback for immediate linguistic improvement, encouraging proactive engagement with tools to refine their writing. Most significantly, the teacher's role evolved to focus on content and logic, challenging students to make judgements on higher-order concerns. This guided them beyond surface-level corrections, cultivating the critical discernment needed to  take action for substantive, meaning-level revisions, which is the cornerstone of advanced feedback literacy.

## Implications

The findings of this study carry significant implications for L2 writing pedagogy and curriculum design. They underscore that an integrated, multi-sourced feedback system is not merely beneficial but essential for developing comprehensive feedback literacy. The synergy between peer, AI, and teacher feedback creates a scaffolded learning ecosystem where each source addresses distinct aspects of the writing process. Peers fos ter evaluative judgment and resilience, AI provides immediate, low-stakes linguistic support and fosters learner autonomy, and the teacher guides high-order conceptual and rhetorical development. For educators, this model necessitates a strategic redesign of writing tasks and feedback practices. It is crucial to leverage technological tools like Peerceptiv and AI to streamline the feedback process and manage teacher workload, thereby freeing up instructor capacity for more profound, content-focused interventions. However, this integration must be implemented with clear guidance. Students require training to use AI critically and avoid over-reliance, while teachers must refine their role to provide t he unique, insightful commentary that machines cannot. Ultimately, a deliberately balanced feedback ecology is key to preparing students for the complex literacy demands of the future.
