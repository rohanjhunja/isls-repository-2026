# Designing for Expansive Forms of STEAM Disciplinary Engagement

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Increasingly, calls from within the natural and computational sciences are recognizing the need for more expansive forms of disciplinary theory building and practice to connect social theories in the study of natural systems. Within science education and science teacher education, there is an increasing recognition of the need for expansive forms science teaching that connect sociopolitical contexts of science with both the professional learning of teachers and the science taught in K12 classrooms. In this symposium, we explore designs for expansive STEAM disciplinary engagement across classroom, professional learning, and informal Indigenous STEAM settings to understand the ways in which work at the edges of disciplines can transform STEAM teaching and learning for educators and students.

At the same time, a new report from the National Ac ademies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine (NASEM): Equity in K-12 STEM Education: Framing Decisions for the Future   (NASEM, 2024), calls for a clearer articulation of the kinds of powered decisi on-making that work towards particular purposes for eq uitable STEM education. The report presents the field with five “equity frames”, or “conceptions of equity tha t can be and a re used as rationale for decision-making” (p. 133). These are: (1) reducing gaps betw een groups, (2) expanding opportunity and access, (3) embracing het erogeneity in STEM classrooms, (4) learning and usi ng STEM to promote justice, and (5) envisioning sustainable futures through STEM. While all five decision-making frames are important in working towards equity acro ss all levels of educational systems, some may be m ore present than others. In this symposium, we focus on  frames 3-5 as key decision-making frames for the kinds of expansive designs of disciplinary engagement that we argue for. For example, in reference to Frame 3, embracing heterogeneity in STEM classrooms, the report states that Rather than move toward a ‘single story’ or narrati ve of disciplines, disciplines are understood as evolving and internally heterogeneous, and as cu ltural practices for developing and warranting knowledge claims...STEM [is] a cultural and sociopolitical en deavor, situated in time, place, and within networks of power.”  (NASEM, 2024, p.6-12) All these examples (and more) necessitate a re-imagining and de-settling of how we engage all learners (K-12 students and their families, educators, educational  leaders, and even scientists) with expansive forms  of STEAM disciplines, where the disciplines themselves are re-imagining more just futures of their work. The five studies in this symposium will describe wo rk at the intersection of STEAM disciplinary learning, social theories, and changemaking towards  heterogeneous and expansive learning in the discip lines. Across the symposium, the papers will give us visio ns of design for expansive STEAM learning across multiple contexts, grade levels, and disciplinary domains. Paper 1: Expansive engineering: Artificial intelligence use and development in education toward justice, sovereignty, and equity Elizabeth Starks, Veronica McGowan, Symone Gyles, Bryan White, Megan Bang, Carrie Tzou

## Framing and study design

We acknowledge our moment in time when artificial i ntelligence (AI) is an integrated and growing part of our designed world and also acknowledge our responsibility as educators, designers, researchers, learners, and as part of ecosystems, to understand the  powered decisions that have led to current inequiti es in order to build a more just future. In this paper, we describe the design and implementation of a 3-day professional development series, CS and AI toward Justice, Sovereignty, and Equity.  In this PD series, we build on the work of Indig enous information scientists and computer scientists who have been thinking critically about data sovereignty on cultural and tribal levels, building data systems that refle ct sovereignty across scale and access (Lewis, 2020; Hendrix,

2023) We use critical sociotechnical literacy (McGo wan & Bell, 2020) as a method for educators to prac tice

equitable approaches to both the use of AI by educators and learners, and teaching of computer science. In the PD series, educators spent time researching their school’s guidelines or standards about AI use and developing  their own stances based on their interactions with data and conversations during the professional development. We ask the following questions: (1) How does the professio nal development series shift educators’ understandi ng of the intersection between CS/AI and power and historicity? (2) What sense do educators make of sovereignty and how that applies to AI and data systems?

Data sources and analysis Participants were from 13 states, in both in and out of school settings, and spanned K-16 contexts, state leadership, and instructional coaches. Data were collected from  online professional development recordings (Zoom),  digital artifacts created by participants in the sessions, daily exit tickets, and post-interviews with participants. For space purposes, we will focus below on qualitative and qu antitative exit ticket data. We used emergent codin g and grounded theory (Strauss & Corbin, 1997) to analyze exit ticket data and understand educators’ sensemaking and shifting stances on AI and equity.

## Findings

Exit ticket data show that as they participated in the professional development, educators’ confidence  increased in their knowledge around topics related to AI, the  social context of new technologies, and their fami liarity with their school’s policies and standards on AI. Educat ors also gained a deeper understanding of the ethic al considerations surrounding AI, particularly in rela tion to data sovereignty, bias, and equity.  The PD  also helped educators to expand their understandings of the imp ortance of data sovereignty, especially for marginalized and Indigenous communities, as it involves control over one's own data and its use. For example, educators recognized that respecting cultural data and preventing its mi suse by corporations or AI systems are essential fo r ethical AI practices. This realization led them to consider in tegrating discussions about data ownership, privacy, and the consequences of corporate control over personal sto ries and information into their curriculum. Educato rs' responses also demonstrated their grappling with the bias and equity issues inherent in AI. They described coming to understand that AI is shaped by the data used to  build it, oft en reflecting historical and systemic biases particularly harmful to BIPOC communities.

At the start of the course, 4/19 respondents stated  that they feared AI, and 5/19 said they did not re ally understand what it was (day 1 exit survey). During the course of the PD, educators began to see AI's b roader societal impacts. They described shiftin g their focus towards ethical AI use, emphasizing t he importance of transparency, responsible engagement, and understanding AI's potential to both benefit and harm societ y, including guiding students to become creators of AI  tools rather than just users and fostering critical thinking about the data and algorithms behind AI systems. Th is deeper awareness marks a significant shift from their previous views, as educators moved towards a more n uanced understanding of AI's complexities and responsibilities in education. They reported that this course highlighted the need for an equity-centered approach to AI, where students are encouraged to question AI’s fairness and how it impacts various communities. Below is an excerpt from exit tickets: Data sovereignty [was a powerful idea I took away f rom this course]. I had no sense of what this term really meant prior to the session, but no w I'm thinking about it in many settings/contexts. Integration [was another]. This was already on my radar, but t his learning emphasized the importance of integrating CS into and across all content areas in meaningful ways. Bias [is a third powerful idea I took away from this course].  I have a better sense of what this means in the context of AI and how I can use this to support broader understanding of bias and equity issues with data.  (Educator Exit Ticket, Summer 2024) Many educators had never considered data sovereignty as related to education or been asked to consider their social histories as connected with technologies.

## Implications and contributions

Much of the concern around generative AI in educati onal settings centers around fears of plagiarism and “cheating” (Lee, et al, 2024). While we acknowledge  these concerns, we also position AI (and educators) as situated within larger systems of power, wher e generative AI represents a larger ecosystem of technologies that have the power to reproduce or resist existing societal inequities. For example, by sharing the work of Indigenous technology developers and information scientists wh o are developing systems that contribute to soverei gnty in AI, we not only foreground the presence Indigenous people in STEM, but highlight the importance of Indigenous research and ways of knowing in innovating toward cultural revitalization. In this way, we show how these same technologies can be used toward justice and help educators imagine more hopeful futures. Paper 2: Designing for expansive futures: Sex and gender justice in teacher education Veronica McGowan, Bryan White, Elizabeth Starks, Symone Gyles, Megan Bang, Carrie Tzou

## Framing and study design

Science is a powerful set of knowledge and practices that can be simultaneously liberating and oppressive, as they shape the lived realities of individuals either see n or invisibilized solely through Western scientifi c theories (Krishnamoorthy & Tolbert, 2022). Understanding the  impact of science on people and communities calls for science to address social theories in their work (G raddy-Lovelace, 2017). There is a need to prepare preserv ice and in-service educators to address the sociopolitical contexts of teaching, learning, and doing science in order to pave pathways for STEM education to contribute to t he cultivation of just, sustainable and thriving communities (NASEM, 2024).

In this work we describe the design and enactment o f an interdisciplinary undergraduate STEM course module entitled Queer Ecologies and Gender Affirming Biology. This module serves as part of a sequence of two “science for elementary teachers” science content c ourses that center racial justice and gender equity  as a part of STEM teaching and learning for educators. These cou rses take the place of required science content cou rses typically taken by preservice educators towards com pletion of their degree path ways. Here we share enactment data from both our pre-service course module and the adapted version of this module for an in-service professional learning series. Each course module in this project  was created through co-design with an interdisciplinary tea m of faculty across STEM disciplines, learning scientists, and community experts. Queer Ecology seeks to disrupt heteronormative projections onto nature and to trouble how cultural dualisms get grafted onto entangled, complex ecosystems (Roughgarden, 2013).The module described in this research engages learners in exploring 1) The social context of sex, gender, sexuality, 2) examination of data from scientific studies of same sex behavior in the natural world, 3) diversity of sex determination in the natural world, social vs. sexual selection,  and 4) examination of data and case stu dies on the range of mechanisms and hormone signals  related to human sex development, gender, and sexuality.  I n our work we ask, how can we move away from “singl e story” dominant disciplinary narratives of sex, gen der and sexuality towards more accurate as well as equitable disciplinary ways of knowing that are understood as  evolving and internally heterogeneous and encompas sing cultural as well as empirical ways of knowing.

Data sources and analysis We use framing theory which recognizes how frames a re taken up or not depending on powered relationshi ps assumed within interactions (Hand, Penuel, Gutiérre z, 2013; NASEM, 2024) and master narrative theory which acknowledge how culturally shared stories, beliefs,  values that shape thought, decision-making, and behavior (Bradford & Syed, 2019) as theoretical framings for  our coding and analysis. Data for this analysis co me from a 10-week online professional learning course that met f or 1.5 hours/ week for approximately 25 practicing educators across content areas and contexts. The online course was an adapted version of our undergraduate course module, which was co-taught by two instructors with backgrounds in queer ecology and gender-affirming biology. Data include weekly whole group zoom recordings and tran scripts, exit ticket reflections, recorded small gr oup discussions, in-class artifacts, and course design artifacts. We co nducted emergent coding for themes (Miles, Huberman, Saldaña, 2019), and then used grounded th eory to make sense for emergent patterns across dat a sets (Corbin & Strauss, 2014). Our first level coding includes themes related to two “master narratives” as frames, and second level coding included application of queer ecology theories to understanding how educators were pushing against, making sense of, and/or becoming attuned t o master narratives. Third level coding focused on the mechanisms and processes of teaching and learning expansive science.

## Findings

Our findings show that this work made visible to educators the “master narrative” of gender essentialism in science that upholds gender binaries and ideas of fixed bio logical sex in texts and other teaching and learnin g materials. As one participant reflected, “The binary language of gender and sex is SO i ngrained in my teaching of genetics even though I see the human world as complex, queer  and non-binary.” Participants also shared that engaging with data around the diversity of sex, gender, and sexuality in the natural world illuminated aspects of ecology and evolution that were never made visible to them in prior learning contexts (such as their own biolo gy education). For example one participant asked, “why  have I never learned this and why aren’t we set  up to teach this?” With raised awareness educators became more attuned to the pervasive language of gender binarie s used in science and science standards and curriculum, and surfaced the need for more scientifically accurate materials for teaching about sex diversity in classrooms. As one participant shared, “I can't stomach teaching t o a test with the reality of science knowledge being so different.” In this professional series, participants co-designed genderaffirming activities for their own contexts t hat included engaging students in analysis of expan sive data sets and using case studies from humans and other species to support expansive notions of sex, gender, and sexuality.

## Implications and contributions

Our work shows that engaging educators with expansi ve scientific data was a powerful tool for making s ettled knowledge visible, and helped move participants to both more inclusive and more accurate framings of t he diversity of sex, gender, and sexuality. Engaging w ith the complexity within and between scientific th eories allowed educators insight into the ways in which sc ientific knowledge evolves over time and opened up opportunities to engage with data-informed gender-affirming teaching and learning to create more just, equitable, and inclusive science teaching and learning environments. Paper 3: Relationality with water as a design ontology Forrest Bruce, Megan Bang

## Framing and study design

This paper will discuss relationality with water as  a design ontology in the context of an Indigenous STEAM program (ISTEAM). ISTEAM is a multisite community-based design research (CBDR) project that seeks to build thriving communities, composed of human and more-than-human (MTH) life, through the regeneration of Indigenous land-and water-based systems of education. Through CBDR, ISTEAM en gages Indigenous families and community members to dream and enact educationa l possibilities for their communities (Bang et al.,  2016). One purpose of this study is to further explicate t he process by which co-design cultivates iterative processes of dreaming, future making, and proleptic enactments in educational spaces in the her e and now with co-designers, youth, and families (Ishimaru et al., 2018; Hall et al., 2020). We analyze the processes of dreaming and enactment in the context of a three year, multisite design ar c where In digenous community members designed for water education in each of their respective communities.

While there has been significant work to unearth the epistemologies and axiologies embedded in design processes (Bang & Vossoughi, 2016), there remains a  need to examine the understandings of reality – the ontologies – that underpin the design of learning environments. We put forth that co-design involves the navigation of multiple ontologies and that the enga gement with ontological multiplicity further create s grounds for both ontological and axiological innovations (B ang, 2016). For Indigenous people, designing for le arning through Indigenous ontologies means that we engage with lands and waters as co-designers, a process that we call place-based design. The corporeal arrangement of activity  is closely tied to the ontological assumptions abo ut humans and their relationship to the natural world (Marin, 2020). This paper seeks to further understand how the corporeal arrangement of co-design creates new ontological grounds for dreaming  and enacting thriving socio-ecological futures. In doing so we examine Indigeno us community members’ dreams for water education, a nd how these dreams emerge temporally over the lifecou rse of design and spatially across various corporea l arrangements.

Data sources and analysis ISTEAM is a three-year, multi-site project that involves multiple streams of audi ovisual data that reflect both community specific design as well across community design work. There are a total of 123 co-design sessions included in the data corpus. The design process del iberately engaged in distinct corporeal arrangement s ranging from in-person, place-based design work to virtual cross-site sessions. We conduct a longitudinal micro-ethnography (Erickson, 1996)  of microinteractions in the design process over time and space. Our first round of analysis focuses on the conceptual ecology in each design meeting with respect to water. The second la yer of analysis characterized the different dreams for wat er and water education that were articulated by co-designers. Once both rounds of codes were complete, we selected “hot spots” in the data that reflected the codes from round 1 and 2 of analysis – places where there are a density of codes – and conducted  interaction analysis for pivotal micro-moments in the desig n process (Jordan & Henderson, 1995). Additionally,  we have conducted design interviews where co-designers reflect on their relationship to local wa terways and their participation in the last three years of ISTEAM. Using these different data streams, we conducted a spatialized diachronic analysis (Saxe & Esmonde, 2005) that traces how co-designers dreams for water and water education emer ged from a series of micro-interactions that take place across multiple spatia l configurations involving humans, MTHs, la nds and waters.

## Findings

Though we are continuing to refine our analysis, we  have two preliminary findings to share. First, the re was a deepening of human-water relations over time. While ontologies of respecting water were present from the beginning, relations with water were often enclosed  by colonial, human-centered ontologies. This enclosure was evident in the way that early co-design meetings would sometimes focus on the feasib ility of learning along waterways (e.g. safety, logistics, staying dry). La ter meetings tended to focus on water pedagogies or  ways of enacting relations with water (e.g. water-based practices, songs and stories involving water). We see this turn towards enactment as evidence for the cultivation o f kinship ontologies with water over time (Van Horn et al., 2021). In other words, there was a shift from more psychologically distant notions of human-water relationality mediated by coloniality towards more psychologically close discussions of how to be a good relative – how to be kin – to water (Van Horn et al., 2021). Second, physical engagements with nearby waterways through place-based design became a site of engagement for asserting a kinship ontology (Bang, 2016; Todd, 2014). Specifically, canoeing, a central practice to Indigenous lifeways  for countless generations, was instrumental for cu ltivating a kinship ontology. As some co-designers have discussed in their interviews, trave rsing water on canoes was a corporeal arrangement that opened up new possibilities for enacting human-water relations in a lived way.

## Implications and contributions

This work has important implications for the design and pedagogical practice as it will articulate design principles for creating water-based learning environments that support more ethic al human-water relations. Specifically, findings will demonstrate the  role that corporeal arrangements play in establishi ng ontological grounds for dreaming and enacting educational possibilities in co-design processes. These findings will contribute to  a framework that allows design-based researchers to more deeply understand the imp lications for the spatial organization of design. A deeper understanding for the spatial implications of design will be  beneficial for establishing design processes grounded in relationa l ontologies in which humans are interconnected wit h lands and waters. Such design processes are necessary for  building systems of educati on that cultivate thriving socio-ecological futures.

Paper 4: Each one teach one Mario Guerra, Megan Bang

## Framing and study design

Our planet is currently experiencing unprecedented changes as a result of human actions, yet a significant portion of the population remains unaware or chooses to ignore this reality. The ecosystem and ongoing climate crisis are complex systems—"usually made up of many individuals displaying a variety of behaviors; and yet, they exhibit a predictable pattern as a group (Levy & Wilensky, 2008, p. 2).” Understanding complex systems means grasping the interconnectedness of individual entities and h ow their int eractions lead to the emergence of new, distinct patterns. These emergent patterns wouldn't exist wi thout individual agent-level interactions. Frameworks for understanding these interactions —which can better conceptualize complex ecological s ystems—are fou nd in many Indigenous communities. As Muller et al. (2019) conclude, "Indigenous ways of being and worldviews based on connection, reciprocity, and ethical relat ionships epitomise what is missing in the mainstrea m doctrine of environmental management" (p. 18). These Indigen ous perspectives, with their deep connections to th e land and intricate interweaving of place and thought (Watts, 2013), are thus vital in addressing climate change issues. As Watts (2013) concludes “Thus, habitats and ecosystems are better understood as societies from an Indigenous point of view; meaning that they have ethical struc tures, inter-species treaties and agreem ents, and further their ability to interpret, understand and implement. Non-human beings are active members of society." From this deep connection to the land emerges what we term a "cult ural aggregate" —ideas shaped through iterative learning processes spanning time immemorial. These concepts aggregate a collective understanding of agents and embody teachings about relational responsibility. As a res ult, ecological decision-making can more ethically incorporate non-human perspectives.

Data sources and analysis This paper examines the interaction of Indigenous y outh through the ISTEAM program and “Each One Teach One” activity. During this activity, students learn ed about specific plants and then shared their know ledge with others. Over the 1.5 hour session, we gathered audio and video data from students and facilitators, allowing us to closely observe and analyze how students engaged wi th Indigenous perspectives and the natural environm ent. The groups in the activity consisted of between 2 a nd 5 students, one faci litator, and one plant card with information about their specific focal plant. Of th e 8 total groups in the activity, 5 had a student r ecording the activity through a chest mounted GoPro resulting in  a total of 8 hours and 12 minutes of video. In ana lyzing the data, we first created excerpts from ambulatory seq uences as our primary unit for interaction analysis  (Hall & Stevens, 2015; Marin, 2020). After creating excerpts, we analyzed the discours e to characterize the epistemic ecologies with respect to complex systems reasoning within each sequence. More specifically, we examined these sequences for agents or aggregates in student reasoning/activity.

## Findings

The results of this study reveal how Indigenous sto ries and cultural knowledge shaped students' unders tanding during the E1T1 activity discussions. Throughout th e activity, students engaged with both individual p lants (agents) and broader species-level concepts (aggregates) through traditional stories and teachings. The analysis revealed that what we term “cultural aggregates” - knowledge passed down through generations-helped students toggle between agent and aggregate level reasoning while considering complex systems. Closer examinati on of excerpts with similar ratios of agent and aggregate-level actors unveiled insightful conversations abou t focal plants, their ecological communities, and humanity's role within the ecosystem. In sum, students readily employed both agent-level thinking (when directly interacting with indi vidual plants) and aggregate-level understanding (through cultural teachings or “cultural aggregate”) while learning about or teaching their focal plants.

## Implications and contributions

The design and implementation of the "Each One Teac h One" activity demonstrated how learning with natu re, particularly through Indigenous and relational ontologies, fostered complex systems thinking and deeper learning among participants. Perspective-taking—facilitated through plant cards and storytelling —helped refine the "cultural aggregates" and integrate them into the conversation. These aggregates enabled students to consider the well-being of an entire species. These findings suggest that outdoor learning activities, designed to foste r agentaggregate toggling, can effectively facilitate comp lex systems thinking among students. By interacting  directly with the natural world, students can apply abstract  concepts to t angible experiences, thereby deepening their understanding of ecological systems. The use of cultural aggregates presents a promising model for education that fosters deep, multifaceted understanding of ecologi cal systems. By combining perspective-taking, relational ontologies, and outdoor learning, we created a rich  learning environment that encouraged students to t hink critically about their place within the natural world. This approach might not only enhances ecological sensibility but also has the potential to nurture a generation of individuals who are more attuned to the complexities of nature and more inclined towards sustainable practices.

Paper 5: Socio-ecological systems modeling: Place-based design for ethical deliberation and change-making with youth and their families Jordan Sherry-Wagner, Christa Haverly, Carrie Tzou, Megan Bang

## Framing and study design

In this conceptual paper we describe an empirically-engaged approach to socio-ecological systems modeling we are continuing to develop through the Learning in P laces project. Grounded in the Learning in Places m aterial ecosystem and Field-Based Seasonal St oryline for Transdisciplinary Education, we elevate  how the process of model development contributes to desettling normati ve expectations across multiple dimensions of inqui ry, and to generate innovative modeling practices that supp ort imagining and enacting more healthful, sustaina ble, and just forms of living with the rest of the natural w orld (Bang et al., 2013). In our work, these inclu de: contextualizing inquiry within particular places th rough routine and extended outdoor sensemaking (i.e. wondering walks); grounding models in the noticings, values, histories, and expertise of learners, families, and communities as central to sensemaking with models; and expanding normative epistemic foci of models to  also attend to ontological and axiological dimensions of possible decisions in complex socio-ecological systems. Data sources and analysis Drawing on data gathered from our co-design work with educators, youth, and families, th is paper describes preliminary analysis of modeling data to develop key dimensions and trace important implications of our approach to modeling with educators. Data sou rces include socio-ecological systems models co-designed in professional learning sessions with educators over three years a nd their related whole and small group discussions;  reflection ticket results across the PL session; artifacts from classroom implementation of socio-ecological systems models developed with children; and iteratively revised in structional materials to support modeling in classr ooms. We analyzed the data over time to understand (a) our own trajectory of understanding the role of modeling for educator and student learning, (b) our own mediation to supp ort educator learning about modeling, (c) educator sensemaking and implementation of modeling practice  in learning environments,  and (d) how children en gage with this practice over time.

## Findings

Over the course of our engagement with educators and children in socio-scientific modeling, we have come to understand modeling as a sensemaking practice desig ned to support learners in understanding why, how, and under what conditions socio-ecological phenomena happen the way they do, as well as how place, time, power, and human intervention influence socio-ecological relationships. Used for both individual and collective sensemaking (i.e. ensemble modeling (Learning in Pl aces Collaborative, 2021), our des ign elevates emergent forms of modeling (Lehrer & Schauble, 2005) in whic h learners build models that represent their collec tive sensemaking about socio-ecological phenomena and relationships as grounded in their particular field-based experiences. Rather than static and wholly explanatory, these models ar e designed to be dynamic and ever-changing based on new data and other types of infor mation learned through the investigation of phenome na, discussion with others (i.e. wondering talks) and learning from other sources of media (books, video, etc.). In the recent report on equity in K-12 science education, the National Academies of Sci ences, Engineering, and Mathematics (NASEM, 2024) defined equity as a process and an outcome. We see the proc ess of creating, revising, and sensemaking with socio-ecological systems models as equitable modeling and pedagogical practice. The equitable outcomes of thi s practice we recognize as expanded views of who do es science and a change in relationships to the discipline of science and with places. We also see equitable outcomes as young children being given opportunities to atte nd to complexity, understand out responsibilities w ithin the rest of the natural world, and engage in deeper not icing, wondering, and ethical deliberation in ways that expand and diversify possibilities for life within (and beyond) context of disciplinary learning (Warren et al., 2020).

## Implications and contributions

We understand our approach to modeling as having si gnificant implications for how we imagine possible decisions and approaches to sustainable change-making as connected to particular places. In this w ay, our approach intervenes in a persistence of de-placed, decontextualized, or abstracted forms of inquiry  and representation that can reproduce coloniality and w estern rationalist approaches to learning and chang e-making (Vossoughi et al., 2023). Through iterative cycles of model development informed by routine learning experiences within particular outdoor places, our approach to m odeling supports expanded construction of agency wh ereby places, lands, waters, and more-than-humans are understood as active mediators of comple x socio-ecological sensemaking (McDaid Barry et al., 2023). Situated in particular plac es aimed at informing possible courses of action we could take, such models support psycholog ically close construals of family, self, and commun ity as existing as a part of the rest of the natural world. In Learning in Places, we see an opportunity to expand modeling practices to include other forms of representations  (such as artistic representations of socio-ecological systems) that hold deep potential for ethical reflection, decision-making, and change-making.
