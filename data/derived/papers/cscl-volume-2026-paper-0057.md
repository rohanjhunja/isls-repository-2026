# Linking Physiological Data and Self-Reports: Exploring Engagement in Collaborative Learning

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study investigated the relationship between physiological synchrony and engagement in an authentic collaborative learning context. Heart rate data and self-reported engagement surveys were collected from 32 postgraduate students during a semester-long collaborative writing course. D ata were analyzed using Monte Carlo shuffling, Spearman correlation, and Welch's t-test. Spearman correlation revealed no significant associations between heart rate-based physiological synchrony and any dimension of self-reported engagement. However, pairs exhibiting significant physiological synchrony reported notably higher socio-emotional engagement compared to non-synchronous pairs, with this difference approaching statistical significance. These findings suggest that in authentic learning environments characterized by dynamic complexity, a direct linear relationship between physiological synchrony and engagement may not always be evident. Nevertheless, when physiological synchrony reaches a statistically significant threshold, it appears t o serve as a sensitive indicator of the socio-emotional dimensions of collaboration, highlighting its value for uncovering complex dynamics of student engagement in naturalistic educational settings.

Collaborative learning has been recognized as an important pedagogical approach due to its potential to foster learners’ collaborative problem-solving, critical thinking, and cooperative competence (Li et al., 2023, 2025). However, effective collaboration does not automatically emerge from merely grouping learners together; it requires their sustained engagement with the task and one another (Haataja et al., 2025; Skinner & Pitzer, 2012). Student engagement is generally conceptualized as commitment or effortful involvement in learning (Henrie et al., 2015). It is a multidimensional construct encompassing behavioral, cognitive, and socio-emotional dimensions (Fredricks et al., 2004; Xu et al., 2023). Behavioral engagement involves observable effort and participation; cognitive engagement reflects deep learn ing strategies; and socio-emotional engagement captures learners’ affective states and the quality of their social interactions  (Fredricks et al., 2004). Due to its importance for learning, researchers have continuously sought to investigate engagement in collaborative learning  to reveal its characteristics.

Traditionally, researchers use post-hoc self-report questionnaires or observations to study engagement in collaborative learning (Appleton et al., 2006; Henrie et al., 2015). While valuable, these methods have limitations: self-reports provide only static accounts, and observations are labor-intensive, difficult to scale, and cannot fully capture learners’ internal cognitive and affective states (Haataja et al., 2025). These limitations have led to calls for more objective, instantaneous, and efficient measures of engagement (Zhang et al., 2021). With advances in biosensing technologies, physiological measures such as skin conductance and heart rate have been introduced to explore engagement (Haataja et al., 2025; Hammond et al., 2023; Stuldreher et al., 2023). However, this line of research remains early and findings are inconsistent, with most studies conducted in laboratory settings (Zhang et al., 2021). To address these gaps, this study examines the relationship between heart rate –based physiological synchrony and self-reported engagement in an authentic collaborative writing course. Despite the small sample size, it provides preliminary evidence and supports further research.

## Literature review

Physiological synchrony in collaborative learning Learning is an active process that leaves a physiological footprint —for example, elevated heart rate or increased skin-conductance responses frequency (Haataja et al., 2018). These indicators are key measures of the Autonomic Nervous System (ANS), whose Sympathetic (SNS) and Parasympathetic (PNS) branches work in tandem, dynamically changing as they regulate the body in preparation for and in response to environmental conditions (Palumbo et al., 2017). Extensive  studies indicate that ANS responses are closely linked to  learning-relevant processes, including enhanced emotion, increased attention, and better memory  (Kreibig, 2010). As learning is becoming increasingly social, this motivates a shift in focus from intrapersonal ANS activity to interpersonal 384 autonomic physiology (IAP), which is defined as the relationship between individuals’ physiological dynamics, as indexed by continuous measures of the autonomic nervous system (Palumbo et al., 2017).  Within this framework, physiological synchrony (PS) is a key indicator,  referring to group members ’ similar, simultaneous changes in physiological  signals, e.g., electrodermal activity (EDA)  and heart rate (HR) (Dindar et al., 20 20). Although ANS activity is a well-validated measurement of the learning process, it does not indicate valence or context. Therefore, secondary measures are needed to jointly interpret their meaning and to illuminate the learning process (Palumbo et al., 2017). Many studies continue in this vein, assuming that these interpersonal physiological processes can reflect invisible social processes co-occurring with observable interactions  (Haataja et al., 2018). For example, Dindar et al. (2020) linked dyadic physiological synchrony derived from electrodermal activity with self-reported cogniti ve, motivational, and emotional self-regulation in learning, and found that physiological synchrony was only significantly related to cognitive self-regulation. Physiological synchrony and engagement Only recent studies have begun using physiological data to investigate engagement in collaborative learning, suggesting it offers a continuous and implicit measure compared to traditional approaches. Typically, such studies employ continuous signal analysis methods to compute dyadic  or group-level physiological synchrony indices, which are then compared with engagement assessed through other measures to examine their relationships. For instance, Hammond et al. (2023) found that inter-subject heart rate (HR)  synchrony was associated with selfreported attentional and emotional engagement, whereas no such relationship was observed for skin conductance. Conversely, Haataja et al. (2025) reported a negative association between electrodermal activity (EDA) synchrony and perceived value of collaboration, suggesting that high synchrony does not always indicate productive engagement.

However, research in this area remains in its early stages, and further studies are needed to validate these examine the relationship between group members’ physiological synchrony and self-reported engagement. This study is guided by the following two research questions: RQ1:  How is group members’ physiological synchrony associated with their self-reported behavioral, cognitive, socio-emotional, and overall engagement?

RQ2:  How do the self-reported behavioral, cognitive, socio-emotional, and overall engagement levels differ between pairs with significant physiological synchrony and those without?

## Participants

This study was conducted at a normal university in northern China, involving 32 first-year postgraduate students majoring in education (mean age = 22.3 years, SD = 0.4; 6 male). All students were enrolled in an English academic writing course and participated voluntarily after being informed of the study’s purpose. They were assured of confidentiality and the right to withdraw at any stage. The students self-organized into ten collaborative groups consisting of three to four members each.

Collaborative activity The study was conducted in a semester-long, two-credit compulsory English academic writing course for postgraduate students majoring in education. In addition to weekly lectures, students were required to work in groups to produce a 4,000– 6,000-word academic paper on a self-selected topic. Based on prior teaching and writing experience, the instructor encouraged each group to engage in at least one hour of face-to-face collaboration per week, accumulating a minimum of eight hours of joint work over the seme ster. Due to time constraints, groups were permitted to use previously collected but unpublished experimental data for the writing task. During the eight-week writing period, the groups were responsible for identifying a suitable dataset, formulating research questions, and collaboratively composing their paper. In each collaborative writing session, group members used their own computers t o log in to Tencent Online Share d Document, where they jointly developed and revised their manuscript.

Before the course began, participants’ prior experience in reading and publishing English academic papers was examined. The results indicated that participants had comparable prior knowledge, with no significant differences among them.

385 During each collaborative writing session, participants’ heart rate and self-reported engagement data were collected. Each participant’s heart rate (HR) was continuously and unobtrusively recorded using the Scosche Rhythm+ armband heart rate monitor (Valencell, Inc., Raleigh, NC, USA). The Scosche Rhythm + is a precision biometrics device designed to monitor HR. To collect data, the device was  installed on each participant’s nondominant arm, and HR data were sampled at a frequency of 1 Hz. In addition, students’ self-reported engagement was collected immediately after each session using the Collaborative Learning Engagement Questionnaire (Xu et al., 2023), a validated five-point Likert-scale instrument developed for the Chinese higher education context. The questionnaire measures three dimensions of engagement —behavioral, cognitive, and soci o-emotional—and demonstrates high reliability, with Cronbach’s α coefficients ranging from 0.82 to 0.98 for the subscales and 0.93 for the overall instrument.

## Data analysis

Due to missing physiological and questionnaire data for some group members, and the absence of HR recordings in several sessions, this study  obtained valid data for 19 of the 75 sessions.  Preprocessing of the HR signal followed a commonly used workflow (e.g., see Dindar et al., 2020; Haataja et al., 2018). First, the HR data were visually inspected for clear signs of  movement art ifacts. Second, each participant’s HR series was Z-score standardized toneutralize the individual differences among the students. Subsequently, following prior methods (e.g., Dindar et al., 2020; Haataja et al., 2018), physiological synchrony (PS) was estimated pairwise within each collaborating group by computing a Single Session Index (SSI) for each pair in every session. A sliding-window procedure (30-s window; 1-s step) was appli ed to obtain pairwise Pearson correlations between members’ HR signals, yielding a time-resolved correlation series per pair for the session (Figure 1). SSI was computed by taking the natural logarithm of the ratio between (i) the sum  of positive correlations and (ii) the sum of the  absolute values of negative correlations across the session. Larger positive SSI values reflect stronger pairwise PS over the session. Accordingly, a three-person group yields three pairwise SSIs, and a four-person group yields six per session. Monte Carlo shuffling was further utilized to determine the significance of synchrony (e.g., see Dindar et al., 2020). Only actual SSI values higher than the highest shuffled concordance at  p <.05 were considered to present significant synchronous pairs.

Overall, the findings revealed no significant association between heart rate– based physiological synchrony and any dimension of self-reported engagement. However, when significant synchrony occurred between pairs, their self-reported socio-emotional engagement was higher than in pairs without significant synchrony, with this difference approaching statistical significance. This finding diverges from some previous studies that reported positive relationships between physiological synchrony and some facets of engagement, such as cognitive engagement (Zhang et al., 2021), or both cognitive and socio-emotional engagement (Stuldreher et al., 2023). The results of this study suggest that, in authentic collaborative learning contexts, a direct linear relationship between physiological synchrony and engagement may not always be evident. Unlike lab-based research (e.g., Hammond et al., 2023; Stuldreher et al., 2023)  —where task demands and social dynamics are more limited — real classrooms involve fluctuating engagement  and diverse interaction styles  (Haataja et al., 2025). Under such variability, physiological synchrony and engagement may not consistently manifest as simple linear relationships (Haataja et al., 2025). This study also uncovered that when physiological synchrony exceeded a statistical threshold (Monte-Carlo shuffling, p <.05) and achieved significance, dyads tended to report higher socio-emotional engagement. This finding reaffirms that significant physiological synchrony captures meaningful moments that warrant attention during collaborative processes (Dindar et al., 2020;  Palumbo et al., 2017). Additionally, physiological synchrony appears more sensitive to socio-emotional aspects of collaboration than to cognitive or behavioral dimensions, consistent with prior research (e.g., Li et al., 2023). This may be because socio-emotional processes—encom passing learners’ emotions and interaction quality (Fredricks et al., 2004) — are inherently interpersonal, and physiological synchrony, as an index of shared autonomic activity (Palumbo et al., 2017), is well suited to capturing these affective and relational dynamics. This study examined the relationship between heart rate –based physiological synchrony and self-reported engagement in an authentic collaborative writing course. Although no significant linear correlations were found, dyads with significant physiological synchrony reported higher socio-emotional engagement than nonsynchronous dyads, with the difference approaching significance. This suggests that physiological synchrony may reflect meaningful affective and relational aspects of collaboration. By linking physiological data with self-reports in a naturalistic setting, this study contributes to understanding engagement from a physiological perspective. Several limitations should be noted. The imbalance between synchronous (n = 7) and non-synchronous pairs (n =

56) limits statistical power. Although robust methods (e.g., Welch’s t-test and bootstrapping) were used, this

imbalance—common in authentic class room contexts—requires cautious interpretation.
