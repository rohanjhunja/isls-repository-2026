# Making Drawings Count for Assessing Quantitative Knowledge

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
We used student-generated drawings to assess students’ understandings of dynamic equilibrium. A pre-/post-design was used to determine how students track physical quantities across time and space. Initial and final subsystem drawings revealed conceptual dynamics depicting entities in dynamic equilibrium. The findings suggest students productively extracted quantitative information from a computational simulation but in a spatially fragmented fashion. These findings underscore the potential of drawing assessments that determine how students understand quantified complex systems.

## Introduction

Scientists draw diagrams to represent complex syste ms. In science education, student-generated drawings have received increased attention because they offer ins ight into students’ developing understandings of sy stems (Ainsworth et al., 2011). We used stude nt-generated drawings to assess the micro-analytic changes in students’ representations of a quantified complex system —the Resting Membrane Potential (RMP). RMP involves balancing chemical and electrical potentials—aggregate physical quantities critical to physiological homeostasis. Drawing, however, demands that students must decide  where to depict the individual entities that make up these quantities (Ryan & Stieff, 2019). Our qualitative a pproach contributes an analytic technique for chara cterizing students’ developing understanding of quantified complex systems in dynamic equilibrium (Lira, 2020b). Drawing as an assessment strategy Student-generated drawings support students’ science learning but with limitations. Although drawing can support students’ comprehension of science concepts, the ex act psychological mechanisms that underpin this lea rning phenomenon remain unclear (Cromley et al., 2020). T his has led to instructional designs that produce m ixed results. Growing consensus sugges ts that to learn from drawing, students must use in structional materials and techniques that guide their drawing practices. With  strategic support, drawing helps students track t he causal mechanisms for how individual entities interact to produce emergent properties (Cooper et al., 2017). This motivated us to determine how students’ drawings re veal their developing but fragmented  understandings of complex systems, as opposed to how drawing induces learning effects (e.g., Van Meter & Garner, 2005). We therefore depart from the drawing-to-learn approach to instead use drawing as an assessm ent (cf. (Chang et al., 2020). Drawing offers an assessment strategy that reveals distinct knowledge compared t o other modes, such as written answers (Ainsworth et al., 2 011). For example, Ryan and Stieff (2019) demonstra ted that students’ drawings reveal the chemical composition of a system—i.e., the system’s physical entities—more easily than causal interactions. Thus, drawing affords hol istic spatial insight into systems, as has been dem onstrated by seminal investigations (Larkin & Simon, 1987). Just as drawing supports some forms of learning, it also constrains the types of knowledge that educators might wish to detect but miss (e.g., temporal dynamics). Knowledge-in-Pieces (KiP) perspectives add to this assessment conversation. D ifferent representations change the knowledge that students reveal (Elby, 20 00; Sherin, 2001; Sherin et al., 2012). We aim to d etermine how students represent physical quantities in space  and across time. Our prior work demonstrated that students understood dynamic equilibrium as involving one physical quantity, not two (Tang & Lira, 2024). As part of a larger intervention, we aimed to determine how students change the spatial and temporal organization of physical quantities during learning. We therefore ask: How do students’ drawings represent temporal changes in the spatial distributions of chemical and electrical potentials across cellular compartments?

## Methods and results

Population. 30 undergraduate students (Juniors and Seniors) from a Research-Intensive University in the US were recruited via email and class announcements. All we re enrolled in a 200-level course titled “Homeostasis: The Physiology of Plants and Animals.” The students were recruited because the course taug ht the content we aimed to assess—in particular the physical quantities of chemical and electrical potentials in dynamic equilibrium. Procedure. Recruitment occurred between Weeks 6 and 15 of the semester—after the first exam and before the final. Participation was voluntary, and the study was conducted independent from students’ coursework. A pretest-posttest design was used to examine the impact of a  brief laboratory intervention. The pretest include d writing, calculation, and drawing tasks. This paper  focuses on a drawing task that asked students to s egment dynamic equilibrium into initial, transient, and final conditi ons. After the pretest, students engaged in a think-aloud protocol while using a multi-representational learning environment (NetLogo) modified from  models similar to presented in Levy and Wilensky (2011). Our model  simulated how a cell generates resting membrane potential (i.e., dynamic equilibrium). During the think-aloud, students reflected on the three equilibrium stage s and explained how they determined whether chemical or electrical potentials were present in the initial and final conditions. The interviewer also asked why, despite students’ predictions, ions did not reach equal concentrations. Students were add itionally asked to make predictions and extract qua ntitative data from the visualization (cf. Parnafes, 2007). A modified version of the assessme nt was then administered with adjusted variables to  serve as a posttest. The study had no control group; the goa l was to help students distinguish between chemical  and electrical potentials and understand how these forc es contribute to dynamic equilibrium. Sessions last ed approximately one hour. Participants received 20 USD upon completion and were then dismissed. Initial codes were borrowed from Ryan and Stieff (2 019). The coding scheme was developed by employing a priori codes and iterative refinement (Saldaña, 2021). Nonparticulate represents the absence of both ion types when students sketch inscriptions other t handrawings (e.g., graphs). One Entity reflects drawings with only positive ions and thus only a chemical potenti al. Two Entities indicates the presence of both ion types and hence the possibility to represent chemical and electrical potentials. Last, Balanced Quantities describes drawings whereby negative ions equaled or exceeded positive ions, thus approaching the depiction of physical qu antities needed to represent the target phenomenon (i.e., dynamic equilibrium). Empirical exemplars were associated with each code to serve as a benchmark reference for cod ers. The coding scheme underwent iterative testing and refinement. It was applied to a sample of pre-and posttest student drawings to assess its reliability. Inconsistencies or disagreements were resolved to agreement via discussion (Krippendorff, 2018). Results. Drawings revealed shifts from students representing one physical quantity to two in balance—a feature critical for understanding dynamic equilibrium (see Figure 1). Further analysis, however, revealed spatial and temporal disorganization in student drawings. At posttest, more students balanced quantities than at pretest, but the canonical way to represent balancing would involve the same number of opposite charges between  fluid compartment. Students, however, often balanced opposite charges within not between compartments and balanced quantities at both initial and final conditions as opposed to balanced quantities within (initial) and between (final). ## Discussion

This study demonstrates that drawing supports our a ssessment of students’ developing knowledge of physical quantities in complex systems. Although students show an initial insensitivity to balancing physical quantities when depicting a quantified complex system, a brief inte rvention can shift them towards representing balanc ed quantities, but with sustained spatial and temporal imprecision. In this case the intervention involved extracting physical quantities at initial, transient, and final conditions (Lira, 2020a). Drawing reveals that students recognize that they need to balance quantities, but that germ ane to their learning is the process of determining  when and where to represent balance. This disorganization remains consistent with KiP approaches as when students select visually salient representational elements (Elby, 2000). The upshot, however, is that many students  readily draw upon computational simulations to improve their representations of quantified complex biological systems.
