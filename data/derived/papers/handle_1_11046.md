## Introduction

A common learning activity in STEM teacher education courses consists of teachers observing classroom practice (video recorded or “live”) and reflecting on various kinds of observed events (e.g., van Es & Sherin, 20 21). We contribute to these research efforts by theorizing a class of classroom events that have heretofore re ceived little attention—namely, teachers’ surprise. As defined here, teacher surprise refers to classroom events that the teacher identifies as containing an unexpected occurrence in the unfolding of the lesson. A teacher’s surprise is thus a kind of discontinuity between a teacher’s expected (projected) unfolding of classroom activity (along instructional dimensions of the activity) and that which obtains in action. In addition, either upon reflection or in action, the teacher judges such a discontinuity to be consequential for the co ntinuation of instruction. We report on several cases of preservice (PST) teacher surprise events in their field teach and their attendant reasoning and consider the relevance of teacher surprise for teacher professional development activities.

## Theoretical framework

In line with research on teacher noticing, we take it that during classroom time teachers attend to, i nterpret, and act upon myriad concurrent events that bear on the conduct of the lesson (van Es & Sherin, 2021). Here we expand on this notion to have teachers noticing their own lived experiences in the classroom, specifically those judged to be of surprising, unexpected nature. Following Gupta et al. (202 2), we contend that these moments reveal teachers’ assumptions—and their underlying ideologies—that are core to the conduct of classroom teaching.

## Data and research context

Data were collected in a  STEM teaching methods class (called Project-Based Instruction, or PBI)  offered in a teacher certification program at a large public Uni versity in the US. The program serves undergraduate students in the natural sciences, engineering, and mathematics who pursue disciplinary academic degrees while simultaneously certifying to teach  high school. PBI is the capstone course in the curricular sequence and it aims to introduce preservice teachers to ambitious pedag ogy in various forms. The course includes a three-day field teach experience in which preservice teachers (in pairs or alone) design and teach a complex less on at a local school. They then engage in reflecting about these experiences and sharing them in whole-class presentations. We report on the 2022 instantiation of the PBI course, for which we specif ied that PSTs’ reflections should focus on what they judged to be successes and failures of their t each. By doing so, we aimed to address issues that the class had raised during the semeste r regarding how various systemic structures impinged on their classroom practices. Reflections were shared in 20-minute, whole-class presentations, followed by 10-minute discussions. In all, we collected some ten hours of video records on those meetings and PSTs’ presentation decks.

## Analysis and results

The research team first watched and logged the vide o records individually, noting in particular events along the interpersonal, group, and collective planes of activity (Rogoff, 1995). We then transcribed the video records and individually open coded the data, specifically with an eye trained on PSTs’ assessments of successes and failures and their reasoning throughout. Further coding and analysis surfaced emergent themes around which PSTs’ narratives of success and failure developed, and teachers’ surprise—expressed directly in preservice teachers’ use of the very word “surprise” or equivalent, such as “unexpected”—became particularly salient, along with attendant explanations. We then asked: What are preservice teachers surprised by? And what reasoning undergirds these events? We consider two categories of teachers’ surprise and expand upon them at conference time. Surprised at students’ abilities/competence Angkor’s computer science lesson to a 10 th grade class integrated computer coding and data analysis, with emphasis on what he called “ethics issues” (e.g., gender salary disparities across careers). When describing a successful aspect of his teaching, Angkor was surprised by stu dents’ unexpectedly inventing problem solutions that he had not envisioned —i.e., he was surprised at students’ competence.  As he put it: “And then… students came up with solutions to questions that I didn't think of. Um… I had some basic ways I thought they would solve it when I created my example solutions, but they ca me up with completely different ways and um, yeah, this led to actually some discussions which I totally wasn't expecting…” (Angkor, 4/18/22; ~8:50). As it turns out, following sound pedagogy, Angkor had designed his lesson to afford multiple solutions, then proceeded to work out various possible coding approaches to the task. By doing so, he sought to anticipate students’ reasoning and likely solution paths, and thus better prepare to conduct the activity. To his surprise, however, students were able to create various alternative and unanticipated solutions, all of which were perfectly valid and which “led to actually some discussions which I ((Angkor)) totally wasn't expecting.” Surprised by students’ interest and care for the lesson content Jackie and Jason were chemistry majors and collaboratively they designed and taught a lesson on the solubility of liquids to a class of 11 th and 12th graders. For the first day of the lesson, students would work  on a short activity to model the concentration of sugar in commercial soda s. Following that, Jackie and Jason planned to show a video that illustrated the same points considered in the modeling activity, “just to reiterate all that.” This is when a breakdown in the technology made the video unavailable and forced the team to improvise; Jackie took on fixing the technology whereas Jason led an impromptu discussion on the modeling activity. Jason described it: [~35:34] “So then I went in front of the class … okay, well do y'all want to talk about like the sod as activity that we just did?… And pretty much half, if not more th an half the class was like… ready to speak. Like I heard three kids just like initially started speaking and then they stopped ‘cuz they were like speaking over each othe r…” He elaborated: “And uh, it was just great to see like they actuall y like cared about what we talked over the, during the first half and they were actually interested to  like continue talking about this kind of stuff… ” We interpret Jackie and Jason to be surprised by students’ emergent interest, particularly as expressed in Jason’s remark about the event: “Uh, it was kind of like a mistake that happened, but I think it was great that it happened, to be honest.” What underlies teachers’ surprise?

Why should PSTs be surprised by their students’ abilities, interest-based participation, and others (e.g., students’ inabilities, to be expounded at conference time) ? We argue that a teacher’s surprise follows from comple x inferences about ongoing classroom events, in the face of expected ones. Fundamentally, such inferences are ideologically based (Louie, 2018), as revealed in assumptions evident in discourses commonly circulating in that classroom. For example, some PSTs’ assertion that “We gave them ((students)) too much information” reflect (1) a transmission mode of instruction in which (2) students are inert in sensemaking and (3) knowledge is reduced to “information.” At conference time, we will articulate how various ideologies of teaching and learning undergird PSTs’ reasoning in each category of teacher surprise.

## Discussion

Reflecting on classroom events is key to teachers’ d eveloping pedagogical knowledge and classroom practice. Reflecting on surprising events seem s particularly relevant in this regard because those often instantiate foundational assumptions about the nature of teaching, knowing, and learning, and which ‘hide’ ideologies of deficit regarding students’ abilities and academic interests.
