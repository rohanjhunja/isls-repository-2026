# Trans-Ecological Disruptions and Pedagogical Interventions in GAI-Supported Collaborative Learning

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

Trans-Ecological Disruptions and Pedagogical Interventions in GAI-Supported Collaborative Learning Yue Han, Wen Qi 24301716@muc.edu.cn, qiiiwen89@outlook.com Minzu University of China, No. 27, South Zhongguancun Street, Haidiandistrict, Beijing, 100081, China.

Abstract: In generative AI (GAI) integrated collaborative learning, conflicts are often misattributed to interpersonal dynamics, ignoring the learning environment's critical role. This mixed-methods case study of 41 middle school students applies a micro-ecological framework to investigate how environmental design flaws trigger trans-ecological disruptions. Using qualitative analysis, Lag Sequential Analysis, Social Network Analysis, and Temporal Exponential Random Graph Models, we robustly reveal that coll aborative conflicts propagate from environmental triggers to group interaction networks via individual cognitive pathways. C ollaborative learning is a critical strategy for knowledge co-construction, heavily influenced by its environment (Garrison, 1999). While Generative Artificial Intelligence (GAI) shows immense potential in enhancing these environments, it introduces unpredictable  dynamics that frequently disrupt group processes(An S et al., 2025; P ark, S et al., 2020;  Yan et al., 2024). Traditionally, these collaborative failures are misattributed to interpersonal friction, leading to interventions that target surface-level symptoms while ignoring structural root causes. Existing literature, highly dependent on discourse coding and probability statistics, fails to explain the complete causal chain of conflict(Feng, S., 2025; Liu, J. et al., 2024). We lack a psychologically grounded understanding of how AI acts as an invisible socio-cognitive force, shaping the emergent organization of group dynamics.

To address this gap, this study investigates the environmental roots of conflict by answering:

• RQ1: Which environmental elements are the key triggers and regulators of collaborative conflicts?

• RQ2: How effective are pedagogical interventions guided by trans-ecological analysis, and how do they

impact the individual and group levels?

## Conceptual framework

This study is anchored in a micro-ecological approach, conceptualizing learning as continuous, reciprocal interactions across nested systemic layers (Environment, Individual, Group) (Borge & Mercier, 2019; B r onfenbr enner, U.,1979). We extend traditional conflict models by introducing Trans-Ecological Disruption: a disruptive event at one level—such as an ambiguous rule at the Environmental Level —does not remain isolated. Instead, it propagates across the ecosystem, triggering changes at other levels.I n thi s study,  we use this fr amewor k specifically to tr ace the pathway of G AI-induced conflicts.

## Method

This m ixed-methods case study was conducted i n a 7th-gr ade collabor ative design class (N= 41).  Six gr oups utilized a text-to-im age GAI m od el, w hile six utilized  trad itional hand craft tools.

•   GAI as an Environmental Factor: Crucially, GAI is an active, dynamic environmental variable. Without

explicit pedagogical scaffolding, its opaque algorithms introduce hidden constraints that significantly disrupt group dynamics.

•   Data & Coding: Multi-modal data included 3-angle video, field notes, and interviews. Behaviors were

coded using a custom 11-category scheme (Kappa > 0.93).

•   Analysis: We divided the ecosystem into three analytical layers. Layer 1 (Qualitative): Mapped causal

chains of disruptive events to pinpoint root causes. Layer 2 (Quantitative): Lag Sequential Analysis (LSA) and Temporal Network Analysis (TNA) at 3-minute intervals compared pre/post-intervention dynamics. Temporal Exponential Random Graph Models (TERGM) captured longitudinal dependencies of network evolution.

Conflicts in GAI environments are dynamic symptoms of environmental friction, not merely interpersonal disputes(Somaraju et al., 2024). By integrating qualitative tracing with TERGM, we explain how minor design flaws escalate into group failures via a self-reinforcing "Matthew Effect." Our mixed-methods validation (LSA, TNA, TERGM) makes three core contributions: (1) systematically linking conflicts to environmental design flaws;

(2) isolating individual cognition (the GAI-induced IM-centric model) as the critical mediator of trans-ecological

propagation; and (3) proving that interventions targeting environmental rules effectively restructure fragile networks into resilient collaborative states.

## Implications

(1) Educational: Educators must transition from managing interpersonal friction to acting as "ecosystem engineers."

Effective conflict resolution requires diagnosing environmental root causes (e.g., clarifying tool properties, redesigning task rules) rather than treating behavioral symptoms.
