# Enhancing Team-Based Medical Simulations: Learning Through Reflection with Analytics and AI Tools

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Simulation-based learning has become a cornerstone of healthcare education, fostering essential skills like communication, teamwork or decision-making in safe, controlled environments. However, participants’ reflection on simulations often rely on subjective recollections, limiting their effectiveness in promoting learning. This symposium explores how multimodal analytics and AI can enhance simulation-based education by automating teamwork analysis data, providing structured feedback, and supporting reflective practices. The papers examine real-time analytics for closed-loop communication in cardiac arrest simulations, multimodal data use to refine feedback in ICU nursing simulations, generative AI-powered chatbots facilitating nursing students' interpretation of multimodal learning analytics dashboards, and culturally sensitive, AI-based scenarios for Breaking Bad News in an Indian context. Collectively, these contributions highlight the transformative potential of using data and AI-enhanced solutions, emphasizing personalization, cultural sensitivity, and human-centered design, and invite dialogue on the pedagogical, technological and ethical implications of introducing data-based practices and AI-based tools in medical education.

## Introduction

Current teaching and training methods in the healthcare sector, such as medical and nurs ing training, often involve (team-based) simulations, followed by reflection sessions in which participants a nalyze their individual and teamwork performance. This type  of training allows learners to practice their teamwor k or collaborative skills, such as communication, teamwork, leadership, and decision-making, which are essential for providing safe and effective patient care.

Team-based simulation involves groups of learners workin g together in a simulated environment to manage a clinical scenario and can be used to train a both students and healthcare professionals, including nurses, physicians, and emergency medical technicians. Teamwork can encourage individuals to externalise their personal perspectives to reach a shared understanding (Häkkinen, et al., 2017). Studies have demonstrated the positive effects of teamwork, such as increased knowledge of  complex pathologies and p atient profiles, problem identification, clinical reasoning, coordination, a nd communication skills (Considine et al., 2020). T he scenarios used in team-based simulation are often complex and challenging, and t hey are designed to mimic real-life situations as closely as possible. Team-based simulation offers a safe and controlled envir onment for learners to practice their skills and make mistakes without put ting patients at risk (Ballangrud et al., 2014). Th e fidelity of the simulation can vary, from low-fidelity simulations using simple manikins to high-fidelity simulations using sophisticated computer-controlled simulators.

Regardless of the fidelity, the key to effective te am-based simulation is the debriefing session that follows the simulation. Reflection after simulation  sessions is valuable in learning patient-care behaviour and stimulating knowledge consolidation but of ten uses limited data (e.g., video recordings).  During the debriefing, learners reflect on their performance, identify are as for improvement, and receive feedback from their  peers and instructors. Effective reflection, grounded in obje ctive recollections o f (team) activities, is crucial for students’ learning and professionals’ development, especially  in environments where collaboration is key. Stress ful teamwork situations, cognitive dissonance or commun ication and coordination challenges can lead to err ors and poor recollection of collaborative efforts, obstruc ting meaningful reflection and learning from valuab le experiences from the simulations. While viewed as highly valuable for learning, reflection sessions are frequently compromised by their reliance on participants’ subjective recollections and the f act that it is difficult to use data from the simulation as an objective display of the performance under the simulation. Collocated teamwork and mediated by digital technol ogies form an effective strategy for team skills training. While there is abundant evidence of simulation and teamwork’s impo rtance in learning, ways of automating measures of teamwork, automated feedback  and support for reflection are scarce, especially in authentic settings (Echeverria, et al., 2024). Learning designs that embed multimodal analytics and AI-based tools offer opportunities to facilitate sensemaking of multimodal data related to such phenomena. These techniques and approaches allow the automatic or semi-automatic capturing, analysis, and distillation of data about collaborators’ speech, actions, and interactions.

The contributions in this symposium emphasize the importance understanding how communication and teamwork skills are being reflected on high-stakes environments, such as ICU simulations, resus citation training, and Breaking Bad News (BBN) scenarios, and how differen t analytics and AI-powered designs can support this reflection. Each study also highlights the role of structured personalized feedback, whether through a utomated analytics, multimodal data, or AI-powered tools, in fostering reflective practices and skill development. The symposium shares a common focus on using advanc ed technology, particularly different types of artificial intelligence (e.g., LLM, learning analyt ics), to enhance the potential of simulation-based learning in medical and nursing education. Leveraging AI for automated evaluation of closed-loop communication in VRbased acute care team training Vitaliy Popov, Yaxuan Li, Kunpeng Huang, Alanson Sample, Bryan Harmer, James Cooke, Michael Cole

## Introduction

High-reliability teams need frequent training opportunities and precision feedback to develop complex skills such as communication (Mathieu et al., 2019; Baker et al., 2006). In healthcare specifically, resuscitation teams utilize closed-loop communication (CLC). Closed-loop communication (CLC) is a structured three-stage protocol that ensures clear information exchange and shared understanding among team members, vital for effective teamwork in high-reliability organizations including healthcare, ens uring that critical information is communicated and confirmed, reinforcing cohesive teamwork. Despite t he widespread recognition of CLC’s importance and i ts promotion by organizations like the American Heart Association (Cheng et al., 2020), our ability to systematically assess and improve CLC quality remains limited by subjective, non-standardized evaluation methods. Simulation-based training is the most common method used to teach CLC to healthcare teams. However, evaluating CLC during these simulations relies heav ily on subjective instructor observations, manual v ideo reviews, or self-reported surveys, which limits t he efficiency, precision of feedback, and scalabili ty of this approach. The assessment of closed-loop communication (CLC) in clinical simulation training presents a complex Instructor Noticing challenge. Through the lens of Teacher Noticing framework (van Es & Sherin, 2002):  1) instructors must attend to multiple team interactio ns simultaneously, which limits their ability to no tice and interpret CLC patterns (attending challenge); 2) lack of standardized frameworks and recall bias limit instructors' ability to draw consistent inferences about CLC qua lity (interpreting challenge); and 3) without systematic and timely CLC assessment, instructors struggle to crea te targeted learning opportunities that address spe cific communication gaps (shaping challenge). To  address these challenges, our research goal was to develop and evaluate an automated system that support all three  dimensions of teacher noticing: attending (by high lighting relevant CLC instances), interpreting (by providing an analytical framework for quality assessment), and shaping (by enabling rapid, targeted feedback based on observed patterns). This study addresses two research questions: 1. How can an LLM-based CLC assessment be designed to provide targeted feedback? 2. How do instructors perceive and integrate LLM-based CLC assessment into their evaluation and debriefing practices?

## Methods

We performed a three-part study that included: system development, valid ation, and evaluation. First, we developed a GPT-powered system (Generative Pre-trained Transformer, a class of NLP models develope d by OpenAI) that detects, computes, and classifies C LC instances. Leveraging the GPT-4 large language model (LLM), the pipeline accepts transcripts of clinical  training sessions as inputs, predicts CLC instance s, and generates a report with selected examples of CLC and performance metrics. This approach eff ectively identifies call-outs, predicts corresponding check-backs, and classifies the quality of each instance based on the check-back’s specificity. Second, we conducted a technica l accuracy assessment by comparing the tool’s predi ctions against human annotations. Three annotators with background in emergency medical services independently rated 108 good CLCs, 105 poor CLCs, and 102 unresponded CLCs. Performance metrics were calculated using standard information retrieval techniques, computing Precision, Recall, and Inter-rater Reliability for Call-Outs and CheckBacks to quantify the AI’s detection accuracy and a nnotator agreement.  Finally, we conducted an instructor evaluation study involving eleven cardiac arrest instructors from three training sites in the Midwest, United States. In 45-60-minute interviews, participants received a CLC over view and shared their current evaluation practices before observing and assessing a 10-minute VR cardiac arrest simulation using their typ ical teaching methods. The participants then reviewed an automated CLC rep ort generated by the tool and discussed how it infl uenced their evaluation of team communication quality and potential feedback approaches. Finally, participants reflected on the tool’s impact on their noticing of team comm unication and provided feedback on its usefulness, usability, and potential improvements.  We conducted thematic analysis of part icipant feedback, evaluating perceived accuracy and utility of our automated CLC assessment approach.

## Results and discussion

Our findings demonstrate the potential of AI-assisted assessment tools to provide actionable feedback that can be used in simulation-based training. The proposed system achieved techni cal performance (78.90% precision for call-outs, 74.27% for check-backs), approaching human expert benchmarks with a 5% dif ference. The system’s ability to quantify communication patterns and prov ide categorized examples of varying quality (good/poor/unresponded) effectively scaffolds instr uctors' assessment practices, which c urrently rely on unstructured note-taking and live observations. Instructors prefer using AI-generated insights as reference points rather thandefinitive assessments. They suggested adding visual charts describing CLC frequencies and  options for viewing transcripts of all CLC instances.

These findings contribute to CSCL literature by dem onstrating how automated assessment can support instructors and learners in complex collaborative l earning scenarios.  This is the initial step in developing and validating our approach. Future work will involve e xperimental studies and potentially expand to other  critical care scenarios, such as trauma resuscitation and operating room procedures. AI-Supported Reflection in Nursing Education: Student Perceptions of a GenAI-Powered Chatbot and Multimodal Teamwork Analytics Dashboards Vanessa Echeverria, Mikaela Milesi, Ariel Jin, Linxuan Zhao, Riordan Alfredo, Lixiang Yan, Dragan Gasevic, Roberto Martinez-Maldonado

## Background and aims

Team-based learning (TBL) in nursing education immerses students in realistic, simulated clinical environments, providing a safe space to develop critical skills such as communication, teamwork, and task management (Maran & Glavin, 2003). Reflection is ess ential in TBL, as healthcare professionals often fa ce high-stakes situations requiring effective teamwork. Reflection helps stud ents internalize and apply cognitive and social ski lls by identifying areas for improvement and delineating actions for future practice. This reflection typically occurs after the simulation (i.e., reflection-on-action, Schön, 1992), allowing students to assess t heir performance and its impact on patient outcomes. Enabling students with personal reflection spaces could enhance learning outcomes. To support this reflection, providing evidence of t eam dynamics from the simulation would be beneficia l, as it may spark meaningful insights and discussions using this evidence. Multimodal learning analytics dashboards (MMLADs) p rovide evidence and a comprehensive view of team performance and interactions. By capturing, an alyzing, and visualizing students’ learning traces and behaviors, MMLADs can facilitate meaningful reflect ion (Giannakos et al., 2022). When paired with GenAI-powered chatbots, these tools further enhance indep endent reflection by offering grounded explanations  and improving interpretability (Okonkwo, 2021). However, as GenAI-powered chatbots are relatively new in educational contexts, their role in supporting refl ection, particularly when integrated with MMLADs, r emains largely unexplored. Building on prior work using MM LADs for reflection on teamwork (Authors, 2024), th is study explores students’ perceptions of u sing a GenAI-Chatbot to support the interpretation of MMLADs and facilitate reflection. Specifically, it addresses t he question: How do students perceive the role of the GenAI-Chatbot in facilitating reflection?

## Methods

The learning scenario was a team simulation where f our students, role-playing as primary and secondary nurses, worked together to manage patient care. An AI-powered multimodal learning analytics (MMLA) system captured team dynamics using lapel microphones for audio and  location tags to track movements and body orientat ion (Authors, 2024). We generated four visualizations a nd developed a contextualised GenAI-Chatbot to support the interpretation of visualisations and answer scenari o-related prompts (Fig. 1).  Of 134 third-year nursing students (33 teams), 23 voluntarily participated in post-hoc reflection sessions, which included exploration of the MMLAD and use of GenAI-Chatbot, and providing their perceptions using a Likert scale (1–5; strongly disagree-strongly agree; GenAI-Chatbot could enhance my reflective practices) and open-ended responses (GenAI-Chatbot helped you to reflect on your team’s performance and nursi ng practice? Why or why not?). Quantitative data were summarized with medians and percentages, while qual itative responses were analyzed using a thematic an alysis approach and open-coding (Braun & Clarke, 2012) to identify emerging themes. Two key themes emerged: the GenAI-Chatbot’s role in facilitating reflection and limitations in supporting reflective practices.

## Findings

GenAI-Chatbot’s role was perceived positively by many students, with 95% agreeing and strongly agreeing that it could enhance their reflective practices (Median  = 5). Qualitative analysis revealed that 11 studen ts found the GenAI-Chatbot helpful for facilitating reflection, with six emphasizing ts ability to explain what went well, what went wrong, and why, shifting their perspective tow ard constructive reflection. Five students highligh ted its usefulness in generating actionable suggestions for  improvement, while three appreciated its role in interpreting visualizations and abstract concepts like “prioriti zation.” However, four students expressed reservati ons, noting limitations, such as its inability to capture personal experiences, lack of response depth for detailed analysis, and challenges in framing meaningful questions without guidance. ## Conclusion and implications

This study explored students’ perceptions of using GenAI-Chatbot and MMLADs to support reflection on nursing practice. GenAI chatbots can offer support on reflective practices by providing actionable feedback and fostering constructive self-criticism. However, additional strategies and mechanisms are required to facilitate and promote deeper levels of reflection. This work represents a step toward scaling reflective practices in healthcare education through the integration of MMLADs and GenAI-powered solutions. ## designing a personalized and context sensitive tutor for Breaking Bad News

Olga Chernikova, Xuechen Zhang, Frank Fischer, Meltem Akoyak-Yildiz

## Background and aims

Simulation-based learning represents a powerful pedagogical to ol in higher education, particularly in medical training, as it provides controlled environments fo r practicing complex, ethically and culturally sens itive tasks like Breaking Bad News (BBN, see  Mahendiran et al., 2023). It offers a practical alt ernative to real interactions with standardized patients, which are limited by et hical concerns, high costs, and resource-intensive training requirements. However, creating content for such simulations, enabling continuous practice opportunities remains a challenging task. The high incidence of fatal dis eases, inequitable access to health care, and socio economic disparities in India, as well as high diversity in population regarding  cultures, religions, and vary ing patient expectations, can complicate BBN formal training and require a lot of culturally specific knowledge and experience from the healthcare professionals (e.g.,  Martis & Westhues, 2013). Internationally recogniz ed communication protocols might introduce even more cultural dilemmas (e.g., Holmes & Illing, 2021). On the one hand, recent technological developments in artificial intelligence (AI) models might contribute to generating any amount of practice scenarios, of fering a promising solution, but, on the other hand, many of these models might inherit and propagate gender and cultural biases, disadvantaging the minority population (Shrestha & Das, 2022). In this study we aim at (1) contributing to basic f undamental research on cultural aspects of BBN in India to create information that can be used to tra in Large Language Models (LLM) in creating realisti c BBN scenarios; and (2) identifying features of typical and atypical situations that can be encountered in BBN to address the possibility of introducing typicality scaffold in BBN simulations (see Fischer et al., 2022). Typical and atypical BBN scenarios are defined by patients´ emotional (e.g., distress, d enial or acceptance), cognitive (e.g., understanding and engagement in decision making), a nd socio-demographic (e.g., age, gender, religion, education) attributes, all of which shape patient expectations and communication preferences. These attributes are crucial for identifying culturally sensitive approa ches in BBN (e.g., Foronda, 2008), ensuring that he althcare providers can address diverse patient needs effectively.

To address the goals of the study we rely on a professional conversation competence model (Gartmeier et al., 2015), focusing on establishing supportive relationships, advancing problem solutions, and str ucturing conversations transparently; and in-depth structured interviews with Indiandoctors, wh o share their experience and observations. The interviews focus on patient e motional reactions, and individualized communicatio n strategies, used by the doctors and therefore contr ibute to cultural sensitivity of BBN scenarios, pro moting knowledge, understanding, and respect, which leads to effective communication. We explore two primary research questions: (1) What are the features of ty pical and atypical BBN settings in Indian context c onsidering emotional, cognitive, and socio-demographic attributes. (2) To what extent can AI d riven tutor prompted with expert information create realistic typical and atypical BBN scenarios and support developing learners´ skills.

## Methods

The study involved interviews with four experienced  Indian physicians (three males, one female) from a  South Indian multi-specialty tertiary care hospital, averaging 19 year s of experience as physicians and 18.5 years in delivering bad news. Selected for their proficiency in English and expertise in BBN within Indian cultural context, these physicians provided insights into typical and  atypical experiences with patients. Using thematic  analysis (see Braun & Clarke, 2006), the study examines key themes in th eir communication practices  (e.g., patient reactions), offering an in-depth look at the nuances of breaking bad news in a n Indian medical context.  The insights from the interviews were translated into p rompts for an LLM and several typical and atypical scenarios were generated. The experts have evaluated quality and authenticity of typical and atypical scenarios created by LLM, which can be embedded in simulation-based learning environment.

## Findings and implications

To outline the typical and atypical cases in BBN scenarios, research can focus not only on disease characteristics, but also on the emotional and cognitive patient dimensions. Many identified sub-themes (e.g., anxiety and distress, emotional outbursts, denial, suspicion, and aggress iveness) aligned with other research results on doc tor-patient communication issues (e.g., Sander, 2010). This stu dy first examined typical and atypic al BBN scenarios in the Indian healthcare system. It then explored the use of LLMs for generating BBN scenarios.  By interview ing experienced Indian physicians, we collected 9 scena rios capturing both typical and atypical BBN dimension confinements to inform the development of AI-generated training materials. We practiced a well-defined prompting strategy to train the model in creating s imilar scenarios  to represent different cultural as well as cognitive and emotional attributes of the patients and reduce occurances of gender or cultural bias. A dditionally, this study discusses possible uses of obtained data  to develop prompts for individualized feedback bas ed on professional conversation competence model and SPIK ES protocol, as well as prompts to support peer fee dback on BBN performance in culturally sensitive context.

We conclude that AI-powered adaptive simulations offer a promising solu tion, allowing for personalized feedback and scenario progression that  aligns with each learner's specific needs. The fin dings underscore a significant opportunity: AI-driven adaptive s imulations specifically trained to address nuanced cultural contexts could set a new standard in simulation-based learning, particularly in medical education, by creating highly effective, culturally attuned training environments for sensitive communication tasks. Sense-making through Reflection supported by Multimodal Dashboards in Team-based Simulations in Nursing Education Daniel Sanchez, Crina Damşa, Rachelle Esterhazy, Hugh von Arnim, Lena Heyn, Edda Johansen, Susanne Olesen, Ellen Larsen, Carmen Louwerens

## Background and aims

Collocated teamwork, where participants share the s ame physical space while using advanced technologie s to solve complex problems, remains prevalent across va rious domains. Hence, research about how automated feedback using teamwork analytics generated through  computational means can assist students and teache rs is crucial for good learning and effective guidance when applying knowledge into situations in practice (Cukurova, 2018). Simulations of teamwork offer a bridge betwe en theory and practice, providin g students with realistic environments to develop critical clinical skills. Reflection after these sessions is essential for learning patient-care behavior and consolidating knowledge but relies on limited data, such as video recordings (Verkuyl et al., 2020). A significant gap exists in understanding how students and learners interpret and utilize feedback derived from diverse data sources presented in multimodal formats within these environments, as feedback's value lies in how well learners comprehend and apply it (Winstone et al., 2017). Although the research in this area is relatively small, it is growing. For example, Zhao et al. (202 2) integrated positional and audio data in nursing simulations to distinguish team communications, enhancing our understanding of team dynamics under stress. Similarly, Yan et al. (2023) found that physiological synchrony and arousal offer insights into how stress levels aff ect team dynamics and individual performance, and a need to explore physiological measures in educational practices. This study investigates the gap within the framewor k of deliberative practice (Ericsson, 2008). Deliberative practice in nursing simulations involves timely feedback, specific goal setting, and opportunities for repetitive exercises —key components essential in the high-stakes environment of ICU simulations, where nuanced understanding and skill application are critical.The central research question is: How do nursing students make sense of and use multimodal feedback in ICU si mulations? This inquiry aims to deepen our understa nding of feedback utilization in high-fidelity nursing education and training.

## Conceptualizations and methods

This study was conducted with students enrolled in a master-level nursing course at a Norwegian university. Thirteen students were divided into three simulation teams  to ensure optimal participation and engagement. Each participant was assigned a specific  role such as Leading Nurse; Supporting Nurses; Hand over Responsible; and Observer. Ethical approval and informed consent wer e obtained in accordance with GDPR and the universi ty's research guidelines. After each simulation session,  a debriefing was conduc ted where instructors facilitated student reflection, and critical thinking about the ir performance. Before the second simulation, stude nts engaged in a data-based reflection session, using the multimodal inputs from the first simulation as discussion prompts. The data included video recordings, audio captured with unidirectional microphones, and physiological data using a medical-grade sensor (eq02+ LifeMonitor). The audio recordi ngs were transcribed and coded in collaboration with an experienced nurse to identify moments when students applied a communication protocol (closed-loop communication). These moments were captured into vi deo clips with captions and paired with coded trans cripts. Heart rate and net acceleration were visualized as stress indicators, including pivotal moments (from manual log files) of the scenario. These visualizations also incorporated baseline heart rate estimates from pre-simulation data to identify potential stress moments during the scenario enactment. Both the debriefing and reflection sessions were recorded, and a thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 20 06), summarized in Table 1, was conducted on the transcr ipts. It identified and categorized meaningful inst ances concerning feedback. This comprehensive approach afforded a nuanced understanding of student interactions with and interpretations of the multimodal feedback in the reflection.

## Findings

The findings reveal the nuanced reactions of nursin g students to multimodal feedback in simulation, te am-based learning environments. The data session demonstrate d that students gained a deeper understanding and appreciation for precise, goal-oriented feed back, preferring actionable insights with clear dir ectives for skill improvement. This actionable feedback was effective ly integrated during the debriefing session, showin g deliberate implementation by students in the second  simulation. Moreover, evidence-based feedback highlighted discrepancies between students' self-assessments and actual performance, underscoring th e dual role of multimodal feedback in reinforcing teacher observations and challenging students' self-perceptions.

## Conclusion and implications

The results suggest that multimodal feedback, when aligned with specific performance goals, of individual and team and provided contextually, enhances students' comprehension and application of feedback. It also fosters the development of refined self-assessment capabilities, aligning with the principles o f deliberative practice that emphasize timely, specific feedback and repeated pr actice for skill proficiency. Additionally, multimo dal feedback can challenge and reshape students' self-perceptions, leading to more accurate self-and team-awareness. This study advocates for ongoing research to refine  the integration of multimodal feedback within simu lationbased learning, enhancing the feedback process and, ultimately, student learning outcomes.
