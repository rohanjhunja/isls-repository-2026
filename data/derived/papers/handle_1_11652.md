# Interpreting Student Nonparticipation in Teams

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
We explored nonparticipation in teamwork through an analysis of 42 studies about equitable teamwork, which revealed two prominent perspectives. The dominant view blames individuals, labeling nonparticipation as social loafing—a term we contest. The second perspective views nonparticipation as a result of structural barriers, emphasizing equity but ignoring personal agency. We propose a Domain Theory-based approach that considers nonparticipation to be a complex issue, encouraging balance between individual responsibility and structural factors and reevaluation of how student nonparticipation is perceived.

## Background

Frustration with nonparticipating teammates is a common source of student dissatisfaction in team-based learning (Borrego et al., 2013; Lacher & Biehl, 2019). How w e characterize nonparticipation or reduced particip ation in teamwork has implications for how we remedy the problem, and indeed for our understanding of student behaviors and motivations. In our recent scoping review (Moff at et al., 2025), two distinct characterizations of  student nonparticipation emerged. One treats participation, or contribution, as a responsibility that individuals may neglect (e.g., Jansson & Cort, 2020). The other regards par ticipation as a right that may be denied to certain  students through the reproduction of social hierarchies with in the team (e.g., Quinn et al., 2020). Research in  the former strand often invokes the long-standing psychological concept of social loafing  to diagnose reduced or nonparticipation, a term we argue is unhelpfully loaded with connotations of laziness. Having made this diagnosis, such research often fails to explore the causes of the observed lack of participation, considering instead means of mitigating it through task and team design that inc orporate visibility, accountability, incentives, and penalties (Karau & Williams, 1993). Research in the latter st rand, by contrast, seeks to understand what structural  barriers lead to a team member not participating fully. In focusing on detecting and mitigating such barriers, this research often fails to consider individual factors and deci sions that may result in stu dents not seizing available opportunities for participation. Herein, we critique both positions, drawing on insights f rom the scoping review to illustrate how these central terms are used and the broader discussions in which they are situated. We argue that nonparticipation in teamwork is a complex phen omenon that should be approached and analyzed using  a multi-dimensional framework that accounts for intra-individual, interactional, and wider structural com ponents, and we sketch the foundations of one such approach.

## Theoretical framework

We argue that these contradicting perspectives on nonparticipation stem from their origins in opposite sides of the agency/structure debate in sociological theory and,  thus, that any framework seeking to reconcile them must acknowledge both agentive and structural components  in the totality of social psychological reality. S uch a resource is found in Domain Theory, developed by La yder (1997) through a synthesis of theories from cl assical, modern, and post-modern traditions. Layder proposes four interacting  domains of social life, termed psychobiography, situated interaction, settings, and contextual resources.  Though separable for analytical purposes, th ese domains are closely interconnected through discourses and social  practices. They together describe a trajectory from the micro, intrapsychic level of personality characteristics and motivations to the macro, structural level of social forces that constrain th e actions available to the individual. In this r esearch, our goal is to reinterpret both nonpar ticipation perspectives using the lens of Domain Theory, proposing an approach to studying nonparticipation in teamwork that recognizes the complexity of students as both agents and subjects.

## Methodology

This research explores a subset of findings from ou r scoping literature review (Moffat et al., 2025, w here full methodological details can be found), undertaken to  explore broader issues of equity and equality in t eamwork. Briefly, the review was conducted to map the conceptual landscape around the terms equity and equality in teamrelated academic discourse. We searched Scopus, Web of Science, ERIC, and PsycInfo with terms constructed to find team-or group-related terms and mentions of equity or equality (or derived forms such as equitable) in titles or abstracts. The date range was set from January 2017 to December 2021 to focus on recent academic discourse, and 2159 results were identified at the search stag e. Through filtering detailed in the full article, the data set was reduced to 42 publications  for review. These publications were subject to a th ematic analysis, beginning with inductive coding of explicit mentions of equity or equality and related concepts not overtly linked to equalness or fairness. One prominent facet of team equity that e merged was labeled participation, which, as noted, treated nonparticipation in teamwork from two quite different perspectives. Participation as individual responsibility: Social loafing From this perspective, teamwork requires that all t eam members do their “fair share” (Sithole, 2018, p. 14; Tenenberg, 2019, p. 1703) and “pull their weight” (Cooney & Darcy, 2020, p. 8). The implication is that oversight is required to ensure that team members comply: the term “accountability” is often used in this regard (Cooney & Darcy, 2020, pp. 1 –2, 10; Theobald et al., 2017, pp. 2 –3). This perspective emphasizes the agency of the individual, the autonomy in their decision to parti cipate, and the possibility that they may choose no t to. This latter case is often analyzed using the terms social loafing and free-riding. There is a substantial body of literature on both concepts; they appear in twelve and nine sc oping review publications, respectively. Clearly, t hese terms can provide a  useful, known, social psychological phenomenon with  which to label instances of reduced or nonparticipation. However, this labeling is often d one with little discussion of the terms regarding t heir appropriateness for describing student behaviors as observed by researchers or reported by students participating in teamwork studies. An example is when Lacher and Biehl (2019) state: “Most prominently [in past stud ies], students find ‘social loafing’ to be incredibly cha llenging—this is a behavior seen by team  members where individuals do not do a fair share of project work” (p. 36). This passage is followed by citations to studies in which students have indeed reported nonparticipation as a serious problem, but the application of the term social loafing is made by the researchers rather than emerging from the words of the study participants. Participation as an inalienable right: Barriers and constraints Another approach to nonparticipation emerged in the  scoping review, though it was less easily detectab le as it lacks comparable keywords. It is sometimes characte rized by the use of the term equity, where the individual responsibility perspective prefers equality; for example, Quinn et al. (2020) write, “Increased student agency… is insufficient for the creation of a supportive and e quitable learning environment, where each student h as the opportunity to freely pursue their own path … Equitable participation must be actively built into  curricula to eliminate implicit inequities …” (p. 11).  This discourse sees participation as a right that m ay be denied to individuals by intra-team power dynamics that reproduce societal-level social hierarchies. The mechanics of this process are explored in Nieswandt et al. (2020) using the notion of status characteristics, observable markers of social prestige that differentially influence the action opportunities available to team members (Berger et al., 1972; Henderson, 2024). Status characteristics can be task-specific (i.e., specific status characteristics) or  related to societal-level performance expectations (i.e., diffuse statu s characteristics), such as the belief that White, male scientists and engineers are inherently more competent than women or people of color. However, extant literature suggests that when individuals in a task-oriented group are distinguished by external diffus e or specific status characteristics, power and prestige orders form, sh aping opportunities to contribute to the task (i.e., action opportunities), performance outputs, performance evaluations, and patterns of influence (Simpson et al., 2012). Towards a more holistic analysis of nonparticipation Neither of these perspectives on nonparticipation i s sufficient alone. Equity-focused studies of students from historically marginalized populations might draw na turally on the “participation as right” perspective, while studies of team effectiveness might focus on team members’ responsibility to participate. However, it is unlikely that team effectiveness studies won’t involve parti cipants who face structural barriers, and equity-driven studies may involve participants whose disinclination to pa rticipate could include an intrinsically selfish component. A framework is needed that enables the analysis of no nparticipation from both perspectives; Domain Theor y supports such a framework for such a comprehensive analysis. It enables the simultaneous analysis of individuallevel “lifeworld elements” and structural-level “system elements” (Layder, 1997, p. 78), and our poster will expand on examples of such analyses through the lenses of an individual’s psychobiography, settings, contextual resources, and situated activity. Our essential argument is that ap proaching the phenomenon of nonparticipation using Domain Theory as a framework can help prevent reductive conclusions and imputed motivations. This framework provides a means of considering individua l-level, psychological factors as well as societally-derived, structural factors in understanding why students do not participate. The holistic perspective that a framework such as this provides has the power to enhance the analy sis of team processes and improve outcom es for all team members.
