# Engineering and Robotics Learning in Haitian-Dominican Communities: An Analysis of Positionalities as Latine and Haitian Researchers in Multilingual Spaces

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Researchers designed learning experiences to engage Haitian-Dominican youths’ curiosities about engineering and robotics through multilingual/multidialectal and multimodal lessons in the Dominican Republic. Researchers and community organizers who identified as Dominican, Haitian, and Latine conducted an analysis of their experiences and positionalities as multilingual/multidialectal researchers by doing a collaborative inquiry. Researchers considered how their identities shaped the design and execution of the program and how their positionalities framed their perceptions of learning. Findings suggest that researchers perceived multilingual/multidialectal learning environments for engineering and robotics as supportive of (1) learners using their linguistic and cultural practices to develop engineering designs, (2) mutual learning relationships between researchers and students, and (3) interactions that sustained researchers' identities. This work exemplifies how multilingual/multidialectal researchers' identities frame how they view the co-construction of STEM learning and relationships with multilingual/multidialectal learners and their communities.

## Introduction

The Dominican Republic (DR) and Haiti share an island, Hispaniola, and have a complex and tension-rich sociopolitical relationship. The socio-historical conditions in the region, reflected in t he classrooms, promote the illusion of ‘standard Spanish’ over Dominican-Spanish and Haitian-Creole in educational spaces (Medford, 2022). In the DR, Spanish is the dominant language, and thus, schooling is done in Spanish. However, most people speak a unique variety called Dominican-Spanish or Dominican in their everyday communicatio ns.  Outside of the DR and among other Spanish-speaking groups, Dominican-Spanish is sometimes met with social prejudice (Suá rez Büdenbender, 2009) or held in low esteem (Bullock & Toribio, 2008, p. 179). Haitians in the DR adopt t he local linguistic practices to communicate, sometimes at t he expense of their own practices to assimilate and  avoid linguistic prejudice and violence (Medford, 2022).

Dominican and Haitian communities have cultural and  linguistic practices and ways of knowing (González et al., 2011) rarely recognized in formal  education spaces, as are other historically underr epresented groups in engineering (Wilson‐Lopez et al., 2016). These practices are important for learning in STEM and hardly ever tapped into in many educational spaces (Lizárr aga & Gutiérrez, 2018; Nasir & Cook, 2009). There i s great potential to draw on transnational ways of knowing to design science and engineering learning opportun ities for young people. Such is the case of Nehm and colleagu es (2018)’s design of an international science educ ation outreach programming in the DR to increase Dominica n and Dominican-American students’ engagement in geosciences (2008).

As education researchers, our linguistic and cultur al identities and onto-epistemologies frame how we see learning happening and the relationships we co-construct with our participants and their communities. This is especially salient when working with communities that face socio-political tensions within and in larger national or international contexts. The positionalities of e ducation researchers, the lens through which we vie w the world due to social and political experiences, influence research and praxis, particularly how researchers c ommunicate about their work and their participants. Therefore, to imagine new possibilities for what it means to do engineering and robotics, a reflexive examination of one’s own identities as researchers and practitioners is imperative (Huang, 2015). We collaboratively explore how, as members of the research team, we perceived the role our linguistic and cultural identities might play in the development and implementation of a multilingual/multidialectal engineering and robotics learning experience for primary and middle school-aged children in Haitian-Dominican communities in the DR. We consider how our identities shape our pedagogical practices and our perceptions of the implications of this work for students’ learning.

## Conceptual framework

This work is grounded in Community-based participatory design research, translanguaging, and analysis of researcher positionality. Community-based participatory design research gives attention  to the relational aspects of education research that influence learni ng among participants and researchers and requires specific context-dependent moves from researchers, especially in regions with complex socio-historical conditions (Bang et al., 2010; Bang & Vossoughi, 2016). In their exp loration of  how science educational experiences can be designed to enhance science learning within Native American communities, Bang and Colleagues highlight  the importance of  elevating community members and partners as co-designers. They ensured that learning environments were shaped by those with local lived experiences and cultural expertise by “seeing the larger sociohistorical frame that participants are working with ” (p. 578).  Building in this framework, we also co nsider the socio-historical realities of the communities we work wit h, acknowledging how historical narratives, linguis tic practices, and cultural epistemologies influence le arning experiences. Through sustained partnerships with these communities, we have co-designed educational experiences that are not only contextually relevant but also affirm the knowledge systems and identities of the learners involved. In the context of the United States, researchers ha ve found that multilingual/multidialectal learners see English, the dominant language in the U.S., as the language for science and engineering learning while  non-dominant languages are seen as not associated with scientific and technical education (Pérez & Johnson, 2020). Translanguaging theory, proposed by Ofelia García, challenges these boundaries between languages, emphasizing the dynamic use of students’ linguistic repertoire while recognizing that multilingual/multidialectal students draw on their repertoires for meaning making and learning (García, 2009). Translanguaging serve as our framework for designing engineering learning content and environm ents and as a lens for understanding interactions w ith learners in our reflections.

Through the lens of the particular identities and o nto-epistemological backgrounds that frame how we see learning and relationships in the communities w e work in (Secules et al., 2021), we made sense of language, engineering, and robotics. An analysis of p ositionality emphasizes the reflexivity of doing co mmunity-based research. Collaborative inquiry allowed us to think  together about how we see ourselves in this work, while autoethnographic elements enabled us to reflect on our positionalities and identiti es within STEM education (Secules et al., 2021). Researchers' cultural and l inguistic practices provide insider knowledge but d o not guarantee politicized trust, the continuous negotia tion of “mutual trust and racial solidarity” betwee n the participants and researchers, or sustained engagement in the progr ams we develop (Vakil et al., 2016). The program design considers the nuances of education i n the DR. It was through partnerships with Dominica n and Haitian-Dominican grassroots organizers that the research group was able to initiate and sustain relationships with the participants and the communities.

## Methods

The learning experience involves a team of undergra duate and graduate curriculum designers and researc hers, a primary investigator and engineering professor, community organizers, educators, and learners in the DR. Part of the research team’s self-described identities are depicted in Table 1.

## Context

The research team developed a multilingual, Spanish and Haitian-Creole, engineering and robotics curriculum for elementary and middle school age students in the DR. The undergraduate student researchers developed t he lessons and curated materials aimed at students in the DR.  Each undergraduate researcher facilitated at least one of five lessons: 1.) civil engineering-building design, 2.) biomedical engineering-robotic arm, 3.) robotics-autonomous car design, 4.) aerospace engineering-rocket designs, and 5.) an exploratory day of all t he lessons. The curriculum was implemented at two sites over two weeks. In week one of the program, the group implemented lessons in a rural Haitian-Dominican community on a former sugar cane plantati on, where many of the learn ers in the program have family members who were former sugar cane farmers or harvesters. The program was implemented prior to the start of the school year and was primarily housed in the community's church. All learners in the community spoke Spanish, many  students understood Haitian-Creole, and fewer spoke Haitian-Creole. During week two of the program, the team implemented the lessons at a public school located in a more urbanized city. Learners in this context were from Haitian-Dominican communities. Members of the research team  have varying levels of proficiency in Spanish, Haitian-Creole, and English, and all languages were used throughout the process.

## Data collection and analysis

In the weeks after the program implementation, we c onducted a collaborative inquiry, collective reflec tion, and examination of our experiences and perspectives (Se cules et al., 2021). We drew from Table 2. of the S ecules et al. paper to design the collaborative inquiry promp ts in the work (2021, p. 25) applying these questio ns for our research context. We responded to a set of six prom pts (Table 2) regarding identity, communication, th e implementation process and experience, and implicat ions for engineering  education. We engaged with the questions individually before adding the responses in a shared document, followed by group discussions  and annotations on one another’s responses. The responses and annotations were analyzed using elements of deductive thematic analysis  (Clarke & Braun, 2017) with the goal of identifying  themes especially related to translanguaging, identity, culture, and their impact on learners and the researchers. ## Findings

Findings from this collaborative inquiry suggest th at from the researchers’ perspective, shared lingui stic or cultural attributes with the learners and communiti es may support relationship development with the students. Additionally, researchers made pedag ogical moves to honor students’ linguistic and cult ural identities and saw learners bringing in elements of their selves and c ommunities while learning. The findings also sugges t that the aspects of the educators’ and researchers' identiti es that are share d with the learners and communities were sustained in this context in ways that were not sustained in English-only contexts. From our perspectives, pedagogical mov es made to invite learners to engage as they are may have l ed to more creative and generative learning outcome s by recognizing the ingenuity of the community and the learners in the program. ## Discussion

Researcher identity in community-based design research is relevant and must be explo red.  Researcher positionalities influenced not only their approache s to designing and implementing engineering and rob otics lessons but also the ways they engaged with student s from Haitian and Dominican backgrounds. Shared cu ltural and linguistic experiences shaped their pedagogical choices, creating spaces w here translanguaging served as a tool for fostering inclusion and knowledge construction. Incorporating translanguaging provided a foundation for supporting learners' exploration of their communiti es' ways of knowing and prompted students to use Sp anish, Haitian-creole, and English. At the same time, it called fo r the researchers to use their language practices f or lesson implementation, interactions with the students, and community engagement. Researchers have discusse d the role of privileging non-dominant languages of the communities they work wit h (Teeters, Jurow, & Shea, 2016).  While the research team designed for transl anguaging to engage students, some of the researche rs noted they felt supported in using their language repertoires in this setting in ways not experienced in their learning and teaching contexts in the U.S.

Researchers that shared similar backgrounds to the students or were raised in Haitian-Dominican communities recognized the learners’ perceptions of  what constitutes engineering as valid and valuable  in the learning contexts. They expanded their perceptions  regarding how engineering learning happens in educa tional spaces in the DR by watching students ideate and pr ototype a wide variety of engineering designs throu gh the program. By integrating students’ linguistic and cultural re sources into the learning process, they disrupted traditional, monolingual, and often exclusionary models of STEM education. A limitation of this study is that the responses were from the perspectives of the researc hers and a community organizer who also participate d on the research team. In future works, student and communi ty interviews will be central to showing how learni ng happened from the learners' and their community mem bers’ perspectives. The implications of this work s tretch across multiple aspects of education research and implementation. This work may inform ways to engage learners' linguistic and cultural ways of knowing in engineering learning experiences globally. More specifically, it aimed to elevate the ingenuity of Dominicans and Haitians  in the DR using their daily e ngineering practices in an engineering education setting.
