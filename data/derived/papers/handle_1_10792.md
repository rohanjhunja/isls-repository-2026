# Navigating Tensions within the Co-design Process to Generate Debugging Resources for Middle School STEM Classrooms

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Debugging has long been considered an important skill in computer programming but one that students often struggle to master. While there are existing resources for supporting students in debugging, they have typically been designed to address the needs of undergraduates training to be software engineers. This focus on industry preparation can make adoption of resources difficult for the growing number of K-12 educators who are incorporating programming into non-CS classrooms. To create more suitable resources, we conducted co-design workshops with middle school STEM/science teachers to develop a debugging strategy guide tailored to their students. Through qualitative analysis of five workshops, we examined the tensions that arose during co-design and the resources used, including teacher experiences in debugging and industry vocabulary, to reach a consensus on the co-designed debugging guide. This work holds implications for facilitating co-design experiences that elevate teachers as equal partners in designing classroom resources.

## Introduction

Debugging is a vital skill in computer science (CS) and the development of computational thinking (CT) (Grover & Pea, 2013). But as important as debugging is, decades of research have shown that it is a skill students struggle to learn, and instructors str uggle to teach (McCauley et al., 2008). While exist ing works have analyzed various challenges of debugging (McCauley et al., 2008), mo st debugging research has largely focused on the student with limited discussion on the teacher. However, wi th the growing number of CS offerings in K-12 (Blikstein & Moghadam, 2018) and integration into non-CS and STEM courses (Biddy et al., 2022), recent research has shifted towards training teachers to effectively debug and support student debugging (Michaeli & Romeike, 20 19). We add to this growing line of research by analyzing t he process teachers and researchers took to co-design a debugging workflow meant to guide middle school stu dents through the debugging process. The teachers noted that the models were high-level, generic, and included industry terminology. For their middle  school students, they wanted a resource that provi ded actionable steps for finding common bugs that limit ed the use of industry terms. Our study focuses on the interactions between Author 1 and the teachers as they co-designed artifacts. It is guided by the following research question: What moments of tension emerged while co-designing curricular artifacts, and how was a conse nsus reached to support students’ learning to debug?

## Theoretical framework

Teachers draw upon various resources when they adapt materials for their classroom, including prior experiences teaching similar units, insight from other teachers, and students’ prior knowledge and experiences in and out-ofschool (Shaw et al., 2020). During the debugging wo rking group, we sought to bring together teachers t o codesign an artifact that supports debugging, explici tly offering a space where teachers could develop w ith each other's social resources. Roschelle and colleagues (2006) define co-design as a process where researchers and teachers collaboratively produce  an artifact to address a concrete need. During the co-design process, tensions arise that require mitigating (Rochelle et al., 2006). Such design tensions are “places in the project that (a) directly or indirectly fall within the designers’ scope of i nfluence but (b) where means, ways, and values come int o conflict” (Tartar, 2007, p. 418). Conceptualizing t he co-design process through emerging tensions allowed us  to envision the act as an act of “goal balancing” wher e the negotiations and decisions were based on choices across criteria (Tartar, 2007). For this study, tensions arose due to the conflicting practices of industry and our teachers’ classroom experiences. This study identifies specific tensions, their influences on the artifact, and how they were resolved within our co-design process.

## Methods

This study is part of a broader Research-Practice Partnership (RPP) (Coburn & Penuel, 2016) centered on the collaborative co-design of units to integrate CT into disciplinary m iddle school science instruction (Biddy et al., 2022). Three of the participating middle school science/STEM teachers (Daria, Fauna, and Wendy), with Author 1, formed a co-design team focused on debugging. All three teacher s had 1-2 years of prior experience teaching the units, were actively engaged in debugging activ ities within their respective classrooms and volunt eered to participate in a debugging-focused working group.

Data sources and analysis To study the co-design process, we collected video recordings, fiel d notes, and artifacts from the working group sessions. We inductively coded (Miles et al., 2014)  video recordings of the co-design sessions to identify the resources from which teachers drew support to reach a consensus about the artifact's design. We coded “resources” as any past or future people, ideas, or places participants vocalized to determine what would be best for the artifact. We then iterated on our codes, conducting second le vel pattern coding to deduct reoccurring themes in the data. ## Results

Throughout the co-design process, several tensions emerged that requi red the design group to reach a consensus about the debugging artifact (Figure 1). Analysis o f the co-design workshops revealed three kinds of tensions: tensions around group goals, the g eneral design of the artifact, and the language use d in the artifact. In each instance, teachers developed and drew upon several resources to build consensus on what would be best for their artifact. In this results section, we briefly descr ibe the first two tensions and then fully analyze an instance of t he third tension to demonstrate how teachers pulled on a variety of resources to reach consensus. Tension: Group goal During the first co-design session, the team negotiated goals for their work together, deciding whether they should design a teacher or student-facing resource and what purpose the resource should serve (i.e., should it be to teach vocabulary, to teach the  process of debugging, or to empower students to dri ve their own learning). To start the goal-setting process, Author 1 shared an academic article on productive struggle (Warshauer, 2015), which helped frame a discussion on how teachers can respond to s tudent struggles in debugging (e.g., telling the student the error or guiding them to the error with probing questions). Drawing on their own experiences supporting students’ debugging and the academic article, the co-design team decided to create a student-facing resource that teachers could reference when they approached students in th e debugging process. That provided flexibility in h ow the teacher attended to students struggling to debug. Ideally, the resource would help students become more resilient to the frustrations of debugging and persist in becoming strong independent debuggers. Tension: Design The team also encountered a tension which involved deciding what kind of artifact to create. Author 1 shared several industry approaches to debugging to spark i deas. Teachers drew on a variety of resources from their experiences – including depictions of past and future students – in conjunction with approaches Author 1 shared (recognized by teachers as the “industry” perspective) to reach consensus and then co-create a rough sketch of the debugging flowchart. Author 1 converted this sketch into a more formalized flowchart, which the teachers further simplified for their middle school students (Figure 1). Tension: Language use One area that drew active discussion during the cod esign process was regarding the use of industry ter minology. Teachers suggested both the removal of industry lan guage (e.g., “Sensor Calibration” became “Tilt Scre en Filled”), and its inclusion (e.g., “Ext ra Blocks” became “Inactive Blocks”). But the most active discussion centered around Author 1’s use of the term “trigger”. The initial prompt in the debugging artifact was written as, “Event Blocks-do they trigger when you expect them to?”. Within the context of programming, “triggered” refers to when a pre-set event occurs and activates some code (e.g., a t imer ends, which triggers an alarm to sound). Throughout the discussion, teachers had two areas t onegotiate: deciding if “trigger” is an appropriat e term for middle schoolers and deciding what word, if any, should replace the word trigger. Fauna started the negotiation by theorizing how her students might react to the word “trigger” by stating that “some students connect that to trigger as in t rauma.” With this, she opened the room for discussi on about a word that could have several connotations for middl e school students. Similarly, Fauna offered an exam ple of a student saying, “I -- just because I can think of one specific student, but if I say like a trigger is a trigger for them," as evidence that students could associate the term with an emotional trigger. Such became a jumping-off point for teachers to draw connections among their experience s, such as Daria’s validation “Yes. I know what you 're saying”. The three teachers often worked like this:  often validating peers’ offerings by comparing the m to their prior experiences and theorizing about potential future experiences. Daria cited imaginings of future students, describi ng various ways they might respond childishly or negatively: “I think it's a great word...middle sch oolers...they're either going to get silly with lik e weapons kind of stuff, or you're going to have other kids that are to be like that is a trigger”. Her imaginings had the team explore future student reactions, resulting in a consensus on excluding “trigger.” After reaching a consensus,  a concern did arise about how this exclusion might impact their students’ learning. Within their negotiations around the terminology us ed, the team discussed the importance of adopting industry words to prepare students for future caree rs (i.e., Fauna asked Author 1 to share how common the word “trigger” was in engineering). Author 1 offered examples of how and when “trigger” is used, including examples from the programming environment’s official documentation to demonstrate how it’s commonly used in industry. However, Author 1 noted that using all industry language immediately is unnecessary. Daria took up this industry perspective, describing future students: “when they  get older they can just be like, Oh yeah, we use t his word”. This legitimated the choice to not use “trigger.” In trying to replace “trigger”, the teachers initia lly drew upon their prior experiences teaching the unit (i.e., Fauna offered “I think I just usually said l ike start, when it starts. Yeah, run”), while Autho r 1 offered examples of industry language (“we usually say for event blocks… it's triggered by some event”). Throu ghout this negotiation process, the teachers relied on their prior teaching experiences and Author 1’s industry knowledge to find a balance in language that would serve their students now while allowing for future growth in knowledge. In the final version, the team replaced the word tr igger with a phrasing that more closely matched the  wording used in class, as seen in this updated prompt “Event Blocks-does the code run when you planned it to?”.

## Conclusion

Co-designing curricular artifacts with teachers, as researchers, is an important next step after years of co-designing a curriculum. In this paper, we articulate this tas k as an opportunity to dialogically work together a s tensions, or needs for consensus, emerge. This includes allowing teachers to contribute authentically to decisions and elevate teachers as full collaborators in designing classro om resources. We sketch how teachers and researcher s alike deploy interactional resources from experience, ima ges of students (past and present), and emerging goa ls of the artifact itself. However, to fully collaborate, each stage of a curricular artifact’s design (from the initial idea to the final product) should be included in the co-design process. This enables teachers and researchers to work together to create the best resource for the teachers and their peers across teaching contexts.
