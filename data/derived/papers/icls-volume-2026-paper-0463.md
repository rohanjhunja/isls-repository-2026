# Pathways to Hope in a Critical Place-Based Science Camp

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Youth’s hope has become a central concern as socioecological precarity and anxiety abound. I examined forms of hope youth developed in a critical place-based science camp, and in particular, what nurtured critical hope. Multiple pathways engendered critica l hope, with different youth emphasizing critique and reckoning with harms, focusing on collective action, and rejecting human-nature hierarchies. Findings have implications for designing for hope.

Pathways to Hope in a Critical Place-Based Science Camp Tessaly Jen, University of Massachusetts Dartmouth, tjen@umassd.edu Abstract: Youth’s hope has become a central concern as socioecological precarity and anxiety abound. I examined forms of hope youth developed in a critical place-based science camp, and in particular, what nurtured critical hope. Multiple pathways engendered critica l hope, with different youth emphasizing critique and reckoning with harms, focusing on collective action, and rejecting human-nature hierarchies. Findings have implications for designing for hope. Socioecological devastation and precarity surround us, causing high levels of anxiety for many youth (Brophy et al., 2023; Ojala, 2012) and an increased focus on fostering youth’s hope around environmental issues (Trott, 2022; Vamvalis, 2023). However, not  all hope is equal; naive or false hope —a hollow optimism that status quo approaches might save us —differs from critical hope rooted in critiquing past and present harms (Boler, 2013; Zembylas, 2014). I draw on two interrelated forms of critical hope proposed by Duncan-Andrade (2009): “socratic hope” born from reflecting on painful and oppressive relations and “audacious hope” involving striving in solidarity. Further, building on Jadallah et al.’s (2025) theory of critical hope in climate change education, I view looking back to critique harmful relations and uplift marginalized harmonious relations (which I will call recognizing), and looking forward to imagine otherwise horizons of collective flourishing (which I will call envisioning), as central to developing critical hope. This paper examines hope youth developed in a camp centered around their local watershed. I explore: (a) What is the nature of youth’s hope for the future of the Willow Creek watershed? (b) How does this connect to their engagement in recognizing and envisioning? This study was part of a larger project, Teens Re-Storying the Creek with STEM, which engaged youth in a twoweek critical place-based science camp focused on socioecological issues in their local Willow Creek watershed (pseudonym). 28 youth from two middle schools participated in the camp. They explored the watershed through play, inquiry, data analysis, socioecological histories, interviews, and a civic design project, as well as created digital stories via podcasts, zines, and augmented reality (AR). The  research team recorded video, collected camper artifacts, and took field notes, as well as conducted mid-and post-camp interviews. I followed Maietta et al.’s (2021) process of “diving in” and “stepping back” to guide my analysis toward a “hybrid story of data content and researcher knowledge” (p. 2046). First I reviewed youth’s zines, AR projects, civic design plans, and interviews, because these revealed stories related to their recognizing  and envisioned futures. I annotated these with noticings and used diagrams, analytic memos, and episode profiles (holistic stories of each data source) to make sense of and synthesize the data. Then, “stepping back,” I began “bridging and threading” across data sources to “[discover] themes in the dataset that weave component ideas together” (Maietta et al., 2021, p. 2047). Noting patterns between youth’s recognizing, envisioning, and forms of hope, I constructed participant profiles for each of the 12 campers who completed post-camp interviews, which included a question on their hopefulness. Youth’s reflections on what caused their hopefulness led me to dive into additional data. I then continued the process and ultimately created four portraits of hope drawn from the reflections and experiences of eight campers (four were excluded due to missing data and inconsistencies). Gia and Jordan expressed hope with an uncertain relationship to socioecological thriving. Both suggested that awareness could spur action toward a healthier watershed. This mirrors a widespread assumption that positive experiences in “wild” areas foster pr o-environmental behaviors (Chawla, 1999), but implies a nature-culture divide where caring for nature is separate from day-to-day relations with more than humans (MTHs). Reflecting this divide, Jordan separated human areas (e.g. stores, clinic, library) from natural areas (e.g. water) in their civic design plan for Creekside Plaza—a defunct mall site, and planned to create a human area (business buildings) in AR. So despite their hope that appreciation would nurture care for Willow Creek, in their envisioning, they perpetuated a status quo separation foregrounding capitalist aims. While Gia declared “nature’s my passion AND my element,” she perceived others as not nature people and grappled with anthropocentrism in her projects. For example, she advocated for a waterpark and an arcade in her group’s Creekside Plaza design because these were elements that community members she’d interviewed desired. The nature-culture divide thus constrained these campers’ hope in ways that limited transformative possibilities. 2818 Nathan and Addison expressed socratic critical hope, critiquing oppressive socioecological relations and emphasizing accountability for injustices. For example, Nathan’s zine focused on the Trail of Tears (which crossed Willow Creek) from the perspective of a Cherokee person. Through his zine, he engaged in recognizing— critiquing oppressive relations between settlers and Indigenous peoples and uplifting the oft-overlooked brilliance of Indigenous peoples—and urged others to reckon with the pain Cherokee people endured. Nathan’s envisioning further critiqued harmful relations and urged accountability by centering the perspective of a MTH harmed by dominant relations; his AR project featured a raccoon lecturing on the impacts of deforestation. Addison drew on lived experiences in a more developed and polluted part of the watershed to advocate for entwined human-natural well-being. Her zine highlighted environmental injustice as an issue that impacts both “humans and nature” in the watershed; and her Creekside P laza plan balanced providing amenities for people while avoiding intense development that might harm MTH. Addison and Nathan both recognized  harmful dominant relations and envisioned thriving futures created in part by increased accountability. Avery and Adrian leaned into audacious critical hope. Avery emphasized adults’ carelessness and the need to rally youth for a better future. Her zine featured a young person protesting a bill that would enable wetland development; and her AR project illustrated a daycare designed to foster connection to nature. Adrian’s hope emerged from a newfound sense of agency and responsibility. He shifted from utter hopelessness to “if we don’t like, just be triflin’… there’s a good chance of us [preserving the] watershed” and believed others could too. Connecting to an endangered crayfish in the creek, Adrian realized “how what you doing is… affecting them.” His zine shared the crayfish’s story to spur others to become responsible, and highlighted collective work by featuring protests to protect the crayfish. He emphasized the collective again in his envisioning around Creekside Plaza where he advocated for a farmers market to help to “change the community.” Jesse and Carly expressed audacious and socratic critical hope. They blamed development and production/consumption for environmental destruction, emphasized MTH justice, and found hope in collective action. Carly conveyed the power of solidarity in their zine about the camp community, in which they included MTH; they illustrated “[learning] about the creek and how it’s feeling” as part of community-building. Their AR project further contested human-nature separation, merging peers’ ideas for an animal park and a quiet reading area into a shared human-MTH space—a reading garden where “you can cuddle with animals.” Jesse similarly opposed human-MTH hierarchies. She reflected that development cornered animals “so nature will be not a democracy,” suggesting animals ought to be viewed as members of a democracy with a right to land. Her AR project also catered to a human-MTH community—she designed an outdoor food area in lieu of a typical indoor food court in order to “tear some of [the mall] down and turn it into a natural place with food.” Notably, both Carly and Jesse primarily critiqued structural and sociocultural issues rather than individual actions, which may be why their envisioning focused less on spreading awareness and more on different relations with MTHs. Campers’ engagement in recognizing and envisioning influenced the nature of their hope. Without recognizing work, youth’s sense of hope is unlikely to nurture socioecological care because they do not problematize or question assumptions about status quo relations (Gia and Jordan). When youth focus on critiquing harmful relations, though they may not feel partic ularly optimistic about the future, they express socratic critical hope (Duncan-Andrade, 2009) that foregrounds accountability for harmful actions (Nathan and Addison). By calling attention to status quo oppressions, they powerfully emphasize the need for transformation, though exactly how that transformation may occur, beyond convincing people to change their behaviors, may remain somewhat elusive. When youth uplift harmonious relations, they create space to envision a more caring future; they express audacious critical hope (Duncan-Andrade, 2009) that collective efforts may shift the status quo (Avery and Adrian). And when the harmonious relations youth uplift disrupt dominant human-nature hierarchies, they may also express socratic hope by inherently critiquing and countering the anthropocentrism of the past and present (Carly and Jesse). None of these portraits of hope are comprehensive, and I do not  mean to suggest a hierarchy. Rather, I aim to highlight generative seeds that different youth foregrounded through their recognizing and envisioning and the need to nourish all of these seeds toward socioecological care. The diversity of youth’s pathways to hope—leveraging perspective-taking, personal experiences, anger, and newfound agency—is also noteworthy. Prior literature highlights how perspective taking attunes learners to MTH personhood and spurs care (McDaid Barry et al., 2023), anger motivates engagement in environmental action (Stanley et al., 2021), and agency plays a critical role in cultivating youth’s hope (Trott, 2019). My findings suggest that designs ought to offer multiple avenues for youth to enter into socioecological care and hope in ways that are comfortable and relevant. While challenging youth to consider dimensions of care and justice that they might not initially gravitate to is also important, the goal isn’t for youth to converge on one future, but to envision multiple possible horizons toward justice and care.
