# Modeling with Primary Sources: An Approach to Teach Data Bias for Artificial Intelligence and Machine Learning Education

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Data bias in AI (artificial intelligence) and ML (machine learning) remains a prominent challenge, with implications ranging from healthcare to criminal justice. As we lean more on these technologies, it becomes vital to educate the youth about the nuances of data bias and its implications. In this paper, we present the SourceML approach, teaching data bias by engaging students in building ML models with primary sources as text data in high school history classrooms. We have developed a web-based tool, StoryQ, designed to allow K-12 students to create ML models. Our results demonstrated that this approach enhances students’ understanding of data bias, helping them recognize diverse bias origins, societal implications, and comprehensive strategies for bias mitigation. The SourceML approach underscores the importance of fostering historical thinking in teaching data bias, highlighting the intricacies of data origin, generation context, and subtle influences shaping data.

## Introduction

The issue of data bias in AI (artificial intelligence) and ML (machine learning) is a critical concern that has garnered increasing attention in recent years (Mehrabi et al., 2021). It highlights challenges associated with relying on biased or skewed datasets to train algorithms, potentially leading to biased predictions and outcomes. Data bias can emerge from various sources, including historical prejudices, societal inequalities, and human judgment in the data (Caton & Haas, 2020). When unaddressed, these  biases can perpetuate existing disparities, affecting decision-making processes in finance, healthcare, criminal justice, and beyond. Tackling data bias requires careful dataset curation, algorithmic adjustments, and ethical considerations to ensure that AI systems are fair, equitable, and accountable, thereby fostering trust and responsible deployment of AI technologies (Chen et al., 2023). In the AI education community, there has been a recent surge of work focused on teaching data bias in ways that are applicable and scalable in both STEM (science, technology, engineering, and mathematics) and non-STEM education contexts (Irgens et al., 2022). However, there is little research exploring the specific challenges and effective strategies for integrating data bias education into K-12 curricula, where students can gain foundational knowledge about data bias in the context of AI and ML. This is especially lacking in non-STEM learning settings, which is critical for equipping students with interdisciplinary skills for an increasingly AI-driven world, extending access beyond STEM (Tatar et al., 2022). This gap calls for comprehensive investigations to develop age-appropriate instructional materials, innovative pedagogical approaches, and interdisciplinary collaborations that empower young learners to critically engage with and address the multifaceted dimensions of data bias, thereby fostering a more informed and ethically conscious generation of AI technology users and creators (Wang et al., 2022).

In this paper, we present a SourceML approach (Figure 1) to teaching data bias in high school history class rooms. This approach involves engaging students in exploring and building ML models with primary sources as text data. On the one hand, primary sources offer authentic and diverse perspectives from the past, enabling students to critically analyze and understand the intricate interplay of biases in historical data (Johansson, 2017). On the other hand, in history education, this approach fosters a deep appreciation for the complexity of narratives, encourages critical thinking about sources, and empowers students to recognize bias and question the construction of historical accounts (Fitzgerald, 2019). By engaging students in using primary sources as text data for exploring and building ML models, we create a holistic learning experience that bridges the domains of history and AI, preparing them to navigate the ethical complexities of a data-driven world. Specifically, we first present a review of the relevant literature. Then we describe a three-year designbased research project that shaped the development of StoryQ technology (i.e., a web-based machine learning and text mining tool) and the corresponding curriculum module tailored for high school history classrooms. Subsequently, we provide an overview of a case study that investigates students’ understanding of data bias after implementing the SourceML approach in a public school in the Southeastern United States. Findings from this ICLS 2024 Proceedings 514 © ISLS case study serve as a foundation for a discussion in which we explore instructional and technological strategies to effectively teach data bias in K-12 AI and ML education. ## Literature review

Revealing data bias in K-12 education The issue of bias, with a particular emph asis on algorithmic bias, has emerged as a pressing concern within the learning sciences community. Algorithm bias, also known as AI bias, refers to systematic and un fair discrimination or inaccuracies produced by mach ine learning algorithms or automated decision-making systems (Belenguer, 2022). The skewed predictions, recommendations, or decisions generated from algorithmic bias may disproportionately benefit or harm certain groups or individuals. For example, a bi ased algorithm may inadvertently perpetuate existing inequalities by recommending distinct educational pathways to studen ts based on factors su ch as their gender, eth nicity, or so cioeconomic back ground (Ferrer et al., 20 21). Algorithm bias primarily arises  from two major sources: inherent limitations within the algorithm itself and a biased dataset (Hajian et al., 2016). Addressing algorithmic bias requires algorithm developers to design algorithms and models that are robust, fair, and unbiased. Although an  algorithm developer may have no in tention of discrimination, a robust algorithm can still exhibit discriminatory outcomes. This is b ecause many datasets u sed to train AI algorithms are biased and d o no t represent the diversity of the human population (Hajian et al., 2016). Thus, educators and researchers are actively working to diagnose and rectify  data bias (Abebe et al., 2022; Offenwanger et al., 2021). In p articular, educational researchers are exploring methods to mitigate bias in datasets that are used  for educational purposes, aiming to ensure that technology-d riven learning environments are fair, in clusive, and equitable for all students (Hu & Rangwala, 202 0; Sha et al., 2 022). More importantly, educators are also emphasizing the significance of cultivating  students’ awareness and skills in understanding data bias (Melsión et al., 2021; Moss, 2023).

In this paper, we draw upon studies that incorporate data bias into  various learning settings to enhance students’ awareness and understanding of d ata bias since this body of work in K-12 educatio n remains in its infancy. Heiser et al. (2023) examined students’ perceptions of bias in learning analytics. They found more than half of the students were not only concerned about bias they also expressed their awareness that learner data might be used to justify decisions that could be b iased. Some students were skeptical of “the reliability of the source” that was used for analysis. In another study, Melsión et al. (2021) dev eloped an educational platform to increase students’ awareness of gender bias in machine learning. Moss (2023) taught elementary students da ta bias in critical algorithmic literacy lessons. K-12 computer science teachers reveal the importance of data bias in conceptualizing computer science ethics (Hu & Rangwala, 2020). Building upon these reviewed studies in uncovering data bias in K-12 computer science classrooms, we attempt to expand the teach ing and learning of data bias to K-12 histo ry classrooms to examine high school students’ perception of data bias. Our work not only aligns with the growing body of work concerning the teaching and learning of algorithm bias but also adheres to th e “AI for K-12” curriculum’s guidelines (Touretzky et al., 2019). Teaching data bias in history classrooms requires educators to consider history teaching as a constructivist process where effective methods should be placed on ad dressing the concept of data b ias and detecting bias in historical sources (Vella, 2020). Leading students towards the discussion of historical context to understand bias in data collection or interpretation, presenting historical case studies where students can highlight bias and its ICLS 2024 Proceedings 515 © ISLS conse quences, and exploring sources of data bias are all feasible methods to teach bias in history classrooms. In particular, delving into the sources of data bias makes it possible for students to recognize various bias factors (i.e., selection bias, cultural bias, and omission bias), which may cause a distorted historical narrative (Belenguer, 2022).

Leveraging primary sources to support the learning Primary sources provide students with an authentic glimpse into past events, cultures, or discoveries, allowing them to construct knowledge based on evidence and fostering critical thinking. By analyzing primary sources, students candiscern biases, contextualize information within larger narratives, and draw personal connections to the material (Johansson, 2017; Woyshner, 2010). This method not only promotes a rich understanding of subjects but also cultivates essential skills like analysis, evaluation, and synthesis (Fitzgerald, 2019). With increasing access to vast digital archives of primary sources, educators have an unprecedented opportunity to integrate these materials into the curriculum, enhancing learning experiences and fostering a more inquisitive and informed generation of students. In particular, primary sources have been used in data science education to enable students to delve deep into the cultural, social, economic, and political contexts from which the data emerges (Hammond et al., 2020).

We designed SourceML to engage students in building ML models with primary sources as text data. We argue that perspective and bias are two concepts that share similarities and can be understood by students from both historical and AI perspectives. Students could gain a deep understanding of how perspective and bias influence our interpretation and analysis of primary sources, whether it is through studying historical events or learning about the algorithms used in AI. By recognizing the role that perspective and bias play in shaping our understanding of the world around us, students could become critical thinkers and make informed decisions about how to use data appropriately by reflecting on whose perspective the data represented. We hypothesize that a historical perspective could support the understanding of and reasoning about data bias in AI systems. Development and design Technology development In this project, we have been developing the StoryQ app (Figure 2), a web-based machine learning and text mining tool that allows young learners (grades 6-12) to engage in machine learning practices and work with unstructured text data without coding. StoryQ features dynamically linked data representations that promote meaningful inquiries across tables, graphs, and texts —a unique user experience that makes machine learning models transparent, explainable, and engaging. Students can use StoryQ to train, test, and troubleshoot text classification models using both standard feature extractors (e.g., N-grams) and special feature extraction tools and visualizations designed to support young learners and non-computing teachers. Curriculum design We have developed a five-hour SourceML module centered on a student-led inquiry into a redlining dataset. This dataset includes around 400 neighborhood descriptions developed by the Home Owners Loan Corporation (HOLC) in the late 1930s which served as the basis of discriminatory lending policies. Students study the history of redlining and then use the StoryQ app to explore words HOLC agents used in their descriptions and their relative associations with neighborhood ratings. They discuss bias in data sourc es and language patterns in these sources and reflect on the historical and racial context that produced redlining. The module included a sequence of five lessons that combine historical thinking on redlining and machine learning workflow to investigate patterns across large sets of primary sources. Figure 1 shows the key phases of learning in this module. All lessons have adhered to a consistent format encompassing the initial introduction of the subject matter, engaging in activities that delve deep into the topic, and collaborating in groups to address questions directly related to the activities. Lesson one, “History  of Redlining,” introduced the context and history of redlining in the Eastern and Mid-Western United States, along with an introduction to the Mapping Inequality project (Connolly, 2018). Besides fostering a sense of historical context, a central goal of this lesson was to comfortably introduce sensitive vocabularies such as white flight, racial liberalism, and redlining. The second lesson, “Introduction to AI,” focused on an introduction to AI concepts, the machine learning workflow, and StoryQ technology. The main purpose of this lesson was to have students play with the StoryQ technology for the first time in a guided lesson. The third lesson, “AI & Mapping Inequality,” allowed students to examine hundreds of neighborhood descriptions produced for the fe deral government’s “residential security maps” in the late 1930s by the HOLC through StoryQ technology by producing a Unigram Model that classifies A and D neighborhoods. Students observed textual patterns from the weighted and ranked descriptions. For exa mple, ICLS 2024 Proceedings 516 © ISLS Figure 2 shows students’ exploration of the feature “wooded”. It shows that the feature was highlighted in the feature table, feature distribution graph, neighborhood descriptions, and primary source generated in the 1930s. This feature often implies subur ban areas associated with wealthier white communities, which were frequently characterized by natural elements like trees and greenery. Using these terms can serve as “coded language” to imply the racial makeup of a neighborhood without explicitly stating it. In the fourth lesson, “Counternarrative,” students examined the contrast between the HOLC’s depiction of neighborhoods and the actual lived experiences of the residents of Bronzeville, shedding light on the untold narrative of these communities. Students individually wrote descriptions of the community of Bronzeville which is used in lesson five as testing data in the Unigram model. Students continued in the fifth lesson, “Data Bias and Reflections,” armed with an understanding of Unigram models, students were now able to load the collective Bronzeville descriptions written by themselves as testing data. Also, they discussed the following questions: 1) What kinds of words are picked up by the Unigram model? 2) Do the words reflect your unique perspective of how the neighborhood looks like? and 3) How do you think these perspectives are shaped, connecting with who you are and your life experiences? At the end of this lesson, students reflected on the representation of human perspectives in models, the origins of these perspectives, and strategies to address bias when creating data-driven AI technologies. ## Case study

In this section, we describe the impact of SourceML on student learning of data bias with 42 high school students. This study took place in a public urban high school in the Southeastern United States in two High School History classes. The two classes of 42 students had diverse backgrounds: 40.5% White, 23.8% Asian, 9.5% Black or African American, 7.1% Indian/Asian, 4.8% Filipino, 4.8% South Asian, 2.4% Hispanic/Middle Eastern, 2.4% Latino, 2.4% Vietnamese, and 2.4% White/Chinese. Gender distribution was 54.8% female, 42.9% male, and 2.4% other. The five lessons (described in the Curriculum Design section) were implemented over a span of six class sessions in six days, with each session lasting 40 minutes. The research team taught the lessons, with the classroom teacher supporting classroom management. To study the impact of SourceML, we collected pre/post assessments, field notes of classroom activities, student-generated responses, and semi-structured interviews. The ICLS 2024 Proceedings 517 © ISLS assess ments measured students’ awareness of data bias in machine learning and beliefs about the influence of human factors on data bias. It includes 9 multiple-choice questions and 1 short answer question. In addition to these assessments, two researchers wrote field notes. The field notes told a story of student-instructor, studentstudent, and student-StoryQ interactions in each session. While students were completing learning activities, they answered open-ended questions. The purpose of these questions was to allow students to reflect on their own learning. Their responses were collected using Google Forms. Also, at the end of each activity, groups shared their reflections on what they learned on a Jamboard, allowing others to view and respond to these insights in real time. Furthermore, at the end of the classroom implementation, we conducted semi-structured interviews with 11 individual students and 5 student pairs for approximately 30 minutes to learn about their experiences from their own perspectives, totaling 21 student participants.

This study draws on a mixed method design (Creswell & Cresswell, 2017) which allows for data triangulation, bolstering the validity and reliability of quantitative and qualitative findings. The quantitative data analysis phase involved conducting the Mann-Whitney U Test to examine changes in their understanding of data bias. The quantitative analysis focused on 36 students who completed both pre-and post-assessments. In the assessment, multiple-choice questions were scored as either correct (1) or incorrect (0). Meanwhile, open-ended questions were scored as correct (2), partially correct (1), or incorrect (0). Given this scoring system, the total possible score for the assessment ranged from 0 to 11. In the qualitative data analysis phase, we transcribed interviews and performed thematic analysis (Clarke et al., 2015) to identify recurring themes in students’ responses, allowing for a deep exploration of their perspectives and insights on data bias. Moreover, field notes and student-generated responses were thoroughly reviewed to uncover nuanced viewpoints and individual reflections, adding depth to the qualitative understanding of their learning experiences. The qualitative analysis focused on 21 students who were interviewed. The combination of quantitative and qualitative approaches provides a comprehensive understanding of how SourceML impacts student learning of data bias, both in terms of measurable changes and nuanced understandings.

## Findings

The data bias instrument demonstrates satisfactory reliability, as evidenced by a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.82. The Mann-Whitney U Test revealed a statistically significant difference in students’ understanding of data bias (U = 462, p < 0.05). After the project implementation, students demonstrated a notable improvement in their understanding of nuanced factors contributing to data bias in machine learning contexts, such as the influence of biased training data and the ethical implications of biased model outcomes. In the following, we present findings from qualitative analysis, delving into the themes related to their understanding of data bias. Theme 1: Recognizing the multifaceted sources and causes that can lead to biased data Among the 21 students, 15 of them demonstrated a strong understanding of the diverse origins and complex factors contributing to biased data. These students exhibited a keen awareness of how bias could stem from a range of sources, including the demographi c composition of data sources, historical inequalities, and the subjective decisions made during data processing processes such as data collection and labeling. For example, student A shared, The people that work on the AI, create these programs and algorithms, I feel like that’s where the bias can come in, If they just simply put in biased articles, opinions, everything... It depends on what they feed it (referring to the algorithm).

This student’s perspective underscores the significance of human influence in shaping biased data. Another student (Student B), emphasized the role of historical context, noting, “Biased data can come from history. If the majority of the history is biased against a certain group, that’s going to carry forward.” Their responses collectively reflected a deep comprehension of the intricate interplay between various elements that can introduce bias into datasets, highlighting the importance of addressing these root causes in efforts to mitigate bias in machine learning models.

Theme 2: Extending beyond model performance to consider its broader implications on society 11 of 21 students exhibited a deep awareness of the significance of model performance beyond its accuracy alone. Student insights reflected an understanding that biased model outcomes can perpetuate social inequalities and reinforce existing biases present in the data. For instance, Student C remarked, AI is definitely evolving a lot. And it’s, I mean, we’re kind of seeing it everywhere, like, in a lot of the technology that we use, like in Google, or like Netflix recommendations that use these ICLS 2024 Proceedings 518 © ISLS patter ns based on like a ton of data that gets input. And so it’s like, in our everyday lives. And so like, for example, if like, my Google search engine knows that I’m a woman, it will use like, 1000s of searches by other women. And like, when I’m typing something in, it will make a list of those for me to choose from, even though I might not have any interest in that it’s just biased because it thinks that I think like other women, and so patterns like that, like show up in our everyday lives.

This student’s response underscores the notion that biased predictions could amplify harmful stereotypes and impact marginalized communities disproportionately. Another student (Student D), while reflecting on her learning experiences, articulated how models can influence decision-making processes, leading to a cascading effect on real-world situations such as resource allocations. She recognized that AI systems, if not properly addressed, can perpetuate systemic biases, widening the gap between different g roups of people. This understanding aligns with the broader theme of the class discussions, where students demonstrated a grasp of the multifaceted implications of AI beyond mere accuracy measurements. Collectively, these narratives show students’ understanding of the importance of considering the ethical and societal ramifications of biased machine learning models.

Theme 3: Tackling bias with a focus beyond technical to ethical and human-centered ap proaches 8 of 21 students embraced a holistic perspective by recognizing the need to extend beyond purely technical approaches to effectively address bias in machine learning models. These students acknowledged that while technical approaches play a critical role, it is equally vital to intertwine ethical considerations and human-centered methods. For instance, student E articulated, “Humans kind of inherently have biases built on our own personal experiences. I think that for AI, you can sort of help reduce that bias by including more diverse sources, and more viewpoints.” This student’s statement exemplifies the emphasis on incorporating diverse perspectives to counteract inherent biases. Additionally, student F highlighted that addressing bias is not just a technical challenge but also a matter of ethics, stating, “Data is powerful, but without ethics guiding it, we could end up with, AI makes decisions that are technically correct but morally questionable.” Furthermore, their insights showed an understanding that combating bias requires a multidisciplinary approach, involving collaboration between data scientists, ethicists, social scientists, and affected communities. By acknowledging the interconnectedness of technical choices, ethical considerations, and the real-world impact on individuals and societies, these students demonstrated a nuanced understanding of the complex nature of bias mitigation. This theme highlights the students’ awareness of the multifa ceted nature of bias in AI and the importance of a comprehensive strategy to tackle it effectively.

## Discussion

This study found that students significantly improved their understanding of data bias, with themes emerging from qualitative data showing their recognition of diverse sources and causes of bias, an awareness of the broader societal implications of biased models, and a holistic perspective on tackling bias by integrating technical, ethical, and human-centered approaches. This study highlights the effectiveness of SourceML in cultivating nuanced understanding and ethical awareness among students regarding da ta bias in machine learning. In the following discussion, we elaborate on how these findings can guide the development of instructional and technological strategies to teach data bias effectively in K-12 AI and ML education. First, we argue that it’s critical to cultivate historical thinking in teaching data bias in K-12 AI and ML education. Historians meticulously use primary sources to trace the origins of events, people, and circumstances to gain a comprehensive understanding (Belenguer, 2022). By fostering a mindset of historians, students could delve deep into the historical roots of data bias, critically analyzing the underlying causes and implications. The SourceML approach could prompt students to question not just the data itself, but also the context in which it was generated. By asking critical questions such as who produced the data, from what perspective, and within what historical contexts, students could uncover the subtle biases and assumptions that may have shaped the data. In this study, we used a redlining dataset, a clear example of biased data as the data was generated for the purpose of systematically discriminating against marginalized communities based on race and socioeconomic factors during historical pe riods of housing segregation and inequality. However, even for datasets that may not immediately appear biased, such as restaurant review data, the application of historical thinking may remain advantageous in teaching students about data bias. By considering the historical and cultural context in which the reviews were generated, students candiscern how societal attitudes and cultural biases might have influenced reviewers’ perceptions and evaluations. They can question whether certain demographic groups were more likely to leave reviews, or whether the reviews were influenced by prevailing stereotypes or expectations. By ICLS 2024 Proceedings 519 © ISLS encoura ging students to explore beyond the surface and delve into the factors shaping the review data, educators can empower them to recognize and critically assess potential biases, even in seemingly neutral datasets. Second, we suggest that learning technology for K-12 AI and ML education should be designed to demystify the working mechanisms of machine learning models. In the context of developing language-based models, this involves creating a bridge between the exploration of words as features, such as understanding how textual data are turned into numerical values, and the ability to reason about their broader significance within the context of sentences. Also, context reasoning can go beyond sentences. For example,  in this study, students used neighborhood descriptions as text data. To enhance their learning experience, a transformative approach could involve integrating these textual descriptions onto a map interface. By fusing textual data with geographic context, students would gain the unique opportunity to critically analyze and question why certain areas were described in specific ways. This approach provides an avenue for understanding the contextual layers and societal implications associated with the deployment of machine learning models (similar to the pedagogy of consequential learning from (Hall & Jurow, 2015)).

Third, we propose that it is essential to expose students to a variety of perspectives in teaching data bias. By exploring diverse viewpoints, students could recognize that bias is not a one-size-fits-all problem, and its resolution often requires a nuanced understanding of different cultural, social, and ethical contexts. Furthermore, it’s equally important to equip students with the practical skills to measure bias effectively. This measurement process could enable students to quantify bias in data, algorithms, and decision-making systems, allowing them to track and visualize changes over time. For example, students could be guided to calculate bias metrics, such as disparate impact, equal opportunity difference, or demographic parity, to quantify bias in the model’s predictions (Chen et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2022). Moreover, students could be supported to create visualizations that showcase the bias within the model. This might involve plotting accuracy rates for different racial or gender groups, showing how bias manifests visually. Through quantifying and visualizing bias, students could witness the tangible outcomes of their work and gain a deeper understanding of the effectiveness of various bias mitigation techniques.

In conclusion, this study has shown the potential of blending historical thinking with AI principles, as demonstrated through the SourceML approach in high school history classrooms. Leveraging primary sources as text data has been shown to instill a deep and nuanced understanding of the complexities surrounding data bias, pushing students to think critically about the sources, implications, and ways of addressing biases. As we move into an era where AI technologies deeply influence our daily lives, equipping the next generation with the knowledge, critical thinking skills, and ethical considerations related to data bias becomes paramount. Such educational endeavors not only empower students to be informed users of AI but also potential innovators who candrive forward a more equitable and unbiased technological landscape. We invite researchers to delve deeper into instructional and technological strategies to teach data bias, especially those that leverage primary sources to equip the youth with the perspectives to navigate and shape the future AI-driven world in a responsible and informed manner.
