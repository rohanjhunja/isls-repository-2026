# Discussion-Oriented Teaching in the Digital Classroom: Teachers’ Collaborative Adaptation in the Face of Disruption

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
We examine how a researcher-practitioner research group collaborated to adapt noticing practices in the face of disruptions brought on by the COVID-19 pandemic. We used a progress monitoring tool and cognitive field notes to support teacher partners’ systematic reflection and to gauge teacher learning. Initial findings suggest these tools helped identify tensions around implementing online class discussions. We highlight the process the teacher partners engaged in as they re-conceptualized engagement, participation, and interaction.

## Introduction and Theoretical Approach

The COVID-19 pandemic and the move to online teaching and learning disrupted established teaching practices. We analyze how a group of in-service English Langua ge Arts (ELA) teachers who were committed to dialog ic teaching (Mercer, 2008) collaboratively adapted their pedagogy in response to two pandemic-related challenges:

(1) students’ reluctance to offer spoken responses during synchronous online classes, and (2) identifying students’

needs without access to typical feedback sources that teachers have during co-present teaching. We conceptualize teacher learning as an active process (Bakkenes et al., 2010), and we ask: How did a group of ELA teachers collaboratively adapt their noticing practices in the face of disruptions brought on by the COVID-19 pandemic? We define teacher noticing as a teacher's capacity to attend to, analyze, and  respond to student thinking (Barnhart & van Es, 2015; Hand, 2012). Such noticing is intentional and guided by experiences, pedagogical commitments, and other factors (Patterson et al., 2020). Here, we examine teachers’ adapta tions in noticing in their remote teaching, as they facilitated through a series of collaborative workshops.

## Data Collection and Analysis

In Fall 2020, we conducted seven workshops to support teachers’ noticing in this new context, centered on digital tools: a “progress monitoring tool” (PMT) where tea chers recorded student behaviors (e.g., unmuted to speak, turned camera on) over time, and a “cognitive field  notes” (CFN) tool (Gutierrez & Vossoughi, 2010), w here teachers engaged in guided reflective writing on th eir noticing practices. Each teacher implemented th e PMT and/or CFN in their class and reported back to the group. During workshops, teachers discussed teachin g challenges, uses of the tools in their classrooms, and data they collected. Additionally, they critically renegotiated meanings of key terms such as engagement, participa tion, and interaction prompted by our team’s focus on classroom discussion. Study data include written teacher reflections, notes on development and implementation of noticing tools, teacher-provided transcripts of classroom discussions, and meeting notes, recording s, and transcripts. Analysis examined how teachers adapted  their practice in the digital space.  Inductive co ding of reflections, notes, and transcripts resulted in identification of several key themes, outlined below.

## Findings

Analyses revealed that teacher partners adapted the ir teaching practices and their conceptualization o f what it means for students to engage, participate, and interact in discussions in the digital space. Teachers’  interactions with the tools surfaced tensions around what they could observe in a new teaching context. In particular, noticings aligned with teachers’ shifting definitions of key constructs: engagement, participation, and interact ion. These definitions were sometimes inherited from teachers’  in-person interactions with students, and teacher partners worked collectively to make sense of these terms to  help them adapt their noticing practices to remote  settings. The teacher partners used phrases like, “I’m currently struggling with the right questions to ask,” and “what would be best to record/observe” in their CFN reflections.

Collaboratively, the teacher partners worked throug h these disruptions by co-constructing a collective learning space that supported reevaluation of what it means to participate and engage in classroom learning when going dialogic in the digital space. ## Discussion & Next Steps

Our data speak to how teachers adapt their teaching practice by learning to see anew in changed circumstances. These teachers were working within a broad and multifaceted crisis – the COVID-19 pandemic – which disrupted many of their teaching practices. Introduction of new noticing tools (the PMT and CFN) highlighted a particular disruption in their ability to notice student engag ement, participation, and interaction, which prompt ed a collaborative and cascading set of reconsiderations of the definition of engagement and its various instantiations within an online classroom, all facilitated through a variety of reflective activities. Our initial findings point to the power of collaborative sensemaking, particularly ar ound noticing student thinking, facilitated through  the introduction of explicit noticing tools and reflective activities.
