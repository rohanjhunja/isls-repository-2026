# Supporting High Schoolers’ Epistemic Vigilance in Everyday Science

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
The rise of misinformation places new demands on science education to prepare students to critically evaluate information in their daily lives. One approach is to foster epistemic vigilance (i.e., critical awareness of potentially false information). Instr uction that explicitly prompts students to critique information and emphasizes the different components of epistemic vigilance may improve student ability to discern misinformation. This exploratory study examines students’ epistemic vigilance during a task in which they evaluated four everyday sources of scientific information. Students demonstrated situational vigilance toward sources they perceived as untrustworthy. Their evaluative strategies and values shifted depending on the task prompts and source characteristics. These findings suggest that students’ reasoning is context-dependent, with their evaluative strategies and values used to judge credibility varying across situations. Building students’ metacognitive understanding of how these evaluative activities and values interact may support more reliable awareness and reasoning about scientific information in everyday contexts.

Students are increasingly expected to navigate scientific misinformation in their everyday lives, and science education plays a role in equipping students to evaluate such misinformation (Chinn et al., 2021; Osborne & Pimentel, 2022; Sharon & Baram-Tsabari, 2020; Sinatra & Lombardi, 2020). One way to support students to more reliably evaluate scientific misinformation is by honing their epistemic vigilance. Epistemic vigilance is defined as one’s critical awareness of information and the potential for misin formation (Sperber et al., 2010). This awareness is achieved through competent engagement in epistemic activities that involve evaluating the quality of claims and the trustworthiness of sources, specifically to avoid passive acceptance of false information (Barzilai & Chinn, 2020; Sperber et al., 2010). Bielik and Krell (2025) extend the concept of epistemic vigilance to also include evaluating the receiver of the information (e.g., the student themself), alongside the claim and the source. Therefore, evaluating the source, the claim, and the receiver are three epistemic activities through which one’s critical awareness of misinformation (i.e., epistemic vigilance) may be achieved. Relatively little is known about how these evaluative performances interact in students’ reasoning and how classroom contexts may shape them. The pedagogical approaches to develop students’ epistemic vigilance are not yet well understood (Bielik & Krell, 2025). Explicitly prompting students to critique information (Tseng et al., 2021) and highlighting the three aspects of epistemic vigilance may improve learning interventions that target students’ ability to evaluate misinformation (Bielik & Krell, 2025). This study builds on this work within a larger project that supports teachers and students in exploring how scientific knowledge is reliably produced (Chinn et al., 2023, Yoon et al., 2023). Lessons in the larger project provide opportunities for students to engage with epistemic practices (e.g., revising theories based on new evidence) and social mechanisms of science (e.g., peer review and consensus-seeking). This exploratory study examines how students draw on these and other epistemic criteria when reas oning about everyday information beyond the classroom. Specifically, we explore patterns in students’ epistemic vigilance (their evaluation of claims, sources, and the receiver) across four everyday information sources (e.g., Instagram Reels and podcasts) to inform future instructional design. Our research question is: When evaluating scientific information, what aspects of the information do students attend to (i.e., evaluating the claim, the source, or the receiver), and how does this vary across different source types and evaluation prompts?

Epistemic vigilance Sperber and colleagues (2010) outline the theoretical underpinnings of epistemic vigilance and its evolution in human communication. They emphasize that we cannot be mutually trusting unless  we are mutually vigilant in our communication, and that while communication brings many advantages, there is also a “major risk for the 661 audience of being accidentally or intentionally misinformed” (p.369). Epistemic vigilance is therefore an awareness that others may share false information, whether intentionally or not, and that this potential for false information should be considered during communication. Sperber and colleagues (2010) explore the possibility of two varying mechanisms of epistemic vigilance —vigilance towards the content being communicated (i.e., evaluation of the claim) and the source of communication (i.e., evaluation of  the source). Claim evaluation, also referred to as content evaluation or first-hand evaluation, involves students making direct judgments about the quality of the claim being stated. These judgments can include evaluations of the argument’s validity, its evidence-based reasoning, and the quality of the evidence used (Duncan et al., 2018; Sinatra & Lombardi, 2020). Duncan and colleagues (2018) constructed a framework for lay engagement with evidence that does not require students to have the advanced content knowledge typically expected for an “expert grasp” of evidence (p.19). Their framework provides an understanding of how students’ evidence-based reasoning can be developed and then used when evaluating scientific claims. Sinatra and Lombardi (2020) call for students to “make explicit plausibility judgments about scientific and alternative explanations” as a necessary action when evaluating information (p. 124). This is a form of claim evaluation where students gauge the truthfulness of knowledge claims and alternative explanations.

Some scholars argue against first-hand evaluation as the sole means for avoiding misinformation, because non-experts are often unable to reliably evaluate scientific claims (Osborne & Allchin, 2024). These scholars advocate the primacy of second-hand evaluation, or evaluation of the source itself (Bromme & Goldman, 2014; Osborne & Pimentel, 2022). Source evaluation involves assessing credibility through judgments about the source’s reliability, expertise, and alignment with scientific consensus. For example, Pimentel (2024) found that using reasoning strategies (e.g., lateral reading and click restraint) with credibility criteria (e.g., relevance of expertise and alignment with scientific consensus) improved students’ ability to evaluate the credibility of sources. Because classroom instruction often privileges evidence assessment over source critique, these scholars advocate for a shift toward teaching students how to evaluate sources as a means to counter susceptibility to misinformation. Recent work, however, argues that both claim and source evaluations are necessary for reliable reasoning about scientific information. Pimentel and Zummo (2025) advance this idea through the apt-AIR framework (see Barzilai & Chinn, 2018; 2024), suggesting that students must skillfully and adaptively integrate both forms of evaluation to engage in epistemically reliable judgment. Tseng and colleagues (2021) likewise focused on these two evaluative strategies, demonstrating that they can be intentionally cultivated through pedagogy by scaffolding instruction with explicit prompts to critique. While Pimentel and Zummo (2025) and Tseng and colleagues (2021) emphasize integrating claim and source evaluation, Bielik and Krell (2025) propose the extended epistemic vigilance framework (EEVF) that also calls for third-hand evaluation, or evaluating the receiver of the information (e.g., the student themself). They argue that effective epistemic vigilance requires “self-evaluation of our own experience, biases, and emo tions that filter how we evaluate information [and] reflecting on how these factors affect others when receiving information” (p.877). This aligns well with prior research stating the importance of cognitive biases and motivated reasoning when designing interventions to develop students’ evaluative strategies (e.g., Sharon & Baram-Tsabari, 2020; Sinatra & Lombardi, 2020). Bielik and Krell (2025) argue that all three types of evaluation become important components of students’ epistemic vigilance and that more efforts in science education should attend to students’ awareness of these components. In this study, we conceptualize epistemic vigilance as a critical awareness of potentially erroneous or misleading information that can be enacted through evaluating the claim, source, and receiver of information. While there is extensive research on how source and claim evaluation are operationalized in the classroom, the interactions between them are not well understood, and the interactions between all three evaluative strategies even less so. This exploratory study aims to gain insight into these interactions, with the long-term goal of improving instructional interventions to better support students’ systematic evaluation of information.

## Context

This study was conducted in the fall semester of the 2024-2025 school year and it is part of a larger project that aims to develop student understanding of scientific epistemic practices in the classroom and in science as a discipline (Chinn et al., 2023; Yoon et al., 2023). In the larger project, students participated in an 8-lesson unit that builds their epistemic performance, including investigations into phenomena-based inquiry through modeling, epistemic practices (e.g., evidence-based reasoning), and the social mechanisms of science (e.g., peer critique). In addition to the unit described above, students also participated in a short extension activity that elicited their evaluations of scientific information that they may receive in their everyday live s. For the purpose of this 662 study, we are interested in looking at the manifestations of students’ epistemic vigilance as demonstrated through their evaluations of potentially erroneous information during the extension activity. For the extension activity, students were given a scenario that involved a close friend struggling with mental health conditions (e.g., panic attacks and depression). Students were also told that the friend has no family history of anxiety or depression, t hey are typically a high-achiever (e.g., balancing a part-time job, sports, and school work), and that they drink 3 –4 energy drinks a day to stay focused. Students were asked the question, “Could the energy drinks be behind your friend’s depression and mental health struggles?” To answer, students evaluated four pre-determined pieces of information, described in Table 1, to make a conclusion about the impact of energy drinks. Each piece of information was created by members of the research team to reflect r eal-life examples of information about a scientific topic and to provide students with different characteristics to evaluate. For example, Source 1 was a social media posting that included both scientific and non-scientific information. Source 2 was in the form of a podcast and included information from a meta-review. Source 3 was another social media post that relied on a scientific study and shared personal experience, and Source 4 was an account of only personal experience with energy drinks. Source 2 (t he meta-review podcast) was considered the most credible from the  researchers’ standpoint. Our intention going into this lesson was for students to grasp that the second source had the most typically reliable characteristics and should be used when coming to a conclusion about the question asked.

## Participants

This study includes eight 10th-grade students in the same chemistry class from a school in the Northeastern US. This class was selected because their teacher is a long-time participant of multiple successive projects, demonstrating strong fidelity with stu dy goals and expressing keen interest in supporting student knowledge aligned with the study. As one of several teachers working on this project, her class was the only one that had completed the unit at the time of the writing of this paper and therefore also stands as a convenient yet representative example of project participants. Because this study is only exploratory, we randomly selected eight students out of twenty-two in the class for our analysis. Four of the students self-identified as girls and f our as boys. All eight students completed the entirety of the lesson and had provided guardian consent to participate in the study.

## Data analysis

The data for this study included the student ratings and written explanations provided across three criteria (i.e., goodness of answering the question, believability, and strength of evidence) for each source. There were a total of 96 individual ratings and 96 individual explanations across all eight students. We separated the responses into quantitative (ratings) and qualitative (explanations) measures. First, we calculated the descriptive statistics for the numerical ratings of each source by criterion. Because of the small sample size (n = 8) and the nested nature of the data (i.e., students evaluated multiple sources within each criterion), we opted against running ANOVAs and instead plotted the data using boxplots for each criterion to visually inspect differences between sources. For the qualitative explanations provided by students, we performed content analysis (Schreier, 2012) using Bielik and Krell’s (2025) validated extended epistemic vigilance framework (EEVF). Using the EEVF, we coded each students’ response for evaluating the source, evaluating the claim, and/or evaluating the receiver. If students made judgments based on the reliability of the source (e.g., identifying the source has hidden intentions) or the expertise of the source (e.g., identifying the source had a plausibility of the claim (e.g., identifying the source had quality evidence), the references used (e.g., the claim is supported by appropriate sources), the context of the claim (e.g., if the claim was outdated), or the one-sidedness of the claim (e.g., identifying that the claim only addresses one side of the argument), we coded this as Evaluating the Claim. Finally, if students made judgments about their own knowledge and beliefs (e.g., identifying that they do not have much expertise in this topic), or their cognitive biases and socioemotional influences (e.g., identifying that they may be biased), then we coded this as Evaluating the Receiver. If students’ responses did not fall in any of these categories, we coded their response as Other. The internal reliability of coding was assessed by checking for consistency across points in time (Creswell, 2009). The first author coded 18 responses (~20% of the data) and then coded the same 18 responses again one month later, with an 83% agreement in coding between time points. The first and fourth authors discussed the discrepancies in coding and came to consensus on the latter codes. The first author then coded the remaining data corpus withi n a week’s time frame of the second coding process.

After coding 96 individual responses across eight students, we calculated the descriptive statistics of the code results separated by both criterion and source. To further examine the coding patterns, we calculated the proportion of coded responses across three levels of analysis. First, we looked at proportions within each of the four sources. Second, we examined proportions within each of the three evaluative criteria (goodness for answering, believability, and evidence strength). Finally, we calculated p roportions within each source and criterion combination, providing a more fine-grained picture of how coding patterns varied jointly by source and evaluative criterion. As patterns were identified in the coded data, we went back to the qualitative explanations to further explain what we saw.

This exploratory study investigated through an extension task how students enacted epistemic vigilance when viewing everyday scientific information. Across all provided sources, students primarily evaluated the claim rather than the source, which suggests that claim evaluation was their default strategy. Students also demonstrated inconsistent awareness of source intentions and use of scientific evidence, and they expressed conflicting attitudes toward the value of personal experience as a basis for reasoni ng. However, their evaluative strategies shifted depending on the task prompt and the perceived untrustworthiness of the source, thereby revealing both flexibility in and situational influences on their epistemic performances. Though students made no explicit evaluation of the receiver, and this absence raises questions about metacognitive reflection on their own interpretive stance, the small, exploratory nature of this study and the lack of receiver-focused prompts constrained the analysis of that aspect of epistemic vigilance.

The tendency to focus on evaluating the claim aligns with prior research highlighting the dominance of claim-based reasoning in science education (Osborne & Pimentel, 2022). Because classroom activities often emphasize evidence assessment over source crit ique, students are led to judge plausibility of arguments without also considering source credibility. Students’ reliance on claim evaluation may therefore reflect both curricular emphasis and the intuitive accessibility of assessing content rather than interrogating authorship, motive, or expertise. Although claim evaluation dominated our results, students still demonstrated ability and occasional instinct to engage in source evaluation. When prompts specifically invited students to question believability, they increased their focus on source evaluation. This supports prior research showing that explicit prompts to critique can influence students’ epistemic vigilance (Tseng et al., 2021). A shift to source evaluation also occurred when students autonomously perceived the source as less trustworthy. In particular, they scrutinized an advertisement-like Instagram Reel (Source 3), often noting potential hidden intentions behind sponsored content and skepticism 667 of the scientific evidence the source did provide. In contrast, students were less aware of the hidden intentions in the Twitter post by an energy drink account (Source 1), suggesting that epistemic vigilance may be conditionally activated by media contexts that relate to prior experiences (e.g., prevalence of online influencers’ sponsored content). These findings provide empirical support for Sperber and colleagues’ (2010) theoretical claim that epistemic vigilance is dynamic in response to the communicative environment, rather than a fixed disposition, and that evaluation strategies are interacting mechanisms.

When students perceived a source as untrustworthy, they tended to devalue both personal experience and scientific evidence within that source (e.g., Source 3, the woman’s Instagram Reel), whereas they treated similar evidence from a “credible” source as persuasive (e.g., Source 4, the boy’s Instagram Reel). The conditional awareness students demonstrated shows that the quality of reasoning can be influenced by the perceived trustworthiness of the source (Gierth & Bromme, 2020; Lombardi et al., 2014). As stated above, however, students did not consistently employ successful source evaluation, and their perceptions of source credibility did not always align with typically accepted epistemic criteria (e.g., they failed to notice hidden intentions in Source 1). Our study therefore indicates room for improvement in students’ source evaluation. It also indicates that students may not have been aware of their own evaluative processes, nor how their judgments in source evaluation influenced their claim evaluations, thus emphasizing the importance of evaluating the receiver as a component of epistemic vigilance (Bielik & Krell, 2025).

The absence of receiver evaluation, both in students’ explicit responses and our interpretation of their inconsistently interacting evaluative strategies, suggests that students did not reflect on their own knowledge, beliefs, biases, and roles as interpreters of information. Here, this may result from task design, because no explicit prompt invited this type of evaluation. But it may also reveal limited independent metacognitive awareness of how personal epistemic stances influence judgment (Allchin, 2023; Puig et al., 2021; Sinatra & Lombardi, 2020). This further stresses the need for students to engage in self-evaluation as a means of strengthening their epistemic vigilance. Future research could examine which instructional prompts and contexts best support this metacognitive reflection. Furthermore, while this study captured epistemic vigilance as it occurred under scaffolded prompting rather than spontaneously, developing new tools to capture unprompted vigilance will be important in understanding students’ real-world evaluative performances. Students’ epistemic vigilance in this study was complex and situational, reflecting reasoning that shifted with context but was not consistently aligned across evaluative activities. Students’ shifts in evaluative strategies were not systematic; while our goal is not to cabin students into rigid processes, we recognize the importance of students intentionally adapting their evaluative strategies depending on context, as noted in Pimentel and Zummo (2025). Fostering students’ awareness of how their evaluative strategies and values interact, and how they can adapt their strategies in various situations, may therefore be important targets for instructional interventions. Altogether, these findings suggest that instructional changes are needed to build awareness of and proficiency in all three evaluative performances —evaluation of the source, claim, and receiver —and the interactions between them. Tasks can be designed to e xplicitly elicit evaluation of each component while prompting students to monitor which strategy they use and why. Such metacognitive scaffolding may help students transition from context-bound vigilance (i.e., awareness that varies depending on the charac teristics of information and task framing) and overdependence on claim evaluation to more systematic, transferrable, and balanced critical awareness. Moreover, highlighting how evaluation strategies shift across information types could better prepare stude nts to be vigilant in real-world contexts. Overall, this study contributes to the literature on developing students’ epistemic vigilance, a key capability in navigating a society increasingly affected by the rapid and consequential spread of misinformation.
