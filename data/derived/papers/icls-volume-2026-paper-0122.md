# A Double-Edged Sword: Navigating Hope and Concern in Teachers’ Adoption of AI Image Generation Tools

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Generative AI is rapidly entering classrooms, offering both opportunities and challenges for K-12 education. While text-based models have received extensive attention, the educational implications of AI image generation tools remain largely unexplored. Thi s study examines how K-12 teachers perceive the benefits, risks, and pedagogical uses of such tools. We conducted semi-structured interviews with six U.S. teachers across diverse grade levels and subject areas, combining discussion of current practices with a hands-on prompt-engineering activity. Thematic analysis revealed a persistent tension between teachers’ enthusiasm for efficiency, customization, and creativity, and their deep ethical concerns about bias, stereotypes, and cultural representation. Teachers emphasized the need for knowledge beyond operational skills, including critical understanding of AI’s underlying logic and limitations. Findings point to a pedagogical shift from using generative AI merely for content production toward leveraging it as a tool for fostering critical inquiry, with implications for teacher professional development and equitable learning design.

Generative Artificial Intelligence (GenAI) has rapidly permeated daily life, with tools capable of producing sophisticated text, audio, and imagery from simple prompts. In education,  GenAI promises to revolutionize instructional design by enabling personalized content, automating resource creation, and fostering creative engagement (Yim, 2024). However, this rapid and often unregulated proliferation of these tools has outpaced the development of critical educational frameworks, leaving educators to navigate a complex and uncertain landscape on their own.

While much of the academic and public discourse has centered on text-based models, work exploring the educational implications of AI image generation tools (e.g.,ChatGPT, Midjourney, DALL-E) remains limited, though some notable studies have begun to addres s this area (Reed et al., 2023). In education, visuals are often used to explain complex concepts, engage diverse learners, and foster cultural understanding (Kiss & Weninger, 2017). AI image generation tools offer the unprecedented ability to create high-quality visuals on demand, potentially saving teachers valuable time and expanding their creative capabilities (Giannakos et al., 2025). However, this potential also risks perpetuating harmful stereotypes and representations. These models are trained on vast internet datasets, which often contain and amplify societal biases and stereotypes related to gender, race, or culture (Bianchi et al., 2023). For educators committed to fostering inclusive and equitable learning environments, the uncritical use of such tools could inadvertently harm students by reinforcing those harmful representations. These tensions create an urgent need to examine teacher p erspectives, not only as technology adopters but as learning designers who mediate between GenAI systems and students. Building on these concerns, this study advances understanding in three ways. First, it offers empirical insights into teacher adoption of AI image generation tools in K-12 context, where research is beginning to emerge. Second, it extends technology adoption frameworks such as Technology Acceptance Model and Technological Pedagogical Content Knowledge by incorporating ethical and pedagogical safety considerations for the use of generative AI. Third, it highlights how teachers envision using AI image generation tools not only for producing static artifacts but also for supporting learning processes. Specifically, we investigate three research questions in this study: Research Question [RQ] 1. How do K-12 teachers perceive the potential benefits and drawbacks of using the AI image generation tool for their instructional practices? RQ 2. What pedagogical and ethical concerns do teachers raise regarding the integration of these tools in the classroom?

RQ 3. What forms of support and training do teachers identify as necessary for the responsible and effective use of AI image generation tools in education?

## Research contexts and participants

We recruited six K-12 public school teachers from the Midwestern U.S. across diverse grade levels (i.e., elementary to high school) and subject areas included Spanish, math, English, science, and social studies, and library media. Their teaching experience  ranged from 5 to more than 20 years. The schools they serve enroll multilingual learners and racially diverse student populations, with many of participants teaching in schools that have significant Latinx student populations.

We conducted semi-structured interviews to collect data. Each interview was conducted via a video conferencing platform Zoom and lasted approximately 60 to 90 minutes. The interview protocol was designed in three parts. First, a warm-up section included questions about the teachers’ current practices for using visual materials, their criteria for selecting them, and any challenges they faced. The second part consisted of a hands-on activity where participants generated images of a character after reading th e excerpt extracted from the children’s book titled Stella Díaz has something to say (Dominguez, 2018) using ChatGPT. This book and the excerpt tell  the story of a young Latina girl, Stella, from an immigrant family adjusting to her new school in the U.S. This story was intentionally selected for its direct relevance to the study’s participants, many of whom teach in schools with significant Latinx student populations. The final part of the interview was a reflective discussion about the experience, the perceived potential and ethical concerns of using AI for creating educational materials, and the kinds of support teachers feel needed. All interviews were video-recorded with participant consent and transcribed verbatim for analysis.

For data analysis, we employed a thematic analysis approach to analyze the interview transcripts, following the inductive process (Braun & Clarke, 2006). The analysis began with a phase of familiarization, where the researchers repeatedly read the transcripts to gain a deep understanding of the content. Next, we conducted an initial open coding, assigning descriptive codes to segments of the data that captured teachers’ specific ideas and concerns. This process involved using the constant comparative method to group related codes into broader categories and identify emerging patterns across six interviews. These categories were then reviewed, refined, and consolidated to develop a set of coherent and distinct themes that captured the essence of the participants’ experiences and perspectives. Through this process, four final themes were identified. Table 1 summarizes the four themes emerged from code groups.

Through analysis, we found four key themes that illustrate the complex and contradictory views on teachers regarding AI image generation tools in education. These themes detail their perceptions of the GenAI as a practical tool, a creative catalyst, a source of significant ethical risk, and a new frontier demanding guidance. Tool of efficiency and customization 1095 Across all interviews, teachers expressed a consistent hope that AI image generation tools could alleviate the significant time constraints they face. They contrasted the potential speed of AI with their current, often laborious, job, captured these laborious tasks by stating, “If anything can help make it faster and more engaging and like, fun for kids to look at, I’m like, Cool. I’ll do whatever will make it easier and better for everybody.” This desire for efficiency is intrinsically linked to the need for highly customized visuals that are pedagogically and culturally appropriate for their specific students. However, producing such visuals demands substantial effort. For instance, Laura needed a very specific visual to teach vocabulary to English language learners, an animation for the verb “turn,” because “it’s really hard to get a picture of turn” as a static image. Similarly, Mike noted that his district’s curriculum is “ lacking visuals” and can be “culturally insensitive,” which compels him to spend his own time finding or creating more suitable materials. This context explains why the promise of AI-driven efficiency feels so transformative for teachers.

Catalyst for creativity and engagement Beyond efficiency, some teachers expressed optimism about the potential for AI image generation tools to foster creativity and deepen student engagement. They envisioned the tools as a way to move beyond static, pre-made visuals toward more dynamic and ima ginative learning experiences.  Laura described an activity where students created a “movie trailer for books” based on novels students had read. In this project, instead of using existing artwork, students used AI features (e.g., AI image generation tools in Canva) to generate images of characters as they imagined them. Furthermore, Claire proposed an idea for using AI as a pedagogical catalyst to stimulate students’ critical thinking. She explained that when teaching Shakespeare’s Macbeth, she does not want to be the person that tells them what Lady Macbeth looks like, because there are many different ways the character could be interpreted. She envisioned an activity where students could “play the role of director or producer” by using an AI image generational tool to “cast” the character. For example, students could explore how Lady Macbeth’s motivations might be perceived differently if she were generated as an older woman or a woman who is not white. In this view, the GenAI serves not merely as a production tool, but as a pedagogical catalyst that can spark students’ creative expression.

Double-edge sword of representation Despite teachers’ enthusiasm, the most significant expressed theme was a profound concern regarding the GenAI’s ethical implications, particularly its tendencies towards bias and stereotypes. Teachers were acutely aware that AI models often reproduce and amplify harmful societal stereotypes. Maria explained that, in her own practice, she actively avoids stereotypical images like the “sombrero or poncho or maracas or tacos” when representing Latine to students. When confronted with an AI-generated image of a Latine character (see Figure 1 left), which she described as “stereotypical with the dark hair, dark eyes,” she immediately critiqued it. She pointed out that such depictions erase diversity within the Latine community, noting that a student from Argentina in her class could have “blonde hair and… blue eyes.”  This concern was rooted in teachers’ direct experiences with the biased nature of existing image sources, which they feared AI would inherent or even exacerbate. Laura described how a simple search for “man juggling” on the internet “is almost always a white man juggling,” forcing her to add racial or ethnic descriptors to find diverse pictures. Teachers’ concerns were also remarkably nuanced, extending to the risk of reinforcing biases within communities. Alice was hesitant to prompt for changes in skin 1096 color when generating an image of a character (Figure 1 right) in the excerpt, as she worried it would touch upon the sensitive issue of “racism within their culture, like light skin versus dark skin.” This highlights a deep awareness of the potential for even well-intended prompts to cause harm. Many teachers identified the root of the problem in the AI’s training data, with one explaining it as “the large language model upon which it’s built is largely by things that have been created by white lens.” Navigating uncharted territory Given the complex landscape of AI image generation tools’ benefits and risks, teachers consistently expressed feeling unequipped and unsupported in navigating the integration of these tools. This gave rise to a theme centering on the need for structured training and clear institutional guidance. The lack of formal support was a recurring sentiment. Claire stated, “There has been no training around how to do any of those things, and it’s been a lot of like up to teacher discretion.” This feeling of being unprepared was echoed by Casey who admitted, “with images, I feel kind of lost” and expressed a desire for “more training to help me decide what are the things we should be looking for and aware of.” Crucially, teachers called for professional development that transcends mere technical skills. They articulated a need for guidance on the pedagogical and ethical dimensions of AI use. Laura highlighted this by expressing her hope for a training that wasn’t led by “all people that looked like me,” because she wanted to “love to know what people from different cultures see that I’m missing,” underscoring a desire to understand and mitigate personal and algorithmic bias.  Furthermore, teachers emphasized the u rgent need for clear, school-or district-level policies. Casey recounted her experience of having to “be pushed for policy,” only for the district to eventually adopt a “very broad and generic policy” that failed to provide specific guidance for classroom use. She described the current situation as a “murky area,” leaving teachers to make critical decisions without a coherent framework. This theme highlights a critical gap between the availability of AI image generation tools and the institutional preparedness required to support their responsible and effective implementation of tools in schools. This study explored K-12 teachers’ initial perceptions of AI image generation tools, revealing a profound tension between their optimism for the GenAI’s practical potential and their deep-seated pedagogical and ethical concerns. The findings indicate that teachers view these tools not as simple instruments for creating visuals, but as complex systems that intersect directly with their core professional responsibilities. On the surface, teachers clearly recognized the AI image generation tools’ usefulness and ease of use. Their optimistic view about a tool that could offer efficiency and customization represents a desire to enhance their performance by reducing the laborious task of sourcing visual materials through GenAI. However, at the same time, they showed profound concerns about bias and stereotypes. This underscores a new technology’s pedagogical usefulness. As stated by the teacher during the interview, GenAI has the potential to generate super offensive images that distill stereotypes in the real-world. In an educational context, this capacity is actively harmful and therefore not truly “useful,” regardless of how quickly it operates. This suggests that for teachers, perceived usefulness is inextricably linked to pedagogical safety and ethical alignment. Furthermore, the fact that significant effort is required to write effective prompts and critically evaluate the outputs ironically points to a low perceived ease of use for teachers who did not want to waste their time in laborious tasks for material preparation. This suggests that, because perceived usefulness and ease of use are inseparable from ethical and pedagogical considerations in educational contexts, teachers’ adoption of AI image generation tools will likely remain tentative and cautious.

Our findings should be considered with the following limitations in mind. First, the scope of our sample, while providing depth, restricts the generalizability of the findings. The study is based on a small, qualitative sample of six teachers, all located within the U.S. The institutional policies, professional development opportunities, and specific student demographics of this region undoubtedly shaped the participants’ perspectives. Future research should therefore seek to validate and expand upon these findings with larger, more diverse samples of educators from a variety of national and international contexts. Second, this study captures teachers’ perceptions and initial reactions within an interview setting, not their sustained, day-to-day classroom practices. Therefore, future research should incorporate ethnographic and observational methods to investigate how AI image generation tools are actually integrated or rejected in the daily life of classrooms. GenAI is not a neutral tool; it is a mirror that reflects the biases, assumptions, and complexities of the data on which it is trained. The teachers in this study approached GenAI with a blend of professional optimism and profound responsibility.
