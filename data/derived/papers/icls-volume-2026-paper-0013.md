# When Dialogic and Thinking Moves Intersect: An Exploratory Study of Thinking- Routine-Guided Discussions in an EFL Classroom

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study reports on an English movie discussion course, where Visible Thinking routines were used to guide students’ analysis of films. Thinking routines were aligned with specific thinking moves to scaffold thinking. As a qualitative exploratory study, it investigates how these routines mediated student interaction and contributed to a thinking culture. Data included two focal groups’ discussions and student reflections, analyzed through dialogic and thinking move coding and thematic analysis. Findings show that routines improved dialogic interaction and strengthened the move of considering viewpoints, but deeper analysis occurred only when moves such as expressing and reasoning co-occurred with explaining/interpreting and supporting with evidence. Thematic analysis identified four elements of a thinking culture: teacher guidance, peer support, structured tasks, and confidence in expression. These findings provide fine-grained evidence of how dialogic and thinking moves intersect and how a thinking culture can benefit learning in EFL classrooms.

Approaches to fostering thinking Integrating the teaching of thinking into instruction has become a prominent educational trend. Resnick (1987) emphasized that the development of thinking is shaped by social interaction in social contexts. Such contexts provide learners with scaffolding and opportunities for participation. Collins et al. (1991) further stressed that expert modeling helps make implicit cognitive processes visible, and that learners deepen their reasoning and understanding by engaging in challenging problem-solving tasks with support through interaction. Many scholars have advanced approaches to foster the development of thinking in classrooms. Ennis (2018) proposed the infusion model, embedding critical thinking into school curricula. Mercer and Littleton (2007) introduced the Thinking Together program, stressing that dialogue is a social mode of thinking and demonstrating how exploratory talk enables students to reason jointly, negotiate meaning, and construct shared knowledge. Kuhn (Kuhn & Crowell, 2011)  emphasized dialogic argumentation as a structured social practice, where guided participation in discussion of significant issues develops students’ critical thinking skills. Ritchhart and colleagues

(2011) introduced Visible Thinking approach, which uses  thinking routines to scaffold cognitive processes and

make students’ thinking visible.  Despite their different emphases, these approaches share a common goal: integrating the teaching of thinking into the curriculum while highlighting the essential role of social interaction. Visible thinking and applications in language education Among these approaches, Visible Thinking has gained particular prominence. Developed by Ritchhart and colleagues (2011), Visible Thinking is grounded in a sociocultural view of learning and centers on making thinking visible through four core practices: the use of thinking routines, documentation, questioning, and listening. Thinking routines are simple, repeatable structures that scaffold specific thinking moves and externalize thinking in both individual and collective forms. For example, the See– Think–Wonder routine scaffolds three thinking moves: observing details (See), building explanations and interpretations (Think), and wondering (Wonder) about an artifact or text in relation to a given topic. Different sets of routines can be used to help students explore new topics, synthesize thoughts, and dig deeper into ideas. With the overarching aim of making thinking visible and deepening understanding, teachers may further extend students’ thinking by questioning, encouraging interaction, and requiring them to document their thoughts. Through repeated use, these practices establish a culture of thinking that nurtures thinking dispositions, fosters deep learning, increases engagement, and normalizes classroom discourse as a space for critical, creative, and metacognitive thinking (Ritchhart & Church, 2020). Scholars beyond the Visible Thinking tradition have similarly emphasized the importance of explicit guidance, modeling, and dialogic interaction (Tishman et al., 1993; Zhao et al., 2016). This emphasis aligns with Mercer’s view of dialogue as a social mode of thinking and with Kuhn’s conception of dialogic argumentation, underscoring that guided and carefully structured social interactions are indispensable to the development of students’ thinking.

Empirical research has applied thinking routines across diverse educational contexts. In language education, they are increasingly recognized for advancing the dual goals of language learning and thinking 52 development. Studies show that thinking routines can enhance language performance, for instance improving writing proficiency (Hooper, 2015), oral coherence (Arévalo Balboa & Briesmaster, 2018), and critical reading (Chen & Jan, 2023). They also foster thinking abilities, including reasoning with evidence and critical thinking (Hooper, 2015; Manurung et al., 2022). In addition, routines reshape interaction and classroom culture, encouraging participation (Dajani, 2016), supporting more open and dialogic discussion climates (Salmon, 2008; Chen, 2025), and facilitating dialogic interaction in classroom discussions (Khalid & Foo, 2021). This body of work points to the affordances of thinking routines as structured tools for integrating language learning with deeper forms of thinking and dialogic interaction.

The need for fine-grained analysis and localized understanding of thinking cultures Despite these contributions, two gaps remain. The first gap is the lack of fine-grained discourse analyses of smallgroup discussions guided by thinking routines. Existing studies relevant to thinking routines  have largely relied on standardized tests, ethnographic observations, students’ self-reports, or interviews, and only very few have examined actual group discussion data. Even when discourse data was analyzed (Khalid & Foo, 2021), the and thinking patterns. However, without such analysis, it is difficult to see how thinking routines scaffold the learning process in small-group discussions, which requires examining the intersection of dialogic interaction and thinking. The second gap relates to classroom culture. Ritchhart (2015) stressed that the repeated use of thinking routines helps shape cultures of thinking and outlined eight cultural forces. However, empirical studies have offered limited insight into how such a culture develops in EFL classrooms. To address these issues, this paper explores two research questions: (1) What dialogic and thinking patterns emerge during small-group discussions guided by thinking routines? (2) What elements support the development of a culture of thinking in an EFL classroom?

## Research design

This study is a qualitative exploratory classroom-based study, with the primary aim of exploring how learning occurs through the interplay of thinking and dialogic interaction and the development of  a culture of thinking.

## Context and instructional design

The study was conducted in an undergraduate English movie discussion course at a Taiwanese university. This 3credit, 18-week class enrolled 19 students from diverse majors, with an overall English proficiency around CEFR B1–B2. While classes were conducted in  English and most students communicated actively, some were less confident in expressing their ideas. The author served as both the course instructor and the researcher. This dual role fostered a trusting classroom environment conducive to authentic dialogue and provided a deep familiarity with the student s’ ongoing learning trajectories.  Four English films featuring social issues were chosen as core materials. To cultivate a classroom culture that values thinking and interaction, the course objective were explicitly aligned with four targeted thinking moves: explaining and interpreting, making connections, considering different viewpoints, and supporting with evidence. Visible Thinking routines structured students’ engagement from individual note-taking to small-group collaborative discussions on film plots, characters, and thematic issues. For instance, Story Map supported students in organizing storylines,  and Persona guided multifaceted character analysis. Taking Persona as an example, this routine facilitated character analysis by emphasizing two thinking moves: considering different viewpoints and building explanations and interpretations. Scaffolded by guiding questions and visual templates, students analyzed characters from multiple  angles (e.g., personality, beliefs, struggles) and supported their claims with relevant quotes. Groups then collaboratively constructed character maps on whiteboards. This physical externalization made their cognitive processes visible, enabling students to organize and integrate ideas, which deepened the discussion and provided a basis for later reflection. To examine how thinking routines mediated cognitive and dialogic interactions, Persona was selected as the primary analytical case. This recurrent routine encourages multi-faceted character analysis, offering rich opportunities to observe the intersection of thinking and dialogue. Guided by field observations of their distinct interactional styles and group composition, two focal groups (4 students each) were purposefully sampled. Their 30-minute discussions were recorded at Week 3 (baseline) and Week 9 (with Persona). Transcripts were analyzed using a turn-level dual-coding framework. To capture internal cognitive actions, four Thinking Moves (TM) (Ritchhart et al., 2011) were coded: explaining and interpreting (EI), making connections 53 (C), considering viewpoints (VP), and supporting with evidence (EV). These were selected for aligning directly with the course's media-analysis objectives. To analyze external social interactions, five Dialogic Moves (DM) were selectively adapted from the SEDA framework (Hennessy et al., 2016): expressing ideas (E), building on thoughts (B), positioning (P), reasoning (R), and coordinating (CO).  Rather than utilizing SEDA’s 33 granular sub-codes, these five clusters were chosen to maintain analytical clarity. Crucially, these DMs were operationalized to capture social interaction (e.g., expressing focuses on initiating thoughts; reasoning as the inviting or providing evidence; building on as taking up a peer's thought). This deliberate separation from cognitive TMs enables precise examination of their intersection.  Parsing of transcripts was based on speaker utterances. Because a single utterance could serve multiple functions, it was possible for one line to be coded with more than one move. Task-related repetitions or fillers were excluded. Following coding, the frequency of each code was calculated. This enabled us to examine code sequences, overall patterns, and their co-occurrences, as well as to compare differences across tasks and groups. As this proceeding represents an exploratory phase testing the dual-coding framework, transcript micro-analysis was conducted solely by the researcher. A second coder was not utilized, which I acknowledge as a limitation.

To investigate elements contributing to a thinking culture,  18 students’ reflections were collected and analyzed using thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2012).  The reflections were first deductively screened for instances relevant to the focus of thinking and interaction, and then initial codes were inductively generated from the raw data, which were iteratively reviewed, refined, and consolidated into broader overarching themes. NVivo 14 was used to manage the coding process. Finally, to ensure triangulation and enhance the trustworthiness of the qualitative study, thinking routine artifacts, weekly class observation journals and field notes were also collected. ## Quality of discussion through thinking–dialogic move interconnections

A comparison of the two groups highlights that the effects of the Persona  routine were not uniform, and that the quality of character analysis depended on how thinking moves and dialogic moves interconnected. Differences were determined by  topical continuity and on the quality of “explaining and interpreting” and “supporting with evidence.” Moreover, when “reasoning” was followed by “building on” and co-occurred with “supporting with evidence,” students not only justified their own claims but also invited peers to do so. In particular, when evidence was tied to specific plot details and elaborated through peer contributions, the discussion anchored the topic, propelled subsequent turns, and led to deeper thinking and more nuanced interpretations. Interconnections among elements in building a thinking culture Beyond the individual contributions of each element, the analysis also revealed how they worked together to reinforce one another in building a culture of thinking. Teacher guidance and affirmative responses first created emotional safety, which was then amplified by peer trust and support, generating a classroom climate where students felt safe and encouraged to contribute. Building on this supportive atmosphere, structured thinking-routine tasks complemented the affective support by offering clear analytic frames and collaborative requirements that integrated thinking with interaction. These tasks not only helped students organize and analyze ideas more systematically but also prompted sustained negotiation and multi-perspective reasoning. Over time, the interplay 58 of affective support, peer rapport, and structured routines enabled students to overcome anxiety and develop confidence and fluency in oral expression. In short, teacher guidance laid the foundation, peer relations strengthened openness, structured tasks sustained depth, and the resulting ease of expression further fed back into participation—together forming a virtuous cycle that cultivated a classroom culture where both thinking and social interaction are valued and supported. This synergy highlights that  building a culture of thinking is a dynamic process of mutually reinforcing factors rather than a sum of isolated efforts. This study yielded two key findings. First, the repeated use of thinking routines led to notable growth in students’ thinking moves as well as greater dialogicality in their small-group discussions. Beyond this growth, the analysis highlighted how thinking moves and dialogic moves interconnected during these discussions. This research bridges a gap in prior work by moving beyond product-focused outcomes and ethnographic observations to provide a fine-grained analysis of dialogic and thinking moves, revealing patterns in student small-group talk and the interplay between them that have not been fully examined. The analysis showed that greater dialogic activity —such as more frequent expressing ideas or building on—did not necessarily indicate deeper thinking. Dialogic moves must connect with explaining, interpreting, and supporting with evidence to raise the quality of discussion. These variations in depth appear to be shaped by the interplay between language proficiency and group dynamics. While unelaborated plot accumulation may reflect language constraints, deeper analysis seemed to emerge when group dynamics established a norm of reasoning. Crucially, when group dynamics foster peer scaffolding to navigate language barriers and encourage elaborative descriptions of plot details, it might lay the groundwork for more evidence-based reasoning. This specific emphasis on evidentiary rigor resonates with Kuhn’s (Kuhn & Crowell, 2011) notion of dialogic argumentation and Resnick et al.’s (2018) concept of Accountable Talk. Pedagogically, teachers must look beyond surface-level participation. By monitoring specific move s, educators can better identify where facilitation is needed, model effective exchanges, and design prompts that explicitly press for reasoning, ultimately ensuring that classroom interactions translate into rigorous reasoning.

The second major finding concerned the elements that help cultivate a classroom culture of thinking: (1) teacher guidance and affective support, (2) peer interaction and a supportive climate, (3) structured tasks that foster thinking and perspective exploration, and (4) the development of ease and confidence in expression through interaction. These elements resonate with Ritchhart’s (2015) eight forces that shape a culture of thinking. Among these, thinking routines —together with modeling, interactions, and  expectations—constitute a driving force of cultural formation. Our findings also echo Brown’s (1992) emphasis on social community, showing how students, through dialogic interaction, began to adopt shared discourse, structures, and values. In addition, they support Nystrand’s (1997) notion of dialogically organized instruction emphasizes that learning to think requires effective interaction, and that the quality of dialogue establishes a suitable climate for learning while also communicating the teacher’s expectations and stance. In our study, this is evident in how teacher mediation—through explicit guidance and an encouraging attitude —both directed inquiry and reassured students, fostering a cultural climate in which contributions were validated and participation was sustained. Taken together, these resonances highlight the centrality of classroom culture and clarify the mechanisms by which such a culture is enacted. While the findings align with existing frameworks on classroom culture, they further contribute contextspecific insights for EFL settings, particularly regarding students’ socio-emotional needs and thinking-centered instructional design. On the socio-emotional side, the course reduced language anxiety by creating a safe space for expression, encouraging participation especially among quieter students, and fostering a positive cycle of interaction and motivation when ideas were listened to  and respected. On  the instructional side, it embedded visible thinking routines as structured tasks with cycles of individual and collective reasoning, supporting interpretation, multi-perspective analysis, and knowledge building. While I acknowledge that language proficiency may pose challenges to students’ dialogic performance, the findings indicate that both emotional and instructional support not only enhanced students’ confidence and comfort in expressing ideas but also provide d an essential foundation and atmosphere for more meaningful peer interaction.  Taken together,  t hese findings suggest that effective implementation of thinking routines requires a dual focus on cognitive scaffolding and affective support, ensuring both deeper engagement and higher-quality interaction in EFL classrooms. This study also carries limitations. Because I adopted purposeful case selection, the analysis focused on two groups and compared their discussions before and after the use of thinking routines. While this comparison yielded important insights into dialogic –thinking move interconnections, it does not capture the full range of possible patterns. Future studies could extend this work by involving more groups and exploring varied routines and moves, thereby deepening understanding of these interconnections and offering further implications for classroom dialogue guided by thinking routines.
