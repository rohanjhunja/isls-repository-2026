# Stories, Futures and Histories: Co-design Routines to Center Community Knowledge for Environmental Education in India

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Centering co-design as a site of social justice, we analyze a collaboration between learning scientists and local teachers from a non-profit organization in the Himalayan region of Uttarakhand, India. We ask, what design routines support the emergence of c ommunity-driven ideas for ecological education, and how do they build on each other to deepen cultural, imaginative, and historical engagement? Data for this paper comes from the initial phase of collaboration, which consisted of six online meetings, each two hour long. We conducted thematic analysis along to identify three interconnected routines: (i) autecology narratives surfaced ecological connections grounded in community and personal rituals, (ii) speculative futuring opened imaginative engagement with communication, technology, and markets; and (iii) interactions with community elders supported the development of critical historicity. Together, these routines illustrate a co-design approach which fosters collective sense-making integrating cultural, imaginative, and historical dimensions of community life.

Across the Learning Sciences, researchers increasingly recognize co-design as a central methodology for creating and studying learning environments that are responsive to local contexts (Penuel, 2007; Severance et al., 2018). Initially conceived as a pragm atic strategy to align innovations with classroom realities, co-design has evolved into a powerful site for reimagining the relationship between knowledge, culture, and institutions (Bang et al., 2015). Research in community-based and participatory design traditions has demonstrated how collaborative design can reposition historically marginalized participants as epistemic contributors (Bang & Vossoughi, 2016). Scholars working in place-based pedagogies and Indigenous contexts have further shown that co-design can center relational epistemologies that connect human and more-than-human worlds, linking learning to both ecological and ethical relations.

This shift parallels a growing recognition that environmental crises are also epistemic crises, arising from extractive ways of knowing that separate people from the ecologies they inhabit (Bang & Marin, 2015). In regions such as Uttarakhand, India, fragil e mountain ecosystems face increasing pressures from tourism, urbanization, and climate change. Education that reconnects children to local environments and cultural practices is therefore crucial. Despite its ecological and cultural richness, Uttarakhand’s environmental education remains guided by national frameworks that often overlook local knowledge and biocultural diversity. Building on prior work that views co-design as a site of epistemic negotiation, we extend this conversation by examining how specific design routines can make community-based knowledge visible and actionable for ecological education. We draw on a co-design process between learning scientists and teachers from a local NGO in Uttarakhand, where we collaboratively developed an environmental education workshop for children. Our analyses focus on the early phases of solidarity driven co-design, which some scholars have called “Relationship building & Theorizing” (Ishimaru et al., 2018). We ask: What design routines support the emergence of community-driven ideas for ecological education, and how do they build on each other to deepen cultural, imaginative, and historical engagement?

Co-design and community based design Early work in co-design within the Learning Sciences primarily focused on improving curriculum design and supporting teacher learning. Severance et al. (2018) analyzed a co-design project through a cultural-historical activity theory (CHAT) lens, showing how collaborative curriculum development could foster teacher’s 945 transformative agency as they aligned instruction with reform goals such as the Next Generation Science Standards. Subsequent scholarship expanded co-design’s scope to frame partnerships as enactments of epistemological pluralism, particularly in place-based science learning centered on Indigenous ontologies and human – more-than-human relations (Bang and Vossoughi 2016). We build for the existing scholarship on codesign that seeks an expansion and transformation of existing ways of engaging in relationships broadly. To do so we use the following constructs.

Critical historicity Bang et al. (2015) highlight the role of critical historicity as integral to expanding the scope of what is treated as relevant in educational settings and experiences, especially if we are to contend seriously with what shapes our nature (comprising of human and more than human beings) and culture (as the dynamic network of relationships between all entities). Without a conscious engagement with dynamics of power, the agency that learning experiences can foster tend to circumscribe the radical new possibil ities, and struggle to transform the relationships and patterns that produce ongoing patterns of domination and harm. Gutiérrez & Jurow (2016) through their work in SDEs also describe fostering critical historicity in community actors and learners as the experience of situating themselves with a sense of time, place and journey across systems and powered relationships. This fosters a unique and powerful sense of belonging and agency, which is conscious and critical of past and ongoing challenges both internal and exogenous to different communities. Intergenerational learning This sense of time and place rooted in shared and interacting cultures, cannot be functional without a conscious and sustained engagement with community participants across generations (Bang et al., 2015). This generational isolation and distinction is reinforced across a large variety of learning and professional environments, and often leads to epistemic assimilation and erasure across domains (Alcala et al., 2014). Often identified as emerging from Western-style schooling and a pattern of industrialization and development, “post-colonial” states like India carry a marked fracture of imposed schooling structures and development patterns that widen this generational divide (Chaudhary, 2015). The generational divide is not only a pattern in human relationships, but also in our interactions with more than human beings – diluting a recognition of trees and animals of varying ages and histories in our environment as playing different roles, and engaging in an ever-changing landscape of engaging with each other.

Speculative futuring In pushing for axiological innovations that have a commitment not only to relationships but also to ways of thinking, we draw on the emerging work on speculative education (Garcia & Mirra, 2023) as a guiding star towards imagining and enacting futures beyond the horizon. This explicitly connects to work on critical historicity

– specifically situating learners as historical actors (Gutierrez et al., 2019) is a key step towards helping develop

a critical agency towards making future worlds (Perez, 2025). Simultaneously, providing the opportunity to imagine futures, both radically different and grounded in current realities, are a critical tool for fostering hopeful learning experiences  – with goals that excite, and pathways that feel rewarding and directed (H oulden & Veletsianos, 2023).

Co-design within environmental education Meixi et al. (2022) argues that co-designing schools with Indigenous systems of relationality can cultivate life sustaining educational practices in the face of ecological disasters. Scholars have developed frameworks for codesign to promote sustainable development and enable institutions to embed sustainability holistically across disciplines, fostering student competencies for real-world ecological and social transformation (Ahmad et al., 2023). Complex socio-ecological challenges such as climate change and biodiversity loss have resulted in an increased focus on the co-creation of knowledge (Robinson et al., 2024). But environmental education as a discipline can be hard to unsettle in practice, and community initiatives and knowledge systems may struggle to gain legitimacy. Sharing an experience about building terrace farms in India, Dutta (2022) shares how these activities were seen as' 'extra curricular’’ and teachers were reluctant to “transgress their own disciplinary boundaries and embrace the ‘possibilities of radical openness’ offered at the farm site. Building on such insights, we analyse a co-design process situated in rural Uttrakhand, India, to understand how locally grounded collaborations can surface community driven ideas for environmental education. We ask: how candesign routines aimed at community-driven and centered ecological education deepen cultural, historical, and imaginative engagement?

This study draws on twelve hours of recorded codesign meetings conducted with the aim to explore and design a locally relevant environmental education workshop for children in Uttarakhand. The co-design sessions were structured and facilitated by the US-based researchers via prompts designed to scaffold the work of exploring ecological and cultural connection, intergenerational learning, and historic change. We designed our co-design sessions’ trajectory to traverse scales of people, time, and systems. To do this, we began with surfacing personal connections and awareness of our environments.

Our analytic process was iterative, participatory, and theory-informed. We began with 12 hours of codesign meeting recordings, design artifacts produced during the meetings and data collected by participants from the community, which were transcribed and reviewed jointly by the research team and community teachers from Rang Karavan. Through cycles of open and axial coding, we identified episodes where participant’s talk or actions reflected critical historicity, intergenerational learning, a nd axiological innovation, themes drawn from literature on communitybased and participatory design research. We then traced how these principles appeared across activities and how certain routines more consistently supported community-driven ideas for eco logical education. Rather than treating these as new discoveries, we understand them as intentional yet evolving practices, designed with theoretical commitments, and empirically shown to enable the emergence of community-grounded ecological ideas. We found three important and interconnected co-design routines that helped surface community knowledge. The use of autecological narratives  surfaced existing cultural connections, personal rituals, and intergenerational learning. The use of fantasy narratives  opened imaginative discussions about communication, technology, and local economies. Auto-ecological narratives Our first routine is focused on the first stage of co-design that emphasizes by starting with what people care about as a generative resource for curriculum, rather than by centering disciplinary concepts or tasks (Bang et al). Informed by this framework, we started our co-design process with “auto-ecological narratives”, an activity in which participants were asked to share stories, practices, or experiences that reflect their connection with the more-than-human world. The aim was to build relationships with each other by sharing our relationship with nature. In our analyses of these narratives, we found that participants shared different kinds of ecological connections and also how they were formed, which could inform the environmental education workshop we were designing.

A major theme that emerged across the narratives was how ecological narratives were embedded in cultural rituals. For example, one of the participants, Kiran, shared her connection to a ritual related to Naulas, which are traditional freshwater springs, an example of indigenous water architecture of the Himalayan region. “When the bride comes home after the wedding, she removes the crown and other ornaments from her head, and then they go to the naula (a traditional water source)...When I got married, I also performed this ritual…But now, as I see, this tradition has disap peared. People now perform these rituals in their bathrooms because the naulas are not being preserved. It makes me very sad that people are doing pre-wedding shoots and many other things, but the Naula Poojan, which is a part of our culture and tradition and also helps in preserving nature, is disappearing.” 947 Her narrative conveys the value of rituals in creating and sustaining an empathetic and ethical relationship with the more than human world. It highlights how water is not just a resource but an active part of the social and cultural life of people. It also hints at how she views the temporal rupture around the practice of “Naula Pujan”, contrasting the modern uptake of pre-wedding shoots with declining community based customs. Another participant, Jyothi, shared about a local festival called Harela, during which people plant trees, and “it's said that any tree planted on that day doesn't dry up.” Harela, in the local language Kumaoni, means “Day of Green”, deeply rooted in the agrarian culture of Uttarakhand, and is traditionally associated with the monsoon season (July-August). Apart from festivals dedicated to nature, participants also shared how they improvised some other festivals to extend kinship relationships to plants. Lata shared how as a child she tied “Rakhi”, which is a sacred thread that sisters tie to their brothers, to trees around her house. She added, “I still remember that one tree where I tied a rakhi has grown very big now. I see it while passing by.” Reflecting on her childhood, Lata also shared that, “I used to talk to trees, hug them, and sit there for a while because the path to our house was like a forest.” In addition to collective cultural rituals for building ecological connections, participants also described personal rituals and aesthetic associations. Jyoti shared, “There’s a place in my house from where you can see the entire village, and that place heals me… I have named all the trees in my house. Every single tree has a name.” Her practice of naming trees conveys the deep emotional bonds she has cultivated with her surroundings. She also spoke about the embodied nature of this connection: “I love walking barefoot. I don’t know why, but I get scolded a lot at home for it. Still, I walk barefoot everywhere in the fields. I love the connection I feel with the soil when I walk barefoot.” Similarly, Vishesh described how he has begun to smell flowers more often and learn their names. “Recently, while walking randomly, I ate a fruit from a bush,” he added. These narratives express a form of relationship with the environment that emerges not through organized ritual or formal knowledge systems, but through everyday gestures of intimacy, curiosity, and sensory engagement. They reveal micro-practices that sustain an intimate sense of being-in-relation. Ankit’s narrative highlighted how ecological connection shaped his creative expression. He shared, “When I wrote my first poem, it was about the smoke that spread throughout the environment due to a forest fire.” Another important theme that stood out across auto-ecological narratives was the intergenerational learning of these connections. The stories unfolded as intergenerational threads, where knowing, feeling, and acting toward the environment were shaped through relationships across age, kin, and authority. Participant’s ecological sensibilities appeared less as outcomes of formal education and more as relational transmissions, carried through acts of care, discipline, labor, and storytelling across generations. Kiran shared, “There is a naula near my mother's place, the water from it is very tasty, although it's used less frequently nowadays, my mother still maintains it, keeping it clean.” Her mother’s daily act of cleaning and tending the water evokes the eth ic of persistence and care. Jyoti recollected a moment when she was scolded by a senior in her organization for throwing a wrapper that initiated her own journey of trying to keep public spaces around her clean. Ecological consciousness here is not abstract, but emergent from a relationship where being corrected affirms belonging and responsibility. Ankit shared, “My connection with nature is strong because my father was in the forest department, so I grew up in places surrounded by forests.” His story of planting trees alongside his father, a forest department worker, evokes what scholars have described as observation and chipping in (Rogoff et al., 2015).  His memory of being the smallest planter ties the institutional world of forestry to intimate childho od learning, showing how participation rather than explanation transfers environmental values. Gautam’s memory of walking into the forest with his grandmother deepens this pattern. He shared,  “I felt brave with my grandmother, and we would go to collect wood…We would explore, and I would hear stories like, "Don't touch this plant," or "Don't touch random things," because some plants in the forest, if touched, could make you lose your memory or forget your way.” Taken together, these accounts show that auto-ecological narratives did not merely reveal personal attachments to the more than human world, but they surfaced an ethic of intergenerational learning, where ecological knowledge was carried in gestures, admonitions, labor, and lore. Learning appears as a slow, embodied process of being shaped by others, mothers who clean, mentors who scold, fathers who plant, grandmothers who warn. Starting with auto-ecological narratives surfaced critical connections about ecology and learning. Rather than starting from environmental concepts, the process began from what participants valued and remembered, transforming their lived histories into epistemic insights for curriculum design. As participants heard each other’s narratives, they  noticed how these connections are changing. For instance, Jyoti mentioned, “when I look at my village today compared to five years ago, there have been many changes. I fear that in a few years, my village might not remain a village, it might become a city.” We deepened such collective reflection with co-design routines that focused on historical changes and possible futures, illustrated in the following sections. 948 Speculative futuring Our second routine was to extend our personal narratives, as well those of young people from our communities, to thinking about futures. As Garcia & Mirra (2023) describe, this was intended to see beyond normative horizons of learning, culture, and being in connection with one another. Envisioning fantastical utopias that respond to and still emerge from the world and life as we know it opens up the space for solutions one pursues in creative and joyful manners, while providing unique lenses into our values and concerns. The themes we identified mirrored and extended those found in the autoecological narratives, and also foreshadowed some themes playing a role in our historicizing exercise. A constant and ubiquitous theme across all the fantasies involved deep and diverse dives into connection – often expressed as the ability of communication, but also in other ways of being in community, engaging in negotiation, and also through transportation. Stories of transportation involved both travel to different places, as well as the transfer of materials.  Materials in turn emerged as another theme, explored through the lens of technology, foods, and even construction. In response to a prompt in the futuring activity, we describe how stories envisioned education and learning in novel ways, which in turn spanned aspects of connection and materials. Here we present summaries and vignettes from the futuring activity and reflection that show how these themes were explored and acted as articulations of lived concerns, interests, and val ues. While not recurring across these stories, we also elevate examples of bringing in intergenerational community in unique manners as this theme spans across the sessions we present in this study. Connection: Every fantasy involved the ability to communicate with other plants and animals. For some, it was central to the story created. For instance, Kiran’s story depicted trees, children, and rivers talking to each other, and addressing the trees’ concern of how snowfall is weighing down on them. The river and child share that the upcoming sunlight should help melt some snow, but if it is not enough, the river offers to try and wash some snow off of the trees. This embodies a rich space of communication that involves sharing concerns, knowledge, and even offers of help. It depicts a dynamic of mutuality and care that offers a rich texture of realism in a fantasy that is not devoid of concern, but is handled with love.

Pankaj and Lata imagined a unique reflection of empathy and perspective taking, describing an ability to “exchange heads”: “just as I share very close and important things with a friend, the way of sharing here would be that you can take them on a journey. If I put my head on Lata's body, I am essentially allowing Lata to go through my memories, my past, and all my stories.” Their fantasy also involved people talking to mountains, and asking for trees and fruit from each other, where the river is their medium of transport and exchange: “In our imaginary world, the mountains are lush and full of greenery, teeming with animals, and they all chat and gossip a lot. And the special help we will take from them is that we will call them. So, we call the mountain and say, "Hey, the tree you have there is an apple tree. Send it over to us," and our river will be the medium of transport”. This was an exciting novel envisioning of natural formations like mountains as alive and agentic, and the river as a medium of travel and exchange. They extend this role of the river further, saying, “if a goat needs to graze, we will put it in the river  from our house, and it will go and come back through the river. If we need to go somewhere, we will travel through the river. So, if we need to go from Tanakpur to Champawat, we will use the river”. This story also involved houses as a talking entity: “our houses will be chilling with us in the evenings, telling their stories, like how houses are made of different things, with wood and other materials.” This subtly connected to a pertinent issue of ghost villages (NPR, 2023), which we reflected on during our conversation: “nowadays, in villages, people leave their homes empty. They don't know the value of what happened at that time. And those who know, they can't leave the house. They are so attached that they don't even think about leaving it, like the elders who often stay in the market. They are very troubled, always wanting to go back home. So it would be wonderful if the houses could share their story or someone could record it and share it more”. Materials: There were recurring mentions of considering the materials that shape life. Pankaj & Lata’s imagination of talking houses included envisioning hut-like houses, and houses made of wood. It is likely that this vision maintains a more connected awareness of construction materials. They can see wood around them as living and “natural”, in contrast to cement and steel which often engender a sense of disconnection and otherness around where they come from. There was a playful imagining of all foods as healthy. They imagined that all foods will be organic, and “even pizza and momos will be healthy”. This both valued preserving a sense of joy in the shared universal experience of food, and articulated the harm s of foods coming from the harmful farming and cooking practices than embedded in dishes themselves.

A particularly unique exploration of materials as resources engaged in cycles of development and through layers of systemic power emerged in a story where exploring possible concerns of trees included the vision that there will still be an overcutting of trees. Kirandescribed this as: “ Even in the imaginary world, it seems that development is happening somewhere, so if development is needed, there will be some loss. So, for 949 some reason, my mind is imagining tree cutting even in the imaginary world.” This delved into imagining how people making developmental decisions are still disconnected from the actual trees and lumberjacks, and how the trees, children and rivers might collectively plea for more conversation and negotiation about changing approaches to cutting.

Jyoti and Ankit’s transportation technologies involved “screens” and “anywhere doors” enabling people to travel instantly. This foreshadows a recurring concern around migration that emerged in our critical historicizing activity, but also connected to very creative visions of education. Education: In their dreams of teleportation, they described travel not just to physical places, but also inside books and their worlds. This was a creative take on extending the idea of travel into innovative visions of learning – as being in different times and plac es and living out new experiences. They also overlaid their imaginations of connections with more-than-humans here, envisioning schools as these novel places where human learners can join similar schools of aquatic life and other wildlife and le arn together. This same fantasy explored expanding the spaces of participation in various ways. They also highlighted that both schools and play spaces will be free of age restrictions, enabling people across generations to play together, learn and study together, and there will be no restrictions on who can engage in different activities. This also connected to our recurring theme of intergenerational community, and also engaged around the negative side of (social) connection – addressing that no one will be relegated to being in separate spaces regardless of gender, caste, or other class. Intergenerational and critical historicizing Our third routine extended the auto-ecological narratives to reflect on the history of ecological connections of their region. We used two tools to engage participants in this activity. First, we invited the participants to engage in a semi structured inte rview with community elders (often family members and grandparents). The interview was collectively designed by the participants and focussed on getting the elders to share their own personal memories and future ecological aspirations. Second, we used an activity that invited participants to map a timeline of ecological relations spread across five decades. Guided by elder interviews, they reflected on how these relationships changed and why. To build critical historicity in our design process with elders serves the purposes of including plural epistemologies and intergenerational community. Plural epistemologies are surfaced in handling lived stories, narratives and accounts with care and balancing these alongside narrower but often more dominant forms of historical sources and analyses like textbooks or written documents. Relatedly, intergenerational community is also strengthened in including elders as valuable sources of knowledge – where we can richly learn from their experiences and values, but also handle them with care and criticality towards fostering just and inclusive futures.

Here we highlight instances of reflecting on these interviews with elders, and combining it with our current understandings and values led to a productive complication of understanding the earlier ascribed relationships, values, and challenges between different peoples, wildlife, and systems. Through iterative grouping of the interconnected themes and ideas we noticed in our discussions, we present a sequence of vignettes that depict a layering and complexifying of narratives and factors shaping these changing relationships. These act as the stacking of highlighting markets and technology  as reshaping many relationships from those of leisure, businesses, and changing farming practices causing bush overgrowth and increased wild animal presence. Markets and Technology: Technology was discussed as hampering the ways people (especially youth) connected with the environment. This was in contrast to the plurality of connections we saw through the autoecological narrative activity. Pankaj shared,  “our means of entertainment changed when you got a TV. Until now, your entertainment was sitting on a tree, picking fruits, playing, climbing up and down trees, playing catch. You were playing in the forests. You played with things that were easily available around you…” With the proliferation of technology, roads, and more connected markets, we also engaged with prominent narratives engaging with systems of scale, discussing how policy and businesses harmed the ecosystem of forests and rivers in their state of Uttarakhand. Ankit shared, “One important thing that came out was that around the 1960s, and even before that, the were profitable and were introduced from outside. Because of that, many interrelated problems arose, like forest fires started, and many losses occurred due to it. The losses were such that villagers were appointed to prevent people from entering. So, it was quite interesting. An interconnected analysis of business, loss, government officers, and villagers. And I think in contemporary times, there was talk about rivers and their wastage. As the local population is increasing, sewage is also going into the rivers. And small factories are also contributing to it.” 950 These reflections also connected to a current challenge experienced in the city of Champawat and nearby areas, of shrub overgrowth and animals in urban areas: Lata shared, “there was more farming in the past, so there were good systematic practices where they cleared the bushes and cleaned all the fields so that planting could happen in the planting season and harvesting could happen in the harvest season. But actually, the similar thing about the market is that when the market increased, there was more convenience and availability to buy things, so the work of clearing the bushes decreased, leading to more growth.” Overgrowth from Migration:  These mechanics of markets also played a role in the narrative of migration as a cause of this overgrowth. Some elders described that many younger people have tended to leave the villages for richer educational and economic opportunities in bigger cities. This stood out to them for a reduced capacity to maintain features like avoiding overgrowth of bushes which is associated with an increased presence of wild animals like leopards in the town. Jyoti shared, “By September, all the fields around the village would be cleared of grass. [...] Then, from March, things would start growing again. This cycle kept the village clean, with no overgrown grass or bushes. However, now people have left the villages for better education and jobs. They want good education for their children, which they can't find in the village. [...] This migration has disrupted the cycle of farming and cleaning.” Migration from Social (Dis)Connections:  While migration was first attributed back to markets and technologies (like economic, education, and transport opportunities), a critical exploration emerged through reflecting on past (and evolving) practices of social (dis)connection – specifically the shaping of social networks on the foundations of gender and caste based discrimination. Reflections of how play and other social engagements were shaped by inequitable practices, contributed to a key critical lens of the past and causes of migration leading to the challenge of overgrowth and wildlife. Lata had heard accounts of her grandmother’s socialization being segregated by gender – often boys and girls would play different kinds of games but rarely crossing gender lines. This informed a speculation that prior gender and caste discrimination also likely played a role in this phenomenon of migration. It also stands out how she leveraged a shared value around education and “science” as the basis for casting doubt on the validity of their elders’ misinformed (and harmful) practices around discrimination. Kiran shared, “We talked about gender discrimination and caste discrimination, which are very prevalent in our village. I mean, if we talk to our elders, they still strongly believe in and follow these things. Um, now I don't know if this is positive or negative, but because of this, there are many beliefs in the village that, um, according to the science we've studied, are complete myths. So now, um, people are migrating, leaving their homes or nearby places.” Intergenerational community is reified by valuing elders’ accounts while developing tools and awareness to integrate their viewpoints and learnings. This includes learning from the shortcomings and harmful aspects of their practices, approaches, and logics that we want to consciously interrupt. Developing this capacity is not only critical for today’s community formation and design, but sets a template of sustainable and reflexive growth that provides avenues for our target younger learners to learn from what we share with them as well as build on it towards more informed and community centered futures that learn from the mistakes of the past. Close to the end of this reflection session, Pankaj articulated a description of the interconnectedness across all t hese phenomena and concerns: “I also realized something similar to what Gautam was saying. I think when we talk about the environment, many things are connected. When we talk about culture, the environment is also there. When we talk about issues like migration, they are also somehow connected to our environment. When we talk about education, it's obviously connected. I think initially, what we are trying to do is to show that everything is interconnected. This is something I just realized.” The co-design routine that invited participants to deliberate on the longer history of their place, the changes it has gone through and its possible explanations, helped us look at critical connections between social and ecological systems. Such insights were generative to expand the scope of environmental education should look like and entail.

951 This study examined how three co-design routines, auto-ecological narratives, speculative futuring, and intergenerational historicizing, supported the emergence of community-driven ecological education. Prior work has highlighted the role of personal narra tives in learning and co-design (Gutiérrez & Jurow, 2018; Tzou et al., 2020). We extend this work by showing that when intentionally oriented toward ecological relations, narratives can function not only as expressions of experience but as sites for producing more-than-human relational knowledge and the pedagogical conditions through which such knowledge is sustained, including ritual, affect, sensory practice, and intergenerational transmission. Rather than beginning with environmental problems, curricular goals, or negations of dominant worldviews (Bang, 2020), starting with participants’ relationships to place surfaces of often invisible ecological knowledge systems and positions participants as epistemic contributors from the outset. This move is particu larly generative in early phases of co-design (Ishimaru et al., 2018), where relational grounding is critical, and where affective orientations to place and community can shape the trajectory of the work (Khanna, 2020). For community-oriented co-designers, this suggests the importance of designing entry points that foreground lived experience and consequential real-world contexts, rather than beginning from disciplinary framings.

Speculative education has highlighted imagination as a tool for envisioning alternative futures (Bang & Vossoughi, 2016; Garcia & Mirra, 2023; Houlden, S., & Veletsianos, 2023). Our findings refine this work by showing that speculative futuring is particularly generative when sequenced after relational grounding, where imagination operates as an expansion of current worlds rather than a departure from them. The futures imagined in our study did not simply propose idealized alternatives, but articulated ongoing concerns such as migration, development, and disconnection through relational and material reconfigurations. This sequencing affords the surfacing of values, tensions, and aspirations embedded in present conditions, opening new trajectories for what gets designed and to what ends, and aligning with work on axiological innovation that emphasizes shifts in values and relations (Bang et al., 2016). For co-design practice, this suggests that speculative activities may be most productive when grounded in shared narratives and collective experience, allowing participants to rework existing socio-ecological relations rather than abstractly imagine new ones. Critical historicity has been theorized as the capacity to situate oneself within broader socio-historical processes and power relations (Gutiérrez & Jurow, 2018; Bang & Vossoughi, 2018). Our findings extend this work by showing how historicity can be operationalized through intergenerational dialogue and temporal mapping, and how it emerges across routines rather than being confined to a single activity. While the intergenerational historicizing routine most directly engaged participants in analyzing ecolo gical and social change over time, elements of historicity were already present in personal narratives, such as experiences of loss, and in speculative futures that projected alternative trajectories. This suggests that supporting critical historicity in co-design is not only about engaging history as content, but about designing for the entanglement of memory, imagination, and lived experience over time. For practitioners, incorporating intergenerational engagement, structured temporal tools, and opportunities for critical reflection on both past practices and present inequities can support more reflexive and contextually grounded environmental education. Finally, our study contributes to broader conversations on community-based co-design by offering a process-level account of how epistemic participation is structured and sustained. At the same time, we note that these routines were shaped by the specific relational, cultural, and institutional context of this collaboration, and may require adaptation in other settings. Future research could extend our analysis in three key directions. First, examining how the identified routines (autecological narratives, speculative futuring, and intergenerational storytelling) translate into actual workshop implementation with children could illuminate the practical effectiveness of community-grounded design, including its influence on pedagogical strategies and engagement with local ecosystems. Second, exploring co-analysis with participants as objects of study could reveal how their engagement in interpreting and coding data shapes their own environmental and pedagogical knowledge, highlighting co-design as both a research method and a participatory learning experience. Finally, a deeper interrogation of power dynamics in cross-national collaborations between Global North researchers and Global South community teachers could provide insights into whose knowledge is legitimized, how decisions are made, and how epistemic authority is negotiated, contributing to a more nuanced understanding of equity and positionality in participatory design research.
