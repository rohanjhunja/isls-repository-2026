# Playful Programming with Computational Thinking as a Pathway to Culturally Responsive Pedagogy

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
We examined teachers’ learning and design processes of playful coding and computational thinking for language teaching in English as a Second Language (ESL) contexts. Data from two teachers were analyzed and collected via workshop recordings, interviews, and projects developed in Scratch. Examining teachers’ project development showed the potential of playful coding with computational thinking for culturally responsive pedagogy.

## Introduction

Language educators need tools that promote engagement (Hiver et al., 2021)  and accessibility (Syathroh et al., 2021). Scratch, a visual coding platform, provides a space where students candevelop language skills through interactive projects  (Sarasa-Cabezuelo, 2019). Computational thinking  (CT) principles embedded in Scratch support problem-solving, logic, and creative expression, aligning w ith culturally responsive pedagogy to make language learning more inclusive (Hsu et al., 2020).. We examined how playful programming fosters CRP by enabling teachers to construct culturally meaningful learning experiences

## Conceptual framework

This study  is rooted in playful programming and CT. Playful programming engages students in hands-on, interactive activities within a low-stakes, creative environment (Heininger et al., 201 7; Ashley, 2019). Platforms like Scratch make coding accessible for beginners (Fields et al., 2017) and encourage linguistic experimentation through projects (Vogel et al., 2020). Such projects create a welcoming space for self-expression (Brooks et al.,

2024) while fostering language development (Yacoub, 2016). The iterative, risk-taking nature of coding enhances

language acquisition and communication (Murcia et al., 2020). CT improves problem-solving in programming (Lye & Koh, 2014) and language acquisition (Yu et al., 2024). Scratch helps students grasp computational concepts like sequences and loops (Brennan & Resnick, 2012), transforming them from consumers to creators (Bers, 2018). Digital storytelling and coding promote authentic language use (Ma et al., 2022; Smith & Burrow, 2016)  and allow learners to embed personal themes in projects (Mills et al., 2024). Coding and programming impact reasoning, CT, pattern recognition, and algorithmic thinking (Cafarella & Vasconcelos, 2024; Dinç, 2024; Kim et al., 2023, 2021) and support language learning by fostering critical thinking  and self-expression (Hsu & Liang, 2021). Integrating CT into language instruction improve s learning outcomes (Hsu et al., 2020), and  scaffolding strategies  help teachers navigate coding and language challenges (Kim & Dinç, 2025). Playful coding makes language learning more accessible and enjoyable.

## Research question

How do teachers design language teaching projects using playful coding and computational thinking?

## Methods

We employed a multiple case study design (Yin, 2003) t o explore how two ESL teachers, Alice and Sally (pseudonyms), integrated CT and coding into language teaching t hrough Scratch. We investigated their design decisions, discourse, and actions. Sally, with six years of K-12 ESL teaching experience but no coding background, and Alice, with eight years of adult ES L teaching experience and some website coding knowl edge, participated in  individual one-on-one workshops designed to build CT skills. Both were new to  block-based coding before the study. The workshop (see Figure 1) introduced Scratch as a tool for ESL activity creation aimed at developing familiarity with CT.

Workshop recordings and individual semi-structured interviews were analyzed to capture Alice ’s and Sally’s thought processes while designing Scratch project s for language teaching. Each workshop lasted about three hours. T he i nterviews, approximately 70 minutes each, encourage d the teachers to reflect on how the workshop influenced their views on coding and CT, a s well as their pedagogical choices.  Thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2006) guided the data analysis. Transcripts were reviewed and coded line-by-line, with notes (e.g., justifying CT concepts /practices/perspectives, balancing CT and language). After rounds of team discussions, the findings were finalized.

## Findings and discussion

Both teachers naturally integrated cultural themes into their Scratch projects Although the computational thinking workshop did no t explicitly focus on cultural relevance, Alice and Sally naturally integrated culturally significant themes into their project, demonstrating emergent culturally responsive pedagogy in playful coding.  Research shows  that culturally responsive teaching enriches instruction by considering students’ cultural backgrounds (Bonner et al., 2018). Alice emphasized this by incorporating soccer, a cu lturally significant activity for her Middle Easter n students, into her Scratch project. She explained, “Give them [students] something like, taking into consideration, the nature of our students, they [students] love so ccer… In the Middle East, even girls like soccer, b y the way.” She believed contextualized learning enhances engag ement, stating, “When you build your learning aroun d the project, the learning outcome is better.” Her approach facilitated language practice by embedding familiar themes (see Figure 2). She also avoided culturally unfamiliar topics, noting, “This is baseball. If I’m teaching ESL, I will not teach baseball because that’s another difficult y… they don’t know much about baseball, I d on’t know much either.” Figure 2 A Scratch Project Scene and Code from Alice’s Language Teaching Project A sample scene showcasing soccer   A part of code Alice wrote for her project Similarly, Sally ’s Scratch project simulated an  everyday scenario: ordering food  (see Figure 3). She highlighted the role of role-play in language learning, stating, “I think it’s a lso possible for them [students] like practice, kind of like a role-play [of ordering food]  … they [students] can practice role-play speaking.” By designing an accessible, relatable activity, she en sured students could engage naturally with language. She also considered the appropriateness of the interface, ex plaining, “So, it’s not like adults, because I know  the picture looks childish, so I think that’s one of my assumptions.” Figure 3 A Scratch Project Scene and Code from Sally’s Language Teaching Project A sample scene showcasing soccer  A part of code Sally wrote for her project These findings highlight how playful coding fosters  culturally responsive pedagogy in language education. By aligning Scratch projects with students’ backgrounds and interests, Alice and Sally made language learning more accessible and enjoyable (Lee, 2022).  Connecting abstract language concepts to familiar contexts supports student development (Savić et al., 2022) and reduces cognitive load, reinfor cing language as a practical tool. Their culturally attuned designs validated students’ identities, creating a more inclusive learn ing environment. This suggests that integrating playful  coding into curricula can enhance cultural awarene ss, particularly in diverse classrooms.

Both teachers encouraged self-expression and playfulness in their Scratch projects Both teachers’ projects emphasized self-expression, playful exploration, and low-pressure language practice. Alice and Sally incorporated open-ended design features that encouraged creative enga gement with language, fostering a playful and expressive learning environment. This aligns with research highlighting the role of playful engagement in language education (Heidari-Shahreza, 2024). Alice’s soccer-themed project created a space for personal expression, allowing students to engage with language through familiar interests. She emphasized Scratch’s engaging nature, stating, “Scratch website is more fun, there are cartoons there, they can  play with it [Scratch]. It [Scratch] is more fun, a nd then it is like a game more than coding websites.” By prioritizing enjoyment, she promoted interactive and visually stimulating learning experiences. Alice also advocated for project-based learning, ex plaining, “When they [students] build a story, okay? Sort of like task-based assignment, etc, project-based assignment. PBL, PBL, the project-based learning. When you give them [students], again, learning when  you build your learning around the project, th e learning outcome is better.” This emphasizes the importance of allowing students to create their own stories, w hich inherently fosters self-expression (Kim & Li, 2021). To balance technical c omplexity with creativity, she simplified coding tasks, stating, “I don’t want them [students] to get too much overwhelmed by the technical part. So, I’ll prepare some buttons for them and all they  [students] have to do is just like add the sound o r other sentences.” This ensured students could focus on language use without feeling discouraged. Sally designed her project around ordering food, creating a familiar and low-stakes scenario for language practice. She crafted a visually engaging environme nt, explaining, “My personal preference is some col orful things, which look vivid and relaxing… mostly color ed with pink and blue and yellow which looks pretty energetic.” This approach fostered a supportive spa ce where students could explore language comfortabl y. She emphasized the role of multimodal learning, stating, “They [students] can associate features [in Scratch] and with the vocabulary and texts, things, better for them to memorize things… we can combine audio with everything like texts and pictures which is so helpful, I guess, es pecially for younger children.” By integrating text, images, and sound, she reinforced vocabulary retention and provided an engaging platform for self-expression. Both teachers used playful programming to improve cultural connection and student engagement Alice’s and Sally’s projects highlight the value of culturally aligned, playful design in language learning. Alice’s soccer theme, rooted in her students’ culture and i nterests, made language practice personally relevan t, while Sally’s focus on everyday scenarios ensured accessi bility for all. Both demonstrate how culturally res ponsive design supports engagement and language acquisition by connecting learning to students’ lived experiences. Alice stated, “If I teach like present continuous and past continu ous, they will see the difference etc. And then use like action verbs, so I have them like use verbs of dura tion, etc. So they say that they are playing right now with a beachball. And then yesterday they were playing wit h something else, I don ’t know. They were playing soccer etc. And then yesterday, they were watching like the game, and today they are playing soccer etc. So in that way, I don’t distract them away much from the target to teach…  the learning the tenses and the verbs.” By integrating playful, action-based scenarios, she helped students practice grammar naturally within meaningful contexts (Ahn, 2016). Similarly, Sally emphasized interactive assessment,  stating, “Let’s just play sounds but without words, then we [teachers] can assess if they [students] re member the vocabulary… Or we just play words and le t them [students] make a kind of like lip sync or somethin g to read it out and practice speaking.” Her creati ve approach leveraged sound and movement to reinforce  vocabulary and pronunciation, making language practice both dynamic and engaging.  These findings show  the power of culturally responsive, playful design in language education, fostering deeper engagement and meaningful learning experiences.

## Scholarly significance and implications

This study contributes to digital pedagogy by highlighting how playful coding and CT empower teachers to create inclusive, culturally relevant language-learning resources. It provides insights into designing coding projects that reflect students’ cultural backgrounds and offers a framework for dev eloping engaging, meaningful learning materials. The findings show the potential of digital coding projects to support  language acquisition while fostering culturally responsive learning environments. This study also underscores the role of female teachers in a traditionally male-dominated STEM field. Alice and Sally navigated the ir initial unfamiliarity with computing by using their pedagogical expertise to design cult urally resonant, student-centered projects. Their experiences suggest that greater support for women in coding-related professional development could improve cult ural inclusivity in education.

Educators can enhance language learning by incorpor ating culturally relevant themes that connect to students’ lives and experiences. Playful coding activities encourage open-ended exploration, allowing learners to experiment with language in a low-pressure setting. This approach fosters confidence, engageme nt, and deeper learning. By integrating culturally meaningful cont ent, teachers create inclusive, accessible resource s that make language use authentic and personally relevant.
