# Reading Minds: Exploring Relationships Between Sensor-Data and Self-Reported Cognitive-Affective States of Learners

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Cognitive-affective states (CASs) strongly affect learning outcomes. Therefore, learning systems need to be CAS-aware to truly cater to learners’ needs. In this study, we explore the possibility of multimodal sensor data as reliable indicators of learner CASs. Data collected will include skin conductance, heart rate, skin temperature and global gaze properties, which will be compared with self-reported CASs of learners. Linear mixed models will be applied and results will be presented at the conference.

## Introduction

In this paper, we submit a multimodal approach to d etect cognitive-affective states (CASs) in learning environments. This is crucial because learning outc omes are greatly influenced by the cognitive affect ive state (CAS) of the learner (Pekrun, 2006). Specifically, positive emotions such as enjoyment and pride are associated with high levels of engagement and high learning outcomes, whereas persistent negative emotions such as anxiety and boredom are strongly related to low learning outcomes (Pekrun, 2017). Therefore it is pertinent that today’s intelligent learning systems take into account a learner’s CAS while providing support or feedback. For this to be possible, there must be a way for a system to actually perceive a learner’s CAS. This is the need our study aims to address.

One obvious way of ascertaining CASs is to always ask the learner. Naturally this is obtrusive to the learning experience. Ideally an intelligent learnin g system would, by employing discreet means to coll ect information from various modalities, build a reliable impression of a learner’s CAS. In other words, the key to a highly perceptive learning system is unobtrusive mu ltimodal data collection. Our research project expl ores precisely this with a focus on sensor technology th at can be deployed in real-world classrooms. Theref ore we work with data that can be collected via pervasive computing technology – portable computers, unobtrus ive sensors and wearables. In this study, we focus on skin conductance, heart rate, skin temperature and global gaze features. This is because past studies have found s trong relationships between stress during difficult  tasks (Brouwer et al., 2018), emotional arousal (Jindrová et al., 2020) and skin conductance. Research also finds that changes in heart rate and skin temperature may indicate cognitive-load (Larmuseau et al., 2020). Moreover, gaze features such as fixations, saccades have previously been used to determine attentional or mind-wandering states (Hutt et al., 2017). In this study, we investigate whether (a combination of) these measures can give us a reliable (and fuller) picture of a learner’s state. The possibility of discriminating between sustained positive and negative learner CASs is of particular interest to us. For e xample, low skin conductance accompanied by a large number of fixations could indicate an attentive low arousa l state whereas the same accompanied by a low numbe r of fixations could indicate an inattentive low arousal state such as boredom which in turn negatively affects learning outcomes (Pekrun, 2006). In essence, this is an exploratory study of the relationship between (combinations of) sensor data and CASs as described by self-reports.

## Methods

Participants will comprise of 30 BSc Psychology stu dents from the University of Twente. Participation will be voluntary and active consent will be obtained from all participants before the start of the experiment. Data will be collected via self-reports (described below), the E mpatica E4 biosensing wristband (https://www.empatica.com/research/e4), Tobii Pro F usion (https://www.tobiipro.com/fusion/), a screen based eye-tracker and a generic webcam. All participants will spend an equal amount of time doing three tasks. The first task will be to play Tetris (www.tetris.com). Tetris has been selected because it is an extremely popular and widely studied (Mayer, 2014) game that can bring players into a positive CAS of effortless attention (Harmat et al., 2015). The second task will be to repeatedly match pairs of two-dimensional shapes. The third task will be to “solve” a coordinate geometry puzzle. This will be a wild goose chase because the researcher will never accept an answer as correct. Based on pilot testing it is expected t hat participants will experience CASs that are posi tive highICLS2022 Proceedings 2048 © ISLS arousal (Tetris), negative low-arousal (shape-matching) and negative high-arousal (coordinate geometry puzzle). It is also expected that the baseline (a relaxing m usic video) will induce a positive low-arousal CAS. The study will use a counterbalanced design for the three tasks to mitigate order-effects. A multiple baseline approach will be employed with 1 minute cooling off periods between tasks. At regular intervals during the study, participants will complete 4 self-report items:

1. The Affect Grid (Russel et al., 1989), a 1-item scale of CASs along two dimensions, arousal and valence,

2. a five-scale item, “This task is: 1) Too easy 2) Easy 3) Just right 4) Difficult 5) Too difficult”,

3. a 7-scale item, “I am actively engaged in what I am doing. 1 (not at all) to 7 (very much)” (Kirtley et al.,

2019), and

4. An open-ended fill-in-the-blank statement: “I am feeling __”.

## Results

Within-subject relationship between self-reports and physiological signals will be explored using line ar mixed models while controlling for demographic data. Pair wise comparisons will be computed after having appl ied Bonferroni correction. Results will be presented at the conference. Practical and ethical considerations Detection of CASs is highly relevant because it allows us to consequently design intelligent learning systems that are tailored to the needs of each individual learne r. A sensor based approach to CAS detection means t hat this ‘tailoring’ is informed by a user model that is updated dynamically during a lesson as opposed to the traditional approach of user profiling based on information known before a lesson (for example, sex, age, prior knowledge, race etc). We believe that this increases fairness in the artificial intelligence by mitigating human bias.
