# Engineering a Learning Environment Which Fosters Interaction and Thinking

**Conference:** ISLS 2026

## Abstract

My research investigates how Visible Thinking and dialogic teaching can be integrated to foster interaction, thinking, and a culture of dialogue in EFL higher education. Using design-based research, I have iteratively designed and refined thinking-routine-embedded instruction and am now extending this work along three strands: analyzing thinking and dialogic moves in student discourse, exploring the cultural and affective dimensions that sustain a thinking culture, and refining design principles through curricular-design alignment between conjecture mapping and the curricular spider web. This ongoing program aims to build a theoretically and contextually grounded framework explaining how language, interaction, and design co-mediate learning in East Asian EFL classrooms. My research seeks to understand and design learning environments that foster interaction, thinking, and a culture of dialogue in English-as-a-foreign-language (EFL) higher education. Grounded in a sociocultural perspective of interaction (Mercer, 2000), I examine how students’ reasoning and meaning-making are mediated through talk and through the design of instruction. During my doctoral study, I conducted a multi-iteration design-based research (DBR) project embedding Visible Thinking (Ritchhart et al., 2011) into university English courses. Through iterative refinement, I developed four instructional design principles that demonstrated how thinking-routine-based instruction can externalize students’ reasoning, enhance collaborative dialogue, and nurture a supportive classroom ethos. After completing my PhD, my inquiry evolved from investigating whether thinking routines enhance learning to exploring how and why learning occurs through dialogic interaction and culturally situated classroom practices. Across current and future projects, I aim to advance an integrated research program that connects three strands: (1) analyzing the intersection between thinking moves and dialogic moves; (2) examining the cultural dimension of thinking and classroom ethos, and (3) refining design principles through an aligned curricular-design framework. Thinking and dialogic moves: Exploring the intersections and tensions in collective reasoning The first strand focuses on unpacking how thinking moves and dialogic moves function together to mediate collaborative learning. My current analyses code students’ discourse in two parallel layers: thinking moves—such as explaining, connecting, reasoning with evidence, and considering viewpoints (Visible Thinking)—and dialogic moves, adapted from the Scheme for Educational Dialogue Analysis (SEDA; Hennessy et al., 2016). Rather than adopting SEDA’s full eight-cluster framework, I selected a subset of dialogic categories most relevant to my classroom context, including express, build on, reason, coordinate, and position. These categories capture the interactional mechanisms that keep the dialogue going—how students initiate ideas, sustain discussion, reason, and display agreement or disagreement. In many of my classroom tasks, these dialogic processes are interwoven with the joint creation of tangible objects, such as concept maps or character analysis diagrams. This task structure positions talk and object production as mutually shaping processes, echoing Damşa’s (2014) view of collaborative learning as a multi-layered phenomenon where interaction, knowledge objects, and shared epistemic agency are tightly interconnected. Recognizing this interplay between discourse and object-making highlights the need to examine not only how thinking and dialogic moves unfold, but also how they may be embedded in broader collaborative practices. My goal is not to merge thinking and dialogic moves into a single system, but to examine their co-occurrences, sequences, synergy, and potential tensions. This methodological stance reflects my theoretical grounding in Vygotsky’s view of language as both a psychological and cultural tool (cited in Mercer, 2000), and Mercer’s concept of dialogue as a “social mode of thinking” (Mercer & Littleton, 2007). Both perspectives highlight that thinking and dialogue are mutually constitutive: dialogue enriches thinking, while thinking gives substance to dialogue. However, analysis from my previous study (under review) shows that these two sets of moves can partially overlap or even conflict. Some dialogic moves (e.g., “build on”) may serve interactional continuity without necessarily deepening reasoning. I therefore aim to explore the underlying mechanisms that may account for these overlaps and tensions in classroom d iscourse, potentially revealing how local cultural contexts influence patterns of interaction and meaning-making. As Lefstein and Snell (2011) stress, classroom discourse cannot be easily generalized, as patterns of talk are shaped by distinct national and institutional cultures and may even shift within the same classroom depending on goals, topics, and activities. This insight resonates with my analysis of thinking and dialogic moves, which not only examines their micro-level co-occurrences but also considers how these moves are mediated by the objects, instructional design and by cultural expectations in EFL classrooms. Together, these perspectives foreground how dialogic interaction operates not in isolation but within broader learning ecologies, paving the way for examining how classroom culture shapes and is shaped by collaborative thinking practices. Cultural dimension and classroom ethos: Building a culture of thinking The second strand extends from micro-level discourse to the meso-level of classroom culture. In a recent crosscase exploratory study of two EFL university courses, I analyzed students’ reflections and identified four interrelated elements shaping a culture of thinking (Ritchhart, 2015): teacher guidance and emotional support, peer interaction and supportive ethos, structured tasks that scaffold reasoning, and improved confidence and comfort in expression. This line of work draws on sociocultural theory, Visible Thinking’s eight cultural forces (Ritchhart, 2015), and dialogic teaching’s notion of a dialogic culture (Alexander, 2020; Resnick et al., 2018). While these frameworks highlight different facets of classroom culture, they share a concern for the social and moral fabric sustaining intellectual engagement. Integrating them helps interpret my findings as manifestations of how cognitive, relational, and ethical dimensions converge into a shared ethos where thinking becomes a collective responsibility. In East Asian EFL contexts, linguistic anxiety and hierarchical classroom norms often discourage students from taking intellectual risks (Yuan et al., 2022). Therefore, I aim to examine how teacher mediation and peer relationships foster mutual respect, safety, and belonging, and how such affective conditions enable more sustained and reasoned dialogue. Ethnographic observation and cross-case thematic analysis will help trace how these cultural elements emerge and evolve. Through this work, I seek to show how classroom ethos functions as both a precondition and a product of collective thinking. Refining design principles through curricular-design alignment The third strand advances my design-based research trajectory by refining the design principles that structure thinking-and dialogue-rich instruction. My earlier design principles emphasized general curricular alignment. Building on this foundation, I now aim to develop a more nuanced and context-sensitive framework that interweaves thinking, dialogic, and cultural dimensions. Firstly, thinking design defines the specific forms of reasoning instruction aims to cultivate and explores how these thinking actions can be observed and scaffolded. Secondly, dialogic design focuses on structuring talk opportunities and teacher prompts that move discussions beyond surface-level participation toward deeper reasoning. Thirdly, cultural design considers how contextual values, classroom norms, and relationships can be intentionally designed to sustain intellectual engagement. Methodologically, this strand builds on my previous attempt to align the curricular spider web (vanden Akker, 2013) with conjecture mapping (Sandoval, 2014). In that work, I sought to ensure curricular coherence by connecting two types of alignment: (1) aligning curricular objectives, thinking goals, learning tasks, environment design, and assessment; and (2) aligning the embodied instructional designs with their underlying theoretical principles. This alignment helped visualize how conjectures about learning mechanisms are realized within concrete curricular elements. Although that framework was produced as a conceptual outcome of my prior DBR, I now plan to implement it as a methodological model in my next phase. By integrating it with the three-phase DBR cycle (McKenney & Reeves, 2012; Gravemeijer & Cobb, 2006), I will iteratively design, enact, and analyze new instructional cycles that bridge theory, design, and practice. This model will allow me to observe more closely how designed tasks, classroom discourse, and cultural development interact, thereby generating empirically refined mid-level design principles relevant across EFL and dialogic learning contexts. Future directions and community engagement In the coming years, I plan to initiate a new cycle of DBR that integrates these strands into a unified program of inquiry. I also aim to collaborate with international researchers in dialogic teaching and learning sciences to explore cross-context adaptation of dialogic and thinking-based pedagogies. Through this program, I hope to contribute both a contextualized perspective from East Asian higher education and a theoretical framework that connects cognitive, social, and cultural dimensions of learning, advancing the shared goal of understanding how thinking and dialogue intertwine to transform classroom learning and culture.
