# A Speculative Approach to Civic and Environmental Education

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
We present an interdisciplinary design for civic-environmental education in which 7th graders identified environmental issues in their urban communities and imagined solutions. Our analysis of artifacts and discussions found that how students attuned to social justice and expressed agency depended on the conceptualized scale of the issue (local vs. national) and connections to lived experiences. Our study explores speculative approaches as powerful for learning and youth agency for both civic and environmental education.

Speculative approaches, student agency in environment and civics learning Environmental education designs that develop studen t agency are needed for future collective action to  address planetary health (White et. al, 2024). Student agen cy can also be labeled as civic environmentalism, u rban ecological stewardship, or civic ecolo gy (Tidball & Krasny, 2011). In this context, agenc y is defined as youth’s participation in identifying which socio-ecological challenges they perceive as affecting th em and their methods of action, inquiry, and reflection (Schusler & Kras ny, 2008). In this respect, environmental education  is in part developing youth’s capacity to democratically parti cipate towards a future they envision as more socia lly and environmentally just (Barton & Tan, 2010; White et al., 2024). Speculative civics approaches, in which youth are asked to imagine solutions to civic issues (Mirra & Garcia, 2022), offer an opportunity to support student agency, environmentalism and stewardship at the intersection of environmental and civic education. Here we report from a semester-long design study exploring a speculative civics de sign for community inquiry and storytelling with 7th grade civics students (Deroo et. al, 2024). We focused on a task in which students identified civi c issues in their communities, imagined a better future arou nd these issues, and proposed and advocated for a s olution through letter writing to a government official. We  analyzed students’ expressions of environmental is sues and their proposed solutions and considered connections  between youth civics learning, env ironmental justice concerns and student agency. We asked: 1) What environmental issues did students raise as civic issues and how did they imagine civic solutions that include expressions of agency? 2) How do students’ raise issues of justice in their concerns and solutions in relation to their own lived experiences? The following frameworks inform our research: Speculative civics which foregrounds youth voices and interrogating democratic structures (Mirra & Garcia, 2022). Place-based education, which posits that learning is intimately connected with physical place, a nd students use their communities as knowledge sour ces and engage with sociopolitical landscapes through civic practi ces (Lanouette & Taylor, 2022; Yemini, 2023). Lastl y, an environmental justice framework which interrelates race and class with environmental issues (Mohai, 2009).

## Place, context, and methods

In Fall 2023, our interdisciplinary research team c onducted a second iteration of a design-based research project at a Title I public charter middle and high school in a majority-minority city in the US South. This coastal, large metropolitan area has over 6 million inhabitants and faces challenges of sea-level rise, flooding and related strains on housing. The school is in a predominantly Hispan ic neighborhood made up of mixed-use development and minimal green space. Our project engaged 25 self-identified bi/multilingual, transnational and immigrant youth across two 7th grade civics classes in multimodal, community-based inquiry using digital storytelling tools. Over 12 weeks (20 instructional hours), we explored stud ents’ civic engagement practices and pro mpted them to consider local civic issues and methods of caring a nd relating to their communities to imagine more ju st social futures. We audio and video recorded class meetings and collected student work. For the current analysis, we thematically analyzed student artifacts and in-class discussions across two activities. The first activity took place on a sing le day, where students responded to a think-aloud prompt “What is a civic problem you care about in your community?” through placing post-it notes on the whiteboard (see Figure 1, a), and then discussing what students wrote and why. In the second activity, students responded to a written prompt, “What would be a better, more just future in [city]?” Students identified a civic issue and then identified a civic actor to write a letter or make a video to, describing their proposed solution to the issue. Our research team engaged in content analysis and inductive thematic coding of student artifacts, triangulated with video analysis of recorded classroom discussions (Fazeli et al, 2023)  to identify environmental concerns raised and patt erns in student solutions, including expressions of agency and environmental justice.

## Findings and significance

Across both activities, students cited flooding, st orms, ocean pollution, street pollution, air pollut ion, traffic congestion, plastic overuse, rising temperatures, a nd the lack of shade and green spaces as problems i n their neighborhoods. Other students r eferenced “climate change” and “global warming” but  these were framed as national problems and addressed to national stakeho lders (see Figure 1, b). Students who stayed at a b roader conceptual scale (e.g., “climate change”) struggled  to conceptualize solut ions, compared with students who focused on local problems who had more elaborate ci vic solutions for their particular problems, as evi denced in their letters. For example, Mikey (pseudonym) wrote  a letter addressed to the President to tackle clim ate change which stated, “Put more funds into stopping polluti on and global warming.” In contrast, Lucia addresse d a letter to the city mayor about air pollution, writing, “I am asking for cleaner air and streets in [city]. We alsoneed more nature. There are no places for people to walk around surrounded by nature … I believe there are many solutions to this problem, like clearing out our streets once a week, adding more nature and green [spaces].” We view Lucia as expressing agency by reflecting on specific urba n ecological interventions, grounded in her local e xperience, that reflect the more just society she envisions and wants to live in. Students also raised environmental social justice c oncerns when imagining civic solutions to environmental issues that referenced their families, neighborhoods or school community. For instance, seven students cited the unhoused or financially disadvan taged population as part of the affected stakeholde rs when considering environmental issues in the urban conte xt, such as flooding, heavy rains and tropical stor ms. Samantha wrote her letter to the city mayor and cited her concern as a lack of, “free and more affordable hurricane protections for houses, like shutters, and a shelter and protection for homeless people in the area with food/water.” Other students' view of justice included the more-than-human community, citing animals as important parts of the community whose safety must also be protected (e.g., Aila wrote, “I imagine a peaceful future for all animals and humans not having to think about some animals being abused for no reason”). Our analysis suggests that students were comfortabl e imagining more just futures for themselves and others. In a highly stratified socioeconomic urban landscape, students in our study revealed their und erstanding of the tensions between the economic and environmen tal issues across the city, and potential impacts on humans and more-than-human stakeholders. The more just futures students imagined consistently intersected environmental issues and aspects of justice and equ ity, specifically when students focuse d on their local, lived experiences. Past research on science learning and student agency (e.g., Barton & Tan, 2010) has focus ed on learning designs where environmental issues students should care about are made explicit. Our research shifts the conversation on environmental and civic learning spaces by pl acing an emphasis on students' perspectives, in order to better support their expressions of agency  in their speculative imagining of better futures.  Emphasizing student’s lived experiences of environmental issues supports their engagement in civic processes and enactments of environmental justice, like communicating with t heir local government, offering sustainable solutio ns which consider diverse groups of people, and acknowledgin g the interdependence of human and natural systems; all aspects of student agency (White, 2024). Our resear ch also suggests that how educational designers fra me the contextual scale of environmental problems matters for learning and imagining. Through continued analy sis, particularly of student discussions, we hope to further rese arch on learning designs that foster notions of civ ic environmentalism and encourage youth agency and empowerment in identifying and solving environmental issues in their city’s future.
