# The Sensitive Nature of Feedback as a Sequential Phenomenon

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Social actions such as advice and feedback are sensitive actions, often triggering emotional and defensive reactions. Moreover, just how to understand and describe such actions – for instance, as “constructive advice”, “complaints”, or even “insults” – is a potential point of contention and dispute for participants in interaction. Based on a corpus of video data from higher education, our research aims to develop a conceptual and empirically grounded understanding of the social organization of feedback. This study investigates how students and teachers orient towards the sensitive nature of feedback in critique sessions in architectural education. With a few examples from the data set, the study focuses on three issues: a) the sequential organization of feedback; b) the grounds for two (conflicting) orientations to the feedback; and c) the ongoing instructional work to make the students hear the feedback in the “right” way.

## Introduction

Numerous studies and meta-studies show the massive presence, relevance, and diversity of feedback in h igher education – from formative assessments of completed course work to responsive instructions produced during the performance of tasks (e.g., Evans, 2013; Hattie & T imperley, 2007). Feedback is used to point out mist akes or misconceptions, provide advice on how an assignment could be further improved or differently approached, and strengthen the students’ ability to monitor their o wn performance. While the central role of feedback is agreed upon, not all feedback is equally effective in term s of learning. In a review of research on feedback,  Hattie and Timperley (2007) maintain that praise is ineffective “because it contains such little learning related information” (p 86). They further argue that feedback seems to b e “more effective when there are perceived low rath er than high levels of threat to self-esteem, presumably be cause low-threat conditions allow attention to be p aid to the feedback” (ibid.).

Although claims by Hattie and Timperley could be pr oblematized on both empirical and conceptual grounds, they highlight a persistent challenge for university teachers: how to point out mistakes and deliver advice that is beneficial to student learning in ways that  pose little ‘threat’ to students. Students and tea chers tend to report different perspectives on what feedback is or should consist of (see Carless, 2006; Henderson et al., 2019). It is often possible to understand feedback, advice, or critical remarks in different ways. What the teachers perceive to be constructive feedback, the students might hea r simply as complaints. This tension is not limited  to higher education: feedback, advice, and similar social actions are delicate matters in mundane and institutional settings (Sutton et al., 2012).

When locating feedback as a critical activity within many educational settings, we see a need to enrich the view on what this notion might encompass. This study, therefore, takes an interest in the delicate, ambiguous, and potentially contentious  n ature of critical feedback and how this delicacy is  constituted and handled in interaction. We have examined a larger corpus of recordings of critique sessions in architecture education with an analytical starting point in ethnomethodology and conversation analysis (Garfinkel & Sacks, 1970). To develop our line of argument, we will address a) the sensit ive nature of feedback as a sequential phenomenon; b) the grounds for two potentially conflicting orientations to feedback; and c) the ongoing instructional work designed to make students hear and understand feedback in the “proper” way.

## Background

Before turning to the empirical part of the study, we outline some prior research tied to the interact ional phenomena and context of the study. The first secti on summarizes some findings on the social organizat ion of feedback, advice, and critique, and more specifical ly, studies that investigate the sensitive and pote ntially problematic aspects of this type of interaction. The second section concerns the context of the study, and briefly discusses prior research on ‘critique sessions’ in architecture education. As the term critique session implies, and the existing literature demonstrates, such educatio nal arrangements are useful settings for investigat ing critical feedback and how teachers and students orient to its sensitive nature. ICLS2022 Proceedings 559 © ISLS The interactional organization of feedback and advice The problematic and ambiguous character of critical  feedback is reflected in the English language (as well as other languages). As a verb, ‘critique’ could mean to “evaluate (a theory or practice) in a detailed and analytical way”, but also to “indicate the faults of (someone or something) in a disapproving way” (Oxford Dictio nary of English). Delivering ‘constructive criticism’ might have positive connotations, but ‘being criticised’ seldom does; and although ‘complaining’ is regularly understood to be essentially different from ‘giving advice,’ it is often hard to determine whether an utterance removed from its natural context is advising, critiquing, complainin g, or something else. Whether an utterance is understood to be offering advice or making a complaint is tied  to particular occasions, and its sense might be percei ved differently by speakers and recipients (Heritag e & Sefi, 1992).

Actions are also reflexively tied to activities and membership categories (Sacks, 1992). A remark on the speed of a driver that is uncontroversial during a driving lesson, has the potential of becoming provocative when it is said by a fellow passenger. During a driving lesson, the remarks, directives, and advice from th e traffic instructor are relevantly understood in terms of teaching and learning. When fellow passengers comment on the speed or proximity to the roadside, this might be h eard as a negative remark on the driving. As noted by Heinemann and Traverso (2008), speakers seldom characterize what they are doing as complaining, but in stead use “less negatively valenced labels such as ‘criticizing,’ ‘troubles-telling’ or even ‘factually reporting’” (p. 2381). Nevertheless, the insurance that the passenger is ‘just telling’ does not necessarily lead the driver to hear anything but a ‘complaint’ from a ‘back-seat driver.’ As Edw ards (2005) surmises, “‘complaining’ [and we might add, ‘critique’ and ‘feedback’] is a defeasible vernacul ar category with normative oriented-to properties a nd consequences” (p. 7) In educational settings, where feedback and critica l advice from teachers are expected features of educational exchanges, one might think that the sen sitive aspect of feedback would be absent. As obser ved in previous research on feedback in higher education (Vevhiläinen, 2009), however, the educational context per se does not remove the sensitive nature of feedback. W hen a teacher suggests that something could be done  in a different way – for instance, that the introduction  of a manuscript could be differently organized – i t could potentially be heard by the student as saying that the introduction is not good enough, or that the student has failed to live up to the relevant standards. It is therefore common that teachers orient towards feedback that potentially involves negative assessment as socially problemati c (Skovholt, 2018). As testified by the literature on architectural critiques, moreover, students sometimes experience that feedback is more closely tied to power and authority than to learning and development.

Critique sessions in architecture education All over the world, students of architecture traditionally spend much of their time working with models, sketches, and plans in different kind of design-projects – so metimes alone, at other times collaborating in smal l groups (Schön, 1983). At the end of a course, students and their projects are regularly assessed and evaluated. Reflecting the project-based structure of the education, as we ll as the complexity and multifaceted nature of the  domain, assessments such as written tests are commonly put back in flavor of what is variously called design reviews, juries, dialogues, critiques, or crits. Design reviews typically begin with students presenting their projects to an audience of staff members, professional architects,  and peers (e.g., Lymer, Ivarsson & Lindwall, 2009;  Lymer, Lindwall & Ivarsson, 2011; Webster, 2005, 2007). After the presentation, staff members and invited professionals criticize, comment on, and discuss the students’ work, elaborating on the strengths as well as the weaknesses of the presented projects.

When asked, students and staff members often highli ght that design reviews provide students opportunities to display their work and to articulate their ideas, to receive formative feedback from teachers and peers, and to critically reflect central issues, and to improve and progress in their work (Smith 2021). Even if the strengths of design reviews are brought forward, ho wever, students as well as staff frequently display  mixed feelings about these events. As noted by Webster (2007), for instance, it is not uncommon for “students to describe the purpose of design juries as an occasion for both individual learning and the collective celebration of student achievements while at the same time recalling their own experiences of design juries as profoundly de-motivating and competitive.” (p. 287). Other students express more reproving attitudes towards the design reviews, describing them “as an ordeal devised by tutors to leave them feeling as though they have been ‘undressed in public’” (Doidge, Sara & Pamell, 2000, s. vii).

With titles such as “Design juries on trial” (Antho ny, 1991), “Judging juries” (Anthony, 1989), “Reviewing the review” (Wilkin, 2000), and “Critiqu ing ‘Critiques’” (Dannels & Norris Martin, 2008) se veral studies of design reviews critically assess and eva luate the educational format. Reporting on a one-ye ar ethnographic study at a British school of education, for instance, Webster maintains that the design reviews mainly ICLS2022 Proceedings 560 © ISLS are events where “staff uses their power to coerce students into reproducing staff-centered constructi ons of architectural habitus” (2005, p. 265). She further argues that the practice of critique itself is replete with structural features working against student learning. In light of this argument, “the continuing use of the architectural review is questioned” (ibid.). In a later study based on t he same material, Webster (2007) draws on Foucault to discuss “the analytics of power” and the “startling schism between the official, or declared, intentions of the critics and their actions” (p. 24). From this analysis, Webster concludes that the design review should be “replaced by a new set of pedagogic events that are carefully constructed to support student learning” (p. 21). Other researchers, while acknowledging the problems with the design reviews,  take a less radical approach and point to the need  of “feedback literacy” among students and teachers (Smith, 2021).

## Theory and methods

In this study, and in contrast to some of the resea rch outlined in the previous section, we are less i nterested in evaluating or critiquing the educational format, as in explicating the local and situated organizations that occasions the sensitive or contentious features of feedback. Prior studies that have investigated these features  have drawn on existing models and theories from sociology and social psychology, and conceptualizing the sensitive nature of feedback in terms of face-work (Goffman, 1955), politeness (Brown & Levinson, 1987), and motivation (Swann, 1990). With an analytical starting point in  ethnomethodology and conversation analysis (Garfin kel & Sacks, 1970), our interest is instead directed to the endogenous organization of social actions, and how members continually display their understanding of and orientation to prior actions as a constitutive part of this organization. As repeatedly demonstrated in conversation analytic  studies, social actions have a sequential organization; this means that each turn-at-talk dis plays an ‘analysis’ of the previous, which may then  be either ratified or ‘repaired’ in subsequent contributions.  This, in turn, provides us as researchers the mate rial for our ‘second-order’ analysis – if the members sequentially display their understanding to each other, they do so for us as professional analysts as well (Lindwall, Lymer, & Greiffenhangen, 2015; Sacks, Schegloff and Jefferson; 1974). This sequentially based interactional conception of participant orientations provides a powerful analy tic tool to ground claims that participants in a particular activity can be described as, for instance, orienting towards aspects of feedback as ‘delicate’ or ‘sensitive’. Likewise, an analysis of resistance and/or dispute in relation to feedback interactions can be grounded in the sequential, tur n-by-turn achievement of shared, contested or negot iated understandings.

In conversation analysis, the notion of “sequence organization” refers to the organization of meaningful courses of action enacted through turns-at-talk and embodied practices such as head nods, gestures, and shifts in posture (Schegloff, 2007). In order to follow and to be part of a meaningful interactional exchange, members need to monitor and analyze talk and embodied practices so as to understand what other members are doing and in order to respond in appropriate and coherent ways. The most basic sequential organization is the adjacency pair, which is a unit consisting of two actions produced by different speakers, and where one of the actions initiate the exchange, whereas the next action responds to the f irst. Typical examples of adjacency pairs include g reetings (“Hello”, “Hi”) and question-answers (“what time is it?”, “five a clock?”). In feedback interactions, more complex sequences emerge. Just as with adjacency pairs, however, analytic attention is devoted to examining actions and their responses, along with the ensuing sequences.

The study is part of a larger project where we investigate the sensitive nature of feedback across different settings, including text supervision in social scie nce and ‘debriefings’ in martime and medical educat ion. The examples presented in this study is drawn from a co rpus of 70 hours of video material of critique sess ions in architecture education. The recordings were made du ring a period of four months at a Swedish school of architecture. In total, 159 critique sessions have been video recorded, ranging in length from 10 minutes up to one hour. In the present paper, a highly selective approach is taken, presenting two extracts of interaction as illustrative examples of the issues under discussion. The episodes have been transcribed following the conventions developed in conversation analysis (e.g., Jefferson, 1984). In the transcripts, pauses are represented as numbers of seconds within brackets, with (.) indicating micro pauses. Co-occurring talk is horizontally aligned, with square brackets marking the onset of overlap. Extended vowel sounds are marked with colons, as in e:::h;  >talk< indicates that it was delivered more rapidly than the surrounding talk, and <talk> that it was delivered more slowly. The original material is in Swedish, but given the limited space, only the English translations are offered here. ## Results

Feedback and sequence organization By examining the video recorded material, and retur ning to the notion of sequence organization, it is clear that feedback is not constituted by one specific type of  initiating action with its corresponding next; fee dback sequences do not constitute a particular form of ad jacency pairs. The feedback provided by instructors  and professional architects in the critique sessions take many forms and shapes; among the common variants, we find assessments (“this is a beautiful solution”), sugge stions (“perhaps you could have used marble as the base”), questions (“what was the main concept with this floor plan?”), and directives (“do not read the presentation, but speak freely instead”).

A first issue for students is whether they should explicitly respond to the feedback or not. This issue turns on the shifting and ambiguous addressivity of critic’s talk – and its ‘split audience design’. On the one hand, the critique is addressed to the group of students responsible for the design. Also present, however, are the students’ peers from the same class. In an important sense then, the feedback is at the same time produced to be heard by a larger audience of students. During the course of a session, the relative emphasis put on addressing the particular group, or addressing the audience as a whole tends to vary. The critic might move from asking the resp onsible students a series of questions about the design, to  elaborating on more general concerns. Even when do ing the latter, however, the particular design proposal is often explicitly used as an example. This generates  an interactionally difficult situation for students, in terms of the relevance of responding to feedback. If a first issue for students is when to respond, a second issue is how responses should be designed. Since conversational exchanges are produced sequentially through a ‘paired’ structure, which includes expans ions to the ‘base-pair’, the students need to produce a nex t action that fits the first for the interaction to  be coherent. A request can both be met with acceptance and rejection, but if someone responds with a greeting it could potentially signal some trouble of hearing or understanding. In  the design reviews, students typically respond to initiating actions with “type-conforming” (Raymond, 2003) nexts; i.e., questions are responded to by answers or accounts, suggestions with acceptance or rejection, and assessments with acknowledgement or perhaps second assessments. Going back to the introduction, however, feedback i n its various forms have the potential to be understood in different ways. Regardless of the for m of the criticism – whether it is produced as ques tions, reminders, or assessments – it could be heard as doing ‘complaining’ rather than ‘constructive advice’. Given that interaction is sequentially ordered, moreover, thes e understandings are displayed in and through the s tudents’ responses. When advice is is met by “I am sorry I made these mistakes”, moreover, the response is not only doing an excuse, it also shows that the student heard a complaint in the previous utterance. In our data, such ascriptions are treated as problematic by instructors and invited professionals, and they typically engage in attempts to ‘repair’ the students displayed understanding of feedback as complaints. ‘Counter complaints’ is another next action produced by students which turns the interaction away from issues of learning and constructive advice towards issues of guilt and blame for having done something  wrong. One of the things that the instructor raises in relation to this proposal is t he size of the ventilation shaft. The comments are thereby framed as lessons to be learned from the design review rather than evaluations of previous performance.

Two orientations Design reviews have both summative and formative functions. On the one hand, the activities provide evaluations and gradings on students and their projects. On the other hand, they are instructional activities, aimed at furnishing and scaffolding the learning and development of disciplinary skills – for the students responsible for the project, but also for the peers acting as an audience to the critique. In Excerpt 1, like in many other episodes in our material, there is a tension between how the students and the critics approach the design review. In this example, the students orient towards the summative rather than the formative aspects of the critics’ comments – it is about their design and what they should have done. The manifest orientation of the critic is different: he uses the students’ design proposal to make general and instructive comments to the audience of students in the room, not just the ones that presented their project. The core issue is what the design proposal in front of him can teach all students present in the room. From the latter orientation, the students ‘misunderstand’ the remarks by responding to them as primarily having a summative and retrospective character. Although he initially addresses the issue of the ‘missing advice’, and the rationale for why he did not do what the students wanted him to do, he then turns to the ways in which the comments should be heard – that it is the lesson that is important, not when this lesson is learned. For students and critics alike, however, the situation is inhere ntly unstable. The students are sometimes expected to provide accounts and explain their choices, whereas at othe r times, such explanations are treated as interfere nces. This means that from one situation to a next, the activi ty could turn from an assessment of a particular pr oject to a ‘lecture’ where the work of the students is merely used as an example of some more general issue. The two orientations can partly be understood with reference to the members’ different relation to the design proposal. The students have spent several weeks, working hard to produce a strong design proposal that is now to be assessed and discussed by a professional architect. They are there to present and to some extent defend their proposal, and it might therefore be hard to s witch to a hearing where the proposal is just being  used as an example. For the critic, in contrast, the proposal is just one of several proposals that are being discussed that day, and the point is not just to give feedback to the students who produced the proposal but also to use the occasion to lecture about general issues central to architectural practice. In other design reviews, the issue of ‘fairness’ is  raised by students: ‘how come that the critic rais ed a problem with our design proposal and not with the subsequent proposal which had the same manifest problem?’ From the point of giving advice to the whole group of attending students, however, the very fact that they have already dealt with the issue is a reason for not de aling with it again. There are countless issues tha t could ICLS2022 Proceedings 563 © ISLS potentially be raised during the design review and time is limited so there might be plenty of reasons to not bring the same issue up again. Still, the design review i s also an occasion for assessment, and as such, it is understandable that the students orient to quality – the strengths and weaknesses of design proposals – in relative terms.

In a parallel way, the students (and in a different way, the critics) taking part of the desi gn review are learning how to hear and understand t he critique. Although there are separate occasions where the for mat of the design reviews is discussed, including h ow one gives and receives critique, the instruction on how the critique should be heard is ongoing throughout the actual critique sessions. As already pointed out, the situ ation is in a sense inherently unstable where the s tudents sometimes are expected to give accounts for their decisions and at other times they are just supposed to listen. ## Discussion

A central aim of the project is to use the empirica l results to discuss, problematize, and expand the theoretical literature on feedback. Schegloff (1996, p. 164) ha s argued that the disciplined study of particular a ctions is missing in much of the social scientific discourse.  He adds that sometimes “they appear to be virtuall y epiphenomenal expressions of ‘underlying’ factors, processes, and variables – norms, rationality, conf ormity, power, system functions, and the like, but important largely as the public face, the accessible display and indicator, of those underlying forces.” This characterization is also applicable to theories that set out to inve stigate the sensitive nature of feedback. Although theories of facework, politeness, and self-enhancement have som e explanatory value, our video recorded material sugg est that such matters are secondary to more manifes t and pedagogically relevant concerns. The point of the infamous ‘sandwhich model of feedback’, where constructive or critical feedback are embedded in positive comme nts delivered before and after the critique, might not be to act or come across as nice or polite, as often is claimed; but to make the student hear, understand, and respond to the comments as educationally relevant feedback and  not compliants. We therefore believe that a reconceptualization of feedback, grounded in the in  vivo orientations of the participants and sequenti al organization of the interaction, would be a signifi cant conceptual expansion of and a potential altern ative to current theories and models.

A prominent strand in educational research on feedb ack is a ‘remedial’ agenda, embodied both in the efforts to develop optimal ways of delivering feedback, and in the fostering of student and teacher competencies to relevantly understand the significance of instru ctional feedback. Although there are many mertis to  such appcoaches, it is also important to understand how the ambiguous nature of critique is not limited to educational situations and that the very possibility of hearing an advice in a negative way might be wi thout remedy. At the same time, it is important to understand that that an emotional and defensive reaction to critique can not be discarded simply as a ‘misunderstanding’. The possibility of emotional and defensive responses are to no small degree provided by the interactional nature of feedback activities; “hearing it the wrong way” is one in a range of accountably rational ways in which any instantiation of feedback can be heard. There are of course strong reasons why we should aim for a better ‘climate’ in educational setting and there are clearly benefits of fostering different forms of ‘feedback literacies’. Nevertheless there are also reasons to closely investigate the ‘endoge nous pedagogies’ of settings where critical feedback is a constituve part, and to connect our understanding of feedback with our understanding of how critique is produced and received in other mundane and institutional settings. It might be possible to read Hattie and Timperley’s (2007) conclusions regarding “low levels of threat” through an interactional lens, as saying that feedback should ideally be produced and heard as e.g., ‘constructive criticism’, rather than ‘complaints’. As Edwards (2 005) notes, however, these lables are defeasible ve rnacular categories “with normative, oriented-to properties and consequences” (p. 7). The defeasibility of categorizations point to the fact that ”the same stretch of talk might […] be glossed as criticism or accusation, troubles-telling, or merely story-telling or factual reportage, which ar e potentially relevant alternatives for speakers wh en characterizing their activities” (ibid). This means  that what levels of perceived threat might be ascr ibed to a particular instance of feedback cannot be located solely in the production features of the feedback turns. As our analyses illustrate,moreover, orientations to ‘threat’ is a sequential phenomenon, developed incrementally through student responses as much as the design of the feedback itself. In some cases, something akin to ‘perceived threat to self-esteem’ is even explicitly addressed in critics’ remarks regarding ‘how to take critique’. Excerpt 2 can be read as an exhortation to pay attention to the feed back, instead of responding to its summative elemen ts. The important thing is, again, that these are sequential phenomena with an inherent openness to them. Although the problem of how critique is heard or un derstood is inherently interactional, it is also connected to whatever resources one has to make sen se of the critique. In this respect we could observ e that students became accustomed to feedback as something prospective and formative as they progressed through their education. This form of hearing is thus itself a le sson to be learned where one adjusts to the profess ional expectations at work. Nevertheless, there is still room for instructions in the communication. Whenever there are shifts between retrospective assessments and prospe ctive lecturing these shifts can be more or less cl early communicated. The mitigation of what is perceived as threatening to students could reasonably be managed by critics overtly describing what they aim to accomplish with specific comments. We certainly see that this is happening in our materials (and it is partly present in the example as well). For the future however, this topic is worth further treatment.
