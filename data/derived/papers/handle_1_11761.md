# Teachers' Resistance to Implementing Interdisciplinary Education: En Route to Educational Change, Heed the Objections

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Educational innovations hinge on teacher support, making it important to understand their attitudes. This study examines dispositional, situational, and innovation-specific aspects of teachers' attitudes toward changing a disciplinary humanities program to an interdisciplinary one. We followed 117 teachers in 10 schools, collecting quantitative and qualitative data to compare Supporters and Resistors. Resistors were significantly lower on dispositional Openness and focused on challenges for teachers and on epistemic issues related to the structure of the disciplines, while Supporters emphasized student benefits. Most Supporters viewed interdisciplinarity as a means to other ends, such as class management, rather than valuing it intrinsically for its epistemic benefits. Based on these findings, we recommend addressing teachers' concerns about disciplinary integrity and professional autonomy, providing clear epistemic justifications for interdisciplinarity, and tailoring implementation strategies to align with teachers' motivations and readiness for change.

## Background

Interdisciplinary teaching and learning as a curricular and pedagogical innovation This study examines teachers' attitudes toward impl ementing Interdisciplinary Teaching and Learning (I TL), broadly defined as the integration of two or more subjects or disciplines within educational settings. Although the roots of this approach can be tra ced to early educational thought (e.g., Dewey, 1909), it largely receded during the mid-20th century as a result of  the prioritization of specialized disciplinary know ledge, which was seen as essential for advancing academic rigor and meeting the growing demands of industrial and professional sectors (Tyler, 1949). Interdisciplinary approaches were often dismissed as  lacking coherence or depth, reinforcing the dominance of siloed disciplines in both curriculum and pedagogy for several decades. In recent years, ITL has reemerged as an innovative alternative to traditional discipline-based teaching, offering new possibilities for both curricular organization and pedagogical practice (C ohen et al., 2024a; OECD, 2019; UNESCO, 2021). In 2024, the Journal of the Learning Sciences dedicated its annual special issue to ITL, highligh ting a growing interest in this approach among educational researchers (Novis-Deutsch et al., 2024b). A recent scoping review (Cohen et al., 2024c) identified four primary forms of ITL implementati on: 1) Using knowledge from one discipline to expand the boundar ies of another;  2) Applying multiple disciplinary perspectives to a single issue;  3) Focusing on foundational knowledge that spans multiple discipli nes and – 4) Creating new disciplines through the combination of  existing ones.  Given the diversity of its applications in education, ITL is also referred to by other names, including transdisciplinary education, project-or problem-based learning with interdisciplinary elements, integrative teaching, and cross-curricular instruction. These approaches can be realized in various ways, including individual teachers redesigning their teaching to incorporate multi-disciplinary sources, co-teaching partnerships in which two teachers bring several disciplines into dialogue, or coordinated efforts by a team of teachers. These varied implementations highlight th at ITL involves both curricular and pedagogical change.

A key finding of the review was the fragmented natu re of ITL research. Studies addressing its definiti on, implementation, benefits, and constraints are relatively sparse, disconnected, and often lack a cohesive framework (Cohen et al., 2024c). This aligns with earlier critiques of ITL as “remarkably underconceptualized” (Applebee et al., 2007, p. 1004). Understanding the attitudes of teachers (Fullan, 2007)—who are central to implementing these changes in practice—could help to advance ITL theoretically and practically. Currently, findings on the efficacy of ITL for student learning, engagement, and skill development are mixed, with some studies reporting improvements (Spintzyk et al., 2016) and others sho wing no significant change or even negative outcome s (Xu, 2022).

Teachers’ role in the success or failure of educational change Educational reforms and the implementation of educational innovations, or in short, educational change, are both prevalent and significant in shaping educational pr actices and outcomes. However, repeated attempts to  change or innovate  may also lead to teacher fatigue, resistance, and ultimately, the failure of potentially beneficial initiatives (van Veen & Sleegers, 2006).

Teachers play a critical role in the success or fai lure of educational changes (Datnow, 2020). While often less involved in the policymaking or development phases, they hold significant power as implementers over their adoption and effectiveness. Furthermore, there is an ethical imperative to involve teachers meaningfu lly in the change process. As Eisner (2000, p. 347) notes in reviewing fifty years of school changes, teachers need "to feel a part of, if not in control of, the improvement process. The teacher... needs to have a stake in how schools change." Understanding and predicting which teachers will su pport or resist changes has been a central focus of educational implementation research. Differences between supporters and resistors of educational innovations can be categorized into three main types. Dispositional factors refer to individual teacher traits, such as opennes s to change or a tendency toward optimism versus pessimism. Situational factors encompass aspects of the educational context, including the degree of autonomy afforded to teachers, their years of experience, and the strength of their professional identity. Finally, innovation-specific factors relate to the characteristics of the innovation itself, such as its documented success, anticipated benefits, and the clarity and simplicity of its implementation Previous studies on teachers' positioning regarding  educational innovations have found that these fact ors influence their acceptance and implementation behav iors (Ketelaar et al., 2012; Johnson et al., 2017).  It is important therefore to learn what factors correlate with support for or rejection of ITL, and what types of issues concern Resistors versus those that motivate Supporters.

In this paper, we explore the three categories of differences between Support ers and Resistors of educational change, in relation to teachers’ attitudes to ITL, as a first step towards understanding teachers’ impact in ITL implementation. This paper poses  two research questions, which link the case of ITL with broader challenges of implementing educational changes:

1. What dispositional, situational and innovation-specific factors relate to teachers’ support/objection to ITL?

2. What are the concerns of teachers who resist ITL, and what motivates teachers who support it?

## Context and design

This study was conducted among teachers in middle s chools throughout Israel, a context characterized b y heavy workloads, relatively low salaries, and frequent educational reforms. These reforms, typically introduced in a topdown manner, are often subject to abrupt changes resulting from political instabil ity and recurrent elections, leading to frequent turnovers in the Ministry of Ed ucation. Consequently, teachers tend to approach ed ucational innovations with skepticism or resistance, exhibiting protectiveness toward their limited time and capacity due to their already demanding responsibilities.

We conducted a four-year government-funded study on ITL in the humanities in middle schools in Israel. Our goals were both  theoretical and applied, as the Israeli government intended to use the findings to determine whether to transition all  humanities subjects  in secondary education in the country from disciplinary to interdisciplinary.

Ten middle schools throughout the country were recruited to develop and implement ITL programs, with guidance and teacher training offered by the research team.  In Year 1, a pilot phase included 39 teachers  and a model for transitioning to ITL was developed. In Year 2, the program was expanded to include 117 teachers from the same schools. The research design in Year 2 included a pre-program survey (T1 survey) for the teachers, a 6month period of teacher training, designing teaching units and implementing them in classrooms, and then a postprogram survey (T2 survey). This paper focuses on teachers’ attitudes in Year 2. Additional part of the study, including student testing and observations in classrooms, are not reported in this paper. This study was a research-practice partnership. Rather than prescribing speci fic interdisciplinary topics or strategies, the training supported teachers acro ss three key areas: (1) identifying and mapping div erse ITL approaches—ranging from teacher teams collaboratively addressing a common theme from multiple disciplinary perspectives to individual teachers guiding students to explore a topic through multiple disciplinary lenses; (2) providing a modular, adaptable framework for design ing and implementing context-appropriate ITL units; and

(3) actively assisting teacher teams as they developed these units. For example, one team of four teachers created

an interdisciplinary unit on colonialism, beginning with several Judaic studies lessons highlighting both colonialist and egalitarian themes  in the Bible, and continuing through alternating history and ci vics classes aimed at exposing the origins, negative impacts, and potential remedies related to Western colonialism. Their average age was 41.1 years (SD = 9.7), comparable to the nation al average of 43 years (OECD, 2018). The majority (81.1%) were women, similar to the national proportion of female teachers (78%; OECD, 2018). 117 respondents completed T1 survey, and 84 completed T2 survey, an average of 6.3 months later. As the open-ended question was offered only to some respondents (see “Items”), 82 teachers responded to it on the first round, and 55 on the second. A smaller subset of teachers (n=60) also responded to the Openness and Life-orientation scales, on the T2 survey.

## Findings

The average teacher rating of support for ITL was 6.93 on a 0–10 scale (SD = 2.46, n=92) on the T1 survey and 7.16 (SD=2, n=75) on the T2 survey, a non-significant difference, statistically. 18.5% opposed ITL (average rating = 3), and 81.5% supported it (average rating = 8.7)  on the T1 survey while 16% opposed ITL (average rating = 3.3), and 84% supported it (average rating = 8.4) on the T2 survey. Teachers’ answers to another  question — “How many hours would you prefer to dedicate to interdisciplinary teaching versus disciplinary teaching?” — confirmed that these were indeed two distinct groups with different views. A t-test comparing the average responses of both groups  (Resistors’ average: 28% of teaching hours; Supporters’ average: 55% of teaching hours) revealed a significant difference with a large ef fect size (Cohen’s d = 1.64).

ITL Supporters and resistors: Differences on dispositional and situational scales An independent samples t-test was conducted to compare levels of Openness between Supporters of ITL (n = 48) and Resistors (n = 12). The results revealed a statistically significant di fference in Openness scores between the groups, t(58) = -2.99, p =.004, with Supporters (M = 5.00, SD = 0.83) reporting significantly highe r levels of Openness than Resistors (M = 4.00, SD = 1.08). The meandifference was-1.00 (95% CI [-1.67, -0.34]), with a large effect size (Cohen’s d = 1.04).

None of the other variables we tested (professional identity, educational autonomy, life orientation, years of educational experience and years of experience w ith ITL) differed significantly between Resistors or Supporters of ITL at either time point. However, for the Life Orientation Scale, the difference between Resistors and Supporters approached significance, t(55) = 1.72, p =.091, wi th a meandifference of 0.33 (95% CI [-0.05, 0.72]), suggesting a potential trend towards higher optimism and life satisfaction among ITL Supporters. ITL Resistors and supporters: Differences in form and content of rationales Resistors-Pre intervention categories of opposition to ITL Although the number of teachers who initially opposed interdisciplinary teaching was about a quar ter of those who supported it, the Resistors were significantly more detailed in their responses. On average, Resistors expressed their views using 42.2 words per responde nt, while Supporters used an average of only 13.8 words — roughly one-third the length of the Resistors' responses. The teachers who resisted interdisciplinary teaching presented a total of 25 different rationales for their opposition, which we grouped into  seven categories. We next list these, offering some examples of texts  by Resistors: Most of the objections offered by Resistors focused on the negative impact of ITL on teachers, accounting for 29% of the arguments. These included, for example: ̶ Teachers are required to teach subjects in which they are not experts. ̶ Interdisciplinary teaching requires extensive coordination and meetings between teachers. ̶ ITL is perceived as a top-down imposition on teachers.  This last sub-category (feeling of coercion) is noteworthy, as ITL was hypothesized initially to of fer flexibility and autonomy for teachers. Yet, one teacher explained: “There is pressure from the system for teachers to teach subjects they did not choose. If I had wanted to teach Bible studies, geography, or litera ture, I would have studied those subjects at university. To complete my workload, I am required to teach interdisciplinary courses, which means spending a lot of hours learning topics and materials that I never studied and for which I do not have a teaching certification.” Another teacher contrasted the sense of confidence in teaching one’s subject with the uncertainty in ITL: “When the teacher is an expert in their subject, [t hey] feel confident in the material and know how to adapt it to the needs of the class. Interdis ciplinary teaching creates a situation where a teacher who is not confident in the material is exp ected to guide students through it. This is a stressful, problematic situation that requires unreasonable additional preparation.” A second set of objections (17%) related to  defending the disciplines and their importance. For  example, one teacher wrote: “The skills, knowledge, contexts, and uniqueness of each subject can easily be lost when making these cross-disciplinary connections. Later, when students choo se to deepen their studies in a specific field, their foundation is weak, or they f irst need a stable foundati on in each subject before creating connections between them.” Thirdly, Resistors argued that for ITL to be successful, certain conditions must be met (17%), and explained why it is not the case for this initiative. For example, one teacher explained why middle schoo l is not the right developmental stage for ITL: “The ability to connect different fields, recognize the complexity of human phenomena, deeply and creatively explore human creative processes, and analyse societal challenges is not easy for middle school students. In my view, only after acquiring basic knowledge and skills, is it possible to expand and deepen studies in the humanities and interdisciplinary studies within the humanities.” A fourth set of arguments concerned the impact of interdisciplinary teaching on students (14.6%). For example, one teacher described the students’ perspective: “They struggle to grasp the concept of interdiscipl inary studies and don’t understand what is expected of them. Furthermore, there is no real pre paration for essential disciplinary skills or prior knowledge needed for high school. [...] They do not understand or connect with interdisciplinary learning at all.” Fifth, some teachers noted the absence of a coherent ITL methodology (9.7%). For example, one teacher argued: “I feel that the connections between subjects are n ot made professionally, and as a result, students end up missing out on both sides. It’s bet ter to study history properly, and national

In the sixth category, 7.3% of teachers opposed ITL because they felt it was unnecessary, and in the seventh, 4.8% drew on previous unsuccessful experience with such teaching to oppose ITL on pedagogical grounds. Supporters-Pre intervention categories of opposition to ITL The rationale of the teachers who supported ITL was quite different in character. This much larger group presented a total of 38 supporting arguments, which we grouped into six main categories. Although they were the larger groups, their arguments were shorter and less elaborate. The most frequent set of arguments (33.3%) involved support for ITL based on pedagogical considerations. For example, one teacher suggested: “Interdisciplinary teaching enables the creation of connections and contexts for students, enriches their general knowledge, and provides a range of skills and the use of diverse learning strategies." The second series of arguments (26.6%) was related to student’s cognitive learning considerations. For example, one teacher stated: "I believe that interdisciplinary teaching can enha nce learning in the classroom; students will benefit and be able to integrate multiple content areas, resulting in higher levels of thinking." The third set of considerations (12.2%) focused on the potential of interdisciplinary teaching to promote cognitive skills. For example, one teacher argued: "It requires students to engage in creative thinking." The fourth category of considerations (6.6%) pertained to epis temic arguments in favor of ITL. For example: ̶ "Subjects cannot be separated; everything is interconnected, and most learning skills are similar" ̶ "It’s the most accurate approach. The humanities are composed of m any layers, and we want to teach

## Discussion and conclusion

Despite its long-standing presence among  some educational practitioners (Wineburg & Grossman, 200 0), ITL remains largely at odds with the predominantly disc iplinary approach of current teaching practices, ma king its adoption in classrooms  an innovation  and its institutional implementation a reform.  Most empirical efforts to conceptualize ITL to date have focused on its advantages, seeking to show how the shift to ITL might mediate desirable learning processes and outcomes. However,  in our study, teachers who opposed ITL made some carefully thought-out arguments against it. We think this may not be incidental: While studies show that a positive, optimistic life orientation  is beneficial for individuals  (Carver & Schier, 2014), other  studies suggest that pessimism or realism may align more closely with assessment accuracy (Harpaz-Itay & Kaniel, 2012; Moore & Fresco, 2012; Sharot, 2011). Education reformers should hope for a positive and enthusiastic teacher  group to work with but should heed the critiques and counterarguments of the doubters, nonetheless. The findings reveal several noteworthy patterns: First, the statistically significant difference in dispositional Openness between Resistors and Supporters suggests that teachers' willingness to embrace new pedagogies may depend on personality traits that shape their receptiveness to innovation. This indicates a need to tailor implementation strategies to accommodate individual differences. We found greater detail in Resistors’ objections compared to Supporters’ rationales. This demonstrates the efforts made by Resistors, as a minority, to explain their point of view, and suggests that resistors may serve as an underutilized resource for addressing practical and conceptual weaknesses in reform initiatives. We also found noteworthy systematic differences in the way Resistors and Supporters argued their case:

1. Resistors mainly considered the challenges faced by teachers, while Supporters emphasized the potential

benefits for students.

2. Resistors expressed concern for the integrity of disciplines, fearing that ITL would dilute subject-specific

expertise. Supporters, however, largely viewed ITL as a means to achieve broader pedagogical goals (e.g., fostering student engagement and critical thinking) rather than an end in itself. Only a minority of Supporters saw ITL as an epistemically superior approach.

3. Supporters envisioned ITL as an innovation that could enhance skills and engagement. Resistors, on the

other hand, critiqued the current implementation of  interdisciplinary pedagogy, citing a lack of depth, coherence, and professionalism. They viewed ITL as a promising idea but poorly executed, potentially harming student learning. One teacher elucidated this: "Everything they claim can supposedly be achieved with this program can absolutely be done, and I have done it myself, when teaching in a disciplinary approach. The diffe rence is that in disciplinary learning, students get the added bonus of a teacher who actually studied history, literature or Bible studies at the university level — someone who knows and understands the subject deeply, loves what they chose to study, and inspires the students with their passion for that field. The teacher provides thorough and comprehens ive instruction, emphasizing both subject-specific skills and transferable skills that can be  applied across different subjects. Very few teachers are capable of achieving such a level of mastery for  multiple disciplines deeply enough to integrate everything and genuinely create and teach authentic ITL units." Interestingly, the concerns of Resistors mapped onto our findings following an ethnographic study in classrooms where ITL was being closely monitored (reported elsewhere; Cohen et al., 2024a). Analyzing discourse from ITL classrooms helped us identify two key challenges of interdisciplinarity, both of which loomed large in the concerns of teachers opposed to ITL:  First, the concern that students will not understand how different subjects complement each other, and why. This was indeed one of the findings in analyzing students’ feedback on ITL. Often, teachers tended to treat ITL as disciplinary  classes with new titles, and when interdisciplinary comparisons were foregrounded, confusion ensued among students. Second, there was a lack of disciplinary expertise in ITL classes, which prevented teachers from delving deeply into a body of knowledge that they were not experts on. Comparing Resistors and Supporters  of ITL turned out to be somewhat like comparing apples and oranges. Rather than evaluating the same aspects of ITL  and reaching different conclusions, the two groups focused on entirely different aspects of it. This means that, in principle, both groups could be correct in their assessments, as ITL might be better for students than for teachers. This might not be incidental, since teachers received their teacher training in disciplines, but  students might need something different, inter-or trans-disciplinary, to prepare them for the future. And w hile there may be some good epistemic ground for su pporting ITL, these principles may not be well-formulated or familiar to most teachers. We believe  that this highlights a fundamental issue in implementing educational innov ations: while prioritizing student outcomes is understandable, the educational system functions as an ecosystem.  If teachers lack confidence in their ability to implement ITL or lack understanding of its underlying structure of knowledge. student benefits are unlikely to be realized.

## Limitations and future research

In his book on educational change, Fullan (2007) suggest that reforms involve changing the content (curriculum), behaviors (pedagogies) and beliefs (attitudes) of t eachers. He also notes that among the three, “change in beliefs and understanding are the foundations of achieving lasting reform” (Fullan, 2007, p.37). Correspondingly, in this study, resistance to change was found, for the most  part, to depend on  innovation-specific attitudes (and one dispositional one), rather than situational ones. However, this may only be part of the picture. Schools are complex ecosystems, and to fully understand the factors contributing to the success or failure of ITL, power dynamics and hierarchical structures within schools need to be considered as well, which are, of course, situational factors.  As Datnow (2020, p. 433) observes, “gender, power, and  knowledge are inextricably connected and influence teachers’ reactions to reform and the contestation of meaning within schools.” Teacher attitudes towar d ITL represent an essential first step  in its implementation, but they are only one aspect of the broader process of ITL implementation in educational settings, to be explored more thoroughly in future research. In line with this, future research can and should disaggregate supporters and resistors by school characteristics, years of teaching, subject area, and gender. While preliminary analyses in this study did not un cover systematic differences along these lines, a larger and more diverse sample of teachers might do so. Recommendations In broadening the implications of our f indings from implementation of ITL to additional contexts o f education change, we offer the following recommendations for schools and educational teams, with examples focusing on ITL implementation:

1. Consider the Resistors' critiques.  Resistors may offer valuable, realistic critiques that should  not be

dismissed. Addressing these concerns can lead to more effective implementation.

2. Evaluate Pedagogical Goals Carefully. As we saw, most teachers view ITL as a means to serve extrinsic

pedagogical needs. It is advisable to assess whether the innovation at hand is the most effective approach for the actual goals teachers aim for, or if simple r, more direct strategies could achieve the same objectives. For example, increasing the number of hours a teach er spends with their students does not necessarily requite an entire ITL reform.

3. Develop an epistemic perspective to complement the educational innovation. An innovation or reform

that is not  epistemically justifiable is an initiative at risk.  In the case of ITL, teaching teams should articulate its distinct epistemic advantages, contrasting it with traditional disciplinary approaches. For example, the promise of ITL may lie in its alignment with the  multi-dimensional reality of our time, facilitating perspective-taking and dialogue across diverse viewpoints.

4. Before implementing an innovation, offer teachers a  choice. Allow teachers to opt in or out of reforms.

In the case of ITL, positioning it as an advanced structure built on a solid disciplin ary foundation will enable subject-matter experts to focus on core skills while enabling other teachers to engage in ITL.

5. Tailor implementation strategies. Assess teachers' attitudes towards  innovations such as ITL and tailor

implementation strategies accordingly. For ITL, d ifferentiate approaches for those who resist it, those who support it as a tool, and those who see it as an end to itself.

6. When implementing an educational innovation, s elect the appropriate target population. For example,

ITL should not replace the development of foundatio nal knowledge and learning skills  among younger or weaker students. Consider delaying implementation until students are well prepared for it.

7. Adhere to the "First, Do No Harm" Principle. Recognize that educational reforms and innovations,

including ITL, have the potential to hinder as well as advance teaching. Resistors listed quite a few of these, such as confusion for students, lack of time  to focus on disciplinary basic skills, and achievi ng a shallow level of integration. Teams should work actively to prevent such negative impacts on students. In sum, Resistors made thoughtful, well-reasoned arguments about how and when ITL should  and should not be implemented. It is important to heed their warnings —not only because they represent a significant minor ity of teachers or because they clearly feel strongly about their resistance, as evidenced by their effortful responses, but because they may be raising critical issues that could undermine the success of the innovation if left unaddressed.
