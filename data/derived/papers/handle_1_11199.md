# Turning Points Along Embodied Pathways: Bringing Forth Children’s Voices Through Artistic Ways of Listening

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper describes a critical arts-based learning experience for a group of children between 5-and 7-years-old who co-authored and enacted a short story through movement, drama, music, and visual art during a 2-week summer dance camp I co-designed with other artists in a major Northeastern metropolitan city. Following children’s embodied pathways, I find turning points in children’s artistic journeys where embodied and relational encounters reoriented their movements in personally meaningful ways. Turning points along embodied pathways reveal children’s dialogic experiences with artistic forms of representation as personally meaningful in their learning and being, bringing forth children’s embodied voices by decentering adult conventions of dialogue and voice. My findings encourage learning scientists to explore artistic ways of listening to children’s dialogic experiences through centering children’s epistemologies in learning design.

## Introduction

For learning to cultivate healing, resilience, and community in our multigenerational world, children’ s voices must be heard. As Bang (2020) articulates, “Our theories of liberation speak to adult worlds-not multigenerational worlds that include young children or worlds in whi ch children are always full and present participant s or aren’t relegated to the background of adult action” (p. 437). L earning Sciences scholarship can feed  adult-centric orientations that reproduce individualist, age-segregated power constructions  if young children are decentered. To hear children, we must notice how we listen, because children’s voices resonate across multiple forms of representation unfamiliar to adults. I draw from queer phenomenology “to consider how the familiar takes shape by being unnoticed  (Ahmed, 2006, p. 37). In exploring other ways of listening  from queer phenomenological lenses, learning scientists can reorient learning design to bring forth children’s voices from the background. This study centered the embodied voices of young ch ildren in co-authoring and co-enacting their own short story. Through critical arts-based design (Finley, 2017) and the aesthetic-intersubjective paradigm (Leavy, 2020), I respected children’s sensorial, relational, affective, and imaginative ways of knowing, being, seeing, and doing. Following children’s embodied pathways (Vossoughi et al., 2020), I noticed turning points (Ahmed, 2006) in their artistic journeys  wherein relational and embodied encounters reoriented their movements in personally meaningful ways that endured over time. As I elaborate below, attuning to turning points in children’s embodied pathways reveals artistic experiences as dialogic meaning-making, reframing how we “listen” to children’s dialogues by decentering adult methodological conventions that predominantly value spoken and written dialogic experiences in learning.

## Conceptual framework

Invoking other ways of listening, turning points along embodied pathways tend to children’s meaningful, dialogic encounters with creative forms of representation. Embodied pathways (Vossoughi et al., 2020) detail the relational history of children’s value-laden movements and illustrate how these meaningful movements “endure, or become intentionally sustained, over time” (p. 187) and develop into embodied resources for future encounters. Embodied pathways build upon Goodwin’s (2013) concept of substrates: resourceful actions of others that become inhabited and reshaped to produce new meaning through dialogic turns. Following Bakhtin’s (1981) dialogic view-that one’s words are “half someone else’s  [and] becomes ‘one’s own’ only when the speaker populates it with  his intention” (p. 293) - embodied pathways demonstrate how one’s movement are half someone else’s, and it is one’s intention and vitality when inhabiting and transforming other’s prior movements that authors actions as personally meaningful. Thus, embodied pathways expand conventional notions of dialogism to embrace embodied voice and embodied dialogues.

In our embodied journey toward becoming (Bakhtin, 1 981), movements and bodies are living histories of past relational encounters, embodied dialogues of coming into contact with objects and others (Ahmed, 2006). Proximal objects and others orient  bodies in certain directions, rather than others, lead ing bodies to inhabit, reproduce, and perform movements that are not casual, in turn shaping bodies to “extend into spaces that have already taken their shape” (Ahmed, 2006, p. 139).  However, “Life, after all, is full of turning points [emphasis added]” (p. 15). When bodies come into contact with different objects and others, they turn, and “different worlds might even come into view. If such turns are repeat ed over time, then bodies acquire the very shape of  such direction” (p. 15). Therefore, I define turning points as relational en counters that reorient the direction of an embodied pathway toward expansive possibilities that are inhabited, personally transf ormed, and meaningfully sustained and endured over time. By investigating encounters that initiate turning points, learning scientists can better listen to children’s embodied voices in how their embodied pathways take shape as an effect of meaningful dialogic encounters. In this study, children had re lational, embodied, and dialogic encounters that were turning points in their choreographic journeys.

## Methodology

This study took place as part of a 2-week summer dance camp I co-designed with other artists for 18 children between the ages of 5-and 7-years old, who had minimal or no dance training, in collaboration with a community dance center in a major Northeastern metropolitan city. Demographically, 78% of children identified as girls, 10% as boys, and 5% as gender nonconforming; 57% of children identified as Black, Latinx, Middle Eastern, or Asian; and most lived near the dance center. During nine, daily 1-hour composition classes, I guided this group to co-author and bring to life a short story through move ment, music, drama, and visual art. The camp culmin ated in a small hybrid studio-performance for families and a n in-person gallery featuring children’s artwork. As critical arts-based research (Finley, 2017),  I utilized the aesthetic-intersubjective paradigm (Leavy, 2020) t o merge sensorial, affective, and imaginative ways of knowi ng with the intersubjective relational meaning-making between people, objects, and art. This paradigm celebrates the lifeforce of young children and recognizes art and aesthetics as co-constructed, dialogic meaning-making experiences (Dewey, 1934). This study’s criticality laid in my intention to cultivate children’s multiple forms of representation and self-expression whilst positioning myself as a teacher to listen artistically to their voices. Data collection methods of video recording, artistic artifacts, and field notes accumulated 325 minutes of video, 63 artistic artifacts made by children (including self-portraits, character illustrations, backdrops, props, and story drafts), and several pages of fieldnotes. Respecting children’s embodied dramatization of the ir characters as personally meaningful and intentional, I was interested in the artistic choic es that led to children’s character enactments. Ana lytically, I retraced children’s embodied pathways from their final performance back to their interactional firsts by organizing their choreographies into subsequent sets of moveme nts, emotions, and expressions  chronologically in iMovie. Mapping the micro-evolution of children’s choreographies, I coded relational encounters that modified children’s movement patterns in enacting their characters and endured, over time, until their final choreographies.

## Findings

My findings pinpoint relational encounters where children inhabited and meaningfully transformed movements to enact their character. Over time, these meaningful moveme nts endured and stabilized as children’s final choreographies, yet never lost the embodied substrates of prior interactional encounters. I conceptualize these relational encounters as turning points because the y reoriented the movement patterns of children in e xpansive ways they may have not explored on their own.  In my analysis, I found turning points in children’ s encounters with human others, visual art, and sonic art. For t his paper, I focus solely on encounters with human others that opened movement possibilities. ## Scholarly significance of the study

Children’s learning and being, especially those of very young children, is often devalued and infantil ized when adults (researchers included) are unable, or unwill ing, to conceive children as having something valua ble to say, show, or teach. Often, children’s voices cannot be heard because their forms of representation are rec onstructed and reinterpreted by adults (Templeton, 2021), with every variation of analysis  moving farther away from children’s authentic voices. Children are subjugate d and exist within imbalanced power-relations that feed orientations toward learning and schooling supersed ing child agency and capacity. These ideologies silence children, leaving them out of dialogues about the world they inherit and futures they cultivate. In providing artistic modalities for children to “speak,” my paper encourages researchers to learn to listen to children directly by decentering adult interpretation and perception of children’s  voice and dialogue. My findings of turning points along Kai’s embodied pathway show how movements can be listened to dialogically, cont ributing both conceptually and methodologically to the field of t he Learning Sciences. In my analysis of Kai’s embod ied pathway, their turning points surprised me because I as a teacher did not know they existed. Although I had lived through the dance camp alongside Kai and their peer s, I was unaware of the artistic, meaning-making relational encounters that were impactful in Kai’s learning experience. I could not “hear” Kai’s voice until I attuned to their movements. Without listening to Kai’s embodied voice, I would have missed their dialogic, responsive, and intentional movement negotiations toward manifesting a personally meaningful character they were proud to share with the people they loved. In offering turning points along embodied pathways as an artistic way of listening to children, my analysis expands conventional notions of dialogue to include embodied voices in learning and reveals how artistic design can bring forth children’s voices from the background.
