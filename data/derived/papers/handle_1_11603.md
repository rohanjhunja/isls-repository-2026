# Toward a Method for Visualizing Interactional Copresence

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This poster presents a provocation for methodological advancements in qualitative inquiry into spatial pedagogy. Using classroom data that includes student talk during groupwork sessions and a dynamic map of teacher location, we offer several visualizations to invite speculation about how we might amend these tools to think more deeply about the joint accomplishment of copresence in classrooms.

## Overview

Existing literature on spatial pedagogy predominant ly originates in the field of learning analytics and tends to promote a cognitivist framework and/or an evaluativ e stance (e.g. Lim et al, 2012; Martinez-Maldonado et al, 2022). Researchers in the Learning  Sciences have made some movement toward promoting s ociocultural perspectives on this emerging topic, as well as int egrating it with teacher perspectives and issues of  responsive teaching (Shapiro et al., 2024). Nevertheless, there is much to explore with regards to how concepts of space and place may be developed as an interactional achievem ent of people in classrooms, both for teachers conc erned about spatial equity, and for researchers thinking about moving past analytic concepts like location and proximity and toward interactionist notions of copresence (c. f. Campos-Castillo & Hitlan, 2013). Furthermore, with the increasing prominence of computer-aided investigative tools, and particularly their u tility in mapping space and movement, it behooves us as a field to think about how these complex, dynamic concepts can be embedded  in tools that inherently privilege unitarily defined variables. In this poster, we present counter-intuitive examples and possible alternative uses of  a GIS tool (described below) as provocations for considering m ethodological innovations in visualizing teacher-student copresence beyond simple proximity. We do so with illustrative data from an ongoing project involving this same tool. Even so, we are not making an argument about what these new visualizations do or do not show, only positing that such visualizations could help disturb the idea that location or proximity  alone are the best window into the interactional accomplishment of thinking together i n a classroom. This provocation contributes to the emerging focus of spatial pedagogy in the Learning Sciences, and particularly how an interactionist view can push this work without eschewing computerized tools.

Theoretical orientations We take an interactionist view grounded in sociocul tural theories of learning (Vygotsky, 1986) and bui lding on the work of Lave and Wenger (1991), which holds tha t learning takes place in a community of practice t hat both shapes and is shaped by the actions of individuals in coordination with other people, as well as cultural tools and practices, as they work toward some goal. More spec ifically, we aim to connect this work to notions of placemaking (Zitcer, 2020) and to previous literature on copresence (Goffman, 1964; Campos-Castillo & Hitlan, 2013), highlighting how the subjective experience of being present with a more knowledgeable other (Moll, 1990) is intimately tied to temporally emergent routines and their attendant expectations and awareness patterns.

## Methods and research context

Typical questions about teacher presence assume a s urveillance stance toward students, and often focus  on whether students are “on task” or not once the teac her walks away, or whether the group dynamics are t ruly collaborative instead of replicating IRE discourse patterns. We are using video records and location d ata to describe classroom patterns and thus cannot claim to escape the surveillance idiom. Nevertheless, by complicating the notion of proximity we invite a more descriptiv e rather than evaluative stance, and suggest that teachers be considered as partners in learning rather than mere  monitors of behavior. Furthermore, we suggest that  the phenomenological experience of copresence is interactionally emergent in space and time. Our entry point is a tool called the Interaction Ge ography Slicer (IGS), which was developed for use i n a broad range of learning environments (Shapiro et al., 2017), but also specifically honed for use in classrooms (e.g. Shapiro & Garner, 2022) and in co llaborative co-inquiry with teachers (Metts et al., 2024). In cont rast with learning analytic tools such as heat maps or even d andelion diagrams (Pencheng et al., 2020), the IGS is interactive, dynamically displaying not only a teac her’s location over time  with reference to a floor plan of the classroom, but also navigable transcripts and video  clips. The design is meant to promote robust inqui ry into dynamic spatial relations. Even so, current use of the tool is limited to individualized professional development contexts. A goal of this provocation is to further develop the tool so that basic features reflect the deeper levels of engagement that teachers have achieved in interaction with peers and professional development organizers. Data for these specific visualizations were taken from a multi-year research-practice partnership in which researchers are embedded in a large, urban school d istrict. In using the IGS to support teacher learni ng about groupwork facilitation, we noticed that teachers’ i nitial questions tended implicitly to privilege the  teacher’s perspective, including the culturally embedded assu mption that teachers are required to monitor and ev aluate progress along a prescribed learning trajectory. Th e following are some  preliminary provocations for how we might disrupt these assumptions and use our data to tell a more complex story. Assumptions and provocations: Possible avenues to support complex inquiry One way to shift from a proximity based concept of copresence might be to imbue it with a theory of wh at copresence does. The most basic surveillance-based version is that students are on task because the teacher is watching them, and if the teacher looks away they may be goofing off. Copresence keeps kids on task. A slightly more complex (and more charitable) version invol ves the Zone of Proximal Development (ZPD) —the idea attributed to Vygotsky (1978) that while working with a more knowledgeable other, children are capable of more than their current developmental level (i.e. what t hey cando on their own). In this way, adults offer  a “loan of consciousness” to children, allowing them to rise a bove their current intellectual status. From this p erspective, a teacher’s goal might be to keep kids in their ZPD, thus maximizing learning potential. Assumption: Presented with spatialized data, a common assumption is that students might be in the ZPD when the teacher is close by (generally talking wit h students) and the farther she goes (or the longer  she’s been away), it becomes less and less likely they are sti ll in it. In other words, copresence facilitates ch ildren being in their ZPD, and that requires proximity. Based on th is assumption “spatial equity” means visiting groups equally, and the work of the teacher while physically present would be prodding them back into the ZPD. Provocation: Figure 1 shows two visualizations from a classroom where the teacher’s goal is to support kids to work independently. In 1A we see that stude nts are involved in rich, personally meaningful sen semaking when the teacher is away. In 1B, we see that her ar rival prompts a brief shift to more procedural discuss ions, arguably moving the group out of the ZPD.

Figure 1 What is Proximity Doing?

From this disconfirming example, we argue that prox imity is not achieving copresence, if copresence is  what nudges children into the ZPD. Further, we argue tha t the simple metric of “visiting groups equally” wi ll not achieve a goal of achieving copresence.

Neither does it suggest, however, that the teacher’ s physical location is entirely irrelevant. Instead, this teacher appears to have achieved her goal of having student groups work independently. Is she done? Would these same students work independently in another context? Would they be at a loss if she gave them another teacher’s worksheet? Put another way, what are the other elem ents of this activity system that are supporting th e students to work independently, and how might those be related to the histories of participation in this classroom, including the teacher’s movement? How does a teacher’s moveme nt around the classroom influence the achievement o f copresence? How might it contribute to the rhythm o f student collaboration? How do students use the t eacher’s presence (or absence) to make progress on their goa ls? How do students take control of the teacher’s p resence (e.g. calling the teacher over; reanimating the tea cher in light of physical absence (e.g. verbally or  pictorally)? This poster will explore some of these questions and invite many more.
