# Making Sense of Integer Story-Interpretation Problems

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Exploring students’ conceptions of integers is more complex than simply explaining the elements of integer knowledge and certainly is challenging to describe how those elements are structured. In this paper, I highlight how fifth graders restructured their whole-number knowledge elements to establish an integer knowledge structure by exploring their interpretations of two integer story-interpretation problems. Students with correct integer order and value interpretations were most successful in solving these problems. Students who knew negative numbers are less than positive numbers but had difficulties interpreting integers’ values were more successful than those who relied on numbers’ absolute value. However, they attempted to use the negative signs in their number-sentences. This study informs the importance of triangulating students’ integer knowledge elements, the process of forming an integer knowledge structure, and further highlights the significance of using problem-solving situations that allow for eliciting diverse mathematical thinking and learning processes.

With introducing negative numbers, the knowledge elements around order and value, minus sign interpretations, addition and subtraction, and associated mathematical language requires restructuring to form a coherently structured integer knowledge system. From whole-number knowledge to integer knowledge, students need to make sense of multiple meanings of the minus sign (i.e., unary, symmetric, and binary; Vlassis, 2004, 2008), distinguish between absolute value (|-5| > |-3|) versus linear value (-5 < -3), and interpret the meanings of addition and subtraction while accompanying them with the mathematical language (i.e., directed magnitude language; e.g., adding a negative number is getting more negative; Aqazade & Bofferding, 2021; Bofferding, 2014, 2019). These knowledge elements may seem mutually inclusive. However, some students may have developed one or more of their whole-number knowledge elements into integer knowledge elements without framing a coherent structure of integer knowledge among them. For example, one candistinguish the linear values versus absolute values by connecting to the directed magnitude language (e.g., -5 is more negative than-3) but may not integrate the directional aspects of more and less with values when adding and subtracting with

negative integers (Aqazade, 2021; Aqazade & Bofferding, 2021). To develop a coherently structured integer knowledge system, the knowledge elements need to be reorganized into a theory or schema (Aqazade & Bofferding, 2021). Thus, it is crucial to provide problem-solving situations that allow for students to exhibit their interpretations of knowledge elements individually and collectively within the knowledge system. Such problem-solving situations candepict a more holistic view of students’ knowledge development and open space for their diverse mathematical thinking. By drawing from and building on prior research around students’ integer knowledge (e.g., Bishop et al., 2014; Bofferding, 2014; Whitacre et al., 2017), in this paper, I aim to illustrate a more holistic picture of how students’ integer knowledge elements were in formation towards establishing a coherent integer knowledge structure using a particular problem-solving situation (i.e., storyinterpretation problems). I argue that using story-interpretation problems challenged students to rethink their integer knowledge elements and revise or restructure them for forming a cohesive integer knowledge structure. Thus, this work provides perspectives on learning processes and problem-solving situations within domainspecific knowledge. From whole-number to integer knowledge To develop integer order and value knowledge elements, students need to make sense of the numbers’ order and how it relates to their values. When introducing negative numbers, integer values can be represented as absolute value (|-7| > |-2|) or linear value (-7 < -2) perspectives (e.g., Bofferding, 2014). Prior studies have explored students’ interpretations of integer order and values when comparing or ordering integers (e.g., Whitacre et al., 2017).  For example, Whitacre et al. (2017) investigated students’ reasoning about integer comparison by having them find the larger number between a pair of integers. They found compa ring two positive numbers was the easiest and comparing twonegative numbers was the most difficult. Bofferding and Farmer (2019) used temperature context to explore how students responded to different integer comparison question phrasing (e.g., most hot, least cold). Students performed better on most cold than least hot comparisons. They also did better with most hot for positivenumber-only comparisons and least cold for negative-number-only comparisons. With negative numbers, not only does the minus sig n take different meanings (i.e., binary, unary, symmetric) but also adding or subtracting integers involves a directional movement (e.g., Bofferding, 2014; ICLS2022 Proceedings 163 © ISLS Vlassis, 2004, 2008). For example, adding a negative integer no longer relates to getting more or larger rather it represents more in the negative direction or downwards on a vertic al number line (Bruno & Martinon, 1999). Previous related work focused on detailing students ’ addition or subtraction solution strategies and t heir understanding of minus sign meanings for open number-sentence or story problems (e.g., Aqazade & Bofferding, 2021; Bishop et al., 2014; Whitacre et al., 2014). For instance, Murray (1985) found students’ correct responses to integer addition or subtraction problems often happened when they could ignore the negative signs, operate as numbers were positive, and append the negative signs to their answers (e.g., -9 – -4 à 9 – 4 = 5 à -5 or-3 + -5 à 3 + 5 = 8 à -8), which refers to add or subtract make negative strategy leveraging the symmetric meaning of the minus sign (see Aqazade & Bofferding, 2021; Bofferding et al., 2017; Vlassis, 2004, 2008). Using counting up or down from a negative number strategy (e.g., 8 + -3 is-3, -2, …, 5) representing the unary meaning of the minus sign was a less common strategy (Aqazade & Bofferding, 2021; Bishop et al., 2014; Bofferding et al., 2017; Vlassis, 2004, 2008).  Often, students who did not know about numbers below zero ignored the negative signs and add or subtract the numbers’ absolute values (e.g., 4 + -6 = 10; i.e.,  absolute value strategy) or treated the negative signs as subtraction signs showing the bin ary meaning of the minus sign (e.g., 4 + -6 à 6 – 4 = 2; Aqazade & Bofferding, 2021; Bofferding et al., 2017; Vlassis, 2004, 2008). Although several integer knowledge elements were implicitly involved in the prior research, this work explicitly unfolds students’ interpretations of integer knowledge elements and the extent to which they represent a structured knowledge system. Thus, the purpose of this paper is to describe the triangulation among students’ integer order and value interpretations, integer ad dition and subtraction and minus sign interpretatio ns, and associated directed magnitude language. I used integer story-interpretation problems involving temperature and directed magnitude language and sought to explore the following research questions:  When solving integer storyinterpretation problems, how did fifth-grade studen ts’ order and value knowledge elements relate to (a) their knowledge elements of the directed magnitude langua ge and addition or subtraction as represented in th eir number-sentences? and (b) their drawn thermometers?

## Participants and setting

Forty fifth-grade students (24 girls and 16 boys) p articipated in this study from an intermediate scho ol in the Midwest, United States with 73% economically disadvantaged and 12% English-Language-Learners. Data sources and analysis I used task-based interviews involving a problem-solver (student) who interacts with one or more problem-solving situations and an interviewer (researcher) who follows up with nondirective questions (e.g., “can you tell me more about that?”). The goal of task-based interviews is  to delve into students’ complex problem-solving pr ocesses (Goldin, 2000; Maher & Sigley, 2014). In this study, students individually solved and explained integer-related test items: order and value items (e.g., circle the temperature that is most cold? 15, -27, 10); directed magnitude language items (e.g., when it gets 5 degrees more h ot, where does the arrow on the thermometer change to?); story-interpretation items (see Table 2); and integer addition and subtraction items (e.g., 8 – 12 =). To answer the research questions, I first classified students’ responses to integer order and value it ems into whole-number, transitional, and integer categories that describe their interpretations of o rder and value knowledge elements (see Table 1). Second, using these categories, I aimed to explain students’ responses to the story-interpretation items, which contained three p arts: (a) directed magnitude language terms, (b) wr iting a number-sentence, and (c) drawing a thermometer. In this paper, I only focused on two story-interpretat ion problems (see Table 2). I chose items that target the temperature change from a positive to a negative number and crossing zero (item 1) and within negative numbers (item 2). ## Significance, contribution, and implications

In attempts to promote better learning, it is essential to understand what processes are involved in one’s learning.  Understanding these processes helps develop learning environments that support more effective learning experiences. In this study, using story-interpretation problems provided a space to unfold such processes of fifth-grade students and their problem-solving approaches. These story-interpretation problems allowed for eliciting students’ conceptions in a problem-solving situation. By focusing on the concept of integers, I explored how students made meaning of different elements within the integer knowledge system and the extent to which these elements represent a structured system. Overall, because students from the whole-number and transitional categories were still making sense of integers’ order and values, it was not surprising to see a looser structure among their knowledge elements when solving the story-interpretation items. Consequently, having a more robust structure for order and value knowledge elements among students in the integer category led them to interpret the story-interpretation items more accurately. Although students in the whole-number category often ignored negatives when interpreting integers’ values and had difficulties making sense of the directed magnitude language terms, some of them wrote number-sentences with negative signs, illuminating their attempts in extending the minus sign meanings by using the add/subtract make negative or count up/down from negative strategies. Students in the transitional category were more successful than those in the whole-number category when interpreting the numbers’ values but some of them still reversed the negatives on their drawn thermometer and had difficulties writing a numbersentence, suggesting their integer knowledge structure was still in formation. The results of this study contributed and added to the research on understanding students’ integer knowledge development (e.g., Bofferding & Farmer, 2019; Whitacre et al., 2017). For example, although Bofferding and Farmer (2019) detailed students’ different responses based on numbers and language involved in the comparison items, they did not provide evidence into the connection between their integer value knowledge and their interpretation of directed magnitude language. In this paper, students’ choice of directed magnitude language terms for a temperature change provides such evidence and shows how using directed magnitude language could draw students’ attention to both direction and the absolute value of which the temperature changes (e.g., the temperature change from-33 to-22 reflects a decrease from the absolute value perspective but shows an upward movement on the thermometer; see Bofferding, 2014, 2019). Further, students’ drawn thermometers were additional evidence to describe their consistency between integer values and directed magnitude language interpretation, which suggests the importance of using an instructional model in determining the coordination between value and directed magnitude elements.  Overall, the combination of asking students to choose the correct directed magnitude language term and drawing thermometers could potentially draw their attention to the differences between numbers’ absolute value versus linear value. Unlike other studies (e.g., Whitacre et al., 2014), in the story-interpretation items, writing a number-sentence to “show the temperature change” highlighted how numbers’ type and direction that temperature changes influence students to write an addition versus subtraction problem. Further, despite presenting the temperatures with their formal notations, the story-interpretation items still represented a realistic contextual problem because of asking to “show the temperature change.” Thus, the results of students’ responses to story-interpretation items opened a new window into ways in which students can generate a number-sentence relevant to story problems. Together, the implications of this study highlight the importance of mathematical language as represented in students’ choice of directed magnitude language terms. Students’ directed magnitude language interpretations not only influenced their term choice, but also in some cases, was reflected in their written number-sentences and drawn thermometers. Perhaps, ways in which students make sense of mathematical language could tell a more cohesive story about how they think mathematically, rather than just their work with numbers and operations. Thus, mathematical language should become one of the central knowledge elements in ICLS2022 Proceedings 169 © ISLS mathematics instruction.  In addition to using mathematical language in the story problems, asking students to demonstrate their thinking in a different way by drawing a thermometer is essential to extend their access to mathematics and exhibit diverse ways of knowing and doing mathematics.
