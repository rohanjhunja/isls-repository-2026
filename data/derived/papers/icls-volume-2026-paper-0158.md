# Curricular Co-Design as Professional Development: Transformations in History Teachers’ Learning and Practice

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper reports on findings from a research-practice partnership that co-designed, piloted, and iterated on discussion-based world history lessons. Drawing on survey responses from design teachers (n=11) across two years, we identify that participation shaped three interconnected areas of teacher learning: (1) sustained shifts in instructional practices towards integrating clas sroom discussion, (2) growth in expertise and confidence as discussion facilitators, and (3) expanded professional identity and agency as mentors and leaders. In addition, the importance of collaboration and relationships w as strongly emphasized by all participants across both years, and analysis suggests that this was the key factor anchoring the co-design process.

Student discussion is recognized as a powerful pedagogical tool for disciplinary thinking in history classrooms. Studies show that discussion can promote deeper historical reasoning, critical engagement with sources, and civic engagement (Kavanagh et al., 2019; Freedman, 2020). Yet, despite its value, ambitious discussion practices remain challenging to implement and sustain, due to a number of constraints, including a lack of high-quality instructional materials (HQIM) that support discussion (Reisman et al., 2018). The Deeper Discussions in World History (DDWH) project sought to address this challenge through a co-design partnership between researchers and teachers. Over three semesters (January 2023-July 2024), secondary history teachers collaborated with researcher s to design, enact, and refine discussion-rich lessons to prepare for a larger research study of the discussion lessons and their impact on students. While co-design has been shown to be a powerful form of professional development (PD) that can foster teacher agency (Penuel et al., 2007; Voogt et al., 2015; Ko et al., 2022; Kyza & Agesilaou, 2022), fewer studies follow-up with participating teachers to track changes over time (Darling-Hammond et al., 2017). Additionally, most studies have occurred in STEM a nd general education contexts, and research on its role in supporting history teachers’ development remains limited (Freedman et al., 2024). This paper answers the following research question: How do history teachers’ expertise, agency, identity, and instr uctional practices in facilitating discussion evolve through and beyond participation in a co-design–based PD program?

## Theoretical framework and relevant literature

To analyze teacher learning in this study, we draw on three complementary perspectives that together illuminate how teachers grow through the co-design process and sustain change over time. First, we conceptualize co-design as PD when the focus is joint knowledge production between researchers and teachers (Penuel et al., 2007), and as a context in which both teacher and researcher learning can become visible through collaborative design work (Ko et al., 2022). In positioning teachers as designers, co-design validates their expertise while creating sustained opportunities for experimentation and reflection. This framing aligns with recent work emphasizing that effective PD is collaborative, iterative, and grounded in teachers’ real instructional contexts (Da rling-Hammond et al., 2017).

Second, we apply identity and agency frameworks that highlight how teachers’ roles are constructed through practice and recognition. Identity is negotiated through participation in activity systems (Holland et al.,

1998) and tied to opportunities for agency, or the capacity to intentionally influence practice and context (Nasir

& Hand, 2006). From this perspective, co-design can support teachers in authoring new identities as innovators, facilitators, and mentors, thereby expanding their agency to influence peers and professional communities. Third, we draw on communities of practice, which conceptualizes learning as a social process that occurs through participation, collaboration, and shared meaning-making (Wenger, 1998). This view aligns with recent studies of teacher researcher collaboratio n that show how professional learning develops through sustained joint 1272 work, shared inquiry, and collective attention to practice (Ko et al., 2022; Kavanagh et al., 2022). Within such communities, professional growth emerges as teachers engage with one another tonegotiate meaning, share strategies, and refine practice. Recent scholarship extends this view, emphasizing that teacher identity and learning are deeply relational and continuously shaped through professional interaction and reflection (Beauchamp & Thomas, 2020). Co-design aligns closely with this perspective by creating sustained relationships and shared inquiry that help teachers learn from and with one another over time (Ko et al., 2022), while also supporting the development of practical design knowledge through iterative collaborative work (Penuel et al., 2022). Together, these perspectives frame co-design as a space where PD, identity development, and collaboration deeply intersect. From January 2023 through July 2024, the project adopted a design-based research approach (Barab & Squire, 2004; Design-Based Research Collective, 2003), engaging teachers and researchers in iterative cycles of co-design, enactment, reflection, and revision. While our aim was to develop discussion-rich world history curricular materials, we found that the co-design process itself fostered a powerful PD experience, through which teachers grew in expertise and reported changes in identity and agency. Teacher co-designers Eleven secondary school world history teachers were selected for the co-design collaboration. All expressed a belief in the power of discussion for student learning; they ranged from early to mid-career to veteran in experience and all taught in Title I sc hools. Co-design officially ended in July of 2024; however, researchers maintained contact with the teachers informally and by presenting together at a social studies conference in fall 2024. In September 2025, we conducted a one-year-follow-up survey (n=10) to better understand the project’s sustained impact.

Co-design structure The co-design process involved three in person workshops as well as weekly virtual design-team meetings. Specifically, teachers and researchers engaged in the following activities: In-person workshops. The first of three workshops kicked off in January 2023. During the first workshop, we established project goals, provided training on relevant frameworks and design templates, and supported teacher teams in initial design activities. Subsequent workshops in July 2023 and 2024 focused on sharing feedback, revising lessons, developing scaffolds and supplemental resources, and reflecting on lessons learned from design and implementation.

Weekly design team meetings.  Design teams were composed of three to four teachers and one researcher, whose role was to facilitate design sessions and support teachers in sourcing historical documents and aligning to frameworks. Each semester, design team membership was reconfigured.  The three design teams met weekly, around 10 times per semester, to engage in cycles of designing, testing, reflecting on, and iterating discussion lessons and scaffolds. The researchers/facilitators also met weekly to norm, to help troubleshoot any design issues, and to share learnings across teams. Conference presentations.  Each teacher, in collaboration with the research team, presented and shared learnings to date at a national social studies conference. For this analysis, we drew on two surveys as our primary data sources. The first survey, the end-of-co-design survey (n = 11, August  2024) included Likert-scale and open-ended items about teachers’ confidence in facilitating discussion, changes in thinking about discussion design, collaboration practices, and intention to continue discussion practices. The second survey, the one-year-follow-up survey (n = 10, September 2025), revisited many of the same self-assessment and open-ended questions to capture enduring changes in practice, confidence, collaboration, and professional roles (e.g. leading PD, mentoring, sharing with colleagues). We also looked at one question from the teacher applications (November 2022), which asked about their perceived level of expertise as a discussion facilitator in the classroom. Quantitative items were analyzed descriptively. Given the small sample, we focused on patterns rather than statistical inference. Open-ended survey responses were coded iteratively using a shared codebook focused on 1273 growth in expertise, changes in instructional practice, identity and agency, and collaboration and relationships. Twenty percent of responses were double coded, discrepancies were resolved through discussion, and one researcher then coded the remaining responses. Responses within each code were reviewed thematically and discussed across the research team to identify and refine patterns. Survey data pointed to three interrelated forms of teacher learning: (1) growth in discussion expertise, (2) sustained shifts in practice, and (3) expanded professional identity and agency, with collaboration emerging as the foundation across all three, consistent with Wenger’s (1998) concept of communities of practice. Growth in expertise Teachers reported growth in their expertise as discussion facilitators. On quantitative items, all participants agreed or strongly agreed that the project influenced their understanding of the purpose of discussion and its connection to student identity, as well as their knowledge of classroom norms for discussion, and the use of texts to anchor conversation. Among the nine teachers who rated their facilitation skills before and after participation, the average rating increased from 2.5 in 2022 (teacher application) to 3.8 in 2025 (one-year-follow-up survey) on a five-point scale. All teachers except one reported improvement, with one sharing that they would never rate themselves an expert due to the belief that they would always have more room for growth. Open-ended reflections further support and explain these findings. Teachers described becoming more deliberate and reflective in structuring and facilitating student talk, developing broader repertoires of strategies and tools over time. One teacher wrote, “Discussion feels like a much more crucial part of what I do now. If I am not planning for discussion, I feel the lesson is not complete.” Another explained, “I’m much more confident in giving students the space to talk without jumping in too quickly. I s ee that as growth in myself as a facilitator.” Many teachers connected their learning directly to the collaborative nature of co-design, noting that the opportunity to develop, test, and revise lessons alongside peers helped them learn both new approaches and new ways of thinking about classroom talk. These findings highlight how co-design created a supportive environment for experimentation, shared reflection, and sustained professional growth (Ko et al., 2022; Penuel et al., 2022). Instructional practices In the end-of-co-design survey, teachers claimed that as a result of participation, they integrated discussion more intentionally into their classrooms, moving from seeing it as supplemental to treating it as central to lesson design. One teacher explained, “This work changed how I think about history teaching. Discussion isn’t an add-on anymore—it’s central.” They described adopting new protocols, scaffolds, and student-led formats, and establishing norms and routines that made discussion a predictable part of class. Notably, these changes persisted after the co-design engagement formally ended. In the one-year-followup survey, all teachers reported having set norms, expectations, and learning goals for discussion with students, considered students’ identities in framing discussion questions, facilitated multiple discussions, and reflected on their facilitation during the year following the co-design. All the design teachers intend to facilitate discussions in the current 25-26 school year, and a number reported setting new goals for continued development, such as increasing the discussion frequency or experimenting with new formats like student-led discussions. This enduring growth highlights how teachers carried their learning forward, demonstrating that co-design supported lasting shifts in instructional practice beyond the project’s conclusion. Identity and agency Participation in the project also shaped how teachers saw themselves and the roles they play in their school and professional communities. Across both years, teachers described becoming leaders and mentors around classroom during the 2024 –25 school year, six mentored peers, and four led PD in their schools or districts. Several also presented at conferences.

Teachers emphasized feeling trusted as experts and valued as collaborators throughout the project, which in turn encouraged them to take risks, share ideas, and step into leadership roles. One teacher wrote, “I felt trusted as an expert, which gave me confidence to try new things and share what I learned with others.” Others described how co-design helped them see themselves not only as curriculum implementers but as active contributors to the field. These experiences illustrate how professional identity evolved through participation and recognition, aligning with frameworks that conceptualize identity and agency as negotiated within social practice (Holland et al., 1998; Beauchamp & Thomas, 2020) and with studies of co-design that show how collaborative work can 1274 foster teacher empowerment and expanded agency (Kyza & Agesilaou, 2022). These findings underscore the argument that PD is also a process of identity formation (Nasir & Cooks, 2009). Collaboration and relationships In open-ended responses, teachers consistently described collaboration and relationships as the foundation of the co-design process. Teachers expressed that belonging to a new professional community was the most rewarding aspect of participation, highlighting the trust, inspiration, and collegial learning that shaped their experience. These comments emphasized that collaborating with colleagues reignited their excitement for teaching and gave them the confidence to try new approaches with discussion. Teache rs also framed collaboration as the enabling condition for professional growth. Teachers suggested that without the strong relationships forged in their design teams, their growth in discussion expertise, instructional changes, and expanded sense of identity and agency would not have been possible. This finding aligns directly with Wenger’s (1998) conception of communities of practice, in which shared meaning-making underpins professional growth, and reinforced by Beauchamp and Thomas’s (2020) account of teacher identity as socially constructed and continually negotiated within professional communities.

These findings illustrate how co-design functioned as PD unfolding across multiple dimensions. Collaboration and relationships emerged as the central theme related to growth. Consistent with Wenger’s (1998) conception of communities of practice, and with r ecent research on collaborative design as a context for professional growth (Ko et al., 2022), this social foundation enabled teachers to deepen expertise, shift instructional practices, and expand professional identity and agency. Teachers repeatedly emphasized that the trust and collegiality of the codesign community gave them the confidence to experiment with new approaches and the support to sustain those changes. Through these relationships, teachers not only refined discussion facilitation but also t ranslated new learning into enduring instructional routines.

At the same time, teachers’ reflections on identity and agency underscore that co-design as PD can promote more than changes in skills and practices. Many teachers took on new roles as mentors and facilitators in their schools and professional networks, leading PD, mentoring colleagues, and sharing lessons. These actions illustrate how co-design can support the development of professional identities that carry influence beyond individual classrooms. This resonates with scholarship that frames teacher learning as a process of identity formation and agency enactment (Holland et al., 1998; Nasir & Cooks, 2009). Survey data show co-design supported a developmental arc of teacher learning. Collaboration and relationships were not incidental but the enabling condition for growth. Through this foundation, teachers deepened their expertise, translating new knowledge into meaningful and sustainable shifts in instructional practice. Teachers reported building on and continuing the practices developed through the project, consistent with recent evidence that sustained, collaborative PD fosters lasting instructional transformation (Darling-Hammond et al., 2017). They also sustained their commitment to discussion, suggesting that participation in a collaborative professional community supported lasting shifts in identity and practice (Wenger, 1998). While limitations exist, such as not being able to know if practices actually changed due to relying on self-report data, this study strengthens the evidence that co-design can function as PD and is associated with enduring shifts in practice and identity. It adds to the knowledge base by focusing on history teachers, who have been historically understudied, and also by examining the longitudinal impacts of engaging in this type of work. Future research will attempt to delve into the  particular aspects of the co-design process that fostered these changes.
