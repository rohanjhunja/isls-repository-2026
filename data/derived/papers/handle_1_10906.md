# Do Undergraduates Use the Criterion of Fit with Multiple Lines of Evidence When Evaluating Conflicting Scientific Claims?

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Evidence is especially powerful when there are multiple lines of converging evidence. However, little is currently known about how laypersons think about such evidence. Therefore, we conducted an experiment to investigate whether non-science-major undergraduates use the criterion of fit with multiple lines of evidence when evaluating conflicting scientific claims on dinosaur metabolism. We found that the undergraduates used the criterion of fit with multiple lines of evidence when one theory was supported by multiple lines of evidence whereas the other theory was supported by single line of evidence.

## Introduction

In science, the validity of claims is evaluated in terms of whether they are based on evidence. Eviden ce is particularly compelling when there are multiple lines of converging evidence, rather than a single line of evidence (Duncan et al., 2018). Scientists use the epistemic criterion of fit with multiple lines of evidence  to evaluate theories (Thagard, 1978). The use of the criterion of fit with multiple lines  of evidence can be valuable for laypersons who are not experts in science, as well. For example, the efficacy of the COVID-19 vaccine was supported by multiple lines of evidence (in vitro studies, animal studies, observational studies, randomized clinical trials). The role of humans in causing climate change is sim ilarly supported by many lines of evidence (e.g., i ce cores, glacier recession, polar ice changes, temperature change on land and in the ocean, and so on). Do students and laypersons use the criterion of fit with multiple lines of evidence when evaluating conflicting claims? In this study, we investigate this research question through an experiment with non-science major undergraduates.

## Method

Participants were 170 non-science major undergraduates at a Japanese national  university, randomly assigned to one of eight experimental conditions. In each condition, partici pants were presented with two conflicting sets of evidence (Evidence Sets A and B, described below in detail) regarding dinosaur metabolism, presenting evidence sets for the cold-blooded and warm-blooded theories respectively. The experimental design was a 2 x 4 between-subjects experiment, incorporating order of theory as the counterbalancing first factor and configuration of evidence  as the second factor. To offset order effects of the evidence’s theory, participants were randomly assigned to one of two counterbalancing conditions:  half read Evidence Set A (supporting the cold-blooded theory) first and then Evidence Set B (supporting the warm-blooded theory), whereas the remaining participants followed the reverse ord er. The factor configuration of evidence had four levels, each varying the nature of the four pieces of evidence within the sets: (1) Single line Cold vs. Single line Warm condition (Evidence Set A: 4 studies solely related to the pineal gland, Evidence Set B: 4 studies solely related to bone vascular density), (2) Single line Cold vs. Multiple lines Warm condition (Evidence Set A: 4 studies solely related to the pineal gland, Evidence Set B: 4 studies each presenting different types of studies, addressing bone vascular density, upright posture, habitat, and nasal cavity, respectively), (3) Multiple lines Cold vs. Single line Warm condition (Evidence Set A: 4 studies each covering four different types of studies, Evidence Set B: 4 studies solely related to one type of study about bone vascular density), (4) Multiple lines Cold vs. Multiple lines Warm condition (Evidence Set A: 4 studies each covering different types of studies, Evidence Set B: 4 studies, each covering different types of studies). In the post-survey after reading both sets of evidence, the participants evaluated the evidence’s strength for both cold-and warm-blooded theories, expressed their agreement with evidence-based claims on dinosaur thermoregulation; both they utilized  a 7-point Likert scale. In addition, the participants provided written justifications for their responses. This paper concentrates on the post-survey written justifications. We systematically coded these responses to determine whether each participant referenced the criterion of fitting with multiple lines of evidence. Because only the configuration of evidence showed statistically significant differences for some variables in the 7-point Likert scale data analysis, the written responses were analysed concentrating on the four conditions of factor configuration of evidence.

## Discussion

In this study, non-science-major undergraduates tended to use the criterion of fit with multiple lines of evi dence when evaluating conflicting scientific claims. The u ndergraduates use d the criterion when one theory was supported by multiple lines of evidence whereas the  other theory was supported by a single line of evidence. When both sides were supported by just a single line of evidence, and when both sides were supported by multiple lines of evidence, they did not use the criterion of multiple lines of evid ence, which is normatively expected because the criterion was not useful in distinguishing which set of evidence was stronger in these cases. This study suggests that students may be readily able to learn from instruction designed to foster sensitivity to multiple lines of evidence to evaluate complicated contemporary scientific claims in the world.
