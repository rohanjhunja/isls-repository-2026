# Global Approaches to AIED: Common Voices and Silences about the Sustainable Use of Generative AI in Education

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
International organizations (e.g., UNESCO, UNICEF, and OECD) have published recommendations for generative AI (GenAI) in education, addressing pedagogical, ethical, and safety concerns. Our study conducted a thematic analysis through topic modeling to investigate global guidelines for GenAI use and explored critical considerations for educators, policymakers, and researchers. We aim to present common voices, and amplify silences about GenAI in education and discuss the ethical and moral implications of its sustainable uses.

## Introduction

The release of generative AI (GenAI), especially Ch atGPT in late 2022, sparked significant educational  interest. The rapid adoption of GenAI can alter the fundamental ways we engage with information, develop understanding, and carry out our work. Technolog ical advances require a critical examination of educational scholarship and ongoing global issues (e.g., climate changes, social inequities, vulnerable geopolitics) for futuremaking (Erstad et al., 2016; Selwyn, 2024). The existing literature, however, is dominated by exploratory studies on conversational AI in language learning (e.g., Bannister, Peñalver,  & Urbieta, 2023; Ji, Han, & Ko, 2023), and scholar s in AI in Education (AIED) have noted the lack of underpinnin g educational theories (Chen et al., 2020). Interna tional organizations have published recommendations for Ge nAI in education, providing global visions for crea ting actionable frameworks at national levels. While the se recommendations emphasize human-centric approaches, we argue that educational solutions necessitate further research into the com plex AI-human-nonhuman relationships. Intentional uses of GenAI should bro aden educational goals to prioritize sustainability  and co-existence with GenAI and other nonhuman entities. W e investigate: (1) common topics of global organiza tions’ guidelines for GenAI use in education; and (2) considerations for educators, policymakers and researchers to take actions for futuremaking.

Taking a posthumanistic approach, which regards human and non-human entities as inseparable agentic entities (Bolter, 2016; Nimmo, 2019), we challenge GenAI apps’ neutrality. From this perspective, we d evelop an understanding of AIED shaped by environment,  culture, policy, and other multifaceted dimensions of the human milieu. Posthumanistic interrogations of AIED would acknowledge that “learning is entangled” (Sheridan et al., 2020, p.1289) between human and non-human entities, and that non-human agency is as important as human agency when examining GenAI for teaching, learning,  and assessment (Tang & Cooper, 202 5). Unlike anthropocentric perspectives that center humans, po sthumanism explores the complex interconnectedness between human and non-human entities. It challenges the notion that human  control is the only form of agency over our world, technology or systems. Such perspec tives disrupt the traditional human-centric educational approaches, offering the potential for more ethical, inclusive, and equitable education. Our approach We conducted a thematic analysis of international d ocuments that provide guidelines for using AI in ed ucation. Focused on documents published from December 2022 o nward, following the release of ChatGPT, we analyze d documents published by international agen cies in the educational domain. Primarily, these do cuments were published by UNESCO, UNICEF, and OECD. The core cri terion for selection was the presence of actionable statements, including policies, guidelines, recomme ndations, and strategies. We excluded documents con taining generalized, abstract or broad concepts, research reports or theoretical discussions without clear recommendations. The following seven documents were selected for the  analysis: (1) Policy Guidance on AI for Children 2.0 (UNICEF, Nov 2 021); (2) Guidance for Generative AI in Education a nd Research (UNESCO, Sep 2023); (3) ChatGPT and Artificial Intelligence in Higher Educa tion (UNESCO, Dec 2023); (4) Digital Education Outl ook: Regulation and Guidance on Generative AI in Education (OECD, Dec 2023); (5) Shaping the Future of Learning: The Role of AI in Education (WEF, Apr 2024); (6) AI  Competency Framework for Students (UNESCO, Aug 2024); and (7)  AI Competency Framework for Teachers (UNESCO, Aug 2024). The UNICEF document, though published before the search period, was included for its rele vance to children and to enrich the diversity of document sources. Using LDA-based topic modeling (Griffiths2004, CaoJuan2009, a nd Arun2010 performance metrics) of the textual data containing regulations, guidelines, and recommendations helped us determine  the optimal number of topics, frequent keywords, word associations and their relationships. The main purpose was to identify the common voices of the global organizations. Coupled with thematic analysis, we identified the theme for each topic and adopted a posthumanistic perspec tive to interpret how non-human agency might be silently implicated in these documents. From the interpretations, we made recommendations on how we could work with the complex entanglements in education for the collective futuremaking.

## Findings

Performance metrics identified five as the optimal number of topics for our data. Based on gamma values and associated keywords, we labeled the topics (see Table 1). We excluded generic terms like AI and GenAI from the keywords as they are default terms in our thematic analysis. Drawing from the identified themes and keywords, we examined how the analyzed documents represent and position various actors within the AI ecosystem. These actors include human (e.g., teachers, students, researchers, polic ymakers), non-human (e.g., AI systems, natural entities), institutional (e.g., governments, organi zations, industries), and inanimate (e.g., policies, regulations) actors. Overall, human agency is the focal point, with rich  discussions on competency development; AI is positioned as a tool for human use (Topics 2 & 3). Recommendations for implementations acknowledge ecosystem-level influences (Topics 1 & 5) while maintaining the focus on the teacher's role in actioning changes. Topics 4 offer a tenuous nascent link to posthumanistic thinking, suggesting distributed agency between humans, AI, and non-human entities. The power of machine automation rev eals critical ethical issues —disclosure of private information, misuse of data, and bias reproduction in AI-generated content—which remain underexplored. Moreover, broader impacts such as environmental sus tainability have been overlooked. Inattentiveness t o these silences may have detrimental impact on the development of AI curriculum and competency among teachers and students. While the relationship between humans (te achers/students), institutions, and AI remains key to future planning, it may be worthwhile to consider posthuma nist approaches to broaden our notion  of education to be adaptive to complex social futures.

## Discussion and conclusions

Knowledge alone is no longer sufficient to meet tomorrow's workforce demands in GenAI age (Aoun, 2017). The complex interplay of educational actors, AI systems, and other non-human entities reveals that GenAI is more than a mere cognitive tool. Our review emphasizes the importance of intentional GenAI uses, critically examining inclusivity, equity, well-being, environmental sustainability, and concerns b eyond knowledge expansion in AI discourse. In alignment with this perspective, a posthumanisti c approach highlights the complexity of futuremaking with GenAI. This involves translating global recommendations across ecosystem levels, and  the dynamic interconnectedness between human, GenAI and  non-human entities. While highlighting organizations’ dominant voices is critical for “AI-proofing” learning environments, it is equally important to examine the silences. Posthumanist approaches offer alternative ways of t hinking about sustainable GenAI uses, challenging anthropocentric perspectives and exploring the enta nglement of human and non-human entities beyond the physical classrooms. Acknowledging these entangleme nts foregrounds ethics, reframes agency beyond huma n control, and compels educators to deliberate on iss ues with serious consequences on equity, inclusiv ity, and sustainability in how we live, study, and work with GenAI. Some limitations of this study should be noted. Since our thematic analysis was limited to seven policy d ocuments, there is a need to further explore both s hared narratives and silences us ing an expanded dataset. Additionally, the topics a nd keywords identified were not mutually exclusive, highlighting the interpretive role of human researchers in analyzing themes. We will continue examining additional policy texts to further unders tand how ethical, inclusive, and sustainable educat ional ecosystems can be envisioned through a posthumanistic lens.
