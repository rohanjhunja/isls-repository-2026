# Caring Relations Across Interaction Analysis Labs

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This symposium brings together a multi-generational group of scholars who are participants, designers, and leaders of Interaction Analysis (IA) labs across multiple universities. Having experience with and taking inspiration from the methodological work of Candy and Chuck Goodwin and Rogers Hall, we will discuss the ways in which our IA labs are striving to push the method forward through norms and practices developed over time. In this session, we will discuss the development of caring relations across our various contexts. Though care is expansively conceived (care between researchers, between researchers and participants, for collective video viewing), our participation in IA labs will organize our work.

## Introduction

This symposium explores the necessity of care acros s theoretical, methodological, and analytical practices in Interaction Analysis (IA) labs to re-center the growth of humans —both researchers and those being researched. Nel Noddings (2013) distinguishes between caring about and caring for, with the former lacking true commitment and actions to support others’ well-being. In research, we nearly always care about the systems and people we focus on, but sometimes fail to care for them within a neoliberal climate that prioritizes efficiency and outcomes. Moreover, this climate takes a toll on researchers’  own well-being, diminishing their ability to care for others. Marin and colleagues (2020) reflect on practices th at cultivate both self-and collective-care in education and research. Th eir work centers Indigenous interdependent views of the  self to draw attention to the interwoven spatial, temporal, and social aspects of care (or l ack thereof) that undergird educational research. B y exploring practices of care in IA research, we continue and expand this crucial conversation. In Goodwin’s (2015) tradition of attending to care in mundane interactions, we specifically explore what “doing” care, as a relational act across space and time, looks like in the context of Interaction Analysis research. This session includes presentations from five group s engaging in Interaction Analysis (Jordan & Henderson, 1995). These groups, representing an int ergenerational lineage anchored in work by the Good wins (Co-Operative Action; Goodwin, 2017; Goodwin & Goodwin,  2013; Goodwin, 2007) and Hall (2000), share commitments to understanding participant perspectiv es. We analyze sequential (inter)action including t alk, gesture, gaze, touch, prosody, movement, and other semiotic modes of engagement through standard group practices like watching video once through, and playing the "stop game" to pause the video and attend to possible findings directly tied to the sequential record (Er ickson, 2014). From our common roots, we explore ou r IA groups’ innovations as new communities form their own idioc ultures (Fine, 1979). As IA moves toward more explicit critical qualitative work (Philip & Gupta, 2020), we need to also interrogate our practi ces through a lens of care. Collectively, we ask: How do we cultivate a larger system of care through caring for each other as researchers and/or collaborators, and caring for ou rselves in research practice? Specifically, each group presents a core practice of caring for each other and the participants with whom they part ner, and during the symposium we will discuss relationships between caring for ourselves, each other, and participants. The practice of gift giving Elizabeth Metts and Tessaly Jen Vanderbilt IA Lab (VIAL) is a voluntary student-led lab with participating graduate students and fa culty across multiple universities. Though the focus of each ses sion varies based on the interests and needs of pre senters, we always conclude meetings with a gift-giving round: each participant shares an idea or advice for future directions based on the session’s discussion. This practice was established by Rogers Hall to allow each participant to share their thoughts, and to express gratitude to the presenter. We view gift-giving as integral to our community's commitment to  caring for (Noddings, 2013) one another as researchers. In the following sections, we will explore the theoretical perspectives underlying our giftgiving tradition and outline the defining commitmen ts that guide our gift-giving practice. These ideas emerged from two IA lab sessions where participants first examined a previously recorded gift round, then developed a list of defining characteristics of our gifting practice.

## Theoretical perspectives

From an anthropological standpoint, gift giving is a deeply rooted form of social action with two catego ries: individual and collective gifts. Individual gifts are offered freely without expectation, while collective gifts create relationships and indebtedness (Yan, 2020). Our gif ting practice embodies both aspects. Individually, it allows members to articulate their interpretations of pres ented work in an evolving manner, with minimal expe ctations on the receiver. Collectively, the obligatory natur e of the gift round fosters interdependence. By con tributing to one another's scholarly work, the lab's communal bo nds deepen and grow stronger with each exchange of gifts. These gifts “move, and their value increases with t heir passage…The more something is shared, the grea ter its value becomes” (Kimmerer, 2013, p. 27). In our gifting round, we offer our unique know ledge and perspectives, which can then be taken up, transformed by the rece iver, and woven into their work. This collectively produces new knowledge to offer to the larger research community. Defining commitments Aligned with Marin and colleagues' (2020) relational dimension of researcher self-care, we identify five defining commitments that underpin our gifting practice, promoting community, inclusivity, and reciprocity (Table 1). Our gifting practice requires trust and commitment from the community—(1) there is a longitudinal acknowledgement of one another and our contributions, and (2) respo nsibility is co-constructed. Gifting also enriches our practice of IA by (3) ensuring everyone has an opportunity to participate, (4) surfacing diverse perspectives, histories, and experiences, and (5) expanding our inquiry beyond traditional IA conventions. ## Conclusion

We offer these commitments as a way to  define and share our process with the larger resear ch community. Importantly, we see our gifting practice as counter  to many neoliberal social norms of the academy. We  aim to foster trust and reciprocity to resist the pressure to produce work quickly. Rather, we make space for collaboration and support, and invest time in each other’s work and ideas. This work is critical to collaborative, relational,  and power-disrupting research. First, disrupting neoliberal, productivity-focused demands of academia requires adjusting priorities to affirm the dignity of learners (Espinoza et al., 2020), children (Kei fert et al., 2021), families, and researchers (Mari n et al., 2020). Second, relational work upholds the needs of both uni-and family-based lab members in/outside of research designs. Finally, centering rest is a radical move needed in  the academy for disabled scholars (in our lab proj ect PI, immunocompromised caregiver) and for scholars and p articipants of multiple marginalized communities (i n our lab COVID-cautious, immigrant-families, multilingual households) whose needs are routinely diminished. Cultivating care through choreographic forms of interaction analysis Lauren Vogelstein, Rebecca Steinberg, Curtis Thomas, Dionne Champion, Lara Appleby, and Aditi Wagh As an interdisciplinary collaborative of choreograp hers and learning scientists engaging in participatory design research (Bang & Vossoughi, 2016), we study how dan ce-based forms of embodiment can support expansive participation in informal STEM learning environments (Solomon et al., 2022; Vogelstein et al., 2019). We design spaces where physical, nonverbal fo rms of communication are privileged and carry this commitment in our analysis (Vogelstein, 2022; Silvis et al., 2022). T hrough video analysis, we use embodied sensemaking as an act of care that acknowledges participants and analysts  as fully bodied sensemakers. We present analysis o f a Zoom recording of our group engaged in choreographic, embodied sense-making to show how viewing the video record as a work of dance and responding to it with our bodies (Vogelstein, 2022) allows us to attend to the compositional nature of interactions, how space, time, and energy are arranged. We co-examined data from a 30-hours corpus of kids and scientists collaboratively exploring scientists’ questions through choreographic activities. As we watched select videos, we moved with focal participants in the record, making similar movements that reflected wha t we saw. Framing data as choreography changed how we understood and engaged with participants’ interacti onal choices. As shown in Figure 1, there is a flui dity and intimacy in this porous nature rather than a distant, impenetrable separation between analyst and participant. Navigating (structural) tensions in care-full collaborations Ali R Blake, Arundhati Velamur, and Jasmine Y. Ma The Interaction, Analysis, and Learning Group at Ne w York University (NYU IAL) began as a small IA lab among graduate students at NYU with the mentorship of Jasmine Y. Ma. Over ten years of group gathering, NYU IAL has grown significantly, both in directions of interest and in participants. We have expanded to b ecome a cross-institutional, intergenerational space. With these kinds of growth have come new conditions to address care as a political stance (Askins & Blazek, 2017), both  for and with each other (participating researchers) and for those we partner with across our various projects, who are present in data and, at times, in data sess ions. Within this symposium we will share our ongoing processes for care-fully navigating this growth. Group history Scholars in NYU IAL are among a group of folks refi ning (specific approaches to) IA methods. Among the commitments that root the practice for NYU IAL are:  viewing audio/video records of human activity as a  sacred act, a gift; tracing across time to charact erize histories of unfolding action; and viewing da ta together (Marin et al., 2019). As a few examples of methodological ref inement, members have been cultivating IA methods w ith relational ontologies (Krishnamoorthy et al., 2021) and as a multi-sited endeavor that can support more expansive contributions (Interaction, Analysis, and Learning Group, 2022). Current care-full negotiations A need for openness There is a field-wide need for IA learning spaces. Group video viewing is a core IA practice (Jordan & Henderson, 1995; Marin et al., 2019). This necessarily collect ive interpretive approach is one that we deeply val ue, though the academy is, in many ways,  not structured to widely support this work and the scholars seeking to work with IA methods. There are no formalized entry points fo r those who work within institutions where there is n’t an IA lab or someone who has been mentored in this tradit ion. In res ponse to this field-wide need, NYU IAL has welcomed scholars-both graduate students and faculty, in-person and virtually-from many institutions. This openness has made it possible for many of us to practice IA. A need for intimacy Simultaneously, there is a need for more intimate s paces and relations within which to nurture specific ideas and analyses. As the group has grown, and due to commitments to making it more possible for folks to join, including joining sometimes, members do not all regularly gather, practice IA together, and, as Espinoza (2021) has described, slowly “[grow] into one another’s lives” (p. 162) as collaborators. Members have found that the widely open learning space of the group is not always conducive to the needs of particular projects and those we partner with within them. As we grow, needs for more specif ic relations for doing analyses remain. To address these simultaneous threads in tension, we are trying out multiple kinds of formations, both open and more  specifically formed based on project and scholar needs. We look forward to being in conversation with a deep attent ion to caring for each other.
