# Item Calibration in Reading Assessment with LLMs: Human Alignment and the Masking Effect

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study evaluates large language models (LLMs) for calibrating item difficulty in grade 8 reading comprehension using 30 NAEP items. LLM-calibrated difficulty estimates were compared with human benchmarks, and masking was used to manipulate input information. Results show no significant differences across human-derived difficulty levels, indicating misalignment with human performance. Masking systematically reduced accuracy, particularly for harder items as more input text was obscured. These findings ra ise concerns about the validity of LLM-based item calibration.

Computerized adaptive testing (CAT) relies on accurate item calibration to ensure the validity and reliability of assessment scores (Hassan & Miller, 2019; Zheng, 2014).  Traditionally, calibration requires pilot testing with representative student samples, which is resource-intensive and difficult to scale. Recent advances in natural language processing (NLP) suggest that large language models (LLMs) may provide an alternative by generating response data for item calibration (Benedetto et al., 2024; Lu & Wang, 2024; Park et al., 2024). Prior studies have shown that LLM-generated responses can approximate human performance in certain domains, raising the possibility of reducing reliance on humandata (Liu et al., 2025; Veeramani et al., 2024). However, two challenges remain. First, it is unclear whether LLMs perceive item difficulty in ways that align with human learners, especially in reading comprehension, where inference, background knowledge, and cognitive effort play critical roles. Second,  LLMs do not naturally exhibit variability  in misconceptions and comprehension profiles comparable to students, making it difficult to simulate a full range of learner responses. This study aims to  address these gaps by comparing LLM-calibrated item difficulty estimates with human-derived benchmarks using Grade 8 reading comprehension items from the National Assessment of Educational Progress (NAEP). In addition, I examine the effect of input degradation through masking, which reduces available textual information, as a method for systematically altering LLM performance. Specifically, I investigate whether LLM-calibrated difficulty aligns with human-derived difficulty levels and how masking influences performance across items of varying difficulty.

## Method

Items and student responses This study uses 30 multiple-choice reading comprehension items from Grade 8 NAEP assessments (NAEP, 2024). The selected items come from a validated pool designed to assess reading comprehension at scale, with empirically derived difficulty levels (Easy: 13, Medium: 12, Hard: 5) based on diverse student responses. These human-derived difficulty levels serve as the benchmark for evaluating LLM alignment. LLM response generation and masking Eight LLMs from different model families and parameter scales were used, including Llama 3 (3B, 8B), Gemma 2 (2B, 9B, 27B), Qwen 2.5 (3B, 7B), and Mistral 7B. Each model was prompted with a standardized instruction to select the best answer given a passage and a question. This uniform prompting strategy minimizes variability due to prompt engineering and focuses on differences in model behavior (Kaplan et al., 2020). To manipulate the amount of information available to the models, a masking procedure was applied in which a proportion of words in each passage was randomly replaced with the special [MASK] token. This method mimics situations where comprehension is challenged by missing information, requiring inferential reasoning, which is a key skill in human reading comprehension (Soto et al., 2019). Masking levels ranged from 0% to 60% in increments of 10%. Each condition was repeated three times, resulting in 168 response sets across all models and masking levels.

Item difficulty parameters were estimated from LLM-generated responses using the Rasch model. Alignment with human-derived difficulty levels was evaluated using analysis of variance  (ANOVA) at the item level (N = 30), 2542 treating difficulty levels  as the independent variable and LLM-calibrated item difficulty estimates as the dependent variable. In addition, the relationship between LLM and human performance was assessed using Spearman’s correlation between correct response rates across items. The effect of masking was analyzed by examining changes in correct response rates across masking levels and difficulty categories. Alignment with human benchmarks The analysis of variance revealed no significant differences in LLM-calibrated item difficulty across human-derived levels (F(2, 27) = 0.65, p = 0.53). In addition, the correlation between LLM and human correct response rates was low and not statistically significant (ρ = 0. 20, p = 0.30).  These results indicate that LLM-calibrated difficulty estimates do not reproduce the relative ordering of item difficulty observed in human learners. Items that are more challenging for students are not consistently more challenging for LLMs, indicating a divergence in the representation of item difficulty.

Effect of masking on performance Masking resulted in a consistent decline in accuracy across all items as the proportion of masked words increased (Figure 1). The rate of decline differed by difficulty level. Hard items exhibited a sharper decrease in accuracy at lower masking levels  (10-20%), whereas easy items showed a more gradual decline and maintained an average accuracy of 80% even at 50% masking. Medium-difficulty items followed a similar but consistently lower trend than easy items.  These findings indicate that LLM performance is sensitive to the availability of textual information, particularly for more complex items.

Figure 1 Average Correct Rate by Masking Percentage and Difficulty Level Overall, the results highlight a fundamental limitation of using LLMs for item calibration in reading comprehension. Although LLMs can generate plausible responses and exhibit performance degradation under masking, they do not align with human-derived difficulty structures. ANOVA and correlation results indicated that LLMs do not necessarily struggle with the same items as students, reflecting differences in cognitive processing: while human readers rely on inference, memory, and prior knowledge, LLMs generate responses through statistical associations without cognitive strain (Mirchandani et al., 2024). These findings caution against viewing LLMs as valid substitutes for human test-takers in reading comprehension, even though they have shown promise in other domains such as algebra and clinical assessments (Liu et al., 2025; Veeramani et al., 2024). This study examined the use of LLMs for item calibration in reading comprehension by evaluating their alignment with human-derived difficulty levels and their response to input degradation.  Results show that LLM-calibrated difficulty estimates do not align with human benchmarks, indicating a divergence in how difficulty is represented. Masking systematically reduced performance, with harder items showing greater sensitivity to missing information, which may serve as a scalable method to modulate LLM cognitive profiles. However, masking alone does not resolve the inherent differences between LLMs and human reading processes.
