# Designing for More Than Just Product: Student Co-Design of Digital Badge Systems

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Student co-design – the process in which students participate as an integral part of designing learning programs and curriculum – is one avenue for including student perspectives and experiences into the creation of curriculum and learning environments. Historically, the goal of co-design has been to finalize a product (video game, curriculum, badge system, etc.) and/or to validate the usability of a product. Examining data from student co-design of badge systems of three university courses, this paper provides a framework that expands the goals of co-design, specifically the co-design of digital badges, to focus on 1) impacting designers (youth and adult) and 2) impacting educational systems and processes. Findings indicate the value of the co-design of digital badges as a metacognitive process for learners, an assessment tool of learning for instructors, and an evaluation tool for programs.

Major issues addressed With the goals of equitable and consequential learning, educational curriculum and programs often face the challenges of how to make learning meaningful, engaging, and authentic to students’ lives. Student co-design – the process in which students participate as an integral part of designing learning programs and curriculum – is one avenue for including student perspectives and experiences into the creation of curriculum and learning environments (Pitt, et al., 2019; Tierney, Horstman, & Tzou, 2021). Co-design bridges learners' experiences, perspectives and expertise to that of the expectations designed into the educational program (Pitt, et al., 2019; Tierney, Horstman, & Tzou, 2021; Yip et al., 2017). However, when enacting co-design, it is valuable to address what the goals or outcomes of the co-design are. Broadly speaking the goal of co-design has historically been to finalize a product (video game, curriculum, etc.) and/or validate the usability of a product. Typically, with this goal, student and youth co-designers are involved as end-users. Yet, co-design, and specifically codesign of digital badges, creates a unique opportunity to accurately reflect the needs of the learners and at the same time enable the group to challenge implicit hierarchical structures embedded in curriculum design. To understand this further, we have identified three additional goals for co-design: 1) For the learners, it reveals their own thinking and perceptions of the content, prompting reflection and redirection when necessary, and is responsive to collaborators and experts. 2) For the teachers and educators, it brings to light the different perspectives of the learners, captures the unique dynamics of the community, making visible a scaffolding trajectories of learning content, and providing an opportunity for teachers and educators to observe their own learning and teaching. 3) Lastly, the structure of the badge system creates a vantage point from which both the learners and teachers can reflect how they view the hierarchy and relationships of the content itself. However, it is important to understand what the outcomes are when involving students. How does co-design of digital badges impact… a) The learning and development of students involved? b) The learning and development of teachers and educators involved? c) The educational structures and content in which the co-design is situated?

## Potential significance

This paper describes a research project that used the co-design of digital badges as a design tool for student codesigners to map out their development and learning. This work is distinct from other research on the creation or co-design of digital badge systems, in that we used digital badges to design, without intention to create badges as an end product for future students to use. In this way the badge systems that were created act more as a conceptual map of learning and development. The badges describe activities and markers of successful completion but within the larger context of a learning trajectory. The close integration of co-design strategy with the structure of a badge system as an organizational mechanic, allows for highly tailored or customized learning experience for the group that creates it. The potential significance is developing a practice in which this model is scalable across different content types and communities.

## Theoretical perspectives

Co-design can be a powerful approach for designing a curriculum that reflects the needs and priorities of its participants. In student co-design, students are asked to be an integral part of designing learning programs, ICLS2022 Proceedings 1461 © ISLS curriculum, and assessments (Pitt, et al., 2019; Tierney, Horstman, & Tzou, 2021). In this model, students are treated as design partners in which they are “part of the research and design process throughout the experience.” and “…considered to be equal stakeholders in the design of new technologies.” (Druin, 2002). For educational equity, it matters who is involved in design. Student co-design as a process centers students in the design of the learning environments in which they participate. We argue that without utilizing a co-design approach, products such as digital badges may represent only hierarchical concerns while overlooking important but subtle aspects of learning and program experiences that are highly valued by learners. Further, through co-design, students can identify learning trajectories that are of importance to them and reflect the contexts and cultural practices of their communities in combination with the content and pedagogy taught in school courses. Our approach to designing digital badge systems focuses on leveraging badge systems as a mechanism to involve students in the design process as collaborators (Pitt et al., 2019; Tierney, Horstman, & Tzou, 2021). Digital badges are part of what can be described as an achievement system (Hickey & Chartrand, 2020; Horstman, Tierney, & Tzou, 2020). One promise of digital badge systems is how they mark the combined social, emotional, and academic achievements that learners accomplish (Horstman, Tierney, & Tzou, 2020) and create visible trajectories for learners to follow (Pinkard, Barron, & Martin, 2008). Taken together, individual badges can create a pathway that, when followed, indicate what participants have learned, but also indicating membership of a learning community, having achieved valued practices and rites of passage. When badge systems are not used for motivational purposes but for educational program design, it serves as a mechanism for tracking a design framework in addition to highlighting the shifts in perspective around power, participants, and what is valued. Co-design of badge systems shifts the focus of the badge system as a design tool instead of an end product; its value is in its development. Combined, co-design of badge systems creates a unique opportunity that reflects values associated with socio-cultural models of program design. It makes visible and prioritizes the needs of the community, it is responsive to the uniqueness of the community it serves, it redistributes power and equity, and enables the agency of learners (Lave & Wenger, 1991; National Research Council, 2000; Rogoff, 2003). We believe this unique combination creates the right amount of space and structure to capture and reflect the unique needs of different learning communities.

Methodological approaches & data sources In the spring, summer, and fall of 2021, students in the co-design of digital badge systems for three courses connected to a single-subject credential program in a teacher education program in the United States. The first course, Adolescent Development, is a prerequisite for entry into the Single-Subject Credential Program and is often one of the first courses students take in the College of Education. The students co-design for the Adolescent Development course focused on first creating a digital badge system that represented the course as it existed and then revising the badge system to focus centrally on equity and inclusion. Adjacent to this work, separate students and author one co-designed a digital badge system for two courses in the Single-Subject Credential Program: a General Pedagogy course and the Science Subject Methods Course. These two courses both occur in the first semester of the Credential Program and include fieldwork components, as candidates begin their student teaching. For all three courses, student co-designers had recently completed the course. Together, the three courses were chosen to understand developmental trajectories in and across courses at various points in the program.

Data for this project includes video recordings of virtual design sessions for three separate courses (19 sessions total). In addition, student co-design participants were interviewed following their participation in the design process. Analysis for this presentation focused on, first, an examination of digital badge systems as a design tool for mapping developmental trajectories and, second, the co-design of the digital badge systems with students in the credential program in order to identify learning and engagement in and across courses. More specifically, we analyzed video and documents from co-design sessions using grounded theory (Corbin & Strauss, 2008) to understand how students were making sense of the co-design process. The videos were reanalyzed and coded for using a previously developed coding scheme (Tierney, Horstman, & Tzou, 2021) that focused on negotiation of design content, as well as identified learning and development. We then analyzed interviews for perspectives on the design process and individual learning that occurred through co-design. Memos and associated vignettes of data were generated around emerging themes.

## Major findings

While badges have traditionally been used to track and mark achievements in learning programs, this study demonstrates that the co-design process, in conjunction with badge structure, enables learning through program design. Though the final badge structure is important, it is evidence of how the participants interpret and prioritize learning content and activities as discovered through a badge co-design process. ICLS2022 Proceedings 1462 © ISLS Impact for learners Co-design of digital badges reveals designers' thinking and perceptions of the content, prompting reflection and redirection when necessary. Across all three badge design processes, students were asked to identify aspects of their learning and development from the course of focus. Then, as a group, designers organized the content into a system of badges, each badge representing significant learning that had been identified. Across all three design processes, designers identified the personal value they gained from the process. One student designer discussed how the co-design process had helped her transition into her role as a student teacher, helping to solidify what she had learned, while also pushing her learning through collaboration. I think it helped me realize how much I did learn so that I prepped me for the next semester. Like okay, these are all the things that I know and tools, I have in my back pocket now let's take them into the classroom and develop them, so I thought it was a cool way to like reflect but also move forward at the same time. And that the fact that over the summer I didn't just let it fall through the cracks I got to like discuss it and collaborate, I feel, stronger like in the classroom doing it. The student then went on to discuss the collaborative nature of the reflection, how it highlighted new aspects of the course and sequence of the badges that she would not have gained on her own. And so, I had like my linear point of view of what I had learned and then I looked over at others. And they had totally different branches and we're making connections that once I looked at it, I was like that totally makes sense and it's like an aha moment. And then, when we combine them, all together was like I was saying, like the most well rounded version of that class. And I think if I was to do that, alone, it would have just been that like linear view um without, like the other things that they took away that made it unique for them, so I think the co-design made it like a learning experience but also made the badges like more than just like a one point of view, it was just everyone's once you put it together. Similar statements were echoed by other student designers. One designer, who was involved in two of the badge design teams, reflected on her participation in general: I think it was fun, on a personal level for me just to review what I learned and kind of synthesize the class, especially since I’m becoming a teacher. That seeing the back end of design and being involved in it in a class I had just experienced was a really cool experience and I also felt like voices would be heard, without it being like in the class itself, so I think it's a great method to hear student voices. Other students discussed how the conversations in the co-design sessions had transformed how they thought about education. One student who was an aspiring teacher noted how a discussion on whether or not teachers should accept late work changed how she thought about how she would approach and work with adolescents. In this way, the digital badge co-design sessions created a space of collaboration, reflection, and socializing that was distinct from other educational contexts that designers experienced. Impact for teachers and educators For the teachers and educators involved in co-design, the process can bring to light the different perspectives of the learners and learners’ experiences. In this way, co-design of digital badges can act as an assessment tool for teachers on what learners took away from their course. To demonstrate one aspect of teacher/educator learning in the project, we highlight how the digital badge system captured concepts and pedagogy focused on equity and inclusion (a central tenet of the teacher education program in which the courses reside). The first course, a science methods course, focuse s on the epistemic practices for science teaching. This course focus was clearly represented in the digital badge system that student designers created. However, despite conversations around equity and inclusion in the program, these ideas did not initially emerge in the badge system for the course and, when they did, equity existed in a separate node of badges, removed from the primary interlocking system. The results indicate that equity concepts and practices were an add-on to students’ learning about science pedagogy, which mirrored the actual design process of the science methods course. In a second badge co-design process, a general ped agogy course, the course had recently undergone a revision during which equity was built in as one of the major pillars of the course. In the badge system, student designers both created equity-focused badges, but also discussed the ways in which equity pedagogy intersected with other badge nodes, such as building classroom community. While the course centered equity and inclusivity as central ideas, the badge design process introduced new connections and extensions that could be made.

The third badge co-design process focused on an un dergraduate adolescent development course, which is a prerequisite for entering into the teacher education program. This course was already identified as needing to be more centrally focused on equity and inclusion. For this co-design process, student designers first created a badge system of their learning and development in the course. Then they redesigned the badge system to center ICLS2022 Proceedings 1463 © ISLS ideas focused on equity. This process required a reconceptualization of many of the course topics and ideas, a process that both co-design and badge design afforded.

Impact on educational structures and content By involving students to be an integral part of designing the digital badge systems, they introduced concepts more directly linked to their experiences and development in and across the university courses. One key element of this process was how student participants drew links between existing course content and issues of race, equity, and inclusivity. This suggests the value of combining co-design and digital badges as a design tool to integrate development and engagement more deeply into learning environments. For example, after an initial brainstorming session where the participants captured main content ideas they felt should be included in the course, the participants began to organize and sort these topics by using funds of knowledge (Gonzalez, Moll, & Amanti, 2005) as the primary filter or lens. The following topics represent the highest priorities of the learners and the educators of what should be covered in their program: Student Wellness; Educator Role; Identity & Autonomy; and Puberty/Development. It should be noted that Puberty/Development is the only content category that is broken up into three sub-categories: biological, social and cognitive development. The sequence of topics identified by the group represents not only the agreed-upon priorities of the community, but also shows how the group views and interprets the relationship between topics. For these participants, the artifact of the badge system created a vantage point from which both the learners and educators can reflect on how they’ve represented the hierarchy and relationships of the content itself.
