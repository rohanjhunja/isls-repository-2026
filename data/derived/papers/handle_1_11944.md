# Utilizing LLMs to Support Teacher Understanding of AI Literacy

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
AI literacy is imperative for individuals in today’s technology-driven world. Preparing AI-literate students requires teachers to have some AI background, which is often a challenge to teachers. This work-in-progress study explores using LLMs to provide teachers with reports on how AI literacy is addressed in existing materials to inform their AI literacy integration. Our findings aim to offer interdisciplinary solutions to technology design that supports teachers’ AI literacy for achieving AI literacy integration in K-12.

The rapid development of AI technologies has led many nations to emphasize AI education, particularly at the K12 level, to promote widespread AI literacy (Innovation and Science Australia, 2017). In response, various initiatives and frameworks have emerged to define AI literacy, with experts advocating for its recognition as a critical competency for K-12 students (Chen & Zhu, 2023; Touretzky & Gardner-McCune, 2022). One way to integrate AI into K-12 can be for teachers to embed AI literacy into their subject areas through an integrated approach (Ding et al., 2024). Generally speaking, AI literacy can be defined as an individual’s capability and confidence to clearly explain how AI technology function and affect society, as well as to utilize them ethically and responsibly, and to communicate and collaborate effectively with them in any context (Chiu et al., 2024). Although numerous efforts have been made to provide AI materials and implement AI curricula in the classrooms, studies have reported that teachers often lack real-time and accessible support to develop a foundational understanding of AI literacy (Holstein, et al., 2019). Consequently, teachers encounter challenges seeking synergetic and integrated approaches between AI literacy and their disciplinary concepts (Sperling et al., 2024). Given the significant potential in the application of generative AI and large language models in education,, these tools have revealed new opportunities, such as personalized learning for teachers (Abidi et al., 2024), while also bringing challenges, including hallucinations and misinformation. Such challenges are particularly faced by users who lack foundational content knowledge in identifying the quality of generative information (Lan & Chen, 2024). This work-in-progress poster presents how an interdisciplinary research team designs, develops, evaluates, and iterates the LLM-embedded experiments aiming to provide teachers with reports related to identifying AI literacy in curricular materials with a key question: to what extent do LLM-embedded technical solutions (“experiments”) generate accurate reports on text-based AI curricular materials analysis?

## Method

This paper reports on the design process of a larger project that aims to utilize LLMs through a chatbot interface to support teachers in identifying AI literacy components in text-based curricular materials on AI literacy. To achieve this goal, we employed a multi-pronged approach through interdisciplinary efforts. First, we conducted an environmental scan to understand how AI literacy could be defined across different grade levels, including reviewing K-12 AI curricula, scholarly papers, and AI technologies. We further specified three AI literacy components (concepts, practices, and perspectives) (Ng et al., 2021; Williams, 2023) into fine-grain competencies. We then used these components and their respective competencies to develop the AI literacy metrics essential to inform two LLMs-driven experiments (see Figure 1 for example). Figure 1 shows the technology design for Experiment 1 using OpenAI’s GPT-4o. This experiment involves building multiple agents to process information. A document digesting agent is developed to process the documents into text chunks and compile them into a vector database, while the question-answering agent is created to enable the teachers to interact with the chatbot through questions and responses in natural language. The AI literacy metric retrieval agent is created to iteratively retrieve the corresponding AI literacy metrics we developed with users’ inputs. The advanced RAG agent is employed to extract all relevant information while the grading agent simultaneously assigns grades or ranks based on the relevancy of the curriculum materials and the metrics. Finally, the reporting agent comprehensively evaluates the submitted AI curriculum related to teachers’ inquiries. Grounded on similar design, Experiment 2 further utilized LLaMA 3.1-8B-Instruct with long-context generation, leveraging improved context window size provided by the latest LLaMA models (128k tokens), to simultaneously generate evaluations on multiple metrics from our AI literacy components, assign numerical scores in each aspect respectively, summarize the evaluations and assign a comprehensive final score. (OpenAI’s GPT-4o accessed via its “ChatCompletion” API) Identified from our environmental scan, six existing text-based AI literacy materials spanning curriculum, workshops, and courses for various ages and grade levels were used for both experiments. Between experiments 1 and 2, we also conducted a user survey with 100 K-12 educators to solicit feedback about educators’ thoughts on sample LLMs-generated reports to inform our rubric design on evaluating LLMs-generated reports.

Using a two-step analytic approach to evaluate the experiment outcomes, two education researchers first qualitatively coded 126 relevant excerpts from the six curricular materials to identify the key AI literacy components. The overall inter-rater reliability was 86%, and all discrepancies were discussed and resolved to achieve consensus. Secondly, the same researchers evaluated the outputs from the two experiments based on rubrics containing three dimensions: content accuracy, relevant examples and evidence, and report format. There are sub-dimensions nested within each dimension. We then compared rated rubric categories to analyze the outputs generated from these two experiments.

## Preliminary results and implication

Overall, Experiment 1 outputs were rated as primarily inaccurate in determining how AI literacy is presented and defined among the six AI materials. In contrast, outputs from Experiment 2 provided improved outputs, especially in identifying specific AI literacy components. Neither experiment showed a larger variation in performance when evaluating these materials. In the Experiment 1 output evaluation, only one of the six reports contained a mix of correct and relevant information, while the rest contained inaccurate or irrelevant evaluations related to AI literacy concepts, practices, and perspectives. For example, one poorly rated LLM report presented AI examples and evidence from one of the other five materials. In the Experiment 2 output evaluation, all the reports identified at least partial examples and evidence related to AI literacy components included in the respective material. For example, one report evaluated the extent to which each AI literacy component was addressed and identified the missing components of AI literacy in the material. It is worth noting that both researchers had a higher consensus on the Experiment 1 results than the Experiment 2 results. The main cause was the difficulties in evaluating nuanced sub-dimensions in LLM outputs. Despite using the same rubrics for rating, it was challenging to quantify the accuracy and relevance of narrative LLM outputs. Therefore, we are working to qualitatively identify nuanced AI literacy sub-components and respective competencies that could be better or weaker identified by the two experiments.

This work-in-progress paper illustrates one way to promote teacher AI literacy, which is key to meaningful AI literacy integration in K-12 classrooms. When designing LLMs-backed systems or applications to provide real-time and accurate support to teachers’ analysis of and learning from AI literacy presented in any existing materials, it is critical to unpack the black box of module development to weave user inputs and human evaluations to improve the observability of the LLM reasoning path (Xia et al., 2024), especially when a generalpurpose LLM is being applied in a specific domain which the LLM is not trained or fine-tuned on. Fine-tuning LLMs to adapt to specific educational domains could potentially render better results without more complex prompting techniques or application architecture, but it often has extensive and substantive constraints for hardware and technology infrastructure requirements. Our study, in contrast, demonstrated how such a process could be potentially adapted to serve other educational topics by designing compatible LLMs through iterative revisions to the system prompt designs by leveraging human and user inputs. In terms of our next steps, we will continue to review how these reports loop human evaluation and user inputs in refining the system prompts of LLMs. We also plan to recruit classroom teachers to interact with the LLMs to understand how such support relates to teachers’ understanding of AI literacy and integration of AI literacy into K-12.
