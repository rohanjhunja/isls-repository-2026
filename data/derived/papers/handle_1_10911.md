# Examining the Role of Flow in a Technology-Rich Learning Environment

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Experiencing flow while learning is typically linked to positive learning results. This study investigates the relationship between 111 medical students' self-regulated learning (SRL), mental effort, flow experience, and the resulting learning outcomes students as they learn clinical reasoning skills in the context of an intelligent tutoring system. The findings suggest that mental effort mediates the relationship between SRL and the experience of flow. Moreover, the study reveals that students' flow experiences positively predict their diagnostic accuracy.

## Introduction

Flow experience is generally seen as a positive experience where an individual is immersed in an activity without external incentives (Csikszentmihalyi, 2000). Flow is a well-explored concept in sports, gaming, and performance domains, but is relatively understudied in educational contexts. According to the characteristics of flow (Jackson & Cskszentmihalyi, 1999), once an individual reach es the flow  state, there is no mental space left for task-irrelevant self-consciousness. However, mental effort is still actively applied to the learning-relevant task, thereby fostering the state of flow.  We propose that that there is a relationship betwee n self-regulated learning (SRL), mental effort and flow.

## Perspectives and theoretical framework

Flow is a subjective experience that occurs when individuals find a balance between perc eiving a high level of challenge and a high level of skill  (Csikszentmihalyi & Csikszentmihalyi, 1988). To achieve this balance, individuals must invest an appropriate level of men tal effort, which is defined as the non-automatic cognitive exertion applied to a task (Salomon, 1983).

SRL is a well-studied topic in the context of technology-rich learning environments (TRE). SRL guides learners in planning, monitoring, and regulating their feelings, thoughts, behaviors, and motivation s, to achieve learning goals (Zimmerman, 2000). Previous studies have found relationship between flow and SRL  (e.g., Lin, 2021, Liu et al., 2022). Notably, when learners engage in SRL, mental effort is elicited, monitored, and regulated concurrently (Wang & Lajoie, 2023).

However, f ew research studies that explore the connection between SRL, mental effort, and flow experience in medical education contexts. The current study examines the relationship  between SRL, mental effort, flow experience, and performance within a medical education context  in an online tutoring system  by asking: RQ1. How does mental effort influence the relationship between SRL and flow experience during clinical reasoning practice in TREs? RQ2. Does flow experience increase diagnostic accuracy?

## Methodology

A total of 111 medical students (29 males, 82 females) from a Chinese university were enrolled in this study (age: N = 22.32, SD = 1.42). Ethical approval was obtained from academic institutions. This study was conducted within the context of BioWorld (Lajoie, 2021), a TRE designed to help medical students deliberately practice their clinical reasoning skills. These skills include  collecting relevant patient data, conducting laboratory tests, and formulating diagno stic hypotheses for virtual patients.  W e collected  data on students' SRL, mental effort, flow experience, and diagnostic accuracy. SRL was assessed post-learning by adapting four subscales from the Motivated Strategies for Le arning Questionnaire (MSLQ, Pintrich, 1991), covering metacognitive self-regulation, effort regulation, elaboration, and rehearsal (α =.809).  Mental effort (“When you read and understand the case description, how much mental effort do you invest?”) was measured by a 9-point Likert scale. Flow state was calculated using the ability-challenge (skill-challenge) balance formula (Di Mitri et al., 2017). The perceived ability and challenge were measured o n a 0% - 100% scale using questions: "How well do you understand the information presente d in this task? " and "How difficult will it be to solve the diagnostic task?". Diagnostic accuracy was automatically generated by BioWorld.

## Findings

To answer RQ1, a path analysis was run with SRL as the independent variable, mental effort  as a mediator, and flow as a dependent variable. Results of the mediation model showed that SRL positively predicted mental effort (β =.263, SE =.090, p <.05), but not significantly predicted flow experience (β =.156, SE =.089, p =.078). Additionally, mental effort positively influenced flow experience (β =.386, SE =.083, p <.001). Furthermore, mental effort completely mediated the associations between SRL and flow. Namely, the indirect effect of SRL on flow via mental effort was statistically significant (β =.027, SE =.011, p <.05), and the total effect of SRL on flow experience was significant as well (β =.257, SE =.090, p <.05). This model explains 6.9% of the variance in mental effort, and 20.5% of the variance in flow experience. To answer RQ2, a logistic regression analysis was conducted in SPSS 29 to examine the impact of flow experience on the diagnostic accuracy. The logistic regression model was significant, χ2(1) = 3.903, p <.05. The model explained 4.8% (Nagelkerke R 2) of the variance in diagnostic accuracy and correctly classified 56.1% of cases. Increasing flow experience was associated with an increased likelihood of exhibiting correct diagnosis.

## Discussion and conclusion

This study identified the antecedents and outcomes of flow experience within clinical reasoning practi ce and examined the crucial mediator role that mental effo rt plays between SRL and the flow state. Our study did not find a direct relationship between SRL and flow in the medical education context, while mental effort emerged as a potential explanatory factor, serving as the key to maintaining the delicate balance between perceived skills and challenges (Strandberg-Long, 2021). We argue that the optimal level of men tal effort should neither be too low nor too high, as overload or underload caused by ta sk difficulty can lead to anxiety or boredom rather  than flow, according to the flow channel model  (Csikszentmihalyi & Csikszentmihalyi, 1988). Furthermore, our findings reveal a positive relationship between flow experie nce and diagnostic accuracy. This evidence holds particular significance in medical education, as it suggests t hat experiencing a state of flow can benefit learners with the enjoyment, engagement, and success of diagnosis in clinical reasoning. These insights underscore the importance for medical educators and designers of TREs to prioritize and foster conditions conducive to flow experience.
