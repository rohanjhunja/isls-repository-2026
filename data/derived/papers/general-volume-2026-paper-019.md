# Virtual Worlds as Ethical Laboratories: Exploring the Risks and Benefits of AI in Agents of ViTAL: Ethics Missions

**Conference:** ISLS 2026

## Abstract

Agents of ViTAL: Ethics Missions is a narrative-centered learning environment where middle school students explore the ethical risks and benefits of AI technology in a virtual school. In this demo session, we will give an overview of Agents of ViTAL, invite participants to play an activity where they collaboratively rank risks and benefits, and seek feedback on the conversational agent, EthicsBot, embedded in the activity interface. It has become commonly accepted that “AI literacy” includes ethics and understanding social impact as a core component (Ng et al., 2021). However, the consensus largely ends there. Although ethical reasoning is considered essential to AI literacy, scholars and practitioners still have significant work to do in identifying what it means to incorporate these dimensions in curriculum and pedagogy (Ma et al., 2025; Chang & Wong, 2025; Wiese et al., 2025). This challenge is further complicated by the fact that ethics instruction itself is often reserved for older students, with formal courses in ethics historically offered only at the tertiary level (Mohr Lone, 2012). Given that there is agreement that (AI) ethics are important and yet that there is a gap in our current understanding of what this means or how instruction should look, especially for K-12 learners, we explore the potential for game-based learning as an opportunity to engage learners in agentic ethical decision-making. This demo paper presents a narrative-centered learning environment (Lester et al., 2012) intentionally designed to support collaborative ethical reasoning, where students are positioned as influential figures in a school’s AI adoption decisions. As they navigate three dimensions of AI ethics, including privacy, bias, and environmental impact, through authentic scenarios and immediate feedback, students collectively grapple with the messiness of real-world decision-making, ultimately reaching consensus on whether their school should implement classroom monitoring technology. This demonstration exemplifies the ISLS 2026 meeting’s theme “partnering with purpose” by engaging students as co-designers alongside researchers and practitioners. We have been iteratively developing the learning environment, Agents of ViTAL, with local middle school students and incorporating their feedback on player characters, EthicsBot features (an in-environment AI-based conversational agent), and ethical dilemmas that felt most relevant, ensuring the experience is grounded in learner identity and voice. Our project’s advisory board, composed of education administrators and teachers, has also provided feedback on interaction design and facilitating classroom implementation feasibility. Additionally, we have sought disciplinary expertise from philosophers throughout the design process to ensure that our treatment of “ethics” is grounded in philosophical practice and that students engage in authentic, disciplinarily-relevant tasks relating to ethical reasoning. Design principles Computer-supported collaborative learning (CSCL) enables students to build shared knowledge through meaningful collaboration; this is particularly vital in ethics education, where students have to wrestle with complex dilemmas that demand integrating multiple perspectives that can often be at odds with one another. Extensive research demonstrates that CSCL fosters learning gains, enhances critical thinking, and sustains engagement (e.g., Saleh et al., 2022; Vogel et al., 2017). Our design draws on the synergistic principles of CSCL, emphasizing how technology, pedagogical design, and collaboration work together to support student content learning and ethical reasoning (Hmelo-Silver et al., 2023):

1. Technology: Agents of ViTAL creates an immersive learning experience by situating students in a

virtual school context with real-world relevance (Gee, 2008). Scenes function as dynamic simulations that provide immediate, scenario-based feedback responsive to student choice. For instance, when students decide whether the school’s AI feedback tool should store its data locally or on cloud servers, immediate consequences and stakeholder responses are presented. The Ranking Challenge Board serves as a boundary object (Star, 2010), visually representing the group’s ethical stances and supporting collaborative completion of the joint task. The integrated chat box and the EthicsBot, a conversational agent designed as a thought partner, deliver just-in-time support when students need tonegotiate and justify their ranking decisions.

2. Pedagogical design: Agents of ViTAL is grounded in collaborative inquiry learning, enabling students

to collectively build knowledge driven by a shared goal within authentic problem contexts (Slotta et al., 2018). Three design features support this approach: (1) Authentic problem context: The overarching narrative--helping the school principal in making a technology adoption decision-- provides relevance grounded in student life. Game narrative events, like a ransomware attack, are based on reports of realworld occurences and provide a sense of urgency which motivates sustained investigation. (2) Guided instruction: Guidance is essential for effective inquiry-based learning (de Jong et al., 2023). Characters (e.g., the principal, HelperBot, and EthicsBot) guide students through navigating the game and building reasoning from provided evidence resources, while maintaining genuine agency in decision-making. The HelperBot also provides adaptive guidance when students encounter questions. (3) Dynamic feedback: Timely, multi-source feedback supports both deeper learning and student ownership of reasoning (Jeong & Hmelo-Silver, 2016). Our design embeds feedback at multiple moments: immediate consequences responsive to student choices, peer feedback in their Ranking Challenge discussion, and expert ethics feedback for content support. Students must weigh competing variables and tradeoffs within and across three ethical dimensions (privacy, bias, environmental impact). For instance, when configuring the AI feedback tool for the principal, students must decide whether the interaction data is stored locally or on cloud servers, a decision dynamically linked to the school’s limited budget, requiring them to justify ethical tradeoffs grounded in evidence.

3. Collaboration mode: Agents of ViTAL drives engagement in synchronous interaction through the

Ranking Challenge Task and integrated chat box. During the group consensus activity, students engage in shared meaning making by articulating pros and cons, reconciling different perspectives, and collaboratively arriving at a group decision. The chat box supports co-regulation, enabling peer idea negotiation and argument refinement. Additionally, students are encouraged to talk face to face. Together, these three pillars create conditions where middle school students can engage in authentic reasoning about complex AI ethics issues while maintaining agency and benefiting from timely, adaptive support. Technological setup Agents of ViTAL is a browser-based learning environment that uses the Unity game engine and Ink scripts (Inkle, n.d.) to drive the unfolding narrative with branching dialog. Time-stamped log records are created for each action within the learning environment and used for analysis of students’ interactions and collaboration with group members. Learning environment story overview Students act as agents for the Virtual Technology Assessment Laboratory (ViTAL), tasked with evaluating a new AI feedback tool called PresenterPal in a virtual middle school. On their mission, students gather perspectives from different stakeholders (principals, teachers, students, and IT staff) and decide how to configure the technology within the school’s limited budget by weighing each option for its ethical implications, along with its effectiveness and cost. Upon returning to the agency hub space, agents must reach consensus with their group about which aspects of the tool were most beneficial and which were riskiest. Finally, agents prepare a report detailing their ethical analysis to share with the middle school principal. Key elements Part 1: Agent orientation in the libratory Upon logging in to Agents of ViTAL and selecting their avatar, students enter the hub space called the “Libratory” for its combined function as library and virtual laboratory for ethical experimentation. There, they meet the HelperBot (see Figure 1), who orients them to basic game mechanics and directs them to find Professor Mugo, an e thicist who explains the student’s role as an agent of the Virtual Tech Assessment Laboratory (ViTAL) and delivers a mission brief to the student. Players add ethical risks and benefits they have encountered during their visit to the school, then work together to rank them and write a statement sharing what they agree are the major benefits and risks. While they work together to rank the risks and benefits in terms of importance, they can chat with their group members and with the EthicsBot character (see more information on this character below). Visitors to our demo will be able to interact with this user interface in the learning environment to try to come to a consensus with other attendees at the session. Figure 3 Ranking Challenge Board Current development and future plans Current design and development efforts are focused on co-designing and implementing a large language model (LLM)-backed version of the EthicsBot character. The goal of this effort is to deepen ethics learning with a responsive, flexible conversational agent that serves as a thought partner as students evaluate ethical risks and build group consensus. To achieve this, we will conduct co-design sessions with students to define the agent’s role and interaction style. The resulting conversational agent (CA) will be designed to support students in reasoning about ethical impact using philosophical lenses (e.g., rules-based and consequence-based frameworks) without requiring the students to have prior training in philosophy. The CA will also scaffold group discussion in supporting constructive disagreement and consensus-building, while providing accurate, reliable, accessible, and safe responses. In addition, accessibility features such as text-to-speech and speech-to-text will broaden participation for learners with varied needs, while the agent’s modular architecture will enable adaptation across diverse ethical contexts and curricular domains. Demo session overview In this interactive demo session, we will invite attendees to step into the role of a student agent inside Agents of ViTAL and grapple with AI ethics dilemmas. This experience will unfold in three parts: (1) We will present a video showcasing the narrative arc and learning environment, orienting attendees to how students navigate three AI ethics dimensions (privacy, bias, and environmental impact). (2) We will invite attendees to participate in an interactive ranking challenge, experiencing firsthand the collaborative reasoning and consensus building process.

(3) We will solicit feedback and facilitate a discussion of the effectiveness of the learning environment’s narrative

scaffolding, HelperBot features, and CA function and integration. This feedback will directly inform ongoing iterations as we prepare Agents of ViTAL for full classroom implementation and co-design the EthicsBot agent with students.
