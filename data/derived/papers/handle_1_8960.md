# What Does ‘Computer Science Education for All’ Look Like in a Korean Middle School: An Ethnographic Case Study on an Informatics Teacher’s Local Contentious Practice

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Along with the worldwide emphasis on computer science (CS) education for all, informatics, one of the CS subjects in Korean middle school education, has been expanded from the elective to the compulsory since 2018 to offer CS learning opportunities for all students. This study seeks to document how CS for all agenda is enacted in a local school context by exploring an ethnographic case of one informatics teacher. Using the construct of local contentious practice as a conceptual framework, I examine the teacher’s actions and interactions for and related to teaching informatics in the particular school context where larger sociocultural assumptions and narratives about CS teaching and learning operate. Through a reflexive thematic analysis of the ethnographic case data, I identify three patterns of local contentious practice enacted by the informatics teacher and with the teacher’s school community. Implications for future research and practice toward CS for all is discussed.

Major Issues and Purpose of Study Young people are and will be living in a fast-evolv ing world operated by computing systems and applica tions grounded in computer science (CS) knowledge and practices (ACM & IEEE, 2020). It is crucial for young people to have sufficient and sustained opportunities to learn CS, which will help empower themselves as wise and ethical producers, consumers, and problem-solvers of comput ing-based applications, systems, and information. I n this regard, many countries across the globe, including South Korea (Korea, hereafter), have utilized their  public education systems as access points toward the goal of CS for all students (Hubwieser et al., 2015). CS  for all is urgently required to work against injustice exacerb ated by racial, gender, economic, cultural, and reg ional disparities in access to quality CS learning opportunities, and computing-informed lives and career pathways (e.g., Computer Science Teacher Association, 2020).

Since 2018, informatics, one of the CS subjects in Korea, has been expanded from the elective to the compulsory according to the national curricula poli cy revision to serve the goal of CS for all. Korean  middle schools now provide their students with either one or two credits of informatics courses across three academic years. This policy shift is considered a meaningful step forward, given that CS subjects have long been offered as electives (e.g., informatics until 2018), or as parts of other subjects like technology or domestic science. However, to better work toward CS for all, there is still much to be addressed in terms of policy and its implementation at local districts and schools. Utmo st concern is inequity due to insufficient learning  hours assigned to CS subjects. Most CS subjects at primary and high-school levels remain electives or as parts of other subjects, which hinders extensive and sustained learning opportunities across different grade levels (Kim, 2021). Although middle school informatics has shifted to be a compulsory subject, it only takes up one or two credits of a total 99 credits earned across three middle schoo l years. One of other significant concerns is how s chools maintain CS teacher workforce. In the case of middle schools, 77% of informatics teachers majored in CS-related disciplines while 23% are non CS-majors or minors (Korean Association of Informatics Teachers, 2021). Moreover, 49% of teachers who majored in CS-related  disciplines circulate two or more schools to teach informatics while 51% work full-time at one specific school teaching other subjects in addition to informatics. In the situations like these, parents who believe the importance of CS for their children’s future look f or out-ofschool private CS learning programs, particularly when they are affluent enough to afford the additional expenses. Whether students can have sufficient CS learning ex perience is, therefore, highly contingent to and disparate by local communities, schools, and families students live in. It is the disparities that can  contribute to perpetuating educational injustice. Furthermore, these are not only the issues within Korea. Similar injustice issues have been reported across the globe (e.g., Goode et al., 2020). Sustainable and concerted efforts to eradicate such issues and transform policy and implementation is in dire need to actualize equitable CS education and to use CS as a means toward justice, not as a sources of perpetuating injustice. Despite the issues in policy shift and implementations of CS, there exists a dearth of research, particularly in Korean context, investigating how such issues may operate in and around informatics classrooms. Moreover, ethnographic understanding of what CS for all looks like is surprisingly underdeveloped. If the purpose of CS for all is to offer equitable learning experiences, how the policy-level shift has impacted students’ learning via their teachers’ practices in local school contexts should be investigated in ethnographic and case-specific details. Particularly in this study, I attend to Mr. G’s actions and interactions for and related to  teaching informatics in the particular context of his school. By actions and interactions for teaching informatics, I refer to those Mr. G direc tly aimed at teaching students the informatics subject. In comparison, the related to teaching informatics means those shaping and situating his teaching. I frame such actions and interactions with the construct, local contentious practice, which explains on-going formation of the sociohistorical self in the encounter with inst itutional narratives (Holland & Lave, 2001). By usi ng this construct, I sought to articulate how CS for all policy agenda has shaped (and been contextualized in) local school implementations as Mr. G’s case illustrates. The following research question guides this study: Which patterns of local contentious practice do Mr. G’s actions and interactions for and related to teaching informatics reveal?

## Conceptual framework

I utilize local contentious practice as the conceptual framework to analyze and articulate the ethnographic case of Mr. G, focusing on his actions and interactions for and related to teaching informatics in a Korean middle school context. Local contentious practice calls attention  to two histories: history-in-person and history-in-institutionalized-struggles. History-in-person, as a personal narrative of self-making, takes shape via interactional practices with others in local spaces. By history-in-institutionalized-struggles, Holland and Lave (2001) refer to institutional narratives grounded in historicized and trans-local structures, which provide resources for people’s actions and interactions and leave traces in their formation of history in person; as such, I call thi s history as historicized institutional narratives in this paper.

Local contentious practice takes shape via interact ions between people and tensions that arise from people’s different positions in historicized instit utional narratives. Viewing teaching and learning t hrough the perspective of local contentious practice helps to understand different forms of interactions shaped b y multiple actors at different positions of power in their local learning institutions. In their institutional learning spaces (e.g., a school classroom, a teacher staff meeting, school events), multiple actors (e.g., students, teachers, principal) and non-human constituents (e.g., spatial structures, d isciplinary knowledge, school-national curriculum) interact within historicized institutional narratives and dynamic networks of power. Local contentious practice can be a means of transformation as people create and recreate their historyin-person as well as the historicized institutional  narratives (Calabrese Barton et al., 2020). In thi s regard, examining local contentious practice in educational institutions such as schools can help to identify sources and processes of disruption and transformation brought to bear on the institutions. As such, I attend to identifying Mr. G’s local contentious practice, and describing how his practice may have brought about disruption and transformation of his school community’s assumptions, discourse, and practices around CS teaching and learning. Methodological approach I answer the research question, by undertaking an e thnographic case study that utilizes a reflexive th ematic analysis. Taking an ethnographic case study approac h allowed me to ‘be with’ participants—i.e., Mr. G,  his colleague teachers, and his students in real world contexts. Conducting a reflexive thematic analysis allowed me to focus on how the construct of local contentious practice is enacted and contextualized in the specific case.

## Ethnographic Case Study

Ethnographic case study method examines events and individuals real time within a bounded system situated in larger cultural and historical contexts (e.g., Angers & Machtmes, 2005). This method is appropriate for this study seeking to answer the research question by examinin g Mr. G’s practices for and related to teaching inf ormatics (inward attention) and the multi-scalar contexts in which the practices are shaped (outward attention). Positionality To this study, I bring my positionality, which I w ould call my history-in-person as a researcher who seeks to work toward equity in and through CS and STEM education, and as a former teacher who once taught informatics in Korean classrooms.

Context This case took place between January and September  2021 in a middle school within a lowincome neighborhood in one of the Korean metropolit an cities. A total of 220 students (76 6 th, 73 7 th, 71 8 th graders) and 26 adult members (21 teachers, 1 princ ipal, 1 vice principal, and 3 administrative) const itute this school community. Since 2018, this school has been designated as one of Innovation Schools aimed at educational transformation in economically disadvantaged communities. Regarding informatics subject, this school offers 2 credits of informatics, 1 for 6th and 1 for 7th graders. Participants The case teacher is Mr. G, with 20 years’ experience teaching informatics at four Korean public middle schools, including the one in the stu dy. Seven additional participants were recruited th rough snowballing sampling: Mr. G’s colleague teachers (Mr. D and Ms. P) and 5 students (three 6th, and two 7th graders). ICLS2022 Proceedings 1594 © ISLS Data Generation & Analysis I generated data, including individual and group in terviews, instructional and curricular documents, s tudents’ learning artifacts, and the researcher fieldnotes. I analyzed the data by using a reflexive thematic a nalysis. This analysis method facilitated me to find meanings from data through concurrent dialogue and contextualization of theory-based coding framework (Braun & Clarke, 2019). I took three analysis phases. Open coding. Interview transcripts were major data for open coding. The unit of analysis was an instance the interviewees pointed out as important for and r elated to informatics teaching and learning; the in stance was either interactional (involving different actors) o r reflective (involving individuals’ reflections on  systems and practices). Each unit is coded in an exploratory ma nner as I first read transcripts through chronologi cally, and then triangulated the coded instances with other da ta. As codifying interactional or reflective instan ces, I also assigned sub-codes to the components constituting each instance—e.g., individuals, actions, spaces, and tasks. Construct-informed coding. For this phase, I first established a coding frame work drawing on the construct of local contentious practice, and iteratively refined both the coding framework and the codes and subcodes. The coding framework involved concepts used to describe the construct, such as actors, relation ships, interactions, tensions, community, institution, history-in-person, historicized institutional narratives. Identifying patterns of local contentious practice. In this phase, I figured out relations among different codes and sub-codes refined in the second phase, fr om which I identified three patterns of local conte ntious practice as the main themes of Mr. G’s actions and interactions for and related to teaching informatic s in his school context. Instances described in the first analysis phase offer illustrations of how each pattern manifests.

## 4. Findings

Drawing on the reflexive thematic analysis of Mr. G ’s ethnographic case, three patterns of local conte ntious practice were identified from his actions and interactions for and related to teaching informatics in his classrooms and across his school community. The patterns are:

1. Creating a dialogic space that engaged colleague teachers in questioning the normalized practice of treating

informatics teachers as school technicians;

2. Facilitating an agentic learning space where students engaged with academically challenging but achievable

computing tasks, which disrupted the school-wide deficit view on their students as low-achieving;

3. Co-designing with colleague teachers the inter-subj ects learning projects in which students utilized a nd

integrated knowledge and skills across informatics and other subjects. For this proposal, I briefly unpack how the first pattern manifested, and which histories (i.e., Mr. G’s history-inperson and the historicized institutional narratives shared by the school community) operated. The first pattern manifested through sociopolitical actions he took in his school community of colleague teachers and principal. By sociopolitical, I refer to how he called his colleagues’ attention to long-held narrative that reduced the legitimacy of informatics teachers  and informatics. For example, during the third tea cher-staff meeting he had since he joined the school, he made a presentation, through which he highlighted and questioned how informatics teachers are easily treated in schools more as schools’ ‘tech person,’ than colleague teachers. He decided to make the presentation after the first te acher-staff meeting, in which he as a newcomer foun d that he was already assigned to take in charge of the school’s digital device-related administrative work. He recalled: There was no option to me. Managing digital devices, wireless connections, and remote class system, a big deal after pandemic, were all my tasks. One teacher told me, ‘informatics teachers are experts.’ Yes, we may know more. Also, I don’t hate such tasks necessarily, but they are thrown to us because we are viewed as school tech guys than teachers. Some tasks are what every teacher should know. Some others are what can be cared at a higher level. Upsetting.. I experience this whenever moving to a new school. This statement indicates two histories encountered.  His history-of-person as an informatics teacher, shaped through two decades of similar experiences, critica lly recognized power dynamics that positioned infor matics teachers as individuals who would deal with other school members’ technical issues. This history-in-person is not just about him;. As he put ‘we’ and ‘us,’ the similar history-in-person is shared by other informatics teachers. This similarity explains historicized institutional narratives. One of the institutional narratives is that, as M r. G’s colleague said, informatics teachers are ‘experts’ about computers and devices. However, such a narrative worked to reduce, not legitimize, the presence of informat ics teachers. The tasks these teachers were expecte d to do as ‘experts’ were far from enacting CS for all. As Mr. G noted, it was not that informatics teachers always refuse to take technical tasks. However, he pointed out the tenacity of  the reduced view on informatics teachers, the view that can be extended to how informatics as a subject can is. Mr. G continued to recall the presentation: When I was a beginner teacher, I just did what was assigned to me, but now being in this game for about twenty years, I have something to say. This school is Innovation School, I thought. If so, the conversation should take place. They may not know what informatics teachers do and why informatics matter. ICLS2022 Proceedings 1595 © ISLS Although the historicized narratives about informat ics teachers and informatics prevailed in the schoo l, Mr. G held up a hope that he could create a dialogic spac e with his colleague teachers, because the school a s one of Innovation Schools had tried to establish culture t hat allowed democratic and often contentious conver sations among different school community members. After his  presentation in which he put together questions ab out normalized assumptions on informatics teachers and suggestions about different task loads, the atmosph ere apparently went full with tension, according to Mr. G’s reflection. However, some teachers put themselves into Mr. G’s and other ‘minor-subject’ teachers’ positio ns. Some teachers acknowledged that they actually h ad not thought about the normalized discourse highlighted by Mr. G. As they conversed on, they examined critically the tasks given to teachers and the underlying rationales. As a result, some of the tasks assigned to Mr. G were shared with other teachers and staff. Instead, he took the  lead in the interdisciplinary learning project, th e project idea other subject teachers hoped to launch but hesitated as they did not know how to realize the idea. This pattern of local contentious practice, describ ing how he sought to reclaim his presence as an informatics teacher, is closely related to other two patterns. The second pattern describes how he used informatics to disrupt school-wide deficit view on their students, by engaging students in computing projects (e.g., ENTRY, Korean educational programming language platform, a nd international BEBRAS challenges for computationa l thinking), and held school events in which students taught programming other subject teachers. The last pattern shows how some other subject teachers (such as Mr. D and Ms. P who participated in this study) joined disrupting between-subjects boundaries and sought to co-design with Mr. G interdisciplinary learning objects.

## Discussion, Implication, and Significance

Findings of this study about Mr. G’s local contentious practice show how one teacher can bring about exposure and disruption of a school’s historicized narrative, drawing on one’s history-in-person that has become struggles and pathways toward CS for all. Utilizing ethnograp hic case study methods with a reflexive thematic an alysis enabled me to gain the place-based empirical eviden ce of and insights from local implementation of pol icy, undergirded and contested by larger historicized institutional narratives about CS teaching and learning. Although the findings highlight a promising case of teachers and school communities working as change agents, the ultimate source of struggles such as course offerings and teacher supply were left unresolved. Ultimately, concerted efforts should be garnered both from policy and research pa nes. The CS for all agenda should result from conce rted policy revision and systemic support for schools and teachers, for example, about how to resolve inter-subjects tensions of course distribution and budget concerns  of increasing CS teacher workforce. Meanwhile, res earch should support teachers from in-service and pre-ser vice informatics and CS teachers to other subject t eachers seeking for integration with informatics and CS subjects, by providing opportunities of professional development and research-practice-partnership. Some of such opp ortunities should also support teachers to recogniz e and readily respond to power structure that may hinder CS for all; as Mr. G’s case exemplifies. Findings a nd implications of this study inspired by the goal of CS for all can interest worldwide education researchers focusing on CS education and inter-subject integration with CS, and those participating in this year’s ICLS the med with collaborative education for all.
