# Productive Interdisciplinary Engagement and Teacher Professional Learning for Social Studies in Nature-Based Learning

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Outdoor experiences for K-12 youth are often conceptualized as just fun, with little learning benefit. And when disciplinary learning is incorporated, science is often emphasized, to the detriment of other relevant disciplines, such as social studies. One reason is that teachers lack professional learning opportunities to make use of nature-based resources for (inter) disciplinary engagement. Here, we merge and expand Randi Engle’s frameworks to become expansively framed productive interdisciplinary engagement (PIE) in examining a case study of one teacher and sixth-grade students participating in a week-long, school-based outdoor learning program. We examine moments where sensemaking in science and social studies can create PIE and identify examples of teacher-led productive interdisciplinary inquiry as well as missed opportunities to go deeper or better leverage student sensemaking. These findings have implications for making the pedagogical principles of PIE more explicit in the teacher professional learning program offered for School of the Wild.

## Introduction

When not hindered by adults and the artificial constraints grown-ups create, young people are active sensemakers and transformative agents who can generate profound and actionable knowledge. With opportunities for learning in and from nature, young people’s  sense of fairness, their social disposition to care for others (e.g., Rizzo et al., 2016), and interest in how people live together  can be harnessed for sustained stewardship of our environment. Ardoin et al. (2020) found that learning in, and about, their local environments helps young people gain knowledge about habitats, ecosystems, wildlife, and conservation practices needed to become informed citizens and stewards of the land, which can then increase pro-environmental behaviors (James & Williams, 2017). When young people can connect science, technology, engineering, arts, and math (STEAM) learning to concrete, community-relevant issues in their lives, it becomes more engaging for students  (Miller et al., 2015). In less formally constrained learning spaces outdoors, students build understanding of  scientific and socio-political interactions between humans and nature. Social studies connects to nature-based STEAM learning in examining the effects of human behavior on nature. For example, when young people envision sustainable living where humans don’t just exploit natural resources  but focus on stewardship, restoration of habitats, and appreciation of nature’s ingenuity and resilience, their immediate behaviors and those of the adults in their lives can be affected (Damerell et al., 2013). In a research-practice partnership with schools  and local county naturalists in the Midwestern United States, the week-long School of the Wild  (SOTW) program is positioned to merge natural sciences and social studies when students tackle the ‘what?,’ ‘how?,’ and ‘so what?’ of interactions between humans and their morethan-human surroundings (Marin & Bang, 2018). Through SOTW, generative knowledge practices are applied to engage learners in knowledge-building inquiries outdoors. Young people can learn sciences and social studies while fostering conservation practices. Collaboration with each other and natural resources is a way to make sense of complex systems in the exten sive ecosocial learning environment that is nature.  To support student learning and make SOTW scalable, the program includes a focus on teacher professional learning  (TPL) to enable local educators to be confident facilitators of students’ nature-based inquiries. In this context, our study centered on identifying moments that would allow the incorporation of  social studies concepts into what is often mainly focused on scientific content. This analysis occurred within the context of a design-based implementation research study, focused on TPL and its impact on student exp eriences and learning. Here we focus specifically on how educators leveraged or missed opportunities to incorporate social studies into outdoor learning activities. Benefits and challenges of doing social studies outdoors Outdoor, nature-based learning as learning in and from nature has many documented ben efits, among them understanding and sense of relevance of complex concepts and practices learned, as well as improved mental health (Ardoin et al., 2020; Dettweiler et al., 2017; Miller et al., 2015). However, K-12 outdoor experiences, such as nature field trips, often get perceived and posi tioned by teachers and students as just fun, with little learning benefit (Glackin, 2016). Classroom-based educators seem to struggle to appreciate and harness nature’s diverse learning resources, or, w hen student learning in outdoor spaces is acknowledged, it is often conc eptualized as opportunities for science learning about the ‘what?’ and ‘how?’ of natural phenomena. Other (inter)disciplinary inquiry, particularly into the ‘so what?’ of human-nature interactions in which the social studies deal (Dyment, 2005; Kenna & Russell, 2015; Stewart, 2008), is rarely seized upon and may even be discounted as ill-fitting with a focus on the science behind natural occurrences. Such a disconnect represents missed opportunit ies to make visible and tangible the scientific phenomena within natural history and their connections to human geography. It deprives students of important opportunities to examine the impacts these processes have on humans—and of investigations into what we cando to mitigate human impacts on the natural environments around us. Acting on what we perceive as missed opportunities in teacher preparation for nature-based learning, the goal of this study is to improve our understanding of (inter)disciplinary learning opportunities that exist when we take students into nature-based learning environments. From observations and video-recordings of students and their teachers outdoors, we specifically focus on moments of social studies learning in nature. Identifying discrete opportunities for integrated learning can help us better target TPL to teachers facilitating authentic learning about how humans interact with their more-than-human surroundings. Young people’s agency to protect the  natural world from further destruction by humans may grow from such learning and inspire them to improve the resilience of the more-than-human environment and human settlements to withstand localized and larger natural disasters.

## Theoretical framework

Our interest in social studies learning outdoors is framed using Engle and Conant’s (2002) concept of productive disciplinary engagement (PDE). PDE is a situated model of disciplinary learning, characterized by four design principles: Learners: (a) problematize disciplinary content from their own perspective s by drawing on personal experiences; (b) are given authority to generate disciplinary knowledge by solving probl ems they identified; (c) are held accountable to peers and disciplinary norms in the process; and (d) are given access to relevant resources needed to accomplish (a)-(c). PDE-inspired learning can be made more relevant to stud ents, if it is expansively framed (Engle, 2006; Engle et al., 2012) for students to bring in related ideas and see applications of them outside of class. Students generate intercontextuality and make connection s across contexts by linking the content knowledge and skills built in formal schooling to applications with people, places, issues, and ti mes beyond the original learning context. In outdoor learning spac es, expansively framed PDE can be elevated to productive interdisciplinary engagement (PIE) as students tackle complex issues with real-world relevance. Especially when science and social studies come together in nature-based PIE, learners utilize their agency and authority to become authors of knowledge that expresses deep understanding of complex issues and that fosters appreciation of nature. Exploring natural phenomena outdoors merges with investigations into the ‘so what?’ of nature’s interrelationship with humans. In that way, students learn from nature, not just about it (Marin & Bang, 2018) and create memorable learning experiences that support future inquiry (Boxerman, 2013)  that is relevant for making choices in their lives beyond school—e.g., considering the biological impact and economic externalities of items they consume. Many U.S. states codify what K-12 students should learn in terms of content and skills in each subject. In social studies, they tend to align with the National Council for the Social Studies’ Curric ulum Standards and C3 Framework (NCSS, 2010, 2013)  and in natural sciences with the Next Generation Science Standards (NGSS Lead States, 2013) —which include many STEAM learning goals. Both of t hese national guidelines support outdoor education and PIE in that s ocial studies are “crucial to informed civic decisi on-making about human-environmental relationships” (NCSS, 2010, p. 34) and sciences are incorporated at every grade level through the standard ESS3: Earth and Human Activity  (NGSS Lead States, 2013). Attention to human-nature relations combines science and social studies in promoting local and global efforts of land stewardship (Ardoin et al., 2020). Nature-based inquiries help learners “develop their spatial views and perspecti ves of the world, and understand the relationships between people, places, and envir onments. Learners … explore the effects of the environment on human activities, and the impact of these activi ties on the environment” (NCSS, 2010, p. 34). Highlighting social studies, NCSS promotes a strong focus on civ ic competence through rigorous inquiry processes wh ere students “employ skills of data collection and anal ysis, collaboration, decision-making, and problem-solving” (NCSS, 2024). This includes understanding the scientific processes at work as demanded by Next Ge neration Science Standards—e.g., ecosystems (5.LS2, MS-LS2-1; NGSS Lead States, 2013). NCSS and NGSS promote shared practices between social studies and science as foundations for PIE, such as evidence-based inquiry. Currently, teachers are not fully utilizing the opp ortunities for authentic learning afforded by natur e. Even when outdoor learning is integrated into forma l educational experiences, teachers have difficulty letting go of formal content delivery (Ramey et al., 2024) and seeing how outdoor learning activities might be connected to standards and classroom learning. For example, teac hers in our study requested better resources explic itly connecting outdoor learning activities to disciplin ary standards. Teachers also struggle to open up av enues for students to enjoy expansively-framed PIE (Ayotte-Beaudet et al., 2021; Glackin, 2016), in part because examining authentically situated complex issues in nature is sometimes at odds with teachers’ views of content c overage, protection, and control (e.g., Allen & Penuel, 2015; Barton & Levstik, 2003; James, 2008; Parker, 2023; Ramey et al., 2024). To better understand teachers’ approaches to outd oor learning, we aimed to answer two research questions: (1) How are teachers engaging students in social studies learning outdoors? And (2) what evidence of and potential for productive interdisciplinary engagement (PIE) do we see in social studies learning outdoors?

## Methods

To answer these questions, our team investigated te achers’ and students’ experiences during School of the Wild (SOTW). SOTW is a week-long, outdoor learning experience that takes place during the school day at local state and county parks  with the goal of  introducing students to prairie, woodlands, wetlands, and outdo or skills. It is designed to connect fifth-and sixth-grade students with their local more-than-human community (Marin & Bang,

2018) and engage them in authentically student-driven exploration, inquiry, and problem-solving in nature. In the

original setting, where SOTW has been running since 1999, it  is co-facilitated by classroom teachers with direct support from the SOTW team. This study, however, followed the program in  scaling up across the state, where new teachers at new state and county parks were onboarded. These teachers would not have SOTW staff available to consistently co-facilitate the program after a one-time initiation. Thus, their teacher professional learning (TPL) program was specifically designed to prepare local educators as primary facilitators. The SOTW model for student engagement and the teachers’ role in it was novel to classroom educators. In the TPL, local naturalists and SOTW staff modeled a range of activities that can be done with children outdoors. Teachers then had considerable agency within their professional learning community  to decide which activities they wanted to implement with their students in the SOTW week. To allow for distribution of expertise, teachers usually chose one ecosystem and devised activities in it. They would run them each day as different student groups rotated through the ir station. Local county naturalists supported teachers in the TPL and during the  first day or two of their first implementation of the week-long program with the students. In this context of scaling up SOTW, we accompanied 15 educators through their summer TPL workshop and their initial facilitation of SOTW for students in the fall of 2024. The analysis here follows the experiences of one of these teachers, Mr. Beech.

## Participants, data sources, and analysis

Our analysis here focuses on data from one educator, Mr. Beech (pseudonym) and a group of sixth-grade students, selected as a case study of nature-based learning in the prairie. This group’s prairie exploration was initially selected for analysis, because the first author, herself a former social studies teacher,  observed this group during the SOTW implementation and noticed the social studies connections that were ma de or missed in the moment. After conversations with the second author, who had  also observed Mr. Beech on a different day, and fu rther review and discussion of the full data corpus, we identifi ed his case as an exemplar of nature-based learning for social studies in our dataset. Mr. Beech had 23 years of experience and a personal affinity for the outdoors. In our pre-survey, he received the highest-possible score on the Nature Interest Scale (Kleespies  et al., 2021). He made a conscious effort to connect learning to students’  lives and illustrated how social studies and scien ce apply in natural settings by using Socratic questioning to build students’ understanding of complex issues. With Mr. Beech, as with other teachers in the study, we collected pre-and post-surveys, a retrospective focus group interview, field notes, and video data, collected from several wearable, head-mounted cameras worn by Mr. Beech, three students, and a researcher. Vid eo and audio were synced and merged to allow for mu ltiperspectival analysis of learning moments. Like most educators participating in SOTW, Mr. Beech facilitated the same activities every day with a different group of students rotating to him. Here, we focused on one day, Tuesday, because it was a full day in the prairie and neither Mr. Beech’s first nor last iteration of the same activities. We analyzed data using reflexive thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2006, 2019)  and interaction analysis (Hall & Stevens, 2016; Jordan & Henderson, 1995)  to identify moments of social studies-relevant sensemaking among students and opportunties for the  teacher to create such moments. Mr. Beech’s day was broken down by activity and iteratively coded for disciplinary ideas and practices, such as students’ sensemaking about how something works (science) or how we live together with nature (social studies). Attending to the PIE framework, we applied interaction analysis to examine how (inter)disciplinary ideas were presented and taken up (or not) by teacher and students through verbal and nonverbal communication in social studies-relevant episodes.

## Findings and discussion

Throughout the day, students had opportunities to analyze connections among historical events and developments in a specific geographical and cultural context (social studies standard 6.20; Iowa Department of Education, 2017) and human impacts on the environment (science standard MS-ESS3-3; NGSS Lead States, 2013). However, this framing wasn’t explicitly presented to teachers in their preparation workshop and Mr. Beech was thus not in a position to apply PIE’s design principles to the activities he planned for his students. Without prompting, he did draw some social studies connections during the sessions, yet most activities did not realize their full potential to explore both science and social studies together.

Three episodes from Mr. Beech’s day illustrate available opportunities we identified for PIE in nature. All episodes afforded the integration of social stu dies (human geography and history) with science (biology and physics) to understand nature-human interactions and meet academic content standa rds—but these possibilities were not fully realized. In the first episode, student engagement was high, the young people had opportunities to problematize with authority and accountability to (inter)disciplinary norms, and they had ample resour ces to support their sensemaking. In the second, the teacher hinted at o pportunities for complex social and scientific learning but did not take full advantage of the PIE  framework. In the third, a student noticed a pheno menon that held opportunities for both science and social studies engagement, but these were not seized by anybody. The initial activation of students’ existing knowledge was followed by them making educated conjectures about the use of items Mr. Beech held up in turn. Although Mr. Beech moved rather quickly through each item in his large plastic bin, once a satisfactory answer for its use was made by any child, the students were encouraged to link their own lives to how Indigenous American peoples solved problems like hands-free carrying (a bag with straps versus a bison stomach  with a leather strap), water storage (water bottle vs bison bladder) (see Figure 1), and digging (shovel vs antler palm). Mr. Beech’s lesson was engaging for students, but could have happened in the classroom just as well as in a state park. Earlier in the day, Mr. Beech had mentioned bison a nd how they affected the land when the group was in  the prairie, which helped the children relate—but the actual show, guess, and tell did not occur in that prairie. In the above exchange, Mr. Beech also missed a spontaneous opportunity for interdisciplinary learning when he did not follow up on Kenny’s suggestion of using pieces of bison as building materials (turn 17). Maybe Kenny’s comment was prompted by having seen a picture of a teepee or another depiction of Indigenous American ways of living on the plains. The teacher, who controlled this exchange and the validation process of what counted as ‘right’ answers, could have encouraged Kenny to examine bison hide and leather strips for building a teepee. Key features of a teepee are that they serve to shelter humans from the elements, and are sturdy enough for yearround living, yet also easily dis-and reassembled for use by semi-nomadic Indigenous American tribes living on the plains. Aside from possibly engaging engineering concepts i n considering te epees’ structural integrity, this inquiry pathway could have afforded comparison between teepees as minimally invasive  forms of housing  and the more invasive types of housing these students live in. This would have allowed students to draw connections between Indigenous American land uses and changes to the landscape brought on by European colonization, the provision of sustenance (farming, gathering, hunting), population size, and permanent settlements. Mr. Beech did have a set of maps that could have supported this l ine of inquiry, but he used them at another point in the day.  In the episode here, his focus was on the large bin full of authentic artifacts harvested from bison, elk, and deer —a bin that did not include animal-derived building materials. The contents of the bin and Mr. Beech’s desire to show many of them may have been a reason why he validated Kenny’s idea briefly by saying “yeah mayb e building materials” (turn 18), but then quickly returned to the  content of the bin.  It is important to note here that t he Indigenous group associated most recently with this region, the Meskwaki nation of the Sac and Fox tribe of the Mississippi, used semi-permanent wikiups made from willow branches and cattail mats, not te epees. In that moment, Mr. Beech may not have thought of how other Indigenous American groups of the northern plains  use bison hides for te epees and that Kenny’s legitimate association may come fr om a documentary or photograph about other Indigenous groups. The week-long program did include shelter building on anothe r day, but it  was run by a different educator and did not include animal-derived building materials either. In its separate design, the shelter building lesson  did not realize the potential for expansively frame d, sustained PIE that could connect  to Kenny’s association that Indigenous peoples in the plains might use animal products for building shelters either. Episode 2: Hunting with an atl-atl To understand what skills were required to survive in the prairie, Mr. Beech introduced the atl-atl. An atl-atl is an Indigenous American spear thrower (social studies) that uses a lever to generate more force than a human ar m (science) (see Figure 2). Atl-atls are usually carved from wood and include a little notch at the end to hook a long arrow/spear. In trying to hit a large, stationary target 15 feet  away, the students got a glimpse of the difficulty  of bison hunting without guns. In focusing on the “fun ” aspect of this coordinative challenge, Mr. Beech created a memorable experience for the learners. Yet, his focus on safety and technique reduced opportunities for PIE. Throughout the atl-atl activity, Mr. Beech positioned himself as indispensable in the process of students throwing the spears: He always loaded the spears onto the atl-atl, placed the students in position to hold the loaded atl-atl, and checked their stanc es before they could launch a throw. Due to his chosen positioning  as the arbiter of safety, Mr. Beech was always needed to set up throws. He  could not also facilitate exploration of scientific ‘what?’ and ‘how?’, such as the atl-atl’s properties as a lever, the flight path of a spear  (both science), or the challenges that remained once a bison had been killed, such as transporting and preserving meat (social studies) at the same time. Linking the physical skill of hunting to a society’ s ways of living offer s social studies connections that, in this case, were disaggregated from the experience of throwing with the atl-atl. To make the illustration of a physically challenging hunting skill more PIE, the preparation of this activity could include the bison artifacts from Episode 1 more directly and create a  social studies focus on the ‘so what?’. P roviding conversation starters, images, scenarios, and the artifacts could have critically engaged the students who were waiting to throw. Episode 3: A dried-up creek On one of their walks from the nature center to the prairie, a student noticed that a little bridge they were using in a forest patch was currently not needed: it spanned a shallow, dried-up creek bed. Sparked by something they noticed, th e student showed an attempt at problematizing a nat ural occurrence when they shouted out their observation to the group and pointed to the creek bed. Mr. Beech briefly engaged the comment by saying “when there’s a lot of water, yeah, there’s like a creek that runs through there. But it’s been so dry, it’s drie d out completely.” This was as far as the sensemaking went on this occasion. Mr. Beech, and thus the whole group, had not even stopped to  elaborate on the observation or to  investigate the creek bed  for its scientific properties or social studies relevance. The student who had noticed the creek bed —and their peers —lacked the resources, intellectual authority, and interdisciplinary accou ntability to launch inquiries  into causes (science) and effects (both science and social studies) of a dried-up creek. To understand any ‘what?’ and ‘how?’ on a scientific level, they would have needed  at least time to think about how a creek’s water levels impact the  forest they were in. Root depth and which plants were desirable or not i n a specific  ecosystem had come up earlier in the day, but these conversations were not connected to the dried-up creek. To also investigate the ‘so what?’ in terms of social studies, students may have needed prompting, but, above all, time, to consider what the creek’s role is in relation to human settlements nearby. With ways to gather information and time to think a bout causes and implications, PIE would have been possible here. Chances to inves tigate plant life and ecology above and below groun d, their relation to the water table, the effects of drought  (or flooding) on animals, plants, and humans, and the impact of human actions on local waterways were not seized. Changes in weather patterns were also not engaged. The creek was not part of Mr. Beech’s original less on plan, it just happened to be along the path to t he prairie. Seizing the learning opportunity it presented would have required Mr. Beech to be comfortable with PIE’s principles for student-driven inquiry, have both the science and social st udies knowledge to see the connections, anticipate and foreshadow  where such an inquiry might take students, and feel like he could dedicate time to it. This time for spontaneous, locally contextualized investigation would have to come at the expense of another, pre-planned activity. This kind of pivoting did not  occur. It is unclear, whether that was due to  Mr. Beech not having been primed through the TPL to look for such opportuniti es, him not recognizing the possibilities  in the moment, his plans deliberately excluding them, or a combination of these and other factors.

## Implications and future directions

In terms of student learning, productive interdisci plinary engagement (PIE) enhances knowledge-building that harnesses the relationship between science and social studies. Through inquiries out in nature, learning of complex biological and socio-economic processes is made tangible, and the learning becomes relevant to the young people’s own and others’ lives. Thus equipped, they can focus this understanding and choices they make on their shared responsibility for local land stewardship and global environmental concerns—for example, in considering where and how they live and what their consumption’ s impacts are along the way from the extraction of raw materials to the disposal of trash. This is a tall order for nature-based education, but an important one that can begin with teacher facilitators who scaffold students’ sensemaking through PIE. When opportunities for PIE were missed, it was not due to a lack of skills and expertise in the person of the teacher, Mr. Beech—we think it was a lack of awareness that could be remedied via more targeted TPL at this particular site. Going forward, the SOTW team may consider increasing the focus on interdisciplinarity by making the features of PIE more explicit to the participating teachers: Position students to problematize relevant issues, confer them  the authority and accountability to work within interdisciplinary norms, and provide them with resources (most notably, time). When TPL modeling is framed as PIE and the standards that the modeled activities meet are made explicit, it may be easier for teachers to create their own activities as principled adaptations of PIE (DeBarger et al., 2017; Penuel et al., 2007; Penuel  & Gallagher, 2009). Once PIE is established as a set of organizing principles, historical events and develo pments in a specific geographical and cultural cont ext (social studies standard 6.20; Iowa Department of Education, 2017) and human impacts on  the environment (science standard MS-ESS3-3; NGSS Lead States, 2013) will organically become integral parts of SOTW activities. The aspiration of framing SOTW as PIE also has to expand to the naturalists who work with the local teachers. As experts on the ecosystems in specific parks, they are best positioned to highlight the scientific ‘what?’ and ‘how?’ and its effects to then also illustrate the social studies-focused ‘so what?’. For example, if Mr. Beech was not as familiar with the park, a naturalist cou ld point him and his colleagues to the dried-up creek from Episode 3 as a site for learning. As the expert on local wildlife, the naturalist could offer  adjustments for investigations when the creek is flowing and when it is dried-up. Together, they could then come up with prompts or illustrations of how the creek impacts its surrounding flora and fauna, how it, in turn, is impacted by them, and what roles humans play in this.  However, to do this, both the naturalists and the t eachers have to understand the ‘what?’ and ‘how?’ and the ‘so what?’ of PIE and make it an instructional focus.

Our findings offer insight into opportunities for (inter)disciplinary learning afforded by outdoor contexts and how they might be leveraged by educators. We found that teachers struggle with nature-based learning when it is not directly aligned with disciplinary standards and testing requirements. Additionally, the depth of instruction and facilitation skills hinge on the teachers’ individual expertise about the natural spaces and their level of comfort in being outside with a group of young students (Ramey et al., 2024). The opportunities for PIE that we identified here indicate that specific teaching tools can generate interdisciplinary inquiry when the TPL team directly aligns the activities they model with both science and soc ial studies standards (e.g., NGSS Lead States, 2013, Iowa Department of Education, 2017). For example, Mr. Be ech used Socratic questioning successfully throughout the day to let students share knowledge and make conjectures. In Episode 1, however, the activity structure of ‘show, guess, and tell’ mean t that students had to arrive at answers that Mr. Beech  deemed factually correct, which depended on his knowledge of the artifacts and Indi genous American ways of life. It reduces the burden  on teachers and empowers young people when we position the students as capable of investigating complex natural phenomena and their mutual interactions with humans. In future iterations of the TPL, we can benefit e ducators by providing specific examples that go beyond guided questioning and into actual inquir y and students’ own sensemaking. When teachers feel comfortable with interest-based and relevant  outdoor learning, students will inquire into and understand the ‘what?’ and ‘how?’ of STEAM contents, as well as the ‘so what?’ of social studies directly from nature, instead of having facts transmitted by a teacher in the isolation of a classroom. The research-practice partnership between SOTW, teachers, their students, local county naturalists, and researchers supports interdisciplinary learning in nature-based settings. Student learning framed as productive interdisciplinary engagement (PIE) includes social studies. Seeing this in the episodes above  helped us identify where teachers may need more support. By expanding upon the episodes above  as explicit examples of how to foster PIE, we can  provide teachers with locally adaptable  tools to  integrate social studies into SOTW. The episodes presented here also highlight the importan ce of supporting teachers in facilitating conversation s about the Indigenous peoples, past and present, of this r egion. We continue to build partnerships with local Indigenous communities to better incorporate Indigenous ways of knowing into students’ on-site learning experiences. In continuously improving SOTW’s TPL program, we can position educators as facilitators of impactful, ri gorous learning experiences that raise future generations as good stewards of our history and our natural environment.

## Conclusions

For Mr. Beech and his students, School of the Wild was novel in its week-long duration and in positioning learners as inquirers and teachers as facilitators  of these inquiries. Mr. Beech had been  asked to design discrete, yet engaging learning opportunities for sixth-grade students in the prairie ecosystem of a local state park. Ideally, he should be able to incorporate his students’ potenti al ad-hoc inquiries into the many puzzling stimuli that nature offers. Although he was personally fond of the outdoors, Mr. Beech was neither a sciences nor social studies specialist. His personal experiences in the park, artifacts (animal products and atl-atl), and some models and ideas the SOTW team had used in the teacher professional learning (TPL) workshop over the summer were his guides for designing learning activities. Mr. Beech’s knowledge about scientific concepts at work in the prairie—such as food webs—met the state and national science standards, and he had heard about links to math, arts, and literacy during the TPL. Yet, social studies connections, which are integral to productive interdisciplinary engagement. (PIE), were not explicitly foregrounded and seemed to occur only by chance. The summer TPL had focused on explorations about the ‘what?’ and ‘how?’ of science and neglected the ‘so what?’ of social studies. In Mr. Beech’s prairie day with a group of curious sixth-graders, we saw that SOTW has the potential for productive interdisciplinary engagement (PIE). We have shown the potential for students’ sensemakin g of complex processes via PIE  that uses both science and social studies  in nature-based learning. To harness these latent possibilities, teachers need to be positioned as active facilitators of PIE. Developing the above episodes into showcases for social studies learning as an integral part of PIE  can help the TPL program position teachers as promoters of  students’ natural curiosity. The natural world does  not differentiate between science and social studies. In supporting students’ explorations during SOTW,  we shouldn’t either: A wareness and critical appreciation for the scientific and social processes students examine in nature are organically linked.
