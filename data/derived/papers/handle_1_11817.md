# Culturally Sustaining Computing Education for Tibetan Diaspora Youth: Engaging Heritage Identity Through Computing

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study integrates sociocultural dimensions into computing education to support identity preservation and engagement among Tibetandiaspora youth in Queens, New York. Conducted during a 5-week summer program in 2024, the research investigated culturally sustaining pedagogies that integrate technical learning and cultural heritage. Students engaged in collaborative, project-based learning incorporating Tibetan sociocultural and political values into computing practices. Findings highlight six key themes, including pride in accomplishments, personal agency, and cultural resilience, showing how computing education can affirm cultural identity while fostering technical skills. Such integration is valuable to meaningful computing education as well as engaging and preserving their heritage identity. The study underscores the transformative potential of culturally aligned computing education to empower marginalized communities, offering a model for designing equitable, identity-affirming STEM programs that address community-specific concerns.

## Introduction

It is now well-established that science, technology, engineering, and mathematics (STEM) are deeply influenced by sociocultural and political factors that shape i ndividual identities. Optimal STEM learning occurs when students’ STEM identities connect mean ingfully with their everyday life identities (Jegede & Aikenhead, 1999). However, research shows that these everyday identit ies are often excluded from STEM classrooms, leadin g to a loss of interest, disengagement, and lower academic  success in STEM topics (Banks, 2015; Lee, 2017). This disconnect can result in feelings of alienation and  pressure on students to choose between adopting a STEM identity or maintaining the identities rooted in th eir personal and cultural lives (Carlone & Johnson,  2007; Rodriguez et al., 2019).

This challenge is particularly prevalent in computi ng education, where the curriculum often prioritizes technical skills like coding without exploring thei r connections to students' lived experiences (Ryoo,  2019). For instance, many computing education programs emphasi ze technical skills but neglect their relevance to students' sociocultural contexts (Bellino et al., 2021). For marginalized groups such as diaspora communit ies, this lack of sociocultural grounding exacerbates barriers, not only in accessing computing education but also in experiencing it in ways that affirm their identities and engage their sociopolitical re alities (Eglash et al., 2013; Margolis & Goode, 2016; Vakil, 2020). Among Tibetan youth in Q ueens, New York, for example, computing education i s valued as a pathway to economic success. Yet, the d isconnect between computing and their cultural prac tices poses risks of cultural erosion, leaving these youth unable to see how computing can serve as a tool for preserving and advocating their heritage  (MacPherson, 2018). Compounding this issue, computi ng has often been wielded as a tool of oppression against the Tibetan communi ty through surveillance, censorship, and propaganda. However, computing also holds transformative potent ial as a medium for resistance, advocacy, and cultu ral preservation.

In the summer of 2024, the first author designed and led a computing summer program at a weekend cultural school for the Tibetan community in Queens, New York City. This month-long program engaged students in a novel computing program designed for culturally sustaining learning where it leverages computing to preserve cultural identity.  This month-long program engaged students in a novel computing curriculum that extended the principles of culturally sustaining pedagogy (CSP) by Paris (2012)  using computing as a medium for cultural affirmation, preservation, and advocacy to empower diaspora youth to sustain and preserve their cultural identities in the face of external pressures. The primary research questions for this study are: RQ1: How does a culturally sustaining approach to computing education empower Tibetandiaspora youth to integrate technical practices into their cultural identity in meaningful ways? RQ2: What instructional, social, or technological stra tegies facilitate the integration of computing  education into their cultural identity preservation and community advocacy for Tibetandiaspora youth? This research highlights the transformative potential of a computing education that integrates community values, concerns and cultural practices into the process of  learning computing skills. Through project-based learning, collaborative practices, and an emphasis on cultural identity, the program fosters personal agency, strengthens cultural resilience, and connects  computing to students’ lived experiences. By addres sing RQ1, the study explores how culturally sustaining approaches  to computing learning enable and empower students to integrate technical practices into their cultural i dentities in meaningful ways. Additionally, RQ2 gui des the examination of the specific instructional, social, and technological strategies that make this integra tion possible. These insights position computing education not only  as a means of technical skill development but also  as a pathway to framing their narratives, resisting cultural erosio n, and fostering sociopolitical empowerment. This study offers a model for empowering marginalized communities to leverage computing in w ays that affirm their identities and address community concerns.

## Background and theoretical framework

Youth in the Tibetandiaspora Diaspora refers to the dispersion of people from their homeland to multipl e locations, often due to migration, exile, or displacement (Cohen, 2008). Like many diaspora communities, the Tibetandiaspora in New York faces complex challenges in preserving heritage cultural identity within a highly assimilatory environment. Displaced due to political conflict and the ongoing occupatio n of Tibet, the broader Tibetan community prioritizes cultural preservation through language, traditions, and values (Choedup, 2015) for resilience, resistance and sustainability of their identity. Community initiatives, such as weekend cultural schools and summer programs, are vital spaces for transmitting heritage. However, these efforts o ften struggle to engage youth, whose lives are shap ed by dominant host country environments, particularly in  urban settings like Queens, New York. As younger generations experience this disconnect, they may feel alienated from traditional practices, risking cultural erosion (MacPherson, 2018).

While the broader community views cultural preservation as essential for resilience and resistance, youth priorities often reflect their present-day realities, creating a gap between collective as pirations and individual experiences. This dynamic tension underscores the need for educational programs that bridge generational divides, making cultural heritage relevant and meaningful fo r the next generation. Framing computing education as both a technical and cultural prospect can support students’ long-term resilience in preserving their heritage. Research indicates that culturally relevant learning environments foster resilience by validating students’ cultural identities, which supports both emotional well-being and engagement (Nasir & Hand, 2006; Nasir & Vakil, 2017). Social and cultural identity Social Identity Theory (SIT) and Cultural Identity Theory (CIT) provide essential frameworks for understanding identity formation in diaspora communities. SIT posits that individuals derive a positive self-concept from group membership, which is reinforced through shared prac tices, values, and experiences  (Tajfel, 1979). For Tibetan youth, communal efforts such as cultural education and language preservation are the only spaces that foster a sense of belonging and connection to their heritage.

Similarly, CIT emphasizes that cultural identity is  actively constructed through engagement with sociocultural practices, particularly as individual s respond to the pressures of host environments (Collier & Thomas, 1988). Building on these theories, Bamberg et al. (2021)  highlight the narrative nature of identity construction, emphasizing that identity is shaped through storytelling and shared practices that connect individuals to their communities. For Tibetan youth, identity f ormation involves navigating the narratives of their ancestral homeland alongside the realities of their host coun try, creating opportunities for education to serve as a site of cultural affirmation and preservation.

In the context of a rapidly digitizing world, compu ting education has become increasingly relevant to identity formation. As communication s, organizations, and work processes are now deeply embedded in dig ital technologies, social and cultural identities are in creasingly shaped by this digital landscape. For ma rginalized communities like the Tibetandiaspora, engaging with computing education is critical to engaging and preserving heritage identities. Digital tools are apt and enable cultural preservation, advocacy and resistance in ways that were previously inaccessible (Eglash et al., 2013; Vakil, 2020). Pedagogies of computing that support heritage identity Educational frameworks like Culturally Relevant Ped agogy (CR P) and CSP offer critical approaches to integrating cultural identity into computing educat ion. CRP advocates for validating students’ cultura l identities and connecting learning to their lived experiences (Ladson-Billings, 1995). Building on CRP, CSP emphasizes the need to sustain and defend cultural practices, empowering marginalized communities to resist systemic erasure and create their own computing educational approaches most meaningful to them (Paris, 2012). These pedagogical approaches align with the challen ges faced by Tibetan youth, for whom computing education can serve as a tool for both technical skill development and cultural preservation. Research in culturally relevant and sustaining education has shown the val ue of embedding learning in students’ cultural and social contexts to foster engagement and identity affirmat ion (Ryoo, 2019; Banks, 2015; Lee, 2017). For examp le, computational projects that integrate Tibetan cultural practices, address community-specific concerns or advocate for cultural preservation align with CSP’s focus on  empowering marginalized communities to resist cult ural erasure and advocate for self-determination against political oppression such as surveillance, censorship, etc. met through computing practices by the Chinese government (Vakil, 2020). Furthermore, as scholars have highlighted the importance of counter-narratives in computing education to challenge dominant paradigms that alienate marginalized groups (Eglash et al., 2013; Margolis & Goode, 2016; Vakil, 2014), for the Tibetan youth, integrating cu ltural values into computing education can help cre ate a personally relevant narrative to their experience that not only enhances engagement through participation but also empowers their role as cultural agents. The Tibetan government-in-exile implemented a dual education policy in their refugee schools in India to ensure cultural p reservation alongside modern education, an approach  that illustrates the community’s commitment to cultural survival amid external pressures (Basic Education Policy for Tibetans in Exile, 2001) and computing education offers possibilities for a more meaningful way to achieve this goal in today’s digitally advanced world. It is therefor e ideal for both computing and community perspectiv es when computing education and cultural preservation goals complement one another. Drawing on SIT and CIT, this study explores how educational programs can affirm social and cultural identities for Tibetan youth while addressing the c hallenges of navigating dual cultural contexts. Gro unded in CSP it highlights the potential of computing education to empower youth as active agents of cultural preservation and advocacy.

## Participants and research design

We conducted a five-week computing summer program for 14 Tibetan youth (ages 12-18) at a community center in Queens, New York. 4 males and 7 females, including two student assistants (ages 17 and 18) c ompleted the program, most attending New York public schools and  all speak Tibetan at home, but with varying levels  of proficiency. There was a mix of participants who we re either born in the US or moved here with their parents. The participant who most recently arrived in the US is a 12-year-old participant who arrived just a year ago from India. Participants, recruited via their weekend cu ltural school, joined either by personal interest o r parental encouragement, with only one reporting prior coding experience. The program, designed to integrate computing skills with cultural identity, ran two hours each weekday in July 2024. In the first two weeks, the primary i nstructor (first author and community member) taugh t basic programming concepts using Scratch, with projects i mplementing computational concepts such as iteratio n, sequences, abstraction, conditionals, etc. Six gues t speakers addressed themes like Love, Compassion, Preservation of Tibetan Culture, Universal Responsi bility and Non-violence from Dalai Lama’s teachings, Identity and Community Needs. This was designed to encourage students to explore how computing could support core community concerns and ground their computing learning in such efforts. In the final three weeks, students collaborated to create their final projects on Scratch. They also learned how to develop basic websites using HTML, JavaScrip t, and CSS, which was used for publishing their pro jects for the demo day. They were encouraged to integrate insights from guest discussions and family reflections. These projects integrated their cultural values into their technical approaches, culminating in a final demonstration where students showcased their work to parents. This approach highlighted the program’s goal of fostering both technical expertise and cultural advocacy, aligning computing education with the preservation of Tibetan identity.

## Data collection and analysis

As part of the research work, the first author inst ructor and two assistant students created their dai ly observation notes and collected daily exit notes, pre-and post-surveys, and interviews from 11 consenting particip ants. The two student assistants conducted interviews of participants by scheduling individual best times 30 minutes before or after the regular program hours during the third and fourth weeks. Although participants were offered a choice of language for the interviews, all preferred to speak in English. Interviews ranged between 6-13 minutes, which were manually transcribed by the two assistant students and were later reviewed by the first author. The exit notes and surveys were collected in English language through Google Forms at the end of program sessions. The first author conducted a reflexive thematic ana lysis following Braun & Clarke's (2012)  approach. This process began with a thorough familiarization phase, in which the data set was read in full four times to gain a deep understanding of the content. Initial open c oding was applied, allowing codes to emerge organic ally from the data. Throughout several iterations, these codes were revisited and refined, with emergent themes identified based on their relevance to the research questions.  This iterative process involved feedback and criti cal insights from co-authors that led to six key themes, each defined and named to reflect the most significant representations in the data.

RQ1: How does a culturally sustaining approach to computing education empower Tibetandiaspora youth to integrate technical practices into their cultural identity in meaningful ways?

The program empowered Tibetandiaspora youth to leverage technical practices in engaging their cultural identity by fostering pride in accomplishments, personal agency, and cultural resilience. Pride in Accomplishments The program enabled students to take pride in using  computing to authentically represent their cultura l heritage. Early activities such as drawing Tibetan symbols on  Scratch evolved into more complex projects that de eply integrated Tibetan cultural values.  This progression not only reflected technical skill  development but also reaffirmed students’ connection to their heritage.

By combining cultural exploration with computing, s tudents re-engaged with their social and cultural identities in ways they described as unique and impactful. For instance, an interactive story project about a Tibetan girl travel ling to Lhasa to explore traditional fashion allowed  participants to merge technical creativity with cultural narratives. One student reflected, “I liked how I can make a story and use Tibetandialogue with my own abilities,” underscoring how the program va lidated their heritage and challenged deficit-based views of non-dominant cultural practices.

These experiences highlight the transformative potential of culturally sustaining computing education in affirming cultural identity. By enabling participan ts to redefine meaningful narratives about their cu lture, the program empowered them to see their technical accomplishments not only as individual achievements but also as acts of cultural preservation and advocacy.

Personal Agency The program enabled participants to take ownership of their projects, viewing computing as a tool for cultural advocacy. One group designed a Dance Dance Revolution-inspired game based on Tibetandance traditions, with students explaining, "This interactive game allows for engagement with Tibetan arts like music and dance.". This sense of agency transformed students into active contributors, demonstrating how culturally sustaining computing education can empower marginalized youth to express their identities while addressing community concerns like preservation of the heritage culture among the next generation. Computing for Cultural Resilience Projects such as a game critiquing exploitative Chi nese mining practices in Tibet  that disrupts the indigenous livelihood of the native Tibetans  illustrated how participants used computing to resi st cultural erasure and advocate for sociopolitical change. One student explained, "It shows the things the CCP is hiding from the outside world." These acts of cultural resilience underscor ed how computing education not only preserved but a ctively empowered students to assert their Tibetan identity oppressed by sociopolitical measures like censorship. RQ2: What instructional, social, or technological strategies facilitate the integration of computing education into cultural identity preservation and community advocacy for Tibetandiaspora youth?

The program’s success in aligning computing education with cultural preservation and advocacy was underpinned by strategies that emphasized collaborative learning, fun and socialization, and challenging yet authentic learning experiences.

Collaborative Learning Group-based projects fostered a shared sense of purpose and cultural connection. Inspired by a guest lecture from a Tibetan Buddhist monk, one group incorporated lesso ns on love and compassion into their project. As on e participant shared, the goal was “to preach the tea chings and religious beliefs we as Tibetan Buddhist s hold.” These collaborations strengthened both te chnical skills and cultural identity, with students  reflecting, "I liked working with our classmates and brainstorming ideas." Fun and Socialization The program’s engaging and enjoyable environment encouraged participants to connect, fostering both creativity and friendships. Many participants described the ex perience as fun and enriching, highlighting moments  of teamwork and camaraderie. For instance,  one participant shared, "The presentation was prett y fun and finding inspiration from other games on Scratch," reflecting the joy of showcasing their projects and learning from peers. Participants frequently mentioned fun in collaborat ing with classmates, with some noting that they formed friendships during the program. The sense of  fun in working together not only made computing fe el less intimidating but also created a supportive communit y where students felt comfortable exploring new ide as. This combination of social bonding and creative engageme nt made the learning experience both meaningful and memorable.

Challenging and Authentic Learning Experiences Students appreciated the program’s hands-on approach, which connected technical learning to real-world cultural contexts. A group designing a game to teach basic T ibetan grammar described it as "a way to playfully learn Tibetan," blending creativity with cultural relevance. As one participant remarked, "Here we learn how to actually make something, not just play games." This challeng ing yet rewarding approach deepened students’ understanding of how computing could serve as a mea ningful tool for both technical growth and cultural advocacy.

## Discussion

This study explored how culturally sustaining approaches in computing education can support identity preservation, engagement, and advocacy among Tibeta n diaspora youth. Guided by two key research questi ons, it examined how such approaches align with stude nts’ cultural identities and the mechanisms that en able this integration.

Computing learning and identity preservation The findings revealed that culturally sustaining ap proaches enabled students to acquire technical skills by engaging their heritage cultural practices, producing computational artefacts authentic to both computing and their Tibetan identity. For instance, participants created artefacts such as interactive storytelling pieces associated with their aspirations about visiting Tibet passed on fr om their parents, language-learning games  that echo their experiences at the weekend cultural school, etc.  show how students adopted computing to engage their cultural practices and community aspirations. This aligns with CIT, which highlights the role of sociocultural practices in affirming identity. By embedding Tibetan cultural n arratives, community aspirations  or concerns into their projects, students actively participated in learnin g their heritage, illustrating CIT’s role in guidin g culturally sustaining education. For instance, the group that created the Tibetan language learning game has noted that they extensively researched grammar rules that are taught at their weekend cultural school to make sure they achieved the desired final project. Such engagement of their  cultural aspects was visible across groups, and it is certainly encouraging for the community to witness such proac tive engagement from their children, through whom t hey hope to preserve their language, culture, and sociopolitical aspirations in a diverse country like the US. Additionally, SIT highlights how group membership a nd shared goals contribute to identity development. The collaborative nature of the program fostered a sense of belonging, with participants reinforcing their identity as members of both the Tibetandiaspora and a computing learning community. This dual belonging demonstrates the transformative potential of comput ing programs that integrate cultural and technical meaningfulness, aligning with CST (Paris, 2012). Th e themes of sense of pride and cultural resilience expressed by participants highlights the potential of computing education to empower students as cultural representatives in a meaningful way that the broader community strives  to allow for the next generation to preserve.  For instance, students sought inspiration from the dominant socie ty they are part of to express their Tibetaness eit her by extending their traditional dance programs that are  occasionally held or adopting a concept of a popular puzzle game to highlight the dire human rights situation i nside Tibet. While they received motivation from co mmunity members who shared their firsthand experiences of Tibetan collective goals, students d eveloped solidarity and membership in the classroom when their goals and values they value were shared by all the students. While such culturally sustaining approach has added to the con versations of how to increase engagement for such g roups of students with computing (Ryoo, 2019), it is also leveraging such interdisciplinary approach to present meaningful learning opportunities that empower such communities, which is what our first research question explores. Supporting cultural identity in computing education The findings described in the form of six themes ca n be summed into the following strategies that facilitated the integration of cultural identity into computing education:

• Instructional Support: Themes such as fun, socialization, and challenge made computing learning

meaningful and engaging. Researchers (eg., Eglash et al., 2021; Margolis et al., 2012; Shaw et al., 2023) highlight that marginalized students often do not s ee their values reflected in computing curricula. T his program stands out by enabling participants to approach computing learning as a tool for their cultural engagement. This aligns with CRT (Ladson-Billings, 1995), which emphasizes validating students’ lived experiences, and it can be a meaningful extension o f the practical adoption of pedagogical support for such diverse learning groups. This could mean vario us instructional practices, specifically, finding role models in instructors such as the first author, who is a community insider, and community engagement such as local leaders, parents etc., into the classroom to help them explore their concerns more confidently. This also indicates how students need to be challen ged with critical approach es to the learning of disciplinary content while at the same time ensurin g the process is appealing and the space is safe.

• Collaborative Learning: Group-based projects reinforced social bonds and a sense of shared purpose as

shown above, with the theme of collaborative learning. In addition to that, participants showed a deepened sense of responsibility toward their commu nity that motivated them to effectively brainstorm towards meaningful projects oriented at solving community problems, as shown in the theme of personal agency. These findings underscore the value of creating an enjoyable, interactive learning environment. For instance, students expressed having sought moti vation from each other while there were concerns about performance from every group member. Some members suggested they learned how to be patient, while others showed strong appreciation towards their partners.  The program’s culturally meaningful and community-driven nature encouraged engagement and strengthene d solidarity among participants, supporting a positive identity (Tajfel, 1979).

• Advocacy and Empowerment: Students saw their projects as opportunities to i nnovate and take

ownership of their learning as reflected in themes such as pride in accomplishments and computing for cultural resilience. This highlights the program’s challenging and purposeful design, which empowered students to advocate for meaningful concerns. Such adoption of computing education counters the sense of alienation and disconnect with the disciplines a mong marginalized students as discussed in the introduction. Parents, during the program’s final demo, expressed excitement about the potential for their children to support community organizations, includ ing political campaigns and cultural programs, through their acquired computing skills. This resonates with Margolis et al.'s (2012) concept of students as “doers” within their communities that could empo wer the community at large against oppressive agents, especially digitally oppressive measures ca rried out by countries like China against the Tibet andiaspora.

Computing education for diaspora communities The findings highlight computing education’s potent ial to meaningfully support diaspora communities by integrating cultural identity into technical learni ng. This offers a scalable insight into adapting culturally sustaining computing education to other diaspora communities. For example, Indigenous communities ca n implement meaningful computing programs that integr ate their unique cultural narratives, such as linguistic practices, everyday cultural activities like rituals or even critical sociopolitical issues like environmental activism into computing projects. Our findings align with Kafai & Proctor (2022)  and Ryoo et al. (2020)  who call for equity-focused practices in computing education that refle ct diverse cultural contexts. It also asserts that such programs could move beyond issues of equity and est ablish authentic ways computing practices that empo wer their ways of sustaining their unique cultural identities in today’s world. In other diaspora communities, such as the Vietname se and Irish, where culturally contextual programs support identity preservation (Bankston III & Zhou, 1997; Coogan, 2002) and are meaningful to their aspirations, it is also important to consider the sustainability of their approaches. This extends prior research by Ryoo (2019), who demonstrates the power of sociocultural contexts in computing education to help students navigate and affirm their identity across cultural worlds without alien ating them, thus ensuring sustainability through authentic ways of doing computing education. This also aligns with  Shaw et al.’s (2023) findings, which show how cult urally resonant, womanist storytelling in computing education helps marginalized students redefine dominant narratives, using computing to affirm and express their identit ies, helping challenge deficit notions that underva lue nondominant cultural practices for such communities.

## Conclusion

The program’s engaging and interactive nature shows  that culturally meaningful education fosters a pos itive learning environment. Research supports the value o f culturally resonant, inquiry-based education in engaging marginalized students, suggesting that  blending social, cultural, and academic elements ca n promote both social and educational engagement (Vakil, 2014). For Tibet an youth, learning computing in a community-driven, culturally meaningful space fostered not only personal enjoyment but also a shared sense of solidarity and identity, contributing to their resilience. These experiences  align with Freire's (2020) a call for critical consciousness in education for a transformative outcome, enabling students to connect their learning to broader social and cultural advocacy, especially in the wake of the unique challenges and needs of such diasporic communities. While the program’s outcomes were promising, its sm all sample size and context-specific nature pose challenges for generalization. Future research shou ld explore the long-term impacts of such programs, including how to integrate them into formal and informal spaces of education. Expanding the study to include other diaspora or indigenous communities would also provide valuable comparative insights. However, this study highlights the transformative p otential of culturally sustaining approaches in computing education, particularly for Tibetandiasp ora youth. By addressing the unique intersection of technical skill-building and cultural preservation, it demonstrates  how computing education can foster engagement, resilience, and advocacy. The findings offer insigh ts into designing equitable and culturally meaningf ul STEM programs, emphasizing the value of integrating cultural identity into technical disciplines.
