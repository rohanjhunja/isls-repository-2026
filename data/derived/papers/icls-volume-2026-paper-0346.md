# Artificial Intelligence and Human Purposes: Dialogues about Creativity and Control

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This interactive poster symposium brings together approaches to working with and studying AI that are not often explicitly connected (and are often placed in opposition to one another), those concerned with the human purposes of creativity and control. The contexts of our studies vary widely: formal education, afterschool or summer programs, community workshops, and professional performing arts. Learners, users, and scholars grapple with the ethics and challenges, as well as the potentials, of AI in their lives and in society. Putting these research projects in dialogue with one another will result in a session that fosters balance and creates a cautious yet hopeful conversation.

“The essence of technology,” argued Heidegger (1979), “is by no means anything technological…Technology is a way of revealing. It is a human endeavor.” In Heidegger’s philosophy, goals and designs for technology are grounded in deeply human purposes, and our reactions to technology often reveal more about our aspirations and fears as humans than they describe about technology itself. Nowhere is this observation more true  in modern discourse than in conversations about artificial intelligence (AI). Concerns about racism and other algorithmic biases evident in outputs from Gen AI platforms consistently lead back to the inherent racism and bias present in their human-created training data (Benjamin, 2019). Predictions and warnings about AI replacing jobs in the workforce are inextricable from human anxieties about security and occupational identity in a hyper-capitalist economy (Selenko, et al, 2022). Debate about copyright law for AI-generated artistic work uncovers deep divides 2262 in how we define or  value the human endeavors of creation, meaning, and imagination (Garcia, 2024).  As advances in AI continue, the more problematic aspects of our humanity  seem to be replicated and inescapable. Wherever we go, there we are.

And yet. There is also a deeply human ability to see past the explicit designs of tools, environments, and technologies and instead harness them for our own intentions. Gibson’s theory of affordances (1977) articulates how humans arrive at emergent uses  for objects based only partially on the apparent features of said objects and largely on the needs  and ideas brought to the interaction. Hawkins and Collins (1992) described unexpected appropriations of early educational technology designs, as teachers and students adapted the tools provided to them in mundane or subversive ways.  The creative and agentive human spirit, innovating and resisting, is inevitably finding its way to artificial intelligence (Lizárraga, 2023). And although these purposes may be less anxiety-provoking than those described above, they are no less worthy of attention. This interactive poster symposium brings together approaches to working with and studying AI that are not often in conversation with each other (and are often placed in opposition to one another), those concerned with the human purposes of creativity and control. Authors are engaged in research in a variety of settings: formal education, afterschool and summer programs, community workshops, and professional performing arts. The work is highly perspectival, forwarding the practices, meanings, agency, and emotions  of participants. Learners and users grapple with the ethics and challenges, as well as the potentials, of AI in their lives and in society. Putting these different approaches and concerns in dialogue, with a focus on human purposes, will result in a symposium experience that creates balance and fosters a cautious and simultaneously hopeful conversation. Posters Teens addressing ethical issues in the design of their own babyGPTs Luis Morales-Navarro, Daniel Noh, Yasmin B. Kafai While most current AI literacy research has focused on supporting teenagers as productive users/refusers of generative language model (GLM)-powered systems, far fewer efforts have focused on how to engage high school students as designers of these models to foster a better understanding of both the inner-workings of these systems and ethical issues that arise in the design of such systems. In this presentation, building on the rich legacy of positioning learners as designers of computing applications (Kafai, 2006; Oleson et al., 2020), we explore the ethical considerations that teens engaged with when designing AI applications, specifically very small-scale GLMs, which we call babyGPTs.

The design process of computing applications also involves grappling with ethical issues (Iivari et al., 2023). However, how to engage young people with ethics and criticality when building ML models remains understudied. In model-building efforts, ethics are often discussed in relation to commercial ML-powered applications instead of being applied to learner-designed models (Morales-Navarro and Kafai, 2024). Promising efforts integrate ethical considerations into model design activities using cards with qu estions about design decisions (Bilstrup et al., 2020), having students discuss potential misinformation generated by their projects (Lin et al., 2020), or creating ethical matrices for their classifiers (Jordan et al., 2021). More recently, researchers have identified distinct ethical issues for young people to consider when learning about AI/ML, including algorithmic biases, surveillance, privacy, transparency, identity representation, misinformation, environmental issues, ownership, labor issues, and overreliance on AI/ML systems (Veldhuis et al., 2025). We conducted a five-day summer workshop with 16 teenagers (ages 14-15) in a science center located in the Northeastern US, where they designed babyGPTs using the nanoGPT framework (Karpathy, 2024). Throughout the design process we prompted the teens to reflect on their models using cards with questions about design decisions (Bilstrup et al., 2020) and ethical matrices (Jordan et al., 2021). We analyzed their reflections and discussions using thematic analysis to identify distinct ways in which they address ed ethical and societal implications during the design process.

When designing their models, the teens considered ethical issues related to misinformation (trustworthiness of GLM-generated text), ownership (copyright, authorship, attribution), environmental impact (energy consumption), harm, algorithmic bias, and overreliance on AI. They addressed these issues by centering or making explicit their values, limiting the scope of their projects, curating datasets, and challenging each other's assumptions. Centering, or making values explicit, involved reflecting on their positionality as designers and the use cases they envisioned for their projects. By limiting the scope of the projects, the teens scaled down their ideas to create models that addressed specific tasks and specific use cases. Curating datasets included inten tionally deciding what data to use and reflecting on how dataset design decisions influenced outputs or could lead to 2263 harmful outcomes. Finally, they challenged each other's approaches by asking questions and probing their peers to consider any ethical and societal implications they might have overlooked. This work contributes evidence of how teenagers can engage with ethical considerations in the design of generative language models and outlines directions for future research to support young people’s engagement with ethics and societal implications in model design activities. Children, teachers, and robots network: A sociomaterial analysis of agency in early childhood classrooms X. Christine Wang, Grace (Yaxin) Xing, Karthik Dantu Interest in the relationship between humans and nonhuman entities is rapidly growing as intelligent agents (IAs) become increasingly embedded in children ’s daily lives (Yang, 2022; Chen et al., 2020). These agents are reshaping children’s social interactions (Kewalramani et al., 2021) and transforming learning by fostering engagement and adapting to individual needs (Uluer et al., 2023). Unlike passive tools, IAs function as active participants, challenging traditional notions of agency and prompting a reexamination of how agency is co-constructed through child-teacher-IA interactions in classrooms. This raises a critical question: how do children, teachers, and IAs collaboratively enact agency in educational settings? This study was part of a pilot program introducing young children to IAs at a university-affiliated preschool in the United States during the Fall 2023 semester. The focus for this paper is Astro (Figure 1a), an Amazon conversation agent on wheels, whose capabilities include taking photos, playing music, and following instructions. We selected a subset of data comprised of six sessions for analysis using thematic coding to identify recurring patterns. Our analytic framing draws on Actor-Network-Theory (ANT) and sociomaterial perspectives (Latour, 2005; Fenwick & Edwards, 2011). These perspectives conceptualize classrooms not as static spaces where predefined actors operate, but as dynamic networks in which humans and nonhumans act upon and reshape one another. In our study, Astro’s mobility, the classroom layout, and the positioning of spaces shaped how children could join, resist, or expand participation.

Our findings revealed three main themes: (1) Reconfiguring boundaries.  Teachers sought to structure participation by spatially separating children from Astro (Figure 1b). Yet Astro’s mobility and children’s playful responses destabilized these arrangements (Figure 1c). Such actions reflected not resistance alone but the purs uit of learning goals that redefined the boundaries of participation. (2) Astro as both constraint and possibility. Astro sometimes reinforced order—for instance, when its failure to respond necessitated teacher intervention. At other moments, the robot disrupted expectations, such as when a request for a “kid’s song” produced Beethoven’s Für Elise. This unanticipated action generated new interactional possibilities: some children danced, others listened attentively, and teachers adapted in surprise. (3) Negotiating schooling and learning goals.  Teachers’ explicit schooling goals emphasized curricular comparisons (e.g., likening Astro to household devices). Children, in contrast, pursued their own learning goals, improvising new commands when Astro misinterpreted. These episodes demonstrate children’s agentive capacity to adapt within constraints, echoing strategies observed in earlier work where children circumvented timers and waiting lists to sustain play (Wang & Ching, 2003). Our findings position classrooms with IAs as sociomaterial  networks in which humans and nonhumans co-produce participation. Teacher-imposed rules and robots’ programmed behaviors stabilized practices, while children’s improvisations and Astro’s unpredictability destabilized them, creating new trajectories for act ion. Importantly, schooling and learning goals should not be conceptualized as opposing forces but as co-produced in practice through these shifting associations. For educators, this perspective highlights the importance of designing 2264 robot-mediated activities that recognize children’s capacity to appropriate and reconfigure constraints and that embrace the productive possibilities of disruption. Human in the loop: Rhetorics of responsibility in AI-involved online exam proctoring Cynthia Carter Ching Online exam proctoring systems (OEP) harness students’ computers and webcams for the purpose of  detecting and preventing academic dishonesty. During the COVID-19 pandemic, when the use of OEP exploded in higher education, concerns arose about student privacy, human proctor bias, and the capacity of OEP systems for monitoring individual exams on a massive scale (Archer, 2023; Conijn, et al, 2022). Since then, the prevalence of online courses and exams has increased significantly over pre-COVID rates, so many current OEP platforms have introduced artificial intelligence to address  these concerns (Middleton, 2022; Nicola-Richmond, et al, 2024). Many prominent commercial platforms declare that they are not fully automated, however, and that humans still have a role in the process. How they rhetoricize which roles humans  and AI have reveals a great deal about what problems they think they are solving and the human purposes, positive and negative, that drive design decisions. This poster presents an analysis of six commercial OEP platforms, examining their promotional materials and directions to test-takers for how they describe the roles of AI and humans in their proctoring process. Drawing 2265 on Latour’s actor-network theory and his concept of human-nonhuman assemblage (2005), I mapped out the basic flow of actions common across platforms, regardless of whether a human or nonhuman actor is engaged and at which point, with splits in process depending on whether a “suspicious behavior” is flagged, and whether intervention happens in real time (see fig 2). All platforms included some form of launch phase where a student’s identity and testing environment are cleared to begin the exam, an observation phase monitoring student behavior on-camera and on their computer, a problem phase where non-compliant instances are flagged and addressed (if there are any), and then a concluding evaluation of the soundness of the student’s whole exam. Figure 2 AI-Involved Proctoring Process Informed by a human-in-the-loop framework (Knowles, 2024; Wu et al, 2022), for each platform I then placed human s or AI at different action points in the network, depending on how their roles were described. Platforms had varying levels of AI involvement, from automation of nearly the entire process to AI being only responsible for identity biometrics and/or observation and flagging video. The action of flagging, however, was highly rhetoricized across platforms. To quote one, “AI does not decide if you’ve done anything wrong” but only “identifies suspicious behavior.” (There was no acknowledgment of potential biases i n determining suspicion.) Building on a previous study of OEP during the pandemic (Ching et al, 2024), I examined each network for student perspective, looking at how visible or invisible AI and human actors could be to test-takers, depending on their launch and flagging experiences. Many platforms reassure  students about the privacy protections of using AI for live or recorded observation, and they propose AI as a solution to the discomfort test-takers may feel with continuous surveillance. One even declared, “Zero humans. Total security.” Yet privacy or anxiety concerns are not acknowledged in describing how, in many systems, a human or AI actor may interrupt or begin observing a test-taker who has been flagged.

In sum, OEP platforms, even within the same system let alone across systems,  forward conflicting arguments as to whether human actors are problematic and create student discomfort or inequity, or whether only humans can be trusted to make subjective decisions about cheating and its consequences. Platforms also position AI in conflicting ways, describing its function as both student protection and student detection, depending on the “honest” or “suspicious” nature of test-taking behavior. These findings are important to the effort of unpacking the ideologies built into the human-nonhuman networks of AI-involved proctoring, as well as  to the goal of designing more equitable and humane proctoring systems, regardless of how they are assembled. Youth and AI: What young women think about generative AI Jean Ryoo, Michelle Choi, Ryan Clarke, Jacqualyn Blizzard-Caron, Wei Wei, Liza Stark, Lillian Kohn, Daniel Voloch, Joseph Ochterski While Generative AI (GenAI) becomes a global obsession-incorporated into our daily lives through news media, social media, healthcare, etc. - young people may be the most impacted in their current schooling and future careers. Yet, what do youth think? And how can their perspectives inform K12 education in an ever-evolving techno-political context? To answer these questions, this study explores the intersection between young peoples’ ideas and the EnCITE Framework describing why we must teach “about, with, through, and against computing and digital technologies” (Vogel et al., 2024). While this framework focuses on pre-service teachers, we find that putting it in conversation with student perspectives can offer the Learning Sciences valuable reflections on  how to approach teaching and learning with GenAI. More specifically, the EnCITE Framework describes the need for learners to: 1) have conversations about technology to support understanding and decision making, 2) engage with computing technology as tools to deepen learning across subject areas, 3) learn through computing to create and express oneself, and 4) acquire critical thinking skills for choosing when and if to position oneself against computing technologies (Vogel et al., 2024).

Through a research-practice partnership examining student perspectives and experiences in a free, online, 6-week “Girls Who Code” summer program about AI for 9th-12th graders, this paper explores the following research questions: How are adolescents making  sense of GenAI and its impacts on their identities, futures, and actions? What ideas do youth have for using GenAI? And what are they excited and/or concerned about when 2266 imagining GenAI’s potential? Our inquiry is rooted in cultural historical activity theory (CHAT), recognizing that human thought and action are shaped (and shape) a collective activity system mediated by tools, signs, and language that are part of a cultural and historical context organized by rules and divisions of labor that are inherently social and diverse (Engeström, 1987). Furthermore, multiple perspectives that come together as networks of interacting activity systems collide create space for new ideas, illuminating tensions, and expanding possibilities within and across systems because of their open and historical nature (Engeström, 2001). Y oung peoples’ views and engagements with GenAI in this study are analyzed through a CHAT lens, paying attention to the sociohistorical and political contexts of the students, their engagements with technological tools within the social fabric of their school, summer program, and home lives, and how these tools mediate (as well as create contradictions) in how the students make sense of their life trajectories with computing. This qualitative study’s data sources include 15 program observations, interviews with 25 high school girls-all of whom had prior experience with computer science or AI-as well as 320 student projects focused on students’ visions for the potential uses, challenges, and ethical implications of AI in the future. Results reveal that while youth are generally optimistic about GenAI and its impacts, they also have a keen awareness about its destructive aspects related to racial and gender bias, potential neg ative influence on human creativity, empathy, and critical thinking, and broader social and environmental implications. Young people are concerned about the ethical and social impacts of GenAI, and want their educational experiences with and about AI to center these issues in both design and use processes. Ultimately, the majority of young people in this study envision using GenAI to solve meaningful problems in their community and the world, and they want to be taught how to engage with GenAI in productive  and responsible ways. This study’s results offer important considerations for how to shape educational contexts that center youth interests and concerns in a rapidly shifting AI-influenced learning landscape.

Educators’ pedagogical worldmaking and ideological world-shifting: Co-designing new practices and purposes in sociotechnical learning ecologies Arturo Cortez There are various approaches undergirding the design of teacher learning ecologies for today’s rapidly changing technological context (Koehler & Mishra, 2007; Stornaiuolo, et al., 2023). Oftentimes, educators are either positioned as adopters or adapters of new technologies; the former positions educational technology as complete in its design and ready to be launched in a learning environment, and the latter highlights educators’ agency in transforming technological tools in coordination with their local context. L ess attention is given to approaches that help teachers examine the technological infrastructures that technologies might advance, interrupt, or re-imagine. These critical orientations might help teachers facilitate youth in becoming philosophizers of technology (Vakil & McKinney de Royston, 2022), develop critical race algorithmic literacies (Tanksley 2024), or even become transformative worldmakers (Cortez et al., 2022, 2024; Rivero & Cortez, 2025). This study explores how educators learn to develop new values and pedagogical practices around the use of everyday technologies, within the context of an after-school video gaming lab. In particular, it explores how educators leveraged generative AI tools to collaboratively create, drive, and augment storylines about sociotechnical worlds within a long-form roleplaying campaign within the videogame, Grand Theft Auto V, fostering the development of what I call pedagogical worldmaking and ideological worldshifting.

In the social-design experiment of the after-school program, educators are creating alternative realities, social arrangements, and epistemic possibilities through their pedagogical practices, which we call worldmaking. Goodman (1978) notes the process of worldmaking “always starts from worlds already on hand; the making is the remaking” (p. 6). Drawing on Goodman’s (1978) five processes of worldmaking (i.e., composition/decomposition, weighting, ordering, deletion/supplementation, deformation), this study highlights two intertwined processes: pedagogical worldmaking and ideological worldshifting. As such, this study examines how educators create new worlds that have new rules, practices, and values that not only hold importance to their makers, but also re-imagine pre-existing social orders toward more just and equitable ends. Drawing on a social design-based research methodology (Gutiérrez, 2008), the study explores how intergenerational participants integrated AI-generated text, imagery, and dialogue into a speculative world they were collectively building. Data sources includ e approximately 150 hours of video recordings of gameplay, AI-generated artifacts, and post-session interviews. Video sources were logged in two-and 10-minute segments on a variety of metadata (e.g., artificial intelligence, tool use, purposes, values) (E rickson, 2006). Analysis focuses on the pedagogical and relational dynamics that emerged when participants engaged in AI-assisted learning, revealing how the technology mediated role negotiation, fostered improvisation, and expanded the group’s imaginative horizons. Open-ended and thematic codes (Bogdan & Biklen, 2007; Dyson & Genishi, 2005) of these 2267 data were developed. Data were further reduced through analytic memos that helped identify salient themes across the social interactions.

Pedagogical worldmaking refers to the ways educators and youth jointly create new sociotechnical learning ecologies (i.e., designing new roles, tools, storylines, and participation structures) that reconfigure what counts as legitimate knowledge and practi ce. Ideological worldshifting describes the process that educators engage as they transform the values, purposes, and social relations of their pedagogical practices related to the use of everyday technological tools. Through iterative cycles of play, reflection, and redesign, educators leveraged AI as a creative partner that transformed narrative storylines, character development, and deepened critical understandings of sociotechnical systems.

The findings suggest that generative AI, when embedded in justice-oriented, play-based learning environments, can foster educators’ development of justice-oriented pedagogical practices through iterative cycles of collective reflection. Furthermore, educators were able to anchor their practices in larger purposes and values that aligns with expansive and robust examinations of technology. This study has design implications for how we support educators in developing robust explorations of sociotechnical syst ems. Humanistic approaches and student voice to guide AI literacy Shuchi Grover While recent studies have focused on students' conceptions of AI, few examine how students understand AI as a human endeavor shaped by creators with values and identities. This study analyz es questions middle and high school students would pose to AI experts, asking: (1) What questions would students ask AI scientists if given a chance? (2) Does a female AI scientist evoke different questions? (3) What do question patterns reveal about students' perceptions of AI and its creators?

This study draws on three perspectives: humanistic AI literacy, gender representation in STEM education, and student voice in inquiry-based learning. Humanistic approaches emphasize AI as a socio-technical construct shaped by humandecisions and values, expanding beyond technical content to include ethical reasoning and social impact (Grover, 2025). Gender representation research shows persistent stereotypes cast technology as masculine, alienating girls who don't see themselves reflected (Cheryan et al., 2017; Master et al, 2021; Young et al, 2013). Student-generated questions are foundational to inquiry-based education and reveal developing understandings (Chin & Osborne, 2008; Scardamalia & Bereiter, 1991), yet emerging domains like AI often center expert-defined content with little space for authentic student inquiries. This research analyzed open-ended survey responses from 283 students: 109 female students (3 in grades 3-5, 62 in grades 6-8, 44 in grades 9-12), 162 male students (8 in grades 3-5, 66 in grades 6-8, 88 in grades 9-12), and 12 who preferred not to say (9 middle, 3 high school). Data were collected in Spring 2025 from three urban school districts in the US. Students responded to two open-response questions: Q1. If you met a real-life AI expert who has created impactful technologies, what would you ask them about their life or about the AI technology? (There is no right or wrong answer) Q2. If the expert were a female AI scientist, would you want to ask her anything additional? If yes, what? Inductive thematic analysis identified patterns (Braun & Clarke, 2006), with two researchers independently coding 20% of responses (κ = 0.83). Multi-theme coding allowed questions to receive multiple assignments. Descriptive statistics reported theme frequ encies, and chi-square tests explored demographic differences.

Q1: Questions for AI experts: Analysis revealed five major themes: Career & Personal Journey (49.5%), AI Capabilities & Performance (44.2%), Future Impact & Ethics (39.9%), Personal Feelings & Motivations (38.9%), and Technical/How AI Works (15.5%). Students demonstrated sophistication, with 53% addressing multiple themes. Gender showed no significant association with thematic patterns, but grade level showed highly significant differences. Elementary students focused on personal feelings (63.6%), while high school students showed elevated concern for future implications (59.3% vs 22.6% in middle school) and AI capabilities (60.0% vs 28.5% in middle school). A notable subset (7.1%) asked highly complex questions touching four or five themes simultaneously.

Q2: Female AI scientist prompt:  53% of the students responded ‘no’ to having additional questions for a female AI scientist-of which 77% were males (p < 0.001). Additional questions (N=134) focused on four themes: Gender Experience/Bias (dominant theme), Career Path/Support/Challenges, AI-Technical/Societal, and Personal Feelings/Emotions.

This study reveals three key insights. First, students are deeply curious about people behind technology— their career paths, motivations, and daily lives—suggesting that a humanistic STEM framing may resonate with underrepresented students (Margolis et al., 1999). Second, concerns about societal impact and ethics were nearly as frequent as AI capabilities, indicating ethical and civic dimensions should be explored deeply in K-12 AI 2268 curricula. Third, representation's role is strikingly evident: while most male students didn't pose additional questions to female experts, nearly three-quarters of female students did, focusing on questions about gendered experiences. This supports literature emphasizing representation matters in STEM. Student-generated questions reveal not only what they want to know, but what they value and worry about, making them valuable for designing inclusive AI education that treats students as active meaning-makers rather than passive recipients of AI knowledge.

Being human in the age of generative AI: Young people’s ethical concerns about writing and living with machines Jennifer M. Higgs, Amy Stornaiuolo Amid the vigorous debate about generative AI’s impact on reading, writing, and communication (Humburg et al., 2025; Stornaiuolo et al., 2023), a crucial perspective is often overlooked: how young people themselves are experiencing these rapidly evolving cultural tools in their everyday lives and how they are making sense of the questions they raise about ethics, power, and social participation. This qualitative study centers the voices of high school students to explore their everyday experiences with AI-mediated writing. Drawing on cultural-historical perspectives, we ask: (a) How are young people using AI in their daily lives; (b)  W hat ethical and critical considerations inform their practices and sensemaking of these technologies? Our inquiry is designed around the premise that young people are “philosophers of technology” (Vakil & McKinney de Royston, 2019), which positions youth as agents who contemplate the ethical, critical, and aesthetic dimensions of technology and who can work toward social transformation by interrogating their everyday practices, including their literacy  practices (Gutiérrez et al., 2019). Data sources include surveys and focus groups from English language arts classes at two culturally and linguistically diverse high schools. The survey, completed by 131 students, asked about writing practices and perceptions of AI. Three focus groups were conducted to further explore ideas raised in the surveys. Collaborative data analysis involved two coding cycles to identify patterns related to students’ uses of AI, key considerations, and critical perspectives (Saldaña, 2016). We found that students’ use of generative AI was deliberate and thoughtful. Most students (91%) had used AI, and they did so for specific purposes, such as completing routine tasks, finding information faster, and serving as a catalyst for their thinking and writing processes. A significant portion (27%) engaged in “principled non-use,” choosing not to use AI because they preferred their work to be “original” and were “against plagiarism or cheating.” Students’ responses revealed deep engagement with philosophical questions about what it means to be human. They were concerned AI candiminish human creativity and authenticity. They often described AI-generated writing as “soulless” or “a lie,” while asso ciating “real” writing with emotion, effort, and originality. Students also raised ethical concerns, including data bias, plagiarism, and cheating. These anxieties were often tied to fears about the future, including AI’s impact on jobs and the possibility that “human” skills like writing might become obsolete. Despite their contemplation of these issues, students reported a lack of opportunity to discuss them in school, suggesting an educational gap.

This study counters monolithic constructions of youth as uncritical AI users, instead highlighting their nuanced sensemaking. Our findings suggest that young people value the creativity, emotions, and laborious meaning-making of writing and see AI as a threat to these elements. Our research contributes to understandings of how new technologies are reshaping literacy practices by offering concrete evidence of youths’ perspectives on the ethical dilemmas they face. It also underscores schools’ crucial role in  providing balanced, sustained learning opportunities that support students in developing a critical lens to navigate the complex sociotechnical systems they inhabit.

Algorithmic co-dreaming: Designing for speculative futures with AI José Ramón Lizárraga This poster highlights how learners and communities can examine their use of AI as not just a tool for efficiency, but one that can help with engagement of everyday dilemmas, contradictions, and hope and possibility. Automated systems (including AI) are increasingly deployed to sort and surveil communities, especially Black, Brown and Queer youth (Benjamin, 2019). This work revisits previous work on cyborg speculative world building (Lizárraga, 2023) by foregrounding how creativity, play, interdisciplinarity, and intergenerational collaboration with technology can lead to consequential learni ng (Hall & Jurow, 2015).

As demonstrated by the diverse collection of posters in this session, part of the challenge of coherence in any scholarly dialogue about AI is that it can be designed and deployed for many diverging purposes. Several authors in this session describe learners engaging with AI for creative and agentive action (Wang et al; Cortez) or even users designing their own AI bots for specific interests or needs (Lizárraga; Morales-Navarro et al). Other authors investigate users’ and learners’ experiences or concerns about ethical uses of AI and who is behind AI designs (Higgs et al; Grover; Ryoo et al; Ching; Vakil et al). Across all the projects, however, there is a deep awareness of both the potentials and pitfalls of AI, and an assertion that part of the goal of research and education is to center the perspectives of users and learners, rather than claiming one purpose  or application of AI over another. For a field like Learning Sciences, which has long been focused on the importance of design and designers, and on sorting technologies into constructive or instructive types, this is perhaps a challenging shift. We are drawn to typologies, but they can be constraining. Foucault (1988) outlines a distinction in analytical focus over his own career between “technologies of domination” (e.g., policing, surveillance, the panopticon) and “technologies of the self” (e.g., personal writing, meditation, artistic practice). At a surface level, this distinction seems to map neatly onto a n AI dichotomy between control and creativity. Yet Foucault argues that a given system may function as either or both. Introspective practices like journaling and meditation may be used for self-care or self-castigation. Mental health systems can serve to support or suppress emotional expression and empowerment. It is perhaps thus useful to think of AI in a similar way, and to develop AI literac ies and AI research agendas that engage the ethical and the agentive. We should always be in dialogue with the combination, rather than the opposition, of creativity and control.

Session overview This will be an interactive poster symposium, conducted according to the established format for this session type. Opening remarks by the session chair (Ching) will be broad and brief, followed by short “pitches” for each poster to orient the audience to the breadth of projects, and leaving most of the time for the audience and authors to circulate among the posters at stations around the sides of the room. There are nine separate projects in this session, and we will split the poster visitation time in half, with 4-5 projects/posters showcased in each half, so that authors can also circulate and visit at least some of the other projects. We anticipate having a minimum of one author on each poster present and in-person at the session  to engage with the physical posters and in-person participants in the room. In-person authors may, however, elect to have some of their other authors attend the session virtually, hosting them on either their own laptops or additional screens around the room (depending on what is available in the room we are assigned in the Anteater Learning Pavillion). The Chair will create Zoom breakout rooms for posters in each half of the visitation part of the session, so virtual session attendees will be able to engage with posters as well as with the at-the-front parts of the symposium. Part of the reason for splitting the poster-visitation time, and staggering posters around the room such that two active posters in the same half are not placed right next to each other, is to ameliorate the ambient  noise that often impacts audibility at posters for communication among in-person and virtual participants. 2270 During the last part of the session, we will bring the audience and all authors back together.
