# The Zone of Proximal Self Model to Improve Equity in Higher Education

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper introduces a sociocultural framework called the zone of proximal self to reimagine how higher education institutions can support students from first-generation, lowincome, and marginalized backgrounds. Employing mixed-method approaches, we analyze diary entries, surveys, and interviews of 50 students who met with a counselor/advisor during the Covid-19 pandemic. Thematic qualitative analyses reveal a model of effective practices characterized by creating a brave space, validation, and strengthening students’ socialemotional competencies in culturally mindful ways. Case examples highlight features of this model including building working partnerships, understanding students’ cultural backgrounds, validating students’ experiences, and organizational brokering. Results from multiple linear regression models suggest that students, on average, have higher growth towards their professional and personal goals when exposed to more of these model practices. Taken together, the findings call for future research directions and designs of learning environments to consider cultural, relational, and social-emotional interactions in improving equity in higher education.

## Introduction

In higher education, students’ professional and personal development are impacted by educational inequities in socioeconomic privilege, resources, and access to opportunities (Tinto, 1987). The negative impact of educational inequities was exacerbated during remote education in the Covid-19 pandemic, which surfaced challenges including students’ basic needs, social emotional well-being, and familial obligations (Fain, 2020). Thus, it is imperative for educators to reimagine how higher education institutions can support students’ learning and development in a way that is situated in cultural contexts. The purpose of this study is to contribute to this reimagining by understanding an empirically grounded model of practices that can advance the professional and personal development of students from first-generation, low-income, and marginalized backgrounds (FLM). Since previous literature noted how out-of-class spaces such as counseling and advising can be promising for learning, the present study focuses on effective practices that happened virtually during the pandemic between counselors/advisors and undergraduate students from FLM backgrounds (Astin, 1993).

## Literature Review

Culturally organized learning pathways This work conceptualizes learning as a cultural process by drawing on the learning pathways framework in which learners engage in activities that draw them toward s or away from competencies in context-specific sit uations (Nasir et al., 2020). Culturally organized learning pathways 1) are in relation to a sense of identity with relational, affective, and motivational components, 2) are built from socially constructed practices over time, and 3) reveal power dynamics from structural institutions experie nced through interactions with families, peers, and  school. This study focuses on the culturally organized lear ning pathways of undergraduate students from FLM backgrounds: how their identities, or who they are in the process of becoming, are impacted by interactions with counselors and advisors across time and settings which are influenced by college institutions and structures. This work aligns with the literature on culturally organ ized learning pathways by shifting the unit of anal ysis from academic learning outcomes to “specific and consequ ential learning resources or experiences that learn ers are able to access” which include counselors and advisors in their institution (Nasir et al., p.198). Organizational brokerage Previous research in learning pathways have shown h ow parents acted as brokers connecting children to experiences, people, institutions, or information (Barron et al., 2009). The present study examines th e role of higher education counselors and advisors as organizational brokers. Organizational brokerage is defined as “the process by which an organization connects an individual to another organization or to the resources they contain” ICLS2022 Proceedings 767 © ISLS which can be implemented at the individual level th rough behaviors of school personnel and at the inst itutional level through administrative policies, infrastructu re, and culture (Small, 2009, p. 19). There are dif ferent mechanisms in which organizational brokerage can oc cur including storage (i.e., broadcasting opportuni ties), validation (i.e., confirming right to use resources), referral (i.e., direct linking to an organizatio n), and collaboration (i.e., working with another organization to provide social good). Examining organizational brokerage and institutiona l roles moves away from a discourse of student independence and meritocracy to one about organizat ional and collaborative support of students from FL M backgrounds. The dominant narrative in higher educa tion, especially in Science, Technology, Engineering, and Mathematics (STEM), have espoused the myth of meritocracy where successful students are the ones who “pull themselves up by the bootstraps” and navigate the h idden curriculum independently (Carnevale et al., 2 020); however, this myth of meritocracy is detrimental to  equity in higher education since it fails to recog nize how students’ lives are connected in critical ways to social opportunities and/or constraints. Hence, it is important to shift the narrative away from students’ responsibilities in looking for resources by themselves and instead on the role of higher education organizations in supporting equitable learning pathways and resources.

## The zone of proximal self conceptual framework

Aligned with a culturally organized learning pathways perspective and a focus on organizational brokerage, the zone of proximal self (ZPS) is a conceptual framework to theorize how learners interact with people, resources, and materials within their institution to develop competencies in pursuit of their possible selves. As illustrated in Figure 1, ZPS is the distance between an individual ’s actual and possible selves that can be bridged w ith the support of institutional people and resources. Furthermore, ZPS exists across a wide learning ecology in higher education, or the activities, resources, relationsh ips, and interactions that occur across multiple co ntexts in physical and virtual spaces (e.g., classrooms, advi sing/counseling, peer groups, home contexts, progra ms, organizations, etc.) (Barron, 2006). This process t ransforms the relationship between the learner and learning environments, positioning students to explore new opportunities and identities within their institutions.

Figure 1. The zone of proximal self conceptual framework

Lev Vygotsky’s sociocultural theory and zone of pro ximal development form the base of this conceptual framework. Vygotsky (1978) described how children’s  learning exists within a zone of proximal developm ent, “the distance between the actual developmental level as determined by independent problem solving and the level of potential development as determined through problem-solving under adult guidance, or in collaboration with more capable peers” (p. 86). The ZPS framework exte nds Vygotsky’s zone of proximal development from th e context of developing problem-solving competencies to the context of developing social-emotional competencies to pursue possible selves in a professional community. Possible selves refer to the imagined selves that we could become, and also the selves that we are afraid of becoming (Markus & Nurius, 1986). ZPS regards possible selves as more than a dream job: they are dynamic visions of one’s future self connected to holistic professi onal and personal goals influenced by cultural backgrounds, experiences, and home communities. ICLS2022 Proceedings 768 © ISLS In this study, possible selves are operationalized through students’ professional and personal goals f or themselves which can be reached through the develop ment of social-emotional competencies. Social-emotional competencies include cognitive, affective, and beha vioral skills that can be developed over time, such  as selfawareness, self-management, social awareness, and relationship skills (Kraft, 2019; Zins & Elias, 2007). Socialemotional competencies are similar to soft skills; however, the ZPS framework conceptualizes social-emotional competencies differently than skills discourses, or discourses commodifying soft skills for the job market without attention to cultural contexts (Hora et al., 2019; Urcioli, 2008). Instead, social-emotional competenc ies are envisioned as situated, culturally organized, and linked tonew forms of social and learning-oriented activity.

## Methodology and research questions

This study aims to refine the ZPS framework and contribute a ZPS model of practices grounded in empirical data of how counselors/advisors support students’ profes sional and personal goals. Towards this aim, this s tudy employs a mixed-methods approach to triangulate findings to the following research questions: Research question 1.  How are counselors and advisors supporting student s from FLM backgrounds within ZPS? What are effective practices that constitute a ZPS model for supporting students? Research question 2. Does the use of ZPS model practices in meetings between counselor/advisors and students predict greater gains in students’ professional and personal goals over time?

## Participants

Participants of the study include 50 undergraduate students who were recruited from equity-oriented programs at a large public R-1 university in California. Of the students, 74% were first-generation; 74% were female and 4% were gender-nonbinary. Students were from diverse racial/ethnic backgrounds: 52% Latinx/Chicanx, 22% mixed race, 12% East/South Asian, 8% White, 4% Black, and  2% Southeast Asian. Most students were sophomores (42%) or juniors (34%), with some freshmen (2%), seniors (16%), or super seniors (6%). Students were balanced between Social Sciences and Humanities (23%), STEM (22%), and interdisciplinary studies (10%). Additionally, 4 counselors from equity-oriented programs participated in an interview.

## Study procedures and measures

Student participants were asked to meet virtually w ith a counselor or advisor at least three times thr oughout an academic 14-week semester. The meetings were about once per month corresponding to a pre-, mid-, and p osttime period. For meetings, 42% of students met with the same counselor or advisor (n=21) and 58% of students met with different counselors or advisors (n=29). The following were the measures in the study: Diary Entries All student participants completed diary entry subm issions after each meeting with a counselor or advi sor. The diary entries asked for who they met with, the meet ing duration, topics discussed, what happened, and how students felt about the sessions. Example questions include “Describe your meeting and what you got out of the session.” Diary entries were completed through online submissions using the Qualtrics platform. Pre-and post-surveys All student participants completed an online Qualtr ics pre-and post-survey assessing their growth tow ards professional and personal goals. Professional goals were assessed in an open-ended question of “What a re your professional goals?” followed by a Likert-scaled re sponse item on a scale of 1-7 to the question “Sele ct the statement that best represents your progress in pur suing your professional goals.” Personal goals were  assessed with the same questions but with personal instead of professional goals in the question stem. Interviews 44 students opted in to virtually participate in a semi-structured interview at the end of the semester which lasted between 30-60 minutes. Students were asked to elaborate on their diary entry submissions with additional details and specific strategies that their counselor or advisor employed. For counselor interviews, counselors were asked to describe their counseling philosophy and practices.

## Research question 1: ZPS model of effective practices

Thematic coding of diary entries revealed three pro minent categories of codes: counselors and advisors  1) cultivating an authentic relationship with students  through creating a brave space and validation 2) s upporting students with social-emotional competencies, and 3) students’ perceived impact on their professional and personal goals. Taken together, these categories formed the core category: Creating a brave space, validating students, and supporting social-emotional competencies in cultura lly mindful ways is related to students’ profession al and personal goals. Brave space: Building a working partnership. Sofia and both students discussed how Sofia cultivated an authentic relationship by creating a brave space for conversation and collaboration. The following quote from Sofia ex pressed this: “I've had students call me dude and then say sorry. Emilia mentioned how, “She was just in the process, and I never felt like she was in charge of me. ## Discussion and significance

In sum, the findings reveal the significance of applying a ZPS model to support students from FLM backgrounds in pursuing their possible selves. The model, building on extant literature and empirical data from the present study, is characterized by effective practices of creating an authentic relationship with students (i.e., creation of a brave space and validation) and strengthening students’ social-emotional competencies (e.g., relationship skills) in culturally mindful ways. Moreover, the case examples reveal the importance of relational interactions and cultural mindfulness in organizational brokering with students from diverse backgrounds. Results from multiple linear regression models suggest that students, on average, have higher growth towards their professional and personal goals when they are exposed to more ZPS model practices from counselors/advisors. Future research should examine how these interactions impact student experiences across different institutional settings and roles in higher education, including instructors, peers, and family. Ultimately, this study was completed during a Covid-19 pandemic fraught with uncertainties in professional and personal lives; and yet, during virtual conference meetings with students, counselors and advisors were able to support students’ goals. The ZPS model articulated in this study is a testament to the strength and potential of counselors and advisors in bridging the distance between students’ current realities and future possibilities. This work is part of a greater reimagining of equity-oriented work in higher education that recognizes the cultural, social, and emotional processes of student learning and development.
