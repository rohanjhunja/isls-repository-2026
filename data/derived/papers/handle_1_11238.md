# “Nightshade Grows Well Here”: Exploring Rhizomatic Agency with Immigrant Growers in a Community Food Garden

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study explores expansive views on agency and learning, focusing on the coupled assemblages between immigrant growers and more-than-human beings in the context of an urban community food garden. Drawing on the notion of meshwork (Ingold, 2016), we highlight previously displaced people’s evolving agentic capacities to navigate the social and geographic contours as dynamic socio-ecological adaptations emerging from the rhizomatic assemblages of food-growing activities, land, bodies and culture. Using an illustrative example of how a tropical edible plant, Nightshade, is grown in a vastly different cold climate by immigrant growers as they try to “root” themselves in a foreign space, we highlight the dynamic correspondence that simultaneously transforms the land and shapes their perspectives. In drawing attention to the relational and processual growth of Nightshade with respect to its surroundings, the study expands on how embodied, affective multi-species entanglements form critical dimensions of human becoming.

Conceptualizing agency through attending to rhizomatic interconnections in a community garden Scholarship in the field of Learning Sciences has studied the socially-mediated nature of agency, bringing to fore its constitution in relation to context, position, knowledge and identity  (Barton & Tan, 2010; Gebre & Polman, 2020). Rahm et al. (2022) attend to the nonlinear trajecto ries and affective valences involved in figuring sc ience identities among young women of color through focussing on evolving cultural identities and emotions in relation to both formal and informal educational contexts. Our study takes a similar, yet distinct approach through a focus on more-than-human (MTH) beings (Bang, 2016; Simpson, 2014) in co-creating initiatives that guide growers’ sense of agency and belonging. Our study was undert aken in the conte xt of urban community food garden. Broadly, community gardens are promoted as sites th at support the well-being of vulnerable groups, aiding their integration into society through food-based socio-ecological connections (McGuire et al., 2022). Such discourses however neglect the agency of the growers and the l and in navigating the constraints and possibilities  created through the activities. Barron (2017) describes community gardens as socially-produced spaces intersecting with material processes to shape social life. This study explores the process of social emergence of the evolving agency of previously displaced people in relation to their farming activities. The notion of mediated agency involves a dynamic tension between individuals and the material artefacts in terms of the actions that are constrained or ena bled by the environment.  Latour (2014) perceived agency as distributed across individuals and the materials by  treating all human and non-human agents as symmetric nodes/actants across a network. However, later theo ries emphasized the nature of connections that enables the salience of materials regarded as actant  (Sugarman & Martin, 2011).  Ingold (2016) for instance uses the term “meshwork” rather than network to call attention to the interplay of forces that result in emergent forms of agency: “The lines of the meshwork are the trails along whi ch life is lived…it is in the entanglement of lines, not in the connecting of points, that the mesh is constituted”  (p.81).  Ingold (2016)  draws on Deleuze  and Guattari ’s conception of a living organism as a bundle of lines intermingling and growing continuously. Deleuze and Guattari

(1987) invoke the properties of a rhizome to descri be the non-hierarchical, decentralised and structureless

processes “produced by the formation of transversal  alliances between different and coexisting terms w ithin an open system” (p. 10). This ontological shift emphas izes meaning-making along lines of relationship, rather than a superimposition of meaning over matter. Unlike conventional hierarchical models of agency, conceptualization of rhizomatic agency involves attention to the dynamic and co-constitutive nature of relationships which animates the possible sphere of mea ning-making and actions. Exploring the agency of immigrant growers through rhizomatic interconnections allows for the growth a nd shifting salience of the mediating entities, whi ch in turn shape the possibilities and capacities perceived by  the growers. An emphasis on agency as emerging thr ough embodied relations with the MTH beings also contributes to the growing scholarship on unde rstanding learning as attending and caring for constitutive interconne ctedness between humans and their environment  (for review, Vossoughi et al., 2023). Additionally, these discus sions offer generative counter-narratives to anthropocentric discourses on sustainability by disrupting the viol ent dichotomies between social and natural systems, and building more expansive notions of ecological wellb eing through “emergent ethicopolitical possibilitie s” (Blok, 2014, p. 271).

## Context and methodology

The Land of Dreams (LoD) is a 30-acre community urban regenerative gardening initiative located on Calgary in Canada. LoD began in 2018 with an aim to create a welcomi ng space for immigrants who resettled largely as refugees from other countries. This urban community  farm also serves as a space to (re) build relationships with local Indigenous communities and learn about their practi ces to regenerate the land while growing food.  Our research and design partnership with this community   honours  pedagogies of learning from and with the land (Simpson, 2014) thus paying attention to agentic capacities arising from human relations with the land and MTH rather than individual trajectories  (Takeuchi et al., 202 4; see project website: www.soilcamp.ca). Our study, in collaboration with community partners, followed the  narratives of eight racialized immigrant gardeners over a series of interviews and video data conducted across two years in 2023 and 2024. These eight participants hailed from Afghanistan, Cameroon, Eritrea, Syria, Nigeria, and South Sudan. We focused on their efforts, challenges, and connections with MTH communities animated throu gh embodied and  affective encounters  (Dutta & Chandrasekharan, 2025). The interviews were conducted at the request for a public-facing documentary from the community, and thus the authors saw themselves as a  part of community and as listeners enabling the na rrative voices to come out, rather thandirecting them alon g lines of specific inquiry. For the specific purpo se of this analysis, eight field visits totalling 32 hours at the LoD accompanied by extensive field notes, interviews (total of 6 hours), photo documentation, and additional infor mal conversations were used. For our analyses, we s ought to focus on the relational processes and learning emanating from the growers’ interaction with the MTH world, thus centering such relationships as the unit of analysi s (Hecht & Nelson, 2022; Marin, 2020).  W e focussed on narratives and actions that conveyed the growers’ connection with the MTH world, identified through the mention of specific plants, soil, other beings, particularl y in relation to their own intentions, capacities o r feelings. We sought to characterize their personal meaning-making journeys at the farm rather t han more generic description of the space. Here, we present an illustrative case  of growing Nightshade to highlight themes related to growing agency in the meshwork involving immigrant growers with(in) the MTH world. Disrupting the category of weeds and edibles Growers, especially those who had come from African  countries such as Cameroon, Sudan and Nigeria shar ed their efforts of growing green vegetables commonly found in their home countries, but are difficult to source in the local markets. In the backdrop of this struggle to find vegetables th at are personally and culturally relevant, many of them shared their struggles of finding jobs in their field of expertise because recruiters felt that they did not have “relevant Canadian experience.” For instance, in an  attempt to make a living through farming while working at a construction site, Girmay started growing kale at LoD for sale to local restaurants, and has received a good response even though the move has reduced th e diversity of crops he grew earlier. The dynamic trade-off of aspirations, feasibility and land offerings play out in Girmay’s efforts of becoming a full-time farmer. The community urban farm was important for these people as a place where their knowledge and expertise were acknowledged and appreciated.  With their agricultural expertise, growers especial ly run multiple experiments on the community farm. A plant that they found reasonable success which belongs to the Nightshade family is called B lack Nightshade. Despite the difference in climate a nd soil conditions  between their home countries and Canada, growers like Joska, Ghislaine, and Patrick were able to get harvests in amounts that could be shared with the larger community. Nightshade pla nts are not considered edible locally and treated w ith fear because many varieties can be toxic (Olet et al., 2005). Patrick, for instance commented, “Canadians are afraid to consume to Black Nightshade but we grow and eat it.” Commenting on her connection with Black Nightshade in terms of its health benefits, Joska explained: So once I eat my plants that I plant, which is the black nightshade, it will improve my sugar. That's one thing… And it's not only me in my commun ity. A lot of people are going through this diabetes thing. So even my plant, you can see now I have there, is  not enough for everybody… So imagine if the Land of Dreams can have a good and a better land for that plant with my community really, really need them. Then, I will save so many people life in terms of, say, diabetes and all that type of stuff.

As a  versatile vegetable, Ghislaine also described how t hey are able to cook a variety of dishes with the s ame ingredient and is thus a popular vegetable back hom e. Her community’s concerted efforts to grow  the plant stemmed from the unavailability of diverse leafy greens from the  local grocery stores where mostly spinach or lettuce varieties are available.

It (Black Nightshade) is the base of some traditional food in Cameroon because we eat a lot of greens. We eat a lot of leaves. We have so many var iety, but the only one we are able to grow here is Black Nightshade, because the other variety takes like a long time to grow… For us, the Black Nightshade is I mean, it's a healthy food, you know, it's green. It's full  of fibers, full of irons, and it's, like, easy to digest. You can cook  it in many ways. Different ways. You can, like, steam them. You can fry them. You can mash th em with potatoes with plantain. You can also make like stew to eat it with rice.

Growers host potlucks at LoD a few times in the yea r, during which Ghislaine and Joska have prepared d ishes from Black Nightshade to share with the community. The act of sharing has led to other interesting developments that hinge on continued harvesting of Black Nightshade. Other growers are now more familiar with the vegetable, and there is a steady demand from the larger Africa n community in the city owing to its unavailability  in conventional grocery stores.   Paying attention to ways in which Black Nightshade’ s successful adaptation to a different climate  encouraged the growers to explore the context of LoD with a view of creating a suitable habitat for the plant decenters a purely human view of agen tic actions. Thinking along the entangled relations hips being constantly remade in the light of multiple forces p laying out on/from the land allows the recasting of  agency through more-than-human interactive processes. These dynamic encounters also point to shifting socio-ecological relations. Hecht and Nelson (2022) discuss how discussions on invasive specie s often draw on negative themes of immigration as by referring to competition, reproductive growth and associated characteristics. The adaptation and use of non-native plants by the growers calls for reima gining such categories, especially as climate-change induced habitats are changing rapidly.

Apart from Nightshade, growers also described growi ng Amaranth, Sweet Potato (they consume the leaves too, apart from the tuber), and Malakwang. Varieties of Amaranth, also called Pigweed locally are seen as invasive weeds, but are edible, and used by the imm igrant growers who have the relevant culinary knowl edge. Growers also described how they are able to harvest  Nightshade and other leafy greens 3-4 times during the growing season thus providing them with regular pro duce instead of having to wait until the end of the  growing season. Despite the short growing season of 3-4 months due to the cold weather, some of the growers like Girmay have been trying to save seeds from the plant for the subsequent season. Navigating growing conditions of Nightshade: tough soil and resident creatures Historically,  the site where LoD is located was a wetland ecosyst em when stewarded by the Indigenous communities. However, the place was severely degrad ed as it became a dumpsite for debris when roads and commercial storage facilities were being constructed in the vicinity. As such the location of the space seems like a visual anomaly amidst the buzz of vehicles and roads surrounding it. Because of damaging human relationships with the land after colonization, t he soil is compacted, making farming activities challenging  (Swallow et al., 2023). Compared to the fertile areas that growers were familiar with in their home countries, these soil conditions proved challenging, and thus presented growers sign ificant learning and unlearning of eco-agricultural knowledges. Commenting on the soil quality, Ghislaine explained how the idea of buying potting soil or manure was new to her.

The soil here is, like, very challenging, very chal lenging compared to Cameroon… You need, like, manure. We usually get, like, some compost fr om the mushroom farm at Crossfield. Yes. To make the soil, like, more richer, more rich. But the under soil is kind of dry, I think is because of the, the climate, the, the season. And we don't have a lot of rain here. That's one problem. Growing non-native plants such as Nightshade, Amaranth, and Sweet Potato has been difficult in the dry, clayey soils. Located on the edge of the city where water supply system is not established, LoD does not have a city-supplied reliable water supply, which caused crop losses for many gardeners. Additionally, recent climate change is causing drought conditions during the summer. In this context, immigrant growers adopted or adapted practices such as mulching with hay and woodchips to retain s oil moisture. Many young seedlings are also devoured by animals such as ground squirrels, rabbits and mice.  For some growers, the antagonism regarding treatin g other creatures as pests has shifted over the years. Following their interactions with Indigenous Elders and reconnecting with their ancestral worldviews, these immigrant growers came to embrace other animals being a part of the land, and thus having their share. Girmay explained: Because this area is also naturally, inhabited of li ke rabbits, gophers, and other, like, small insects. So, usually we, we will grow for all of us … for rabbits, for gophers, for some of the bugs instead of killing them we just we set aside some for them.

## Discussion: Manifesting multispecies entanglements and rhizomatic agencies

Our analysis reflect immigrant growers’  constant engagement with the MTH inhabitants at the land, while developing agentic capacities  collectively and in non-linear ways. As depicted in Figure 1, people, plants and related actions are continually interacting through processes of growth, decay, sharing, caring, weeding, mulching with lines being disrupted or extended in tandem wi th events such migration from home, memories of nat ive plants, livelihood opportunities coalescing over ti me. Rather thandiscrete entities, the pictures describe a particular instantiation of dynamic entanglements.

Figure 1 Rhizomatic Assemblages of MTH and Growers’ Interactions Extending Across Space and Time Contrary to an assimilation model commonly used for integration of immigrants in their newly settled homes, the efforts made by the immigrant growers indicate a more complex process of negotiat ing their multiple knowings in relation to the land. Rhizomatic agency arises through the possibilities and constraints offered b y human interactions with the soil, plants and other beings, visible in unexp ected success of a tropical plant Nightshade growing in sub-tundra conditions. As Black Nightshade plants grew,  previously displaced people’s agency and hope to serve for broader community’s well-beings also grew. On a new space grown through people’s dynamic efforts to reflect their cultural ways of being, Gutiérrez (2020) asks: “Who belongs and how? What are the relations both locally and historically between sub jects and space, and how does space come to be a pl ace of valued cultural life?” (p. 428). Originally conceived as a regenerative farm helping  immigrants to settle in a new country, LoD is a constantly evolving space that be ars its geographical and social history in its soil  profile while also changing in response immigrant growers’ efforts of growing edible plants relevant to their cultures. The sense of belonging is co-created rather than bestowed. In our exploration, we extend the notion of agency in learning, adapting and belong ing to MTH entanglements to characterize the spatial and cultu ral reconfigurations taking place through attending to the soil, plants, humans and other beings. Ingold (2016) describes inhabiting as a dynamic pro cess with the inhabitant as one  “who participates from within in the very process of the world’s cont inual coming into being and who, in laying a trail of life, contributes to its weave and texture” (p. 81). Rather than static categories interacting with each other, rhizomatic agencies in the form of meshwork continually redefine the growers’ scope and possibility of engagement through the health of the soil, plants and other MTH inhabitants. Understanding the role of these dynamic, consequential relations serve to expand the liberatory potential of collective learning within environments confronted with increasing uncertainty.
