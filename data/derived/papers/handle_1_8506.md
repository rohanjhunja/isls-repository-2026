# Addressing Challenges in Changing Science Teaching in the Global South: An Integrative Model for Science Education Reform in Brazil

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Development of sustainable science education reforms requires transformation, and therefore involves integrative approaches that result from collaboration between researchers, teachers and policymakers. Using a DBIR approach, we developed a model for design and implementation of new science standards that addresses three main challenges identified throughout the research: fostering teachers’ agency and deep conceptual knowledge; translating principles into lesson plans; and creating resources and expertise.

## Introduction, background and context

In recent years, there have been significant effort s to develop new science standards and frameworks t hat emphasize not only conceptual knowledge but also the practices of science and engineering (e.g. ACARA, 2016; British Columbia Ministry of Education, 2017; Natio nal Research Council, 2012). However, implementatio n remains an important challenge. If the goal is to sustainably develop innovative standards, the “document” itself is not sufficient, and the process of implementation must also be prioritized (Levin, 2010). Moreover, to allow for transformation, teacher buy-in is perhaps the most important factor for successful implementation of c urricular reform (Burt & Boesdorfer, 2021). This is even more  critical in developing countries like those in whi ch our research takes place.

In this paper, we describe a science education reform project that was developed over four years in a city in the northeast region of Brazil (Fernandez et al., 2020). The project was initiated in 2017 by the city government in collaboration with a US-based research team to redesign science education for the city's 49 K-9 public schools. Members of the research team designing the program and working with teachers were Brazilian and the work was conducted in Portuguese.

In Brazil, science teaching is often based on lectures, using the textbook as the main reference for lesson planning and students’ activities (Andrade & Massab ni, 2011; Bassoli, 2014). Most teachers do not have  the necessary infrastructure and human support to condu ct science activities in their schools, because of classroom size (usually more than 35 students/classroom) and lack of space and materials (Andrade & Massabni, 20 11). Official reports in Brazil show that only 8.6% of p ublic schools have adequate science labs, and 11.5%  have access to science kits (BRASIL, 2020).

## Methods and Design-Based Development of the Integrative Model

Educational systems in developing countries are very different from those of the US. In Brazil, expend iture per student is one order of magnitude smaller, infrastr ucture for professional development is lacking, and  there is much less funding for research and implementation. As such, reform guidelines developed elsewhere might not work in the Brazilian context, and therefore we had  to create a new implementation model to account fo r those specificities. The work described in this paper com prises three cycles of implementation and data coll ection, in which different components of the project were eval uated and refined using a design-based implementati on research approach (Fishman et al., 2013). We worked  with a group of elementary and middle-school scien ce teachers invited by the secretariat to be part of the curriculum committee, as wel l as with teacher educators from the city’s provider for in-service teachers’ PD (Sc hool of Teacher Professional Development) and munic ipal coordinators for elementary and middle school level s at the Secretariat of Education. A total of eight  teachers actively participated throughout the project (1 ele mentary and 7 middle school teachers). Three other teachers started the process but, due to individual constraints (such as not having enough time, being reassigned to other schools, or not showing interest in trying new appr oaches to teaching), only participated in the initi al meetings and then left the project. New teachers joined the project during the process, and as such the number of participating teachers varied from one implementation cycle to another. Our data sources include extensive field notes, evaluation meetings, interviews, questionnai res and documented interactions with teachers and administrators. Based on the data and on our assess ment of local educational policies, we identified challenges that informed our initial design, as well as further redesigns. ICLS2022 Proceedings 1433 © ISLS

## Findings and discussion

At the beginning of the project, we set out to deve lop a collaborative approach in which the new curri cular standards would be designed together with elementary and middle school science teachers. In preparation for this work, we conducted a pre-survey among the teachers participating in the working group, and found that science teaching in the city was often very traditional: Te achers usually started a lesson by reading the text book or presenting a lecture, followed by exercises from the textbook (Fuhrmann et al., 2018). Experiments were rare, and when used, followed very strict, step-by-step proce dures, serving as a way to illustrate theoretical c ontent previously presented by the teachers.

After gaining this understanding of the context of science learning in the city, we developed an integrative approach to reform science education with four elements: (1) development of new city-wide science standards co-designed with teachers; (2) profession al development for elementary and middle-school sci ence teachers, (3) implementation of infrastructure (low-cost makerspaces with science lab resources), and (4) hiring a dedicated pedagogical redesign teacher who co-designs curricular units with other teachers, develops toolkits for the units, and conducts research on student learnin g, and who we called the  “pedagogical redesign tea cher”. 1. Development of new city-wide science standards co-designed with teachers

This was the initial primary interest of the city’s  administration. They had previous experience with setting up groups of teachers led by specialists developing their math and language (Portuguese) curricular documents and envisioned a similar process.

1.1 Fostering teacher agency and deep conceptual knowledge Our goal was to develop novel science standards in a participative way, considering teachers’ perspect ives in order to provide the conditions to enable sustainable implementation in an agentive manner within the local reality. Teachers were presented with findings from the learning sciences and their implications in science education (e.g., selection of central ideas, spiral progressions, sc ience and engineering practices), and analyzed how science standards worldwide (such as NGSS, British Columbia, Australia) implemented these innovations. The gro up analyzed and explored the science component of the Brazilian National Standards (BNCC), and did extens ive work to integrate new pedagogies aligned with BNCC’ s requirements. Doing this work together with teach ers was fundamental to building agency, deep knowledge about the theory behind standards, and a sense of ownership. 1.2  Translating principles into lesson plans: To connect teachers’ practice with the science education principles we intended to incorporate into the curricular document, teachers had the opportunity to co-design curricular units with the research team in three c ycles, and to progressively incorporate into their practice the main principles guiding the development of the standards. This allowed teachers to experiment and better understand what these strategies could mean in real contexts. 2. Professional development in science teaching for teachers

Our initial survey indicated that it would be necessary to build a common vision of science education within the teachers’ group, and most importantly, that teachers should be given opportunities to implement and test in their classrooms the concepts and principles of science education that were being proposed for the new standards, rather than just learning about them through theoretical discussion. In the professional development program, teachers redesigned curricular units that they would implement in their classroom(s) in the near future, with a focus on the connection with their teaching practice. Participating teachers in this working group were middle school s cience teachers and elementary teachers, as well as  teacher educators from the School of Teacher Professional Development. Through periodic small group co-design sessions with researchers, intercalated with whole group instructional meetings where central themes of science teaching and student learning were reflected upon, teachers were encouraged and supported to adopt new concepts and strategies in their lesson planning as well as in their teaching. After implementation in the classroom, teachers and researchers reflected upon the outcomes and tea chers’ personal perceptions. These learnings were t hen incorporated into the next cycle of redesign. This process was repeated in three cycles of redesign th roughout a period of 16 months.

At each new cycle, new increased levels of sophisti cation/complexity were expected in the planning and/or implementation. To define these requirements, researchers were informed by the experiences and outcomes of the previous PD cycle.

In all cycles, teachers received continuous support  and mentorship from researchers, in the form of periodic meetings and 1:1 encounters in more specific moments (such as when individual teachers were refining their learning goals, researching and designing cla ssroom activities aligned with these goals, or refi ning and planning their teaching strategies for upcoming imp lementation in the classroom). Teachers in the grou p were encouraged to collaborate and share experiences during the course of the PD. They planned in groups, a nd also reused and built upon curricular units that their colleagues had designed.

3. Implementation of infrastructure

It was important that teachers were given access to rich resources and materials, thus in our strategy we designed new spaces for learning (low-cost hybrid makerspace/science lab). Two pilot labs were implemented in one fulltime and one part-time school (the two models of service in the city). We worked with architects that designed a laboratory blueprint for modular areas that include  woodwork, electronics/electricity, digital fabrication, crafts, sewing machines, and science. These laboratories we re the site for teacher training as well as for the  pilot implementation of curricular units by teachers. Cur rently, the municipality is working on expanding th ese laboratories to another eight schools, and has a goal of ultimately expanding them to serve all schools.

4. Creating local expertise

An important takeaway from this project is the importance of the co-design partnerships between researchers and teachers as a strategy to support teachers in redes igning and implementing their lesson plans. Researc hers held periodical meetings to discuss the big ideas and learning goals, helped to research resources and design activities that could support students’ engagement with science practices and promote learning aligned with the established goals, and gave feedback and support for implementa tion. From this experience, we established a new sc hool position of a “pedagogical redesign teacher” center ed on the co-design of curricular units with other teachers, development of resources for teaching and learning, and on researching student learning (rather than on teaching technology classes).

In the second cycle of co-design, the two middle sc hool science teachers designated as pedagogical redesign teachers started participating in co-design meetings with researchers and teacher(s), as well as additional reflection meetings with the researchers as part of  their professional development. In the third cycle, the pedagogical redesign teacher took the lead in the p artnership with the classroom teachers, with the su pport of researchers. This strategy contributed to the sustainability of the program, by creating local resources and expertise. A new cohort of pedagogical redesign teachers is currently being formed to oversee the upcoming new labs. Moreover, one of the pedagogical redesign tea chers has taken a manager role within the Secretari at of Education that involves collaborating, supporting and training new pedagogical redesign teachers, and overseeing the implementation of the new labs.

## Conclusion

To integrate modern science pedagogies to the Brazi lian reality, considering the lack of resources and  teacher professional development, our model identified challenges related to teacher agency, translation to lesson plans, the creation of materials, and the development of e xpertise. Taking into account the local context and  school culture, working with teachers and policymakers, we  co-designed ongoing solutions for those challenges, and created a culture of design-based iteration. Furthe r research is in progress, and our data so far – in  the form of classroom observations and analysis of lesson plans  delivered by participant teachers – indicate that classroom practices and pedagogy are significantly different than at the onset of the project.
