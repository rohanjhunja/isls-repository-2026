# Towards an Equity-Oriented Theory of Instruction: A Sociopolitical Analysis of Concreteness Fading

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In this conceptual paper, I analyze the affordances and limitations of concreteness fading towards advancing equitable student learning in mathematics education. Concreteness fading is an instructional theory that aims to support learning through strategic use of multiple representations. Many studies have focused on how concreteness fading benefits students positioned as less successful in mathematics. However, there is little research that explores concreteness fading in relation to equity. I utilize a sociopolitical lens to analyze the theory’s underlying assumptions about what knowledge is valued, who is valued as knowledgeable, and how contexts are valued as resources for knowing. From this analysis, I conclude that concreteness fading as currently conceived does not support equitable learning, as it reifies narrow forms of mathematical knowing that privilege some learners and ways of thinking over others. I offer suggestions for reorienting concreteness fading theory towards fostering more equitable student learning outcomes in mathematics education.

## Introduction

Within the field of research on cognition and its a pplications to math education, concreteness fading is an instructional theory that aims to support learning through strategic use of multiple representations. The theory suggests starting instruction with concrete representations and gradually replacing them with increasingly abstract representations (Bruner, 1966; Goldstone & Son, 200 5). For example, an instructional sequence could st art with physical manipulatives as the concrete representati on, be replac ed with drawn diagrams as an intermediary representation, and end with formal symbolic notati on as the abstract representation. A variety of stu dies have suggested that concreteness fading can support effe ctive mathematics teaching and learning (e.g., Brai thwaite & Goldstone, 2013; McNeil et al., 2019; Ottmar & Land y, 2017). Already, concreteness fading theory has b een influential and is used to inform the design of mat hematics education curricula implemented in a wide variety of educational settings (see Fyfe et al., 2014, for a systematic review). As concreteness fading is currently being used in m athematics instructional design, it is imperative t o interrogate how the theory’s core assumptions opera te within dominant narratives that sustain inequiti es in mathematics education. Studies have largely explored the benefits of concreteness fading for students positioned as struggling or lower achieving in mathematics (e.g., Fyfe et al., 2015; Davenport et al., 2023) without attending to these narratives. This is problematic because ac hievement gap studie s can perpetuate myths about students’ capabilities when they fail to acknowledge that ong oing systems of oppression operate within education (Gutiérrez, 2008). These myths continue to fuel def icit narratives, which falsely position students fr om subordinate groups as innately mathematically inferior to th eir dominant group peers (Adiredja & Louie, 2020). Deficit narratives are persistent in mathematics ed ucation in the United States, fueling biased and pr ejudiced assumptions that devalue the mathematical c apabilities of Black, Latinx, Indigenous, English Learners, female, and working-class students. In practice, deficit narratives become justifications for unequal systems of schooling that fail to provide rigorous mathematics classes for students from marginalized backgrounds (Martin, 2019). I argue for an interrogation of concreteness fading ’s theoretical assumptions to avoid perpetuating harmful narratives that overly privilege specific f orms of mathematical knowledge, which fuel further assumptions about who is considered capable of prod ucing such knowledge. Mathematical formalisms knowledge, defined as knowledge of mathematical pro cedures, equations, and use of symbolic notation, i s overprivileged in traditional mathematics education (Th anheiser, 2023). This is problematic for developing  robust mathematics learning because students’ prior, infor mal understandings serve as valuable sensemaking to ols that aid their later development of formalisms knowledge (Nathan, 2012). Further, the over-privileging of formalisms knowledge dismisses students’ informal and emergent understandings as inherently flawed (Adiredja, 2019). The devaluing of informal mathematics knowledge interse cts with deficit narratives about who has valuable knowledge for learning mathematics, devaluing the p rior cultural resources that students from m arginalized backgrounds bring into formal education spaces (Yosso, 2005). In this conceptual paper, I draw from sociopolitica l perspectives to analyze the affordances and limitations of concreteness fading as a theory of i nstruction towards advancing equitable student lear ning in mathematics education. Sociopolitical perspective s assume that the social construction of knowledge is intertwined with power dynamics that dictate what t ypes of mathematical knowledge are privileged (Guti érrez, 2013). Sociopolitical perspectives interrogate the norms developed around mathematics learning, norms that often perpetuate historical and social inequities alongsi de the lines of race, class, and gender (Gutiérrez,  2013; Valero, 2007). Thus, sociopolitica l perspectives take on the assumption that mathemat ics learning is not a neutral and objective endeavor but is an area rife with politic al and historical issues of power and domination. I  utilize Gutiérrez (2009)’s equity framework, developed using soc iopolitical perspectives, as a lens to examine assumptions in concreteness fading about what shoul d be known, who is considered knowledgeable, and wh at contexts are valued as resources for knowing.

This conceptual paper starts with a brief review of  concreteness fading literature, including existing research gaps on concreteness fading theory to position this paper’s focus on equity within this theoretical space. I then provide an overview of the soc iopolitical perspectives that inform my analytic le ns, detailing prior theoretical and empirical work that problematizes n arrow learning trajectories and interrogates power structures in mathematics education. I follow with my conceptu al analysis of concreteness fading theory from a sociopolitical perspective. Finally, I conclude wit h recommendations for future research and theory bu ilding towards reorienting concreteness fading as a theory that centers equitable learning in mathematics. What is concreteness fading?

Concreteness fading is a theory of instruction abou t how to effectively teach and learn while utilizin g multiple representations, starting with highly concrete repr esentations and fading the element of concreteness over time. In their conceptual paper, Fyfe and Nathan (2019) d efine concreteness fading as a theory “intended to facilitate connections among multiple representations of a tar get concept along a progression from more concrete to more abstract.” I briefly review prior literature on concreteness fading that defines the term concrete to expand on what it means for a representation to vary by concretene ss. I also review literature on the term fading to highlight the theoretical learning goals arising from the fading process. Defining concreteness The concreteness of a representation is defined by multiple dimensions, including variation in perceptual richness (Goldstone & Son, 2005), physicality or ability to induce perceptual-motor activity (Fyfe et al., 2015; Ottmar & Landy, 2017), or prior familiarity with the represe ntation or narrative context (Fyfe & Nathan, 2019).  A perceptually rich concrete representation might rep resent an ant with a detailed line drawing includin g its thorax and legs, compared to a simple black dot representa tion (Goldstone & Son, 2005). A scale manipulative that students are instructed to physically balance with foam stickers is more physically concrete than an i llustrated scale balanced with paper stickers (McNeil et al., 2019). Familiarity is defined in relation to a le arner’s prior knowledge and experience; for example, a symbolic n otation system specific to vector addition may be less familiar to a student and therefore less concrete (Zhao, 2024). The literature has utilized different terms to refer to a representation  that is deemed not concrete, including abstract (Bruner, 1966; Flores, 2010) or idealized (Goldstone & Son, 2005; Fyfe & Nathan, 2019).

Starting with concrete representations when learning a new mathem atical concept has been theorized to be beneficial for learning. It is thought that the physicality of a concrete representation provides l earners with embodied experiences that aid in developing understanding of abstract representations such as symbolic notation (Abrahamson & Lindgren, 2022; Fyfe et al., 2014; Lakoff & Nuñez, 2000). Concrete representations also provide grounded experiences imbued with real world connect ions that learners candraw upon to make subsequent abstract representations meaningful (Nathan, 2012).  For example, the Arabic numeral “2” is an abstract representation that can be made meaningful to learners when connected to their own experiences with recognizing “2” can signify two apples or two fingers.

Why fading?

Fading is defined as a stepwise progression of abstracting  or formalizing representations over time (Fyfe et al., 2014; Fyfe & Nathan, 2019). The purpose of fading the concrete element of representations is to help students develop a decontextualized understanding of mathema tics in the form of abstract representations. For e xample, students learn that mathematical equivalence can be  represented using symbolic notati on (e.g., 5 + 2 = 2 + 5) rather than a physical balance scale with seven foa m stickers on each side (McNeil et al., 2019). While concrete representations are beneficial for learning, it is thought that reliance on only concrete representati ons is problematic and may lead students to develop mathematical knowledge that is overly context-specific (Goldstone & Son, 2005). Fyfe and Nathan (2019) provide an example of students who rely on physical manipulatives during problem-solving, and who subsequently fail to solve mathema tical problems when the manipulatives are absent. Abstract representations are thought to be context-free, devoid of the elements of concrete representa tions that may be distracting and misleading for learners (Kaminski et al., 2013). The fading progression is also thought to assist th e learner with establishing meaning between multipl e representations by linking concrete representations to more abstract ones. Concrete representations are described as scaffolding understanding of more abstract representations, where the process of fading functions as a gradual removal of such scaffolding (Ottmar & Landy, 2017). Fading is thought to help learners understand that both the concrete and the abstract versions of a representation refer to the same, underlying mathematical concept (McNeil & Fyfe, 2012; Fyfe et al., 2014). Thus, the fading of concreteness fading is theorized to minimize challenges that concrete representations offer while helping learners understand the meaning behind abstract representations. Research gaps in concreteness fading Scholars have noted that few studies examine how co ncreteness fading interventions are implemented wit hin instructional practice and how teachers conceive of  them (Fyfe & Nathan, 2019). For the studies that d o, there is an emphasis on the crucial role of t he teacher in facilitating mathematical sense makin g (Zhao, 2024) and demonstrating how representations link together (Co les & Sinclair, 2019) when implementing lessons wit h multiple representations.  There is adjacent literature in special education o n concreteness fading interventions (e.g., Flores, 2010) that study students with learning disabilities or are identified as at-risk for failure in school. In this conceptual paper, I take on a more expansive lens that draws from sociopolitical perspectives that analyze teaching and learning within the larger context of mathematics education discourses. In the next section, I argue that it is crucial to review teaching and l earning with a sociopolitical lens because prior re search demonstrates how instructional frameworks, even with the best of intentions, can perpetuate inequities in students’ educational experiences. My conceptual analysis of concreteness fading from this lens also complements prior work that has critiqued the applicability of concre teness fading in advancing learning goals  in science (Beier et al., 2024; Kokkonen & Schalk, 2021; Lichtenberger e t al., 2024). Rather than focusing on other knowled ge domains, however, I return to concreteness fading’s  origins in mathematics education. To date, I have not yet found conceptual or empirical research on how concr eteness fading as a theory of instruction intersect s with sociopolitical theories of equity in mathematics education. This paper thus attempts to fill this gap.

## Analytic frameworks: Sociopolitical perspectives

I utilize sociopolitical perspectives in math education to inform my analysis of concreteness fading. Sociopolitical theories assume that knowledge is socially construc ted but extends these assumptions through examining  what knowledge is considered normative, and the social, political, and historical power dynamics that reinf orce mathematical norms (Guti érrez, 2013). For example, in Cognition in Practice (1988), Jean Lave excavates and interrogates the social and historical norming of “everyday thought” as primitive, lower class, and non-rational to build theoretical grounds for empirically studying cognition as situated in context. As norms about th e nature of mathematical knowledge are often taken-for-granted and otherwise invisible, unearthing these n orms will allow us to bring them to the foreground to be examined a nd transformed. In this section, I present two stra nds of sociopolitical perspectives that guide my analysis of concreteness fading: one on the hidden norms conceptualizing mathematics learning  as a linear trajectory, and one on how equity can b e conceptualized in normative versus transformative means within mathematics education. Problematizing linear trajectories of mathematics learning Scholars have identified a mathematics education discourse that conceptualizes mathematics learning as linear, a characterization that privileges certain forms of m athematical knowledge over others. Parks (2010) rev iewed mathematics textbooks, standards documents, and conversations in mathematics educational settings to unearth a discourse that conceptualizes mathematics learning as a linear trajectory. Along the linear trajectory, all students start out learning “basic” mathematics and sequentially progress to learning more “advanced” mathematics. Several scholars, including Parks, have argued that  conceptualizing mathematics learning as a linear trajectory is problematic because value becomes att ributed to “advanced” or “higher” forms of mathemat ical knowledge, at the expense of other forms of mathema tical knowing. In their article, Adiredja (2019) ar gues that over privileging mathematical formalisms, such as s tandard definitions and procedures, leads to an under privileging of informal, emergent understandings of  mathematics. Informal mathematics knowledge can pl ay a valuable role in learning mathematics, serving as s ense making tools (Adiredja, 2019) and as foundatio nal knowledge for understanding mathematical formalisms  (Nathan, 2012). Further, over privileging mathemat ical formalisms is a restric tive framing of what mathematics education can be, ignoring that other framings of mathematics exists that can lead to a more contextu ally relevant and humanizing mathematics education for students (Thanheiser, 2023).

Conceptualizing mathematics learning as a linear tr ajectory also inscribes a hierarchy of student mathematical ability, which in turn mirror social c onstructions of dominant group superiority and subo rdinate group inferiority. With learning conceived as a linear pathway, educators tend to categorize studen ts as either “ahead” or behind” their peers on a single trajecto ry towards attaining mathematical competence (Parks, 2010). Hierarchies of mathematical ability tend to reprodu ce social hierarchies, such as  racial narratives in the United States that falsely construct Black, Latinx, and In digenous students of color as mathematically inferi or to their White and some of their Asian peers (Martin, 20 19; Shah, 2017). A narrow, linear conceptualization of learning leads educators to focus on the shortcomings of stu dents deemed “behind” their peers, a focus that ign ores their existing strengths and fails to interrogate the soc iopolitical factors that shap e assessments of students’ abilities and their opportunities to learn (Adiredja & Louie,  2020).  These narrow conceptualizations become self-perpetuating, as students deemed “behind”  are provided with  less rigorous mathematics curricula that inadequately prepare them for post-secondary mathematics, reinscribing hierarchies of ability (Bratlinger, 2022). Scholars have highlighted how instructional practic es can reproduce hierarchies of ability during implementation of learning trajectories-based instruction. Within mathematics education, le arning trajectories-based instruction is designed to move students thro ugh a specific progression of activities, from “les s to more sophisticated” ways of thinking (Sztajn et al., 2012). However, language about lear ning trajectory stages can be co-opted by educators to categorize students as more or less mathematically competent and be used to categorize students who do not follow the strict learning path way as “deviant” (Myers et al., 2015). Another stud y reported that teachers implemented mathematics problem sets originally created to accommodate diverse student abilities, but the teachers sequenced the problems into a rigid learning trajectory that prevented all students from engaging fully in the material (Louie, 2017). The intent of learning trajectories-based instruction is to foster students’ growth in mathematics. However, conceptualizing mat hematics learning as a linear pathway can unintenti onally reproduce conceptualizations of m athematical ability as fixed and hierarchical, iron ically denying all students from engaging in rich forms of mathematics.

Dominant versus critical approaches to equity I draw from Guti érrez’s (2009) framework to structure my analysis of  concreteness fading in relation to equity. Gutiérrez (2009) defines approaches to equity along two primary axes, dominant approaches and critical approaches, based on whether equity goals attend to  the power dynamics that define and perpetuate soci al hierarchies in mathematics education. Dominant approaches may seek to improve students’ learning t hrough various means, including increasing achievement and  inclusion in advanced mathematics classrooms. However, such approaches take for granted the norms of “trad itional” mathematics education about what mathemati cal knowledge is valued and who can produce such knowle dge. Critical approaches seek to transform traditional mathematics through making transparent which forms of knowledge and practices are socially valorized a nd upheld as successful, as well as uplifting voices and identities that are historically subordinated or excluded from mathematical participation.

Gutiérrez’s framework emphasizes that moving toward equi ty in mathematics education requires both dominant and critical approaches. Dominant equity a pproaches aim to help students from historically marginalized communities succeed within the existin g structures of mathematics education. However, dominant approaches define success in mathematics by existin g norms on what mathematical knowledge is valued. T hus, dominant equity approaches alone are not sufficient  for enacting systemic changes towards equity be cause they tend to perpetuate the same social, historical, and  political power dynamics that marginalize certain students and diminish their ability to participate in mathematic s. Critical equity approaches are needed to interro gate these power dynamics and redefine what is considered normative in mathematics. Other scholars have utilized Guti érrez’s equity framework to analyze how instructiona l frameworks developed from mathematical cognition research alig n with dominant approaches to equity, and that addi tional work is needed to better align with critical approa ches (Maldonado Rodríguez et al., 2022; Myers, 2022). This paper is inspired by the analytic framing from thes e prior works that examines  how mathematical cognition research intersects with equity initiatives in math ematics teaching and learning  and seeks to contribute to this body to literature.

Concreteness fading as aligned with dominant approaches to equity I now analyze how conceptualizations of concretenes s fading align with dominant approaches to equity i n mathematics education. I focus my analysis by analyzing norms around valuing mathematical knowledge, asking

1) What mathematical knowledge is considered valuable? 2) Who is considered knowledgeable? and 3) How are

cultural contexts valued and incorporated into inst ructional frameworks? Through my analysis, I argue that concreteness fading as a theory of instruction aligns primarily with dominant approaches to equity in mathematics education. The theory’s conceptualization and imple mentation within instruction focuses on increasing student achievement according to status quo norms, which up hold narrow definitions of mathematical knowledge a nd hierarchical conceptualizations of student ability.

What knowledge is valuable?

Concreteness fading theory has been conceptualized as a solution for increasing students’ mathematics learning through scaffolding students’ understanding of abstract representations, which are often mathematical formalisms rooted in symbolic notation. The  theory legitimizes concreteness, and the role of co ncrete representations in learning, as rich stores of knowledge that aid stud ents in understanding and utilizing decontextualize d, symbolic representations of mathematics (Fyfe et al., 2014).  The theory p ushes against traditional mathematics curricula that privilege teaching mathematics using only symb olic representations (Nathan, 2012). Additionally, concreteness fading draws from embodied cognition theories, arguing that concrete forms of lived experience and perceptual-motor activity provide the basis of mathematical meaning (Lakoff & Nuñez, 2000). Despite citing embodied cognition theories that argue for a valorization of concreteness, I argue that the current conceptualization of mathematical knowledge  in the concreteness fading literature aligns with dominant equity approaches that valorize a narrow definition  of success in mathematics. Mathematical achievemen t is defined in one way, as an understanding of mathematics using formalisms. The theory valorizes decontextualized, symbolic mathematics, where concrete representation s are useful but primari ly serve the end goal of learning formalisms. In their systematic review, Fyfe et al. (2014) suggests that concreteness fading helps learners “initially benefit from the grounded, concrete context while still encouraging them to generalize beyond it.” The term “fading” reinforces the idea that concrete forms of knowing are to be diminished as one obtains more mathematical knowledge. Concreteness is “faded” and “stripped” i n the process of learning mathematics (Beier et al., 2024). The term idealized representation has been used by researchers to refer to “a ll representations that are not concrete” (Goldstone & Son, 2005; Fyfe & Nathan, 20 19). This term reinscribes a certain value to non-concrete forms, as one definition of idealized is: “regarded or represented as perfect or better t han in reality” (Oxford Languages Dictionary, n.d.).

Therefore, while the concreteness fading literature  legitimizes concrete representations as necessary during the learning process, the literature primarily does not inscribe value to concrete representations as a learning outcome. With few exceptions (Donovan & Fyfe, 2022; Wilkie & Hopkins, 2024), empirical studies that examine concreteness fading only assess mathematics learning outcomes as problem-solving using mathematical formalisms. By and large, assessments of learning i n concreteness fading research studies chose to define knowledge that should be gained and retained as formalisms knowledge (e.g., Fyfe et al., 2015; Ottmar & Landy, 2017).

Who is considered knowledgeable?

Concreteness fading literature has suggested that concreteness fading-based interventions may particularly benefit students with low prior knowledge of mathematical formalisms. Studies have found that students who score lower on pretest measures of domain knowledge experience greater learning effects when participating in concreteness fading-based interventions (Davenport et al., 2023; Fyfe e t al., 2015). Some studies have also suggested that concreteness fading-based interventions benefit students attending reme dial mathematics courses (Flores, 2010). Overall, the literature highlights that concreteness fading increases learning for all students, but students with low prior domain knowledge may exhibit larger learning gains in mathematics from participating in the intervention. This framing of student achievement —that concreteness fading benefits all students but benefits lowknowledge students in particular —aligns with dominant equity approaches that maintain status quo norms of mathematics learning as a linear pathway. Students that start “behind” in mathematics knowledge will “catch up” through effective learning interventions such as those informed by concreteness fading. Labeling students as “low prior knowledge,” without continuously acknowledging that such a label is a specific measure of their knowledge of formalisms, reinforces narrow definitions of who can be good at mathematics. Normalizing definitions of prior mathematics knowledge as prior formalisms knowledge glosses over  other valuable forms of mathematical knowing that students  might bring to the classroom, which helps them as w ell as other students understand mathematics in rich and multitudinous ways beyond understanding formalisms (Civil, 2016). This is not to say that concreteness fading-based interventions are not useful and do not warra nt further study when shown to be effective. It is an importan t equity goal to understand how different types of learning environments benefit different types of learners. It is also important to develop interventions that are inclusive of students who have had limited opportunities to learn formal mathematics, such that all students have opportunities to develop mastery of mathematical formalisms. It is also important, however, to be cautious with how conclusions from the concreteness fading literature can reinforce norms in mathematics education  about student ability hierarchies. These conclusions can be misinterprete d or implemented in ways that perpetuate harmful ou tcomes for students by defining groups of students as having “more” or “less” valuable knowledge. In addition, ability hierarchies can be reinforced at the classroom implementation level that prevent students deemed lower performing from accessing all  the resources available in a learning intervention. The concreteness fading intervention that has been demo nstrated to be effective in prior studies is the pr ogression of representations from concrete to abstrac t. Prior studies  on implementing learning trajectories-based instruction have shown, however, that these interventions can be implemented in the classroom in ways that exclude students from participating in the later stages. For example, in one study, students deemed low performing were excluded from tackling challenging mathematics problems at t he end of a worksheet, which were also rich multi-concept problems that aid students in developing deeper mat hematical thinking (Louie, 2017). With the lack of research published on concreteness fading implementation, it  remains an open question on whether and how teache rs ensure all students have access to the full learnin g intervention. Otherwise, one could look at litera ture on other learning trajectories-based interventions that show how students categori zed as lacking knowledge are not afforded the full opportunity to participate in activities that would assist them in gaining that knowledge. How are cultural contexts valued and incorporated?

Theory on c oncreteness fading has broadened prior definitions of concreteness to be inclusive of how learners’ identities and lived experiences influence their mathematics learning. Literature has defined concreteness of a representation in relation to a specific learner’s prior knowledge and familiarity with the representation, following the line of thinking that what might be highly concrete for one learner might be deemed abstract to another (Fyfe & Nathan, 2019).

However, the concreteness fading literature often defines contextuality in ways that align with dominant equity approaches because the context s studied in concreteness fading  rarely center  students’ cultural and historicized identities and experiences as contexts for research. For example, one study described the use of blocks as the concrete “context” of a concreteness fading-based intervention (Wilkie & Hopkins, 2024). Anothe r study situated the learning intervention on vector additi on in an American football context to align with students’ familiarity and interest in sports (Zhao, 2024). It  is true that young learners are likely more famili ar with blocks than symbolic notation for subtraction, and high sc hool students  may have more experiences with American football than formal representations of vector addition. These interventions would meet the definition of concrete contexts as currently conceptualized in concretenes s fading literature. However, defining contexts as devoid of references to gender, race, class, or other social identities does not meaningfully increase inclusion of the multiple, overlapping, and varied identities that students br ing to mathematics class. A “culture-free” context in fact reinforces the dominance of the dominant culture, e levating it above marginalized students’ funds of k nowledge and cultural, multilingual, and historical resources (Civil, 2016). The current state of concreteness fading literature holds several open questions to how the cultural co ntexts of historically m arginalized groups in mathematics education can be leveraged within concreteness fading-based interventions.

## Discussion: Concreteness fading and critical equity approaches

I offer a reimagining of concreteness fading theory  as aligned to critical equity approaches, with sug gestions for future research and theory development. I also present some broad suggestions and strategies for researchers and practitioners interested in further developing and utilizing concreteness fading theory in educational interventions. Valuing multiple forms of knowledge Critical equity approaches to concreteness fading w ould valorize multiple modes of mathematical thinki ng, including the initial concrete stages of representa tions used during mathematics learning. Concretenes s fading could more fully incorporate epistemological assump tions in embodied cognition that mathematical understanding is rooted in concrete, lived experien ce (Lakoff & Núñez, 2000). Interventions designed acco rding to concreteness fading theory could additionally draw from embodied cognition theory, with one design principle suggesting that initial embodied experiences must be encouraged and valorized (Abrahamson & Lindgren, 2022). A reframing of concreteness fading to valorize mult iple forms of knowledge would align with current st andards of teaching and learning mathematics (NCTM, 2014) w hich suggest that u sing multiple representations is necessary and generative a s a learning goal, not just in mastering formalisms  as a representational mode. How connection making occurs between multiple representations is also a learning goal that has been identified as less researched to date within concreteness fading  literature (Fyfe & Nathan, 2 019). A critical equity approach to concreteness fading would therefore be  more equitable in its valorization of multiple form s of mathematical thinking, more theoretically aligned to its foundations in embodied cognition, and can lead to further  theory development that increases its ability to address a wider range of learning goals. Valuing who is considered knowledgeable Additional concreteness fading research that  is aligned with  critical equity approaches could attend to constructions of hierarchical student ability in mathematics and question how historically marginalized groups of students are positioned as learners in concreteness fading interventions. A large-scale randomized control trial of an intervention with concreteness fading elements  replicated findings that students from underrepresented racial groups and those with designated English learner st atus predictively scored lower on mathematics learn ing assessments, regardless of treatment status (Davenport et al., 2023). Future research in this space that aligns with critical equity approaches could build on these res earch findings and question: how might the implementation of concreteness fading-based interventions continue to perpetuate historic  and systemic inequities in mathematics for historically marginalized students? What can be  done to remedy this within instruction? A critical  equity approach would further question and interrogate the reproduction of hierarchies that match racial narratives about the mathematical capabilities of students of color and students bringing diverse linguistic resources into the classroom. Such an approach would question how positioning for malisms knowledge as more valuable can reinscribe ability hierarchies, a dominant narrative that falsely positions students from marginalized backgrounds as concrete thinkers incapable of accessing “higher” forms of knowledge (Bratlinger, 2022). Valuing cultural resources and contexts Using critical equity approaches to reconceptualize concreteness fading would question how contextuality can be defined and expanded to more fully incorporate lear ners’ race, gender, and class identities, and the intersectionality of those characteristics. Cultural resources of students from non-dominant backgrounds must not just be identified and included as concrete contexts in concreteness fading interventions, however, but be valorized as meaningful and valuable for developing deep math ematical understanding. A critical equity approach would challenge norms of conceptualizing cultural forms of mathematical knowing as primitive or unsophisticated (e.g., Lave, 1988), instead recognizing that students bring rich and meaningful resources and forms of knowledge from their communities into the classroom. Classroom-based interventions have incorporated concreteness fading in conjunction with other learning design principles that bolster mathematics learning (McNeil et al., 2019). It stands to reason that instructional frameworks which aim t o deepen mathematica l practice beyond symbolic representation mastery could be incorporated to dev elop a more robust curricula that fosters a richnes s and diversity of mathematical practice. A critical equity approach to concreteness fading i nterventions would thus move beyond mere inclusion of cultural contexts  in mathematics learning and  would additionally challenge existing norms about how students’ cultural resources and funds of knowledge are valued in the classroom.

## Conclusion

In this conceptual paper, I utilized sociopolitical  perspectives to analyze how and whether concretene ss fading functions as a theory of instruction towards advanc ing equitable student learning in mathematics educa tion. My analysis through this lens reveals areas in which concreteness fading supports equitable teaching and learning. As a theory, concreteness fading offers several benefi ts for instruction through making abstract mathemat ical formalisms more accessible and meaningful for stude nts. My analysis also unearths several shortcomings  i n concreteness fading, however, where current conceptualizations of the theory do not align with critical approaches to equitable mathematics learning. Without critical  equity approaches, concreteness fading theory can be incorporated into educational practice in ways that  reinforce inequitable learning experiences for stu dents from historically marginalized groups. From my analysis, I suggested a reimagining of concreteness fading theory that attends to critical equity approaches. I included suggestions for future research and instructional design for those interested in advancing equitable approaches to con creteness fading theory and incorporating its theoretical principles into instructional practice. Beyond the specific theory of concreteness fading, I offer questions for the field to reimagine what mathematical knowledge is a t its foundation, and how valuing narrow ways of mathematical knowing can narrow conceptualizations about who is valuable in education and in society. Incorporating sociopolitical perspectives into our research helps center our attention to the historic al harms and existing power structures of inequality t hat, if left unattended, would dilute our commitmen ts to developing robust theories of learning that  benefit all learners. In their conceptual paper for  the 2024 ISLS proceedings, Uttamchandani and authors characterize d theories of change that do not effectively or eff iciently promote educational equity or justice as equity detours. I argue that reexamining ratified and accepted theories of instruction such as concreteness fading through an equity lens is a first and necessary step towards c losing off such detours. Analyses of power and inequity in education are necessary to move us away from such detours and steer us on a path towards meaningful systemic change.
