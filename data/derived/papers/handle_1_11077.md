# What Conversational AI Can (and Can’t) Tell Us About Peer Interaction in Higher Education

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In this paper, we describe a study that explored the use of AI as a method for collecting data on the instructional practices used in undergraduate introductory statistics courses. Six instructors were asked to use an AI app to track classroom discussions over a 12-week term. Comparison with classroom observation data confirmed that classes were lecture-centric with only nominal amounts of student interaction. The findings point to the use of AI as a viable method for learning about postsecondary instructional practices at scale.

## Introduction and background

The benefits of peer interaction for learning are well-established in the learning sciences literature (e.g., Bransford et al., 2005; Slavin, 199 5).  Repeated studies have shown that activities such as  group problem-solving, peer instruction, and reciprocal teaching lead to increa sed student engagement and improved retention of co urse material (e.g., Crouch & Mazur, 2010; Palinscar, & Brown, 1984). Much of the research on peer interaction  has relied on classroom observations to identify instructional approaches. Despite the established classroom observation protocols available in the higher education research literature (see Anwar & Menekse, 2021), classroom observations rema in a time-intensive and expensive method of data collection, particularly when conducted at scale. In addition to being t ime-consuming and costl y, classroom observations are subject to researcher bias, provide only a time-limited snapshot of activity, and the presence of a researcher can feel invasive to both students and the instructor. Although techniques such as learning analytics and data mining provide insight from the large datasets available from computer-mediated instruction, they cannot capture the important nuances that take place when instructors and students interact with one another (Baker & Inventado, 2016). New methods are needed that enable researchers to collect data on classroom practices at scale. Thus, the research questions in this exploratory study ask: 1) How does an AI tool compare to traditional in-person classroom observations in capturing peer interaction in undergraduate introductory statistics courses? 2) W hat insights can AI provide into the instructional styles of different instructors teaching introductory statistics?

## Participants and data sources

Participants were six instructors teaching introductory statistics at a public university in the north-eastern United States. Data sources included transcripts of classroom discussion from an AI tool and coded data from in-person classroom observations.

## Data collection

Instructors were asked to download and use the AI a pp TeachFX to record a minimum of six classes throu ghout the semester. Widely used in K-12 settings, the TeachFX program transcribes classroom audio (omitting teacher and student names) into an online report that is av ailable only to the instructor. Instructors are provided with simple measures of teacher talk, student talk, group talk, and silence, along with measures of student and teacher questioning.

## Data analysis

In-person observations of the instructors took place two-thirds into the term. Each instructor was observed and independently coded by two researchers using an ada pted version of the Teaching Dimensions Observation Protocol (TDOP; Hora, 2015). The protocol was desig ned to capture instructor and student behaviors separately in 2-minute segments. See Table 1 for examples of student and instructor behavior codes used in the observation protocol.

We determined interrater reliability of our observations using a fixed-effects intraclass correlation (ICC) calculation. This method was appropriate given the categorical nature of observation codes, the purpos eful instructor and coder sample, and the fact that we h ad more than one rater per class (Hallgren, 2012). The ICC across all instructors was 0.928, with ICCs for ind ividual instructors ranging from 0.817 to 0.988. Ac cording to Koo and Li’s (2016) cut-off values our rating reliability ranged from very good (0.75–0.9) to excellent (>0.90). ## Results and discussion

We calculated the proportion of instructor and stud ent behavior codes that were observed during in-class observations and compared the findings to data collected from TeachFX (Figure 1). On average, instructors were observed lecturing for 86% of coded intervals and students were observed ask ing a question for 13% of coded intervals (student discussion with peers was never observed). Data from TeachFX showed that instructors talked, on average, for 68% of recorded minutes while the average proportion of student talk was only 4%. Data collected from both sources—classroom observations and TeachFX—showed that the instructional style in these introductory statistics courses was overwhelmingly teacher-centered and featured only limited amounts of student interaction. While questions remain about the nature of student engagement (e.g., the t ype of questions they ask the instructor), responsible AI use appears to be a viable method for collecting data at scale on instruction and peer interaction in postsecondary contexts.
