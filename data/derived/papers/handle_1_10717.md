# What Social Representations Tell Us About Teacher Well-Being: The Crucial Role of Time

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Recently, the concept of teacher well-being has received growing interest. However, how teachers perceive their well-being is still unknown. This study aims to understand teacher well-being as a subject of social representation. To grasp its content and structure, a sample of 70 teachers had to complete a hierarchical evocation test and evaluate the valence of each cited term. To analyse the data, the ranked importance method was employed. Results indicate a prevalence of terms connoted positively and a limited number of words with negative valence. Findings suggest the social representation of teacher well-being is constituted of different representational areas such as time management, hedonism, human values, students’ relationships, or teachers’ social interactions. Teachers attached particular importance to time management, which was the most cited term for the social representation of their well-being. By employing the social representation approach to the study of teacher well-being, this contribution provides new research perspectives.

## Introduction

Teachers’ well-being: a diversity of approaches The teaching profession is grappling with shortages  and high attrition rates (Dreer, 2021). To address  the challenges that teachers encounter within their pro fession, a variety of intervention programmes have been developed to foster their occupational well-being. Teacher well-being, understood as “teachers' responses to the cognitive, emotional, health and social conditions pertaining to their work and their profession” (Via c & Fraser, 2020, p. 18), is considered one of the most critical resources for the school system’s efficacy (Dreer, 2022). Research on teacher well-being has increasingly captured the interest of sch olars. This interest is characterised by a diversity of theoretical and met hodological approaches.  This variety is illustrated by the systematic review of Hascher and Waber (2021) which identified five theoretical foundations: psychology of wellbeing, positive psychology, work psychology, teacher well-being, and health studies. The y showed that three methodologies are employed: qualitative, mix ed-methods, and quantitative. These researchers highlighted the prevalence of quantitative  methods with instrument s based on general well-being and warned of the possible methodological bias  and misunderstanding  of teachers’ profession specificities. They advocate for a context-specific, multidimensional approach to investigating teacher well-being. This heterogeneity has also been highlighted in a review by Dreer and Gouasé (2022), which examined the methods and measurement approaches used in scientific intervention programmes aiming t o foster teacher well-being. They therefore recommend developing a more consistent and unified understanding of this concept. Towards understanding the social representations of teacher well-being Considering these recommendations, a complementary perspective is essential to deepening our understanding of teacher well-being. The investigation of social representations will provide an alternative vision grounded in the subjectivity of teachers through a constructivist perspective. Social representations are defined as reading grids of reality. They are a set of information, beliefs, op inions, and attitudes about a given object (Abric, 1994). On one hand, they are a cognitive structure combining vari ous components, stored in memory, and associated wi th an object (Lo Monaco & Lheureux, 2007). On the other hand, they are socially elaborated as they are subject to social sharing (Moscovici, 1961). The social representatio ns approach is relevant to investigating the meanin g of occupational well-being among teachers as it provides valuable information on how teachers perceive their wellbeing at work and their possible expectations of in tervention programmes. This approach enables us to discover the beliefs and attitudes teachers share regarding their profess ional well-being. To the best of our knowledge, no research has been undertaken to study teacher well-being as a social representation object. This conce pt is rather investigated as a construct composed of  different dimensions selected according to the rese archer’s theoretical background.

In the present study, two main theories are employed to approach the social representations of teacher well-being: the structural approach, also called « central core theory » (Abric, 2003) and the two-dimensional model (Moliner & Guimelli, 2015).

The structural approach suggests that social repres entation is a hierarchised system organised around a central core  (Abric, 2003). These elements provide the social representation meaning (Abric, 2 003). They constitute the common base of collective memory and  a normative system to which a group refers (Dany et al., 2015). Around this central core, a peripheral system plays an essential role as it has the functions of concretisation, regulation, prescribing behaviour, protection of the central core, and individualisation of collective representation (Abric, 1994). Compared to the central core element s, the peripheral elements are more diverse (Dany e t al., 2015). The two-dimensional model argues that individuals express a n attitude towards a representation, justified by the representation’s positive, neutral, or negative evaluative elements (Moliner & Guimelli, 2015). Research objectives and questions Our objectives are twofold. Firstly, we aim to desc ribe the structure and content of social representa tions related to teachers’ well-being; secondly, we aim to grasp the teachers’ atti tudes towards their occupational well-being.

To address these objectives, three research questio ns are formulated: What elements constitute the soc ial representation of teacher well-being? How are these social representations structu red? How do teachers evaluate the elements of these social representations?

## Procedure and sample

The study was conducted through an online, voluntary questionnaire hosted on the LimeSurvey platform at Geneva University. The target group was in-service upper secondary teachers in the canton of Geneva, Switzerland. To recruit teachers, 16 upper secondar y school administrations were requested to dissemin ate the survey. In total, the whole questionnaire was compl eted by 70 teachers  working in different schools  (58.6% female, 40.0% male, and 1.4% other). The majority of the participants were over 39 years old. They had, on average, between 16 and 20 years of teaching experi ence (SD = 1.7) and between 11 and 15 hours of lessons per week (SD = 1.0). 52.9% were teaching human and social sciences, while 47.1% were teaching MINT subjects. Social representations questionnaire Teachers were invited to complete three different tasks. The  following two phases are based on the structural approach. Due to the existing fit with the theoretical background, this approach was chosen as it is a rigorous and open method that allows us to get a wider vision  than interviews due to its quantitative specificity.  For the first task, named the  free association technique (Vergès, 1992), participants were asked to produce five words that came spontaneously to their mind based on the stimulus word « teacher well-being ». For the second task, named the hierarchical evocations’ technique, teachers had to classify the words they had produced  according to their perceived importance (from 1= the most important word to 5= the least important word) (Dany et al., 2015). The third task was based on the two-dimensional model. Participants were asked to score each word according to their perceived valence (1 and 2 = negative valence; 3= neutral valence; 4 to 5 = positive valence).

## Measures and data analysis

Due to the openness of the free association task, a lemmatisation process was applied to improve the accuracy of the analysis. This technique involved reducing the produced wor ds to a canonical form. For instance, terms such as “united” become “union” (Di Giacomo, 1980).

To identify the content and structure of social representations of teacher well-being, teachers’ lemmatised terms were analysed using the ranked importance method (Dany et al., 2015). This approach consists of crossing two prototypicality criteria: the occurrence rate o f a produced word and its average rank of importanc e (Abric, 2003). The combination of these two criteria generates a table divided into four different zones (table 1). For the present contribution, focus is placed only on  the central core and the first periphery. To assess the attitudinal orientation towards the produced terms, participant s were asked to rate them on a scale: 1 indicating a very negative valence, 3 for neutral valence, and 5 for a very positive valence.

The frequency, the average rank, and the attitudinal orientation average were calculated for each cited term appearing in the social representation structu re. The produced words within each zone are ordered by frequenc y, from most to least mentioned. In the case where t wo words have the same frequency, their order is determined by their importance rank. It is noteworthy that participants have scored their cited terms according to their pe rceived importance, with 1 being the most important word and 5 the least important.

As indicated in Table 2, the central core of teacher well-being social representation consists of 12 terms, all frequently mentioned and ranked highly in terms of  importance. In this zone, only one  term is cited with a negative valence (“load”). Within the positive connoted words, different representational areas are observed: time management (“schedule”, “time”), empathy (“support”, “listening”), hedonism (“pleasure”, “satisfaction”), social interactions with students (“relationships with stu dents”, “class”), quietness (“serenity”), drive (“motivation”), and environment climate (“atmosphere”). Notably, the words with the highest  positive valence are “motivation” and “pleasure”, with “pleasure”  also receiving the highest average rank of importance. Finally, the most cited word in this zone is “schedule” (16 times).

The first periphery is composed of 12 terms frequently cited with a low rank of importance. This zone contains 9 terms with a positive valence and  3 terms connoted negatively, there are then more numbers with a negative valence in the first periphery than in the central core. The positively connoted terms cover various representational areas such as human values (“respect”, “freedom”), positive social interactions within the teaching community (“colleague”, “relationship”, “t rust”, “appreciation”, “collaboration”), quietness (“calmness”), and resource (“equipment”). The negat ive terms are “school leadership”, “stress”, and “administrative work”. The term with the highest po sitive valence is “collaboration”. ## Discussion and conclusion

This study investigated the content, structure, and valence of the social representation of teacher well-being among upper secondary school teachers in the canton of Geneva in Switzerland.

Our findings highlight a complex and multifaceted s ocial representation, characteri sed by diverse representational areas that span both individual and social dimensions of well-being. Notably, the central core and first periphery of social representation encompass terms related to hedonism, quietness, motivation, and social dimensions such as empathy, environment climate, and interactions, particularly with students and colleagues. These findings suggest that teacher well-being is intricately linked to both the organisational aspects of their work, such as time management, workload, stress, and administrative work, and the availability of resources. Moreover, human values, including respect and freedom, emerge  as integral components of this social representati on. The potential elements within the central core related to hedonism, and relationships with students were also identified by Pasquier et al. (2020). However, the importance of time management is more prevalent in this study. Our results also echo the observations of Hascher and Weber (20 21), who noted that positive and negative component s of well-being can coexist.

The main limit of the present contribution has been the limited nu mber of participants. We are currently expanding the sample to ensure the robustness of the identified central core and allow for a broader application of our findings. We are currently exploring the relati onship between teachers’ social representations of well-being, their teaching contexts, and their sociodemographic  characteristics, our aim being to better understand how such contextual factors might shape these representation s. This research is a key part of a project aimed a t conceptualising teacher well-being with digital technologies. By asking teachers to evoke t erms related to their well-being both in general and specifically with digital technologies, we intend to compare these two sets of social representations.

To conclude, this study reveals the complexity of teacher well-being through a social representation lens, notably highlighting the critical role of time management in their occupational well-being.
