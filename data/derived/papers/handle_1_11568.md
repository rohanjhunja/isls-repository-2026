# Cultivating Artificial Intelligence Literacy in Computer Science Education: A Systematic Review

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
The growing adoption of artificial intelligence (AI) into daily life underscores the need for comprehensive AI literacy education within computer science (CS curricula. In this systematic review, we revealed insights into educational strategies, tools, and evaluation procedures. Our findings highlighted the necessity for a holistic framework that incorporates ethical issues and age-appropriate tools. This study provided recommendations for cultivating AI literacy into CS education and emphasized the necessity for additional research.

## Introduction

The rapid growth of artificial intelligence (AI) technology necessitates the integration of appropriate AI literacy education in computer science (CS) programs (Long & Magerko, 2020; Zhai et al., 2021). There is an urgent need to reexamine the existing c urricula to include AI education, as suggested by  researchers to address the pressing need to broaden AI literacy across all educational levels (Grassini, 2023).  The integration of AI in software development is crucial. It is essential to comprehend the technical and ethical consequences of these fundamental technologies (Dai & Ke, 2022; Joseph & Uzondu, 2024). Students must prepare for the technological shift that AI inevitably brings about, as the demand for the AI workforce continues to rise  (Holmes & Tuomi, 2022; Slimi, 2021).

For this systematic review, we define AI literacy a s “a set of competencies that enables individuals t o critically evaluate AI technologies, communicate and collaborate effectively with AI, and use AI as a tool online, at home, and in the workplace” (Long & Magerko, 2020, p. 2). Drawing from Chiu et al. (2024), we distinguish between AI literacy as the foundation of knowledge, and AI competency as the practical, confident application of that knowledge in real-world contexts. This distinction provides a guiding  framework for our  analysis of AI education in CS learning settings. Despite extensive systematic evaluations of AI in education (Holmes & Tuomi, 2022), a considerable gap persists in comprehending the precise applicability of these findings to CS education settings (dos Santos & Junior, 2024). Our research tackles this limitation by using a two-phase systematic review: first analyzing existing reviews to identify curren t trends and gaps, followed by a comprehensive anal ysis of the existing literature on AI in CS education. Motivated by the gaps in the existing reviews on AI in CS education to address how AI literacy can be cultivated in CS education, leading to our primary research quest ion (RQ): What are the current approaches, opportunities, and challenges for cultivating AI literacy in CS education?

## Method

Our research employ ed a systematic approach following the standard guidelines (e.g., Page et al., 2021)  and consisted of two distinct phases. This dual-phase method provided a complete overview of current research and identified crucial gaps of the studies of AI literacy in CS education (dos Santos & Junior, 2024). Phase 1: MetaReview Analysis: We conducted a meta-review of existing literature on AI literacy. We searched Google Scholar (2018-2024) using five specific search strings: (1) "AI literacy" AND "computer science education;" (2) "Artificial intelligence literacy" AND "curriculum;" (3) "Teaching AI" AND "K-12 education;" (4) "AI education" AND "systematic literature review;" and (5) "Computational thinking" AND "AI." For inclusion, papers had to be systematic reviews or literature reviews, focused on AI education or literacy, published between 2018-2024, and available in English. We excluded papers that mentioned AI only peripherally, focused solely on technical implementations without educational conte xts, or were opinion pieces without systematic methods. Through this process, our initial search yielded 47 potent ial reviews, which were reduced to 22 after screening titles and abstracts for relevance. Full-text evaluation further narrowed this to 18 reviews that met all criteria and provided comprehensive insights into current approa ches and gaps in AI literacy education. Phase 2: Comprehensive Systematic Review:  Phase 1 findings directly informed Phase 2 by revealing three key ga ps: insufficient focus on ethical considerations in AI education, limited research on explainable AI (XAI)  for non-technical learners, and  inadequate attention to cultural contexts. Accordin gly, we used the search string: ("Artificial intelligence" OR "AI" OR "Machine Learning" OR "De ep Learning" OR "computational thinking" OR "XAI" OR "explainable AI") AND ("education" OR "curricul*" OR "Teach*" OR "pedagogy" OR "STEM") AND ("ethic*" OR "cultur*").We searched multiple databases w ith filters for time frame (2010-2024) and relevant academic fields: Web of Science (n = 4,031), ERIC (n = 801), APA PsycNet (n = 3,132), and EBSCOhost (n = 8,141), yielding a total of 16,105 initial results for screening. We have finalized the initial database searches and are currently refining preliminary screening cr iteria based on emerging trends.  While Phase 2 screening and coding are ongoing, this paper presents preliminary  trends and identifies key thematic directions for deeper analysis.

## Findings

The meta-review of 18 systematic reviews provided insights into the current state of AI literacy integration in CS education, revealing prominent variations in educational contexts, teaching method s, tools, and critical gaps. Educational Context: University-level programs have traditionally dominated AI lite racy education, focusing on neural networks,  games and robotics  (Ng et al., 2022).  Although interest in K-12 AI education is growing, integration remains restricted by scarce age-appropriate materials and tools (Lee & Kwon, 2024; Zhang & Aslan, 2021). To better comprehend AI ’s social impacts, researchers suggest crosscutting practices to incorporate  the study of AI literacy with social sciences and humanities (Holmes & Tuomi, 2022; Joseph & Uzondu, 2024). Teaching Methods and Approaches:  Widely acknowledged as applicable and useful  methods for teaching AI concepts, project-based and experiential learning approaches allow students to actively engage  in learning through practical involvement and problem solving (Lee & Kwon, 2024; Zhang & Aslan, 2021). Gamebased learning techniques have advocated to be advantageous for younger students, making AI concepts accessible and engaging (Casal-Otero et al., 2023; Van Brummelen et al., 2021). Furthermore, research showed that practical experimentation and active learning greatly enhance students’ understanding and recall of AI concepts (dos Santos & Junior, 2024; Kong et al., 2021).  Tools and Resources:  Development and simulation platforms, as well as intelligent tutoring systems,  have become increasingly applicable in facilitating interactive and personalized learning experiences (Liu, 2023; Verma et al., 2021). Despite emerging efforts, a  substantial deficiency exists in the provision of age-appropriate resources for young learners, impeding the effective incorporation of AI literacy into the K-12 educational framework (Casal-Otero et al., 2023; Chiu et al., 2024). The absence of standardized assessment frameworks p resents difficulties in evaluating and contrasting learning results across various educational environments (Shiri, 2024). Critical Gaps and Challenges: Although we acknowledge the importance of ethical considerations in AI education, the incorporation of ethics into AI courses is largely insufficient. The scarcity of resources and the lack of organized curricula for instructing AI ethics contribute to this inequality (Holmes & Tuomi, 2022; Joseph & Uzondu, 2024) The lack of standard methods for measuring AI liter acy outcomes makes it harder to assess the educational outcomes, and the lack of longitudinal studies resulted in concerns regarding the long-lasting impacts of educational practices focusing on AI literacy  (Chiu et al., 2024; dos Santos & Junior, 2024). Resource limitations and inadequate teacher learning opportunities impede the extensive adoption of AI literacy initiatives, highlighting the necessity for enhanced support and professional development for educators (Lee & Kwon, 2024; Casal-Otero et al., 2023).

## Discussion and conclusion

The results of our meta-review highlight common methods, promising approach es, and persistent challenges in the implementation of AI literacy within CS education. The concentration of AI literacy education at the university level underscores the urgent need to expand these e fforts to K–12 contexts, ensuring early engagement with AI concepts and fostering equitable access. Project-based and experiential learning emerged as particul arly useful across educational levels, supporting deeper conceptual understanding and real-world application. Despite this progress, significant gaps remain. Our  review reveals a widespread lack of attention to ethical issues, age-appropriate tools, and systematic evaluation methods. Many existing curricula fail to address students’ diverse cultural contexts or provide guidance criti cally reflecting on AI’s societal implications. Additionally, limited teacher preparation and resource availability further restrict the incorporation of AI literacy into CS education across grade levels.

While prior reviews have explored themes such as et hics, explainability (XAI), or cultural considerations, they often address these elements in isolation. A key contribution of our review is the identification of how these dimensions are meaningfully interconnected; ethical reasoning, for example, cannot be fully tau ght without cultural grounding, and explainability is most effective when a dapted to learners’ contexts. By synthesizing these gaps into a unified perspective,  our study offers a holistic understanding of what meaningful AI literacy entails and provides practical direction for designing inclusive, age-appropriate, and ethically grounded AI education in CS learning settings.
