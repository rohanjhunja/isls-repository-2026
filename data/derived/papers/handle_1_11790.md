# Intertwining Epistemic and Relational Work in Scientific Modeling

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
As youth and teachers engage in modeling conversations, they are simultaneously negotiating how knowledge is generated and how people are accountable to each other in a community. Typically, design and analysis have privileged the epistemic focus, including within a recent emphasis on sensemaking and collective knowledge development. In this paper, we examine epistemic and relational work during science classroom modeling conversations. We share a close analysis of a typical conversation structure, where individuals present models and other students evaluate and build from their ideas. We show how classroom members experienced relational challenges and opportunities in these conversations. We also examine how alternative structures taken up by teachers and students presented different opportunities and constraints. This work contributes to the field’s understanding of science practices and suggests implications for work on evaluation and care in science classrooms.

## Introduction

The practice of scientific modeling has received substantial attention within the learning sciences and in the design of instructional materials and technologies. L ike others, we take modeling to be a practice of re‐representing complex phenomena to develop shared understandings of how and why the world works (Schwarz et al., 2009). In classrooms, modeling has been studied for as a way for individua ls to represent their thinking, as a venue for community discussion and development of ideas, and as a support for explanatory work connecting visible aspects of a phenomenon with invisible mechanisms and processes. Modeling is now often central  to designs for classroom learning, particularly in curricula developed to support students’ engagement  in collective epistemic practices. In this paper, we argue that we must take seriously the simultaneously epistemic and relational work of modeling, particularly within classroom conversations in which youth share, evaluate, and revise representations. In these conversations, teachers and youth are  negotiating both how knowledge is generated and war ranted (epistemology) and how people are accountable to each other and for what (relationality). Modeling from an epistemic lens An epistemic lens on science practices focuses on how communities understand the nature of knowledge products, what those products are used for, and how they are developed (Berland et al., 2016; Hutchison & Hammer, 2010). A rich literature base has described the epistemic dimensions of modeling (e.g., Lehrer & Schauble, 2015; Nersessian, 2012). Several epistemic purposes have been consistently emphasized and used to inform curriculum design. The first is how  modeling support s processes of sensemaking, in which  modelers articulate gaps, put forward tentative ideas, evaluat e them, and seek coherence among experience, evidence, and explanatory frameworks (Odden & Russ, 2018). Second, researchers and educators have sought to support mo deling as explanatory and mechanistic work, rather than primarily descriptive activity (Schwarz et al., 2009). Finally, an increasing focus is treating science practices, including modeling, a s situated in an overall purpose of collective knowledge development (Alzen et al., 2023; González‐Howard & McNeill, 2019). One way that researchers have studied how epistemic purposes get communicated, taken up, and shaped is through epistemological framing, or participants’ sense of “what’s going on here?” including the goals, roles, and actions available to participants (Goffman, 1974). These framings are shaped by teachers, negotiated by participants, and can result in stable configurations for engaging in sci ence practices. There has been substantial work on how epistemic framings are  communicated and negotiated in classroom conversati ons (González‐ Howard & McNeill, 2019; Hutchison & Hammer, 2010) and the way that teachers’ messages about what practices like modeling involve shape students’ engagement (Ke & Schwarz, 2021). This research has described the challenges teachers and students may experience taking up the epistemic purpose above, including focusing on models as ways to sh ow correct understandings, completing tasks, or developing individual skills, rather than as tools for sensemaking. These foci have informed classroom  designs that emphasize uncertainty, idea revision, and publ ic sharing and evaluation of models (e.g., Chen, 20 22; Windschitl et al., 2018). Further, this work has led to the development of specific activity structures for modeling that are increasingly evident in reform-oriented research and curricular designs, including  presentations of selected individual models, critiquing researcher-designed models, and combining ideas in consensus models. Modeling from a relational lens Recently, researchers have called attention to the relational work involved in science. By relationality, we mean participants’ understanding of who is responsible to whom and for what – their sense of connectedness, of purpose for interacting in particular ways, and of responsi bility for mutual growth (Krist, 2024). Attention to relational work in classroom science emphasizes the axiologica l and ethical dimensions of knowledge-building and calls for us to  explicitly incorporate these dimensions in our design and analysis. It can help us understand how classroom structures developed from a primarily epi stemic perspective may shape other dimensions of yo uth’s activity, for example, the relationships needed to support argumentation (Chowning, 2023), the sense of risk Black youth may experience in an engineering challenge th at asks them tonegotiate designs (Wright et al., 2 018), or ethical dilemmas about responsibilities to peers and other species (Pierson et al, 2024). We draw from feminist conceptualizations of  care and caring, including Tronto & Fisher ’s (1990) theorizing of caring as an activity “…that includes everything we do to maintain, continue, and repair our ‘world’ so that we can live in it as well as possible” (p. 34). Tronto & Fisher situate caring within webs of interdependence, underlining its contextual nature and the fact that  it is imbued with contradiction. Puig de la Bellacasa (2017) points out that c are must be “continually reclaimed from idealized meani ngs” (p. 8), including  those idealizing caregivers and positioning care as an individual et hical trait. These possibilities and pitfalls reson ate with treatments of care in the educational literature. Valenzuela (1999) followed tensions in youth and teachers’ “mutual alienation”  in a large, predominately Mexican high school, finding  that teachers’ view of caring was largely aesthetic, focused on caring about the objects and ideas of school (ways of dressing, quiet attentiveness) while youth sought to feel cared for. Krist and Suárez (2018) argued that many analyses and classroom learning environments implicitly ascribe to a fidelity-to-knowledge perspective that emphasizes social interaction as an input to the ultimate goal  of knowledge development. In contrast, within a fidelity-to-persons orientation, the development of persons and relationships is of utmo st importance. They described this latter orientation  as emphasizing an ethic of caring, focusing attention on people and processes in knowledge construction  and on moments when participants’ emotions, relationships, and identities are at stake.

In this paper, we explore relational framings and messages through a lens of care alongside, and as intertwined with,  epistemic framings and messages, taking both to be dynamic, situated, and negotiated by students and teachers (Hand, 2010; Krist, 2024). Building on the work above, we emphasize how participants’ relations to each other, their sense of risk, and their sense of themselves are at play in classroom activities. Further, teachers, particularly those seeking to develop equ itable classroom cultures that foster agency and be longing, must navigate the relational alongside the epistemic dimensions of activity, and may experience tensions in doing so. Yet, we know little about how epistemic and relatio nal dimensions intersect in classroom modeling activity, and how teachers and students experience and make sense of these intertwined dimensions.

## Study purpose and context

We examine the practice of three teachers who are simultaneously working to support scientific sensemaking and develop equitable, caring environments. We consider some of the structures of modeling conversations that have been typically examined from an epistemic lens, see king to examine them from a relational lens as well, and further understand how the two might be intertwined. Specifically, we ask:  How do different structur es for modeling conversations shape opportunities for classroom communities’ epistemological and relational work? Our analysis is situated in a larger project focuse d on incorporating uncertainty into elementary-aged children’s modeling and investigation  (Manz & Beckert, 2023; Manz & Georgen, 2023). As part of this project, we engaged in co-design with three fifth-grade teachers between 2019 and 2022. The initial design principles that oriented our work included (1) positioning science as collective activity; (2) positioning students with agency; (3) inviting and valuing heterogeneity in conceptual, material, semiotic res ources; and (4) designing for and responding to uncertainty. As we worked together over several years, questions  related to risk, whose ideas  and ways of engaging were valued, and who was participating and being po sitioned as successful were often raised. We therefore began to attune explicitly to relational questions, strategies, and analytic methods in our work.

## Methods

The project uses design-based research and co-design methods that emphasize learning with children and teachers (Manz & Beckert, 2023). This paper focuses on three fifth-grade teachers, Ms. Lily, Ms. Shaw, and Ms. Larson, and the students in their classrooms, aged 10-12 (all names of teachers and children used in the pap er are pseudonyms). These teachers work in the same socioeconomically, racially, and linguistically diverse urban school district  in the northeastern United States, and the classrooms of study reflect this diversit y. Ms. Lily’s classroom differs in that all students were designa ted as “newcomer” English speakers and instruction occurre d in English, Spanish, and Portuguese.

## Design and data collection

Throughout the project, researchers and teachers worked together to specify goals and designs, as well as to reflect on and adjust designs, both as implementations were  unfolding and revisions between cycles of implemen tation. This work included reflecting on and trying out new structures and strategies for modeling. Researchers, including the two authors, attended most lessons: videotaping, writing field notes, talking to studen ts, and sometimes participating as co-teachers. The conversations we analyzed are drawn from the second modeling experience of the fifth-grade science curriculum, in which students explored ideas related to phase change and the conservation of matter. They dr ew initial models explaining why a  glass bottle filled with water and left outside in freezing temperatures burst. They shared and compared their models to develop investigation questions, return ed to their initial models after investigating if water expands and cha nges weight when frozen, and participate d in final consensus modeling activities after working with a simulation and text. We collected a dense video record for this sequence, ranging from 7 to 23 videos (total=78 videos) for each of four instantiations across three years.

## Analytic methods

To address our research questions, we sampled the d ata set, identifying conversations in which students shared and discussed models in whole class conversations. These included conversations near the beginning of the un it, as students developed initial ideas, as well as after investigative work, as students revised models and made sense of how to apply new information to develop an explanation. This led to twelve modeling conversations to analyze. We used framing as an analytic construct to study the development and negotiation of modeling practice. We built from the work on epistemological framing but also s ought to understand relational frames, and to look for indications epistemic and relational framing were interacting. We moved sequentially through data, rewatching vide os alongside the transcripts. We described and sought to understand the activity structures in evi dence in conversations (and when and by whose actio ns they shifted), beginning with a definition of an activity struct ure as an (often planned) classroom structure that organizes people, materials, and processes in particular way  (e.g., a turn and talk, a brainstorm, a gallery wal k). Within structures, we considered epistemic and rela tional framings (purposes, roles, and actions) and who was proposing, taking up, and/or resisting framings. To  understand relational framing, a construct that wa s newer to us, we focused on moments where teachers were communicating about and students were taking up (or contesting) who was responsible to whom (roles), why and for what  (purpose), and how (actions). We looked for messages and actions related to how students should relate to each other and to teachers, how participation was negotiated, what emotions and identities  were at stake in participation, and larger power structu res at work. We used an iterative process of drawing from the literature, then seeing and refining constructs through our analysis. We treated our developing “codes” not as categories th at could stand in for data, but as ways to point ou rselves back to the data to document and refine t hemes (Hammer & Berland, 2014). We documented our understanding of where epistemic and relational frames appeared to i nteract, in the sense that they were influencing ea ch other or in tension. We used records of co-design meetings and teacher reflections to triangulate findings.

## Findings

We found that the activity structures teachers employed shaped relational and epistemological framings, which in turn led to specific opportunities and tensions to navigate. Teachers tr ied out different structures, based on both epistemological and relational considerations, and would sometimes  shift structures during lessons. These different activity structures afforded and constrained epistemological and relational framings, evident in the roles and actions available to participants and the ways students took up and further shaped th ose roles (Table 1). To exemplify these findings, we provide an in-depth analysis of the Model Presentation structure,  detailing and illustrating our findings  about the intertwined relational and epistemic work  (and challenges) that were often evident in this structure. We then briefly describe the other three structures, which largely emerged in response to the first, describing themes evident in our analyses of these structures. Model Presentations Model presentations are a typical structure used in  lesson designs that seek for students to build kno wledge together. They involve a student or group sharing their mod el and audience members responding  as a structure for the development of community understandings. For example, in the investigation we developed, after students drew their initial models of why and how the bottle  of water broke when it was left out in the cold, teachers reviewed student models to consider how students were using molecules to explain the bottle breaking and chose two or three models that represented different student ideas to have students present. The author presented their model and shared their thinking, and after each presentation, other students asked questions to better understand the model and discussed what they agreed or disagreed with. This structure was used throughout the year, both as students shared tentative explanations and as they returned to their models to revise them. In these presentation and discussions, teachers’ ov erarching epistemic purpose was helping students develop mechanistic explanations that linked invisi ble entities (molecules) to visible aspects of a ph enomenon (water expanding and breaking a glass bottle; how freezing water can expand but not weigh more). Teachers often invited a combination of student roles and actions related to (a) getting different ideas on the table, (b) understanding each other’s ideas, (c) finding questions and disagreements, and (d) holding models accountable to showing agreed-on information. Our analysis also revealed relational opportunities and  demands. There was variation in how students responded to each to other, including overt appreciation or critique for different qualities such as legibility or correctness; sharing their own ideas rather than responding; a nd taking up the presenters’ ideas to think further about. Presenters often showed signs of risk by looking up or away from others or distancing themselves from their models (“I don’t even underst and what I was talking about”). Across classrooms, we saw teachers and students negotiating the relational implications of not understanding or disagreeing with a model. The episode encapsulates themes evident across model presentation conversations. Teachers routinely tried to frame these conversations as showing diffe rent ideas and contributing to community knowledge development. Students and teachers indicated that they were negot iating whether models were individual accomplishments (judged based on accuracy, completeness, and legibil ity) or tools to think with toward understanding the phenomenon. Both ways of responding to models involved evaluation or disagreement. Within frames of communal knowledge development, evaluation  appeared central to moments of naming and resolving puzzles. At the same time, evaluation posed both relational challenges and opportunities.  Student presenters indicated signs of risk and/or distancing.  Other students responded with laughter and signs of  discomfort, by softening critique, by naming the value of others’ contributions, and by positioning themselves as nee ding to understand. Teachers responded to perceived signs of risk by reminding students to listen or be kind, emphasizing the value of the student’s ideas, indicating that not knowing or having different ideas was expected, positioning themselves as a co-thinker, and explicitly highlighting contributions and naming disagreements as valuable for further work.

Anonymous models In response to the risk of model presentation, one strategy that teachers tried was using an “anonymous model,” drawn from another classroom or made by teachers. T he idea here, and one that we have seen elsewhere, is that this would alleviate the sense of risk from critique  because the maker wasn’t present. For example,  in 2021, Ms. Shaw used an anonymous model  for students to practice generating questions that sought to understand another student’s thinking, rather than critique  their ideas. She used this strategy before students engaged in m odel presentations so that they could practice giving fe edback. Across 2019-2022, several teachers used a teachergenerated model that showed molecules multiplying t o prompt students to evaluate an idea that contradicted a finding from the investigation (that the weight of water does not change as it freezes).  In this case, the contradiction could be critiqued without any studen t feeling that their model (and their modeling  skill or knowledge) was the focus of critique.

Our analysis suggested that while the anonymous model has epistemic affordances, these may come with relational challenges. For example, in Ms. Lars on’s classroom (2022), May quickly noted a problem, “if the molecules were multiplying then they would weigh mo re. But if it stays the same weight then that can’t  be happening.” Duncan and Catherine reiterated May’s idea, both using the phrase “it doesn’t make any sense,” and Duncan added, “We should post a blog that’s like a model critic and say why they’re wro ng.” Students were critical of the intentions and ideas of the anonymous student, asking, “was that kid trying to show nuclear fission?” in an incredulous tone. Notably, the vocal students were all white and frequently positioned as knowledgeable in the class community. In this and other instances, we wondered if, without a person (modeler) to be accountable to, students had fewer resources for building an ethi c of care, and what the effects might be. For examp le, in this case, could other students in the class– particularly children of color and those with disabilities – feel worried that their work and intentions might be similarly criticized? We noted examples of teachers responding with aesthetic reminders (Valenzuela, 1999) when using anonymous models to practice feedback, and wondered how they could do otherwise without a person for students to engage with. Similarities and differences Another strategy teachers explored to mitigate feelings of risk and reduce the likelih ood of harsh critique was asking students to look for similarities and differ ences between models, rather than evaluate them. Ms. Shaw increasingly tried out this structure as she worried about student comfort and  feelings of risk. For example, in 2021, she began the lesson in which students shared their initial models by saying, We wanted to show kind of a variety of different ide as. And some of these might be similar to yours, and some of these might be different… Today I want us to focus on this language, what is similar and different to the ideas that we had. As you’re thinking through and you’re looking at the models and things today and you’re hearing i deas, I want you to think, right, ‘this is similar to what I was thinking. Or you know what, this is different from the idea I had. However, we found that this structure was hard for students and teachers to maintain because it lacked sufficient epistemic depth for students to engage with each other’s ideas. Students would often begin responding to each other and disagreeing in light of an explanation, leaving  Ms. Shaw to decide whether to follow their emergin g questions or maintain the chosen structure. When Ms. Shaw cho se to maintain the structure, she often selected students to share ideas to spread participation across students. The drawback appeared to be that students did not need to listen to each other, because they were not responding to or building from each other’s ideas. In response, we often saw Ms. Shaw drawing on aesthetic framings  for relationality, reminding students to engage in  quiet, still listening  and praising instances of this  (rather than noting how they were engaging with others’ ideas or valuing the way that ideas and questions were building). Thinking-with-models Our analysis also demonstrated an emergent conversa tional structure – thinking-with models. A “thinking-withmodels” episode typically emerged as one participant saw a puzzle or incompleteness in a model and began to take it up for  sensemaking (Odden & Russ, 2017). It differed from the other structures in that it could not be explicitly planned for or introduced as a structure, but emerged from moments within other activity st ructures. Nonetheless, it had recognizable characteristics and ways of shaping activity. In these cases, the ele ments and relations of the model became tools to think with. This typically involved participants negotiating the role of the initial modeler’s intent or ideas, but they then moved from using pronouns like “you” and “he or she” and toward “it” and “they,” as the elements in the model became the active agents, for example, Ashton asking Tatum, “Is there any sort of motion, like going on here? Because like, it just looks like it sort of just-is to ice. Because I can see that there are some air m olecules at the top but I don’t really see a force that is pushing them up.” In another conversation in Ms. Lily’s room, Rafael began revising his own model, noting, “the third [water level] needed to be bigger.” He worked over 16 lines of ta lk with Ms. Lily to construct the relationship betw een the water level and molecules using his (now problematic) model. Ms. Lily worked to distance Rafael from the model, referring to “the way you drew it when you didn’t know the informatio n, which how could you, we hadn’t done the investigation.” These  conversations often involved intense participation,  including understanding what was going on in a model, and seemed most likely to result in collective development of puzzles or progress. They also provided opportunities for participants tonegotiate evaluation and to indicate value for uncertainty and incomplete ideas by playing with ideas, co-constructing, and acknowledging risk (Krist & Suárez, 2018). However, teachers sometimes experienced discomfort, partly because the intensity of partici pation often engaged just a few participants willing or able to claim the conversational space  (a teacher and student  in one example; three white boys in another). Teachers were likely to end these moments by beginni ng to call on different participants or shifting to another structure, and often indicated in reflections that they experienced tensions or a sense of dissatisfaction with participation.

## Discussion

In this paper, we took seriously statements from te achers like Sylvia, who muses, “ We all sort jump to that relationship piece which is a big part of argumentation but not something you would ever talk about” (Chowning, 2023). That is, we  worked from the idea that relationality is central to students’ and teachers’ experiences yet understudied as an essential dimension of engaging in practices like modeling or argumentation (Krist, 2024). Further, we explored the idea that the structures of classroom conversations developed to support specific epistemic framings or progress also shape particular relational possibilities and challenges. The analysis underlines the need for further research a nd expanded design perspectives regarding the role of evaluation and critique in sense-making. Evaluation is a common –and necessary –aspect of modeling practice in classrooms. From an epistemic point of view, because models are always incomplete and partial, they are especially productive for the gaps-and coherence-based work of sensemaking (Odden & Russ, 201 7; Manz & Georgen, 2013). Yet,  this brings risk and may conflict with framings  (including indi vidual, skill-based framings) typical in school.  Over the course of our work, each teacher tried out  strategies to mitigate risk in presentations, such as using anonymous models, aski ng students to state similarities and differences w ithout evaluating, or having students  practice giving feedback in supportive ways. Howeve r, some strategies appeared to limit students’ epistemic and relational roles a nd actions, which often led to disengagement and te achers stepping in with relational messages based on aesth etic school values such as quietly listening to eac h other. As we continue to work with educators to support deep engagement in science practice, we  likely need to more explicitly invite conversations about, and design for, the relational dimension of evaluation and critique. We alsoneed to better understand youth’s perspectives –we recognize that a limitation is that we were able  only to draw conclusions from talk and activity, rather than reporting on how youth experienced these conversations. The analysis also helps us develop our understanding of care in classroom w ork. Like others, we see examples of youth caring for each other within knowledge building  (Krist & Suárez, 2018; Pierson et al., 2024). And at the same time, we see examples of the complexity and tension-filled nature of risk and care that Puig de la Bellacasa (2017) highlights and that others have documented  (McKinney de Royston et al., 2017; Pierson et al., 2024; Wright et al., 2018). Puig de la Bellacasa argues that “reclaiming care is to keep it grounded in practical engagements with situated material  conditions that often expose tensions” (p. 11).  This echoes the difficulty teachers experienced in enacting care for persons (rather than aesthetic care) when they used strategies like anonymous models or similarities and differences. It resonates with the hesitations, questions, and contradictions that permeated teachers’ and students’ experiences with model presentations and thinking-with-models.

Using situated approaches to care to consider intertwined epistemic and relational work can help us take seriously the complexity of teaching and understand  how teachers experience the multiple, and potentia lly conflicting, dimensions of science practices. To support deeper, more equitable, and more caring classroom work, we must learn from moments when teachers ask students to compare models rather than  evaluate them, or when they end an episode of students  thinking-with-models. Further, we can learn from patterns in the framings, and tensions among framings, that teachers and students  take up within different structures of conversations –even, and especially, when these are not what we sought from an epistemic perspective.
