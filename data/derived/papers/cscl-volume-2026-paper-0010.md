# Designing Multi-Agent AI Systems for Collaborative Learning: Strategic Questioning Patterns and Cognitive Development in Large-Scale Online Learning

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
As large-scale online learning environments increasingly integrate AI technologies to support collaborative learning, understanding how learners strategically interact with different AI agent roles becomes critical for effective system design. Grounded in Bloom’s Taxonomy and Graesser and Person’s question taxonomy, this study examines questioning strategies employed by 411 doctoral students interacting with three distinct AI agents — Teacher, Assistant, and Companion—in a large-scale online course, and compares cognitive development trajectories between AI-mediated and human peer dialogues. Using clustering analysis on 224 AI-directed questions and sequential analysis of cognitive levels coded via Bloom’s Taxonomy, we identified three strategic patt erns: Teacher-Focused Quick-Response Strategy (35.7%), Assistant-Focused In-Depth Strategy (27.7%), and Teacher-Focused In-Depth Strategy (36.6%). AI-involved dialogues showed higher cognitive-level variability (average progress of 0.48) compared to humandialogues (0.02), suggesting AI agents accelerate cognitive transitions while human interactions provide stability. These findings illuminate design principles for multi-agent systems that balance AI efficiency with human collaborative depth.

The inte gration of multi-agent AI systems in large-scale online learning environments represents a promising approach to addressing persistent challenges of learner isolation and limited interaction. As platforms serving millions of learners globally face completion rates below 10% (Shah, 2024), AI agents powered by large language models offer role-specific support —teachers for explanatory depth, assistants for procedural guidance, and companions for peer-like encouragement. These multi-agent architectures mirror the diverse social roles present in traditional collaborative learning contexts, potentially enabling scalable support for collaborative knowledge construction in massive open learning environments.

Despite growing adoption of AI agents in large-scale collaborative learning, two critical knowledge gaps limit our understanding of their educational impact. First, while research has examined general AI chatbot usage patterns (Goel & Polepeddi, 2021), we lack empirical evidence on how learners strategically differentiate their questioning approaches across role-specific AI agents. Understanding such strategic differentiation matters because questioning behavior reflects learners’ metacognitive awareness of what knowledge they need and from whom they might best obtain it. If learners treat all agents uniformly, multi-agent architectures may offer no advantage over single-agent systems; if learners differentiate strategically, this informs how designers shoul d specialize agent roles to scaffold distinct learning needs. Second, existing studies comparing AI-mediated and human peer interactions focus primarily on learning outcomes (Yan et al., 2024), but fail to characterize the dynamic cognitive progression patterns within dialogues. Understanding trajectory differences is pedagogically important because it can reveal whether AI scaffolding promotes sustained, deep engagement or merely accelerates surface-level transitions—information critical for deciding when to deploy AI support versus human collaboration in instructional design.

Specifically, we operationalize cognitive development as shifts in the cognitive level of learner discourse, coded using Bloom’s revised Taxonomy (Krathwohl, 2002), across sequential turns within a dialogue. This operationalization captures the dynamic tra jectory of thinking within interactions rather than static pre-post outcomes. We examine two research questions: (1) What role-differentiated questioning strategies do learners employ when interacting with multiple AI agents? (2) How do cognitive level tra jectories differ between AI-mediated dialogues and human peer dialogues in collaborative knowledge construction? Using authentic data from 411 doctoral students engaged in collaborative knowledge construction in a large-scale online course, we employed clustering analysis to identify questioning strategies across AI Teacher, Assistant, and Companion roles, and sequential analysis to compare cognitive level sequences coded via Bloom’s Taxonomy between AI and humandialogues.

## Related work

Multi-agent AI systems in collaborative learning Multi-agent AI systems provide role-differentiated support that mimics diverse educational actors, addressing isolation in asynchronous environments by allowing learners to tailor queries to specific agent roles (Burton et al., 2024). Research emphasizes h ow large language models enhance collective intelligence through role-specialized interactions, though calls persist for granular analysis of query patterns across agents (Tian et al., 2022). Studies have visualized AI applications and identified clusters where multi-agent systems facilitate personalized pathways, yet understanding of how learners strategically select agents for different question types— such as goal-oriented queries to teachers versus instrumental ones to assistants—remains limited. This distinction matters because question type reflects the cognitive function learners seek: goal-oriented questions target conceptual understanding, while instrumental questions seek procedural guidance, and the alignment between question type and agent role can either support or hinder learning efficiency. Clustering analysis has emerged as a powerful tool in learning analytics for uncovering behavioral patterns in AI-augmented environments. Studies applying lag sequential and epistemic network analysis to collaborative problem-solving with AI have clustered behaviors into cognitive phases, identifying distinct interaction patterns correlating with performance (An & Zhang, 2024). Extensions to multi-agent contexts using sequence mining reveal that short-answer verification queries directed to assistants and l ong-answer causal exploration queries to teachers predict progression to higher cognitive levels in subsequent discourse (Li et al., 2024). These findings highlight clustering’s potential to reveal strategic differentiation in multi-agent querying. Cognitive trajectories in AI-mediated learning Cognitive levels in post-query dialogues, evaluated through Bloom’s Taxonomy (Krathwohl, 2002), reveal notable differences between AI and human interactions. Bloom’s revised Taxonomy classifies cognitive processes into six hierarchical levels —remembering, understanding, applying, analyzing, evaluating, and creating— providing a systematic framework for coding the cognitive demands of discourse. AI often facilitates higher-order processes through structured responses, while humans provide relational depth enhancing creativity. Studies analyzing trajectory paths in AI-tutoring sessions show dialogues escalating to analyzing and evaluating levels via scaffolded questions, contrasting with human-peer exchanges remaining at understanding levels due to variable expertise (Chen et al., 2022). Recent research introduces a cognitive paradox framework integrating Cognitive Load Theory with Bloom’s Taxonomy, explaining AI’s dual impact: while demonstrating a capacity for cognitive enhancement through adaptive scaffolding, AI simultaneously introduces risks of cognitive offloading that may erode critical thinking (Wang et al., 2024). Comparative research finds generative AI agents with scaffolding significantly enhance comprehension—aligning with higher Bloom levels —compared to passive or human-only conditions (Yan et al., 2024). Network analysis on group interactions reveals AI’s cognitive contributions surpass humans in structured exchanges but lag in spontaneity, highlighting trajectory differences (Feng, 2025). However, concerns about potential negative effects emerge, with research noting higher cognitive performance in AI dialogues but identifying metacognitive laziness risks, underscoring the need for hybrid models balancing efficiency and depth (Abbas et al., 2024). Identifying trajectory differences between AI-mediated and humandialogues is theoretically significant because it illuminates the mechanisms through which each modality supports or constrains cognitive progression, informing decisions about when and how to integrate AI agents within collaborative learning designs.

## Context and participants

This study utilized data from a large-scale general education course titled “China’s Educational Reform and Development” implemented in spring 2025 at a research university in China. The course structure included eight thematic modules delivered through 24 videos spanning 16 credit hours, with assessment comprising 60% ongoing performance—including 15% discussion participation—and 40% final examination. The course adopted a large-scale collaborative learning approach where learners engaged in ongoing collaborative knowledge construction activities through the platform’s discussion areas around course video content, including asking questions, sharing perspectives, responding to peers, and interacting with AI agents. Participants consisted of 411 first-year doctoral students who provided informed consent for research use of their learning data. The study was approved by the institutional review board.

7 This study employed an observational design analyzing naturally occurring interaction patterns. All 411 students had equal access to both AI agents and human peers throughout the course. Thus, both AI-involved and human-only dialogues could be generated by the same students, and the comparison reflects differences in interaction modality rather than between-subjects differences. We acknowledge that students who chose to interact with AI may differ from those who did not, representing a limitation inherent to naturalistic observation in authentic educational settings.

## Research Design Framework and Data Analysis Overview

To address the first research question, we conducted K-means clustering analysis on 224 AI-directed questions extracted from the 163 AI-involved sequences. ‘Valid’ questions were defined as student-initiated queries explicitly directed at a specific AI agent (excluding greetings, follow-up acknowledgments, and off-topic remarks). The unit of analysis for clustering was individual questions, characterized by two features: the target AI agent and question content coded using Graesser and Person’s (1994) question taxonomy. This taxonomy categorizes questions into types including concept completion, feature specification, instrumental/procedural, interpretation, and others based on the expected answer format and cognitive demand. We evaluated K values ranging from 2 to 6, computing silhouette scores for each candidate to identify the optimal number of clusters. 9 The optimal K value was determined through silhouette score optimization.  The silhouette score ranges from-1 to 1, where values closer to 1 indicate that data points are well-matched to their own cluster and poorly matched toneighboring clusters; values above 0.5 are generally considered indicative of reasonable clustering st ructure. Our analysis yielded K=3 with a silhouette score of 0.576, indicating adequate cluster separation. For the second research question, we conducted sequential analysis of cognitive level sequences in 163 AI-involved dialogues and 163 randomly sampled human-only dialogues. Random sampling was employed for human-only dialogues to create a comparison set matched in sample size to the AI-involved group, enabling parallel analysis while controlling for potential distortions from unequal group sizes. Cognitive levels were coded via Bloom’s revised Taxonomy (Krathwohl, 2002), assigning numerical values to six cognitive levels (1=remembering through 6=creating; 0=irrelevant content, defined as utterances not reflecting identifiable cognitive processing of course content, such as social greetings, platform navigation comments, or off-topic remarks). Cognitive progress was quantified as the difference between the last and first cognitive state in each sequence, with sequences classified as escalating (progress > 0), stable (= 0), or declining (< 0). We examined sequence length distributions, cognitive state distributions, and progression patterns to characterize differences between the two interaction modalities.

## Coding procedure

Comment coding utilized automated analysis with GPT-4.1 through the Optimized Large Language Model Coding (OLLM-C) framework, implementing systematic calibration based on human expert feedback  (Niu & Zhang, 2026). Initial manual coding by two independent researchers on stratified random samples achieved interrater reliability via Cohen’s Kappa of 0.82 for Bloom’s framework and 0.85 for question taxonomy. Following iterative calibration involving baseline coding, discrepancy analysis, and prompt enhancement, final accuracy assessments reached 91% for Bloom’s Taxonomy and 92% for question taxonomy against human-coded gold standards, enabling reliable automated coding of the full dataset. Strategic patterns in multi-agent questioning The clustering analysis identified three distinct questioning strategies employed by learners when interacting with AI agents. Among 224 valid AI-directed questions (the unit of analysis; note that individual learners could contribute questions to multiple clusters), AI Teacher received the majority (64.7%, n=145), followed by AI Companion (19.6%, n=44) and AI Assistant (15.6%, n=35). Predominant question types were instrumental/procedural (26.8%, n=60) and interpretation questions (13.8%, n=31), indicating preferences for practical, application-oriented inquiries rather than simple factual verification. Cluster 0: Teacher-Focused Quick-Response Strategy This cluster (n=80, 35.7%) demonstrated clear role differentiation with strong preference for AI Teacher (78.8%) supplemented by selective AI Companion use (21.2%), with no AI Assistant utilization. Dominant question types were concept completion (35.0%) and feature specification (30.0%), both requiring specific factual responses. This pattern suggests learners viewing AI Teacher as the primary source for rapid conceptual clarification, exemplified by questions like “When did vocational education first appear in our country?” The absence of complex questions indicates a tendency to reserve AI Teacher interactions for immediate knowledge gaps, reflecting an efficiency-maximizing approach to AI engagement. Cluster 1: Assistant-Focused In-Depth Strategy This cluster (n=62, 27.7%) demonstrated multi-agent use with balanced distribution: AI Assistant (56.5%) and AI Companion (43.5%) while not engaging AI Teacher. The predominant question type was instrumental/procedural (41.9%), followed by interpretation questions (14.5%), both requiring elaborate process-oriented responses. This reflects learners directing complex “how-to” questions to AI Assistant’s specialized procedural guidance, such as implementation strategies and practical applications. The substantial AI Companion use for similar complex questions suggests these learners view peer-level AI as providing alternative perspectives, reflecting a distributed approach to knowledge construction.

Cluster 2: Teacher-Focused In-Depth Strategy This cluster (n=82, 36.6%) demonstrated exclusive reliance on AI Teacher (100.0%) for comprehensive educational support across all question types. This cluster showed the highest concentration of complex questions, with instrumental/procedural (41.5%) and interpretation questions (26.8%) dominating. Unlike Cluster 1’s 10 distributed approach, Cluster 2 directed all complex inquiries exclusively to AI Teacher, suggesting a preference for centralized, consistent interaction with a single authoritative agent. This contrasts with Cluster 0, which also favored AI Teacher but fo r simpler factual questions, whereas Cluster 2 used AI Teacher for deeper analytical inquiries. Notably, Cluster 0 ’s inclusion of AI Companion (21.2%) alongside AI Teacher may reflect learners occasionally seeking peer-level validation after obtaining factual answers, whereas Cluster 2’s exclusive Teacher reliance suggests that for complex analytical questions, learners preferred a single authoritative source to maintain coherence rather than seeking supplementary perspectives. Humandialogues demonstrated greater sequence length diversity (mean = 2.66, range 1– 6) compared to AI dialogues (mean = 2.07, range 1– 5), suggesting that peer-to-peer interactions naturally extend cognitive discourse through sustained reciprocal engagement. Figure 4 Comparison of Cognitive Level Sequences: Human-Only vs. AI-Involved Dialogues As illustrated in Figure 4, humandialogues (left panel) exhibit longer sequences with transitions between cognitive levels characterized by sustained presence at synthesis and analysis levels (represented by purple and yellow bands). In contrast, AI dialogues (right panel) display shorter sequences with more frequent shifts between cognitive levels, including notable amounts of irrelevant content (gray). Quantitatively, the cognitive state distribution in humandialogues showed synthesis comprising 33.0%, knowledge 20.6%, and comprehension 13.5%, indicating sustained higher-order thinking alongside foundational knowledge engagement. AI dialogues showed synthesis at 23.4% but with substantially higher irrelevant content (24.0% vs. 8.2% in humandialogues). This elevated proportion likely reflects the structural characteristics of human-AI interaction. Learners frequently included greetings, agent-selection commands such as "@AI Teacher," and meta-conversational remarks such as expressions of gratitude or acknowledgment that are inherent to the format but carry no cognitive content. Consequently, while AI can stimulate complex thinking, the interaction overhead of engaging AI agents inflates the share of non-cognitive discourse within these sequences. Cognitive progression analysis revealed differences in trajectory patterns. Humandialogues demonstrated near-zero average cognitive progress (0.02) with 60.0% of sequences maintaining stable cognitive 11 levels, 20.5% showing escalation, and 19.5% declining. This near-zeronet change, combined with the high proportion of stable sequences, suggests that human interactions tend to sustain engagement at consistent cognitive levels. AI dialogues showed higher average cognitive progress (0.48) with a different distribution: 35.2% escalating, 46.5% stable, and 18.3% declining. The higher escalation rate in AI dialogues (35.2% vs. 20.5%) suggests that AI interactions may prompt more frequent upward cognitive transitions, potentially through structured scaffolding that guides learners through cognitive hierarchies. However, this should be interpreted cautiously: a higher average progress score does not necessarily indicate deeper learning, as it may also reflect rapid but potentially superficial transitions between levels. This study illuminates how learners navigate multi-agent AI systems and how these interactions shape cognitive level trajectories compared to human peer dialogues. The identification of three questioning strategies extends prior research on role-differentiated AI use (Burton et al., 2024). Unlike earlier broad categorizations (Long & Magerko, 2023), our clustering approach reveals alignment between question types and agent roles: efficiency-12 oriented factual queries directed primarily to AI Teacher versus procedural questions distributed across Assistant and Companion. While Clusters 0 and 2 both favored AI Teacher, they did so for qualitatively different purposes—factual clarification versus deep analytical inquiry —suggesting that role differentiation operates at the level of question complexity rather than merely agent  selection. This finding complicates a simple narrative of strategic agent differentiation. Rather than primarily distinguishing between agents, learners appear to develop distinct interaction modes with a preferred agent, adapting their questioning depth to match different learning needs within a single agent relationship. For multi-agent system design, this implies that the cognitive versatility of individual agents may matter as much as the diversity of available roles. The cognitive level analysis quantifies AI’s impact using Bloom’s Taxonomy. The higher variability in AI dialogues (average progress 0.48) compared to humandialogues (0.02) is consistent with findings from intelligent tutoring systems where structured sca ffolding accelerates transitions to higher cognitive levels (Cukurova et al., 2019). Yet the negative progression in the Assistant-Focused In-Depth cluster (–0.17) reveals that multi-agent use may introduce coordination challenges, echoing difficulties noted in distributed AI systems (Chu et al., 2022). Humandialogues’ stability (60.0% stable sequences) aligns with meta-analyses highlighting human interactions’ strength in fostering sustained engagement through diverse perspectives (Choi et al., 2024). However, we acknowledge that stability could reflect either deep processing or stagnation, and without direct learning outcome measures, we cannot definitively determine which interpretation applies. Recent evidence from randomized controlled trials demonstrates that well-designed AI tutoring systems can achieve substantial learning gains (Kestin et al., 2025). However, systematic reviews reveal concerning overreliance patterns (Wang et al., 2024). These align with our observation that while AI dialogues show higher escalation rates, they also exhibit greater irrelevant content and variability. The Teacher-Focused In-Depth strategy’s pattern of maintaining cognitive coherence (52.0% stable sequences) s uggests centralized AI architectures may be preferable for sustained cognitive engagement, providing consistent scaffolding without the coordination complexity of multi-agent interactions.

From a CSCL(Computer-Supported Collaborative Learning) perspective, these findings raise important questions about the collaborative dimension of AI-mediated learning. While multi-agent systems can provide roledifferentiated support at scale, the collaborative knowledge construction between human learners—characterized by sustained engagement and balanced cognitive state distributions —appears to offer unique benefits not easily replicated by AI agents. Future work should more explicitly theorize how indivi dual learner-AI interactions contribute to collective knowledge building within the broader learning community. Several limitations should be noted. First, the relatively small proportion of AI-involved interactions (1.4%) may limit generalizability; students who voluntarily engaged with AI may differ systematically from those who did not. Second, our observational design cannot establish causal relationships between interaction modality and cognitive outcomes. In particular, the higher cognitive escalation observed in AI dialogues may reflect characteristics of students who chose to engage with AI, such as higher motivation or stronger prior knowledge, rather than effects of the AI interaction itself.  Third, without direct learning outcome measures, interpretations regarding cognitive “development” are limited to within-dialogue trajectory patterns. Fourth, the 224 AI-directed questions, while sufficient for exploratory clustering, represent a modest sample that may not capture the full range of questioning behaviors. Future research employing experimental designs with larger AI interaction samples and linked outcome measures would strengthen these findings. This study provides analysis of learner interactions with multi-agent AI systems in online education, identifying three questioning strategies that reflect differentiated approaches to AI role capabilities. While AI dialogues show higher rates of cognitive  level escalation through structured scaffolding, they also introduce variability and irrelevant content compared to human interactions’ stability. The exploratory finding that centralized single-agent interaction may support more coherent cognitive engage ment thandistributed multi-agent approaches offers a counterintuitive but practically valuable insight for system design. For practice, results offer guidance for multi-agent system design, suggesting that systems should consider providing streamlined interfaces that reduce the cognitive overhead of navigating multiple agents. Educators can implement hybrid models combining AI’s scalability with human collaborative elements through structured alternation between AI and peer discussions.
