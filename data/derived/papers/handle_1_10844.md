# Affect as Outcome: Centering Emotion and Equity in learning Sciences Research

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Our field often takes an instrumentalist approach to the role of affect in learning. This session examines the topic of emotions in learning environments, but we do not frame emotion as an antecedent or lever for engagement or motivation to learn. Rather, the papers in this session treat emotion as a critical outcome, and as a vital focus for research in and of itself. Whether the learning environments in question are designed or naturally occurring, the perspective and experience of learners takes center stage. Results across the collection of papers demonstrate that emotion is not only a critical factor to investigate in learning, but it can also be an equity issue as well, which makes it all the more worthy of attention in future research.

Symposium theme and overview Learning scientists have recently been investigating the critical importance of emotion in learning, forwarding the role of phenomena like joy, pride, frustration, anx iety, grief, trust, affinity, dignity, righteous an ger, and others (e.g., Curnow, Fernandes, Dunphy, & Asher, 2021; Va kil & McKinney de Royston, 2019; Espinosa, Vossughi, Rose, & Poza, 2020). Other scholars have studied or designed technological tools to emphasize and scaffold socioemotional learning (SEL) as a key component of collaboration, self-regulation, meta-cognition, and other aspects of school success (Farber, 2021; Zhang & Kaufman, 2017). Still other scholars have built models that go beyond an instrumentalist approach framing affec t as a catalyst or motivator for learning, and they h ave argued instead for emotion as a primary focus of study in and of i tself (Curnow & Vea, 2020). In this session, our collectio n of scholars and papers aims to push the conversation again, such that we acknowledge and investigate how emotion, on the one hand, can be  the primary goal  of the learning environments and opportunities we i nvestigate and/or create, and on the other, can sometimes  override other goals to the extent that emotion ends up beco ming the major result. In either case, we ignore affect-as-outcome at our peril. There are three frameworks in existing literature that support our approach and that we will use to frame this session. First is Mary Helen Immordino-Yang’s work on the biological basis of emotion and the role of emotion in learning, identifying the machinery through whic h experiences activate attention and become hardwired in memory (Immordino-Yang, Darling-Hammond, & Krone, 2019). Viewed through this lens, emotion is not just a lever or antecedent for learning; emo tion is learning (Immordino-Yang, 2016). Second, we look to Tanner Vea’s (2020) work on the learning of emotion as a s ociocultural practice, as we situate learner emotio n within institutions, activities, and communities housing a variety of learning environments and learning outcomes. Finally, third is Ruha Benjamin’s Race After Technology, which articulates how technologies and technologica l systems are inscribed with discriminatory mechanism s that privilege some groups and exclude or alienat e others (Benjamin, 2019). Viewed through this lens, it is n ot surprising that we often see learners and participants in our technology-infused projects experience a wide gamut of emotion al responses as outcomes. Benjamin’s critical volume forces us to acknowledge that equity work in  the learning sciences often cannot help but be emo tional work, and vice versa, for our participants and for ourselves. Inequities of anxiety and distrust in undergraduates’ experiences with online proctored exams Yeon Jade Lee aand Cynthia Carter Ching Racialized mechanisms are embedded in video-based online exam proctoring technologies  in higher education, such as cases in which individuals with darker skin are less easily identifiable on video and thus more likely to be flagged for cheating (Yoder-Himes et al, 2022). Greater struggles with COVID-remote learning and assessment in higher education have also been associated with student family income inequality and digital inequality (Katz, Jordan, & Ognayova, 2021). While these findings provide a systems perspective on the impacts of online exams, our goal was to investigate the lived experience of online exam proctoring from an individual student perspective. For this analysis, we focused in particular on the emotional load of preparing for and engaging in proctored exams. Frameworks We employ cultural-historical activity theory (Engeström, 1993) to examine and articulate how the mandates of remote-proctored online exams shift the division of labor and the goal of creating cheat-proof environments from the university and the faculty on campus to the students and their homes. We also draw on the theory of racialized organizations (Ray, 2019) to describe how these shi fts place a greater emotional and material burden o n nondominant and low-income students. Finally, we build on theories of  human cognition and human experience as inherently embodied (Nathan, 2021; Stoltz, 2015) to forward the importance of examining how emotion is related to the embodied experience of taking proctored online exams – a perspective that might seem less obvious, given a prevailing tendency within online learning and assessment research to focus on on-screen activity.

## Methods and data source

We investigated undergraduate students’ experiences  of online exam proctoring during COVID-remote instruction (2020-2022) at a West Coast university. The analysis draws on 16 retrospective qualitative interviews conducted in winter of 2023 with undergraduates who experienced at least one instance of remote exam proctoring during COVID while learning from home. Interviews w ere conducted via Zoom by the first author and were approximately 30 minutes in length. Zoom audio file s were then transcribed, and groun ded theory analysis (Charmaz, 2014) was conducted using the final transcripts and MaxQDA software for thematic coding.

## Results

Negative emotional responses to online exam proctoring were more prevalent among students with less privilege and fewer resources.  In our interviews, low-income and non-dominant students described the intense anxiety involved in exhausting ordeals of attempting to man age their busy, crowded home environments and inade quate technology supports, so as to not be falsely flagged for cheating. Students in live and AI video proctoring situations described feeling as though they had to police their bodies and eye movements, in order to “maintain focus on the screen” and “look studious” at all times during the  exam. In some cases, students said that they spent  more time attempting to ameliorate the inequities of the proc toring technology than they spent studying for the exam itself, while others said that feeling anxious and inherently untrustworthy during proctored exams constituted their most salient memory of those experiences.

## Scholarly significance

The prevalence of online courses and online exams i n higher education has dramatically increased from preCOVID rates. Investigating students’ experiences wi ll help inform policy and practice moving forward, with the goal of designing more equitable testing technologies and environment s for this new reality. If we ignore the discriminatory nature of these activity systems and the compounded emotional burden they place on students, we risk adding yet another piece to “the new Jim Code,” (Benjamin, 2019) in education. Navigating charged topics: Analyzing social-emotional talk in diverse online teams Tugce Aldemir Our relationships with others are influenced by dif ferences, leading to empathy toward ingroup members  and biases toward outgroup members (Dovidio et al., 2012). Bridging these differences and fostering shared meaning is an essential educational objective (Gurin et al., 2013). Recent studies in computer-supported collaborative learning (CSCL) have explored the use of technology to moderate charged discussions (Firer et al., 2021; Slakmon & Schwarz, 2019) and intersubjective meaning-making during historically charged debates (Pollack & Kolikant, 2012). However, there is a lack of a concrete conceptualization to evaluate the quality of shared meaning-making, particularly in discussions about race, gender, and identity. This study offers the first step in our larger agenda to a theoretically-informed and data-driven model to assess communication patterns and b ehaviors during charged discussions. Emotional and social processes gain significance in discussions around politically-charged topics, as different social  identity groups bring in their emotions, creating a symmetries within teams (Nagda & Zúñiga, 2003). Thus, this study examines social and emotion al behaviors associated with intergroup and charged discussions in a text-based CSCL environment.

## Theoretical framework: Social and emotional talk

Emphasizing a positive socio-emotional climate, self-disclosure, and risk-taking, this framework highlights the importance of handling emotions during identity-related discussions. Team members' ability to regul ate their social and emotional interactions i s critical in the absence of a facilitator (Järveno ja & Järvelä, 2009; Järvelä et al., 2010). Social and emotional talk is conceptualized as the degree of engagement in self-disclosure, risk-taking, and mutual support.

## Methods and data sources

The study employs a multiple case study design in an undergraduate multicultural psychology course (Yin, 2018). Thirty-five students were divided into 13 diverse teams to participate in synchronous collaborative tasks using the CSCL environment CREATE. A t rained coder evaluated the discussions and provided  feedback.  The analysis involved initial coding to explore how teams engaged in social and emotional talk, followed by focused coding to determine behaviors related to intergroup discussio ns. Three major categories emerged: personal engagement, social connectedness, and constructive engagement.

## Results

Personal Engagement (31.59%) focused on self-disclosure and risk-taking. Associated behaviors included sharing sensitive narratives, acknowledging biases and privileges, expressing a lack of knowledge, and exhibiting identityrelated guilt or defensiveness. Social Connectednes s (12.01%) involved emotional reactions and support  among team members. Common behaviors were expressing symp athy/empathy, emotional reactions, and emotional support. Constructive Engagement (56.40%) emphasize d creating a productive atmosphere. The main behavi ors were appreciating others' sharing, validating before diverging and appreciating group performance.

## Scholarly significance

The findings revealed desirable behaviors of personal engagement, such as active listening and critical reflection, and social connectedness behaviors that foster posi tive emotions and relationship building. Constructi ve engagement included appreciation and critical evalu ation of group performance. Understanding these communication patterns is crucial in promoting incl usive dialogue and empathy across diverse CSCL team s engaging in politically-charged discussions. This study lays the groundwork  for further conceptualizing the quality of intergroup discussions in CSCL environme nts, requiring multiple case comparisons to develop  an operational model. By equipping learners with these insights, educators can support the development of essential regulatory skills and foster an inclusive learning environment. Frustration as a catalyst for socially engaged exploratory learning Ciara Thomas Murphy and Lee M. Martin When we envision and study vibrant experiences in making and tinkering, we may search for moments of insight and breakthrough, where people meet goals and feel a sense of progress and agency. But there are no breakthroughs without barriers, and in this inquiry, we highlight the uniquely generative function of frustration in playful learning. Tinkering activities typically aim to foster learner-driven exploration and experimentation. It is important to design learning environments with space for a full range of emotional experiences as learners navigate the conceptual, material, and social terrain. We argue for a view of agency that empowers learners to recognize the value of emotions, including frustration, at the leading edge of learning.

## Theoretical framework

Our analysis is situated within literature on learning through tinkering (Vossoughi & Bevan, 2014) and educators’ reflective professional learning (Ball & Forzani, 2009; Kinsella, 2007). For this investigation of the role of frustration in playful, creative learning, we draw upon research on epistemic emotions (Muis et al., 2018) and epistemic affect and meta-affect (Jaber & Hammer, 2016), with focus on the flow of affective states that unfold over time during episodes of deep engagement in learning (D’Mello & Graesser, 2012). In previous research on educators’ professional learning experiences with tinkering activities offered by the Exploratorium through a pandemic-era “Tinkering at Home” initiative, we found that emotions were indeed central to participants’ learning. Prior analysis identified characteristic waypoints in the tinkering process, from initial inspiration and exploration of personally meaningful pathways to moments of feeling stuck and seeking resolution (Thomas Murphy & Martin, In press).

## Methods and data sources

We conducted two rounds of interviews with twelve educators, placing focus on their sense-making and use of resources. While all participants spoke of positive feelings — curiosity, inspiration, excitement — most also discussed feeling frustration, especially around moments of impasse. This paper delves into an additional thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2022) focused on frustration, which turned out to be integral to participants’ engagement and eventual satisfaction and enjoyment of the activities.

## Results

Frustration often arose in moments of especially lively engagement, when participants cared deeply about what they were doing. Typically a participant had become excited about a particular vision they wanted to pursue, then found a disjunction between their vision and what they could realize with the available materials and resources. As nearly all participants recounted, moments of frustration were affectively charged in a way that sparked rethinking and adaptation of ideas.

Notably, many participants reflected that their frustration contributed to a greater sense of meaning, accomplishment, and satisfaction once they were able to resolve the impasse and imagine alternate pathways. A crucial feature of this generative experience of frustration as a catalyst for exploration was the presence of strong relationships within the learning environment. Supportive social connections scaffolded participants ’ ongoing engagement by increasing their capacity to tolerate frustration and grapple with challenges. Within this context, frustration supplied a burst of emotional energy that opened new possibilities for creative play and deepened participants’ sense of meaning in their learning.

## Scholarly significance

We argue that in conditions of strong social support, frustration may serve a unique function of catalyzing new forms of exploration and sensemaking. In order to design learning environments that foster creative agency, it is vital to study the full range of learners ’emotions, both positive and negative. The field may benefit from greater understanding of the role of frustration in exploratory learning, and the conditions that enable learners to make use of frustration as a catalyst for creativity.

Empowering youth through play-based, collaborative design Marcela Borge This paper describes the creation of play-based, after school design clubs that empower diverse students to make sense of and redefine how they interact with technology as they engage in human centered-design. The main aim of these spaces is to introduce learners to theorie s of emotion, design, regulation, innovation, and c ollaborative problem solving by grounding these abstract theorie s to real-world problems and interactions that learners experience as part of their club experiences.

## Theoretical framework

We draw on sociocultural theory focused on group co gnition (Stahl, 2006), sociometacognition (Borge et  al.,

2018) and critical pedagogy (Freire, 1996). We reco gnize the nested and social nature of cognition and  the

importance of helping learners understand and regulate their own socio-technical processes to develop agency to navigate power structures that surround them (Borge, 2023; Borge et al., 2020; Borge & Xia, 2023).

## Methods

In our environment, learners play with technologies  as fodder for working with a team to design produc ts that meet the needs of different “users.” For example, learners have used Legos, Minecraft, and computer aided design and drafting tools to design a ga rden for a family with a deep commitment to environ mental causes. Each team uses human-centered design processes to develop profiles of their “users” to ensure designs meet their needs, i.e., teams research different ways that a garden can be environmentally friendly; they create butterfly gardens, use native plants, create elaborate systems to catch rainwater, etc., all while ensuring that they take the safet y and needs of the family into consideration.

These activities present emotional challenges for t eams, ages 8-12, because most have not worked in collaborative problem-solving teams where resources and ideas must be combined towards one joint product that they care about. Thus, collaboration and the sharin g/use of technological resources presents daily pro blems that we use to discuss key to design/innovation processes and theories. Activities are structured so learners “play” for an hour and then reflect each day for 20-30 minutes, discussing problems that arise, making sense of sociotechnical/collaborative interactions, and working with facilitators to devise strateg ies to prevent or correct future problems. In so doing, we introduce a variet y of real-world issues to help children understand the design s that make up their world, how they choose to intera ct with it, and the power they have to redesign the  world around them.

Data sources Each team’s interactions are recorded over time and analyzed via interaction analysis (Jordan & Henderson, 1995). We have collected data from over 20 teams over five years. We create content logs that identify design talk, joint decision-making, and problems that interfere with design.

## Results

We identify core instructional practices and have f ound that learners can learn to regulate interactio ns over time in these play-based spaces and develop deeper understanding of key design processes.

## Scholarly significance

These spaces go beyond diversity, equity, and inclu sion in STEM towards empowering the next generation  of designers and helping them to develop critical coll aborative problem solving skills.  Balancing emotional investment and collaborative negotiation is key to this effort, given the importance of empowering designers who care deeply about the human-centered issues they are designing for. Agency as outcome: Social emotional learning in and through the arts Erica Halverson and Tracey Bullington Social emotional learning (SEL) is emerging as an important set of outcomes to design for, document and analyze across k-12 learning environments (CASEL, 2013). Most research-to-date on SEL outcomes have focused either on institution-wide assessments (Haggerty et al., 2011) or on the identification of risk factors and student health, rather than growth and outcomes (Arthur et al., 200 2). Bringing a learning sciences perspective to the  study of SEL affords a focus on the processes of learning and evidence of how learners engage with SEL-related outcomes through participation in activities.

Arts-based learning environments and activities are espe cially well-suited to supporting the social and emotional well-being of learners (Farrington et al., 2019; Lashley  & Halverson, 2021). In this paper, we explore how young peoples’ participation in artist-in-residence programs designed for creative expression allows them to develop agency, a core concept in SEL learning outc omes (Jagers et al., 2019). We demonstrate how elem entary school students develop and exhibit agency in the c reative work they produce and in their reflections on their creative processes.

Framework A study of agency allows us to see a) the degree of  autonomy students identify in their art-making; b) the mechanism for the autonomy they have and, c) the causal relationship between actions students take and the effect of those actions on their perspecti ve (Konopasky & Sheridan, 2016). We have been inspi red to see agency as an indicator of SEL outcomes, because the concept of agency stretches across psychological and sociocultural models for understanding human behavior (Jagers et al., 20 19; Konopasky & Sheridan, 2016). Our work also aims  to construct SEL as psychological (outcomes for individuals exogenous to a given learning practice) and as cultural (embedded in the learning practice itself). In other words, to understand SEL we must understand how participants are engaged in personal and collective acts of self-advocacy, self-determination, and resistance within a given learning experience.

## Methods and data sources

This study is part of a larger NEA Research lab project that seeks to understand the social and emotional learning outcomes associated with participation in artist-in-residence programs. The study uses mixed methods fo r collecting data including large scale survey data, classroom observations, interviews with students and teachers, and student work samples including stories, drawings, theatre and dance performances, and drum circles. For this paper, we focus on the 41 students in our sample who participated at least two interviews and who produced original creative work during the resi dency programs. We focus on linguistic data as evid ence of the expression of agency in situ (Konopasky & Sheridan, 2016).

## Results

Our results centered on three themes. First, making authentic choices is a mechanism to enact agency. Interviews demonstrated students’ interest in and appreciation for making choices about what they create, how they create it, and with whom. Second, students demonstrate agency through the use of subject-focused language. In both what they make and in their talk about their creative expressions, students talked about their work in the individual and collective first person – e.g. “When I grow up to be a writer.” Finally third, a linguistic approach can attend to multimodal expressions of agency. Initial linguistic analyses of agency focused on the spoken w ord (Konopasky & Sheridan, 2016). Our analysis demonstrates that s tudents express agency multimodally and these multi modal forms of expression can be documented.

## Scholarly significance

Locating SEL outcomes such as agency within the con text of learning environments and activities allows  us to make direct connections between processes and practices of learning and the emotional development of y oung people. We also continue to demonstrate that learning in and through the arts is crucial for all learners.
