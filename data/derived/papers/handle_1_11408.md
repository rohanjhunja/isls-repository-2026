# Using the Conjecture Map for Designing an Anti-Racist Teacher Professional Development Intervention

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Conjecture maps have long been considered instrumental in systematizing and clarifying various design and theoretical conjectures in design-based research. However, beyond enhancing design-based research, there is opportunity to clarify how the conjecture map may be used to design an intervention itself. Following the backward design logic from curricular and instructional planning, we outline here a process followed to design the first iteration of a multi-year anti-racist teacher professional development intervention that articulated the high-level conjecture, outcomes, mediating processes, and embodiments, in that order. We also present our current conjecture map and invite other researchers and practitioners to engage with in the pursuit of more effective designs for anti-racist teacher learning.

## Introduction

Conjecture mapping (Sandoval, 2014) has become an e ssential tool in helping researchers make explicit various assumptions, conjectures, and outcomes that they are working with. It has been deployed in various design-based research contexts (Chang et al., 2024; Lee et al., 2022; Wilkerson, 2017). These extensive applications of the idea are likely because the concept added to our understanding of how to do design research, as Sandoval (2014) stated they wished to do.

However, there still appears to be some space for clarifying the opportunities for deploying the conjecture map framework in the service of the design itself before it is useful in design research. The complicating factor here is the directionality of the arrows built into  a conjecture map which move left to right from hig h-level conjecture to the outcomes. Does that mean that this is the direction in which the design process also has to move and that designers must populate the map starting w ith the high-level conjecture and move left to right towards the outcomes? In cases where there are explicit design conjectures to be tested, this may certainly be a possibility. However, there may be projects that have explicit o utcomes they want to pursue but are flexible betwee n the conjectures that will most effectively get them to those outcomes. The left-to-right directionality would not be effective in such a design process because that is the direction in which the conjectures operate, but are not necessarily designed.  We hope that this paper will be useful to the teams engaged in this kind of research. In the following sections, we describe how an inter disciplinary team of faculty, postdoctoral scholars, graduate students, and undergraduates used Sandoval ’s (2014) conjecture mapping framework to design th e first iteration of an anti-racist professional development intervention for K-12 teachers. However, we did not move left to right in our design process and, instead, f ollowed a backward design (Wiggins & McTighe, 2005)  logic borrowed from curricular and instructional planning.

Moreover, there is much research and scholarship on anti-racist professional development designs for K12 teachers (Irby, 2018; Seglem & Garcia, 2015; Villavicencio et al.,  2020). However, as Clarke et al. (2023) pointed out, there is a need for more research and PD practices on supporti ng teachers in understanding the historicity of race and racism in the US classroom and enabling them to actively resist the systems of oppression that are operating in their classrooms. Therefore, while the field continues to be engaged in the pursuit of effective design principles and designs themselves, w e offer here our design via a conjecture map that o ther scholars engaged in similar work can use, contest, and refine further. To make the contributions we state above, we ask th e research question-how was the conjecture-map used to collaboratively design an anti-racist professional development intervention for K-12 teachers?

## Methods

We report on the first iteration of a 5-year design-based (Barab & Squire, 2004) Research Practice Part nership with a school district in Southern California. The partnership is part of a district-wide multi-year professional development on equity and antiracism. This project focuses on supporting teacher learning about racism  and its embodiment in classroom pedagogy drawing on Clarke & Hollingsworth’s model of teacher professional growth (Clarke & Hollingsworth, 2001). Our aim is to engag e teachers in the analysis of their facilitation of dialogic pedagogy as a site of social reproduction, but also  a site of expansive possibilities for justice-centered dialogic pedagogy. Conjecture map and the intervention design process When we started the design process with the conject ure map, it was natural for us to start with the hi gh-level conjecture as the foundation of the design. However, in the lack of clear guidance in the field on how  to use the conjecture map framework for de sign and given that many members of the team were trained as educators,  we relied on the backward design process for curricula r and instructional planning (Wiggins & McTighe, 20 05). However, instead of a curricular or lesson-planning template, we used the conjecture map framework to engage in a similar thought process. As a result, our seco nd step was to design the outcomes before moving to wards the more in-flux features of the intervention  like the mediating processes and outcomes. Outcomes The next step after we had settled on the first iteration of the high-level conjecture was to then clearly articulate what outcomes we expected from the intervention. Ou r initial guiding question for this conversation wa s, “What are teacher-learning outcomes that challenge/reverse their prac tices?”. Once we started analyzing our responses to the guiding question, we realized we needed a cl ear distinction between ‘process’ and ‘outcomes’ si nce both were coming up in our responses. Through careful co nsideration, we split our responses into the two categories of process and outcome. We then analyzed our outcom es and found two main categories: 'external justice ’ and ‘internal justice’. These correspond to our outcomes 1 and 2 in Figure 1 (conjecture map figure). Outcome 4 was added to these two since it captured the eventual intended outcome of the intervention. However, we felt the need for a bridge between outcomes 1 & 2 and 4. Therefore, outcome 3 was added later as an intermediate step between awareness to embodiment.

During these conversations, we wondered if we wante d to articulate only teacher-related outcomes or if we wanted to articulate student-related outcomes as well. One of the main questions  we asked in these conversations was, “What kind of student learning o utcomes need to be pursued to challenge racial inju stice?”. However, whatever answers we gave to this question, it was clear to us that we would not be able to measure any of these outcomes so we dropped the idea of having student-related outcomes as part of our intervention design. Mediating processes Once the outcomes were articulated, we moved on to theorizing which mediating processes would be gener ative for the teachers to go through for the desired outcomes to be achieved. At this stage, we already had some starting points that we could build on in the process column of the process vs outcomes conversation in the previous step. We thematically organized all of our theorized proc esses and found the themes of: emotional safety, an tiracism ZPD, interpretive tools/antiracism literacy, toggli ng between m acro and micro, imagineering, and agency. The corresponding mediating processes to each of these themes are given in Figure 1. It is important to highlight here that having the h igh-level conjecture and the desired outcomes already articulated was valuable in the kind of thinking we  were able to do with mediating processes. For exam ple, the initial absence of outcome 3 and our attempts to ge t to outcome 4 from outcomes 1 & 2 via mediating pr ocesses led us to being more intensely focused on scaffoldi ng the teachers’ embodiment of anti-racism in our brainstorming which eventually led to the theme of “antiracism ZPD”. Here ZPD stands for Zone of Proximal Development (Vygotsky, 1978). While we eventually added outcome 3 as a bridge, the notion of antiracism ZPD still seemed incredibly valuable and it continues to be a part of our processes. Embodiments Once all the other elements of the conjecture were tentatively in place, we started working on the emb odiments while making design conjectures about which embodiments would lead to the expected mediating processes. We had two main components to our intervention-one-on-one reflection sessions with a researcher and a professional learning community (PLC) space focused on anti-racism. For each of the two components, we brainsto rmed on the tools & materials, task structures, participant  structures, and discur sive practices. While the participant structures were somewhat fixed because of the natur e of the two components of the intervention, there were rich possibilities for creatively thinking about the emb odiments in the other three categories. ## Discussion

Following this process and using  the conjecture map framework as a tool for design, has allowed our inter-disciplinary team to convert our broad vision of enabling teachers to “embody anti-racism” into a concrete design with design and theoretical conjectures. We  now have a clear framework for our conjectures behind each component (embodiment) of the intervention  which may not have been as clear otherwise. We know that we are engaging in a particular task because it should lead to a specific mediating process which ought to lead to at least one of our desired outcomes. This allows us to make  necessary adjustments to our designs while on the field, as is often necessary, while remembering why we are doing it and not drifting too far away from those intentions. Since this is a multi-year design-based research-practice partnership, our current conjecture map provides a clear substrate for us to  analyze successes and failures. For example, we hav e realized that our current embodiments are not effectively helping the participants to make connections between their micro deficit-oriented actions and the macro racist logics that undergirds  the US society.  While the participants engage in complex analysis of systemic racism they also sometimes rep roduce the very logics t hat sustain systemic injustices. An example of such a logic would be “some communities are culturally disposed to certain (low-paying) professions”. We have also realized that working with the teachers’ anti-racism ZPD is crucial but it may not be entirely feasible with the resources available to us. These are impor tant considerations that inform the changes in the next design cycle of the intervention.

On the note of working within participants ’ ZPD, a very familiar consideration that has been ra ised for us, again, in this first year of design and impleme ntation of this intervention is the complexity of s uch justiceoriented work, particularly anti-racism. As we make the micro-macro connections with the participants and bear witness to others’ complex experiences within the racialized system, one is forced to reckon with the fact that (almost) all of us in some measure are part of the problem. And in that, how much of this is a problem of teaching? And how much of it is a problem of the ways of our being in this world? We have acknowledged this expanse of the problem in the spaces we create d with the participants  and focused on our capacity to effect change in the classroom while keeping the macro at the back of our minds. This has been important in not allowing the expanse to overwhelm and paralyze the participants, and us.

It is because of the complexity and expanse of the problem at hand that we would like to share our process and our conjectures with the field. We invite other  researchers engaged in similar work to critique, m odify, use, or engage with our work in other ways they see as fruitful.
