# Data-Driven Badge Design for Gamified Learning App

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Although badges belong to the most used game elements in gamified learning tools, little is known about their optimal design. This poster will therefore discuss a data-driven approach to determine an optimal set of badges based on usage data from the corresponding learning tools. This approach will be tested with actual usage data from a learning app that was used for four semesters in a university lecture.

## Introduction

Badges are among the most used elements in game-bas ed learning (Antonaci et al., 2019). However, littl e is known about their optimal design in the educational domain. Optimal design in this context means that a badge can only be earned by following a learning strategy that leads to better learning outcomes. The inspiration for the design of badges in game-based learning often comes  from entertainment games. However, the goals of su ch games are usually different from those of learning tools used in school or higher education (Loh et al., 2015). This difference also applies to commercial learning apps such as Duolingo. In Duolingo, for example, it is possible to earn a badge called “Weekend Warrior” if you use the app on a Saturday or Sunday and a badge called “Photogenic” if you upload a profile picture (1). These two badg es are presumably intended to maximize usage time o r identification with the app and are not necessarily aimed at an optimal learning strategy. Since designers of gamebased learning tools may use such apps as a bluepri nt, they run the risk of copying badges that motiva te nonoptimal learning strategies. To date, no study has empirically investigated optimal badge design in a learning context. Easley and Ghosh (2016) only analyzed opti mal badge design with a game-theoretic approach fro m a general and not an educational point of view. In their literature review, Antonaci et al. (2019) link the effects of game elements on learning outcomes in order to inve stigate the question of which game elements cause w hich effects. They find that some game elements can have either a positive or a negative effect depending on the context. The individual effect of a game element seems to de pend on the specific setting, the other game elemen ts used and the personality of the students. This result is  also confirmed by recently conducted meta-studies (Bai et al., 2020; Huang et al., 2020; Sailer & Homner, 2020). They all report an overall positive significant small to medium effect size of game-based learning on learning outcomes. However, it is still unclear which factors contribute most to the success or failure of game-based learning. In addition, the meta-studies above shed light only on the overall effects of combinations of different game elements. A more in-depth analysis of different designs of b adges (or other single game elements) does not exist yet. Facey-Shaw et al. (2017) provide an overview of different badges designs and their functions in various educational applications. Still, they do not link the different designs to the achieved effects on the learning outcomes. The present study will contribute to this research gap. Data Sources & Research Strategy The data set used in this study consists of usage d ata of a gamified learning app that has been in use  for four semesters in an accounting course at a large German university. The app’s core element is a quiz with over 550 questions covering all nine chapters of the course.  In addition to the question types single and multi ple-choice, there are also sorting and cloze text tasks. The students can collect learning points in the app to un lock more chapters and earn badges for different achievements. The use of the app is voluntary and no extra cred its or advantages for the final exam can be earned by coll ecting points in the app. The required achievements  to earn the badges include specific amounts of consecutive usage days or answered questions. The corresponding goals were developed based on learning theories and practical experiences from past lectures but without any empirical validation. In the four semesters that the app has been used so far, there have been no scheduled chan ges to the lecture design. The teaching staff, as well as the learning materials have remained identical. However, due to the outbreak of COVID-19, after two semesters the instructors were forced to switch from classroom lectures to online videos. The goal of this study is to use temporal and sequential analysis to classify the app users into clusters and match these clusters with the goals of the available badges. This will help answer the question of whether students who aligned their learning strategy to the given badge goals performed better on the exam. Based on the results, the basic direction (e.g., surface vs. deep learning) but also the levels of the badges will be discussed. For example, the badge for the number of answered questions in the current data set has three levels. The highest level is earned when a student answered 1,000 questions. Unless a l arge proportion of the top students answered that m any questions in the app, you could conclude that the l evel might be too high. If the level is too high, e arning that ICLS2022 Proceedings 1864 © ISLS badge could even be a predictor for a bad performance in the exam, i.e., students who answer too many questions might overestimate the app as a learning tool and neglect other learning materials. Past studies have already shown that learning performance does not necessarily incr ease with learning time (Plant et al., 2005). In ad dition, the analysis might reveal optimal learning strategies that have not yet been incentivized by any of the existing badges.

## Conclusion

The goal of this research project is to find an optimal set of badges for a gamified learning app. Given an optimal set of badges was found, the follow-up question is whether this set is valid for all four semesters. Especially the different teaching conditions due to COVID-19 could  lead to changed optimal learning strategies. Prior  studies have already shown that the explanatory power of a general model that predicts learning outcomes cand iffer significantly between different courses (Conijn et al., 2016; Gašević et al., 2016). To further contri bute to the topic of portability, the results of the present st udy could be applied and compared to the settings o f other researchers. In the current setting, no data about the personality type of the students was collected. As past studies have already shown that personality traits can play a decisive role in adopting a learning strategy (Matcha et al., 2020), this could also be a promising avenue for future work.
