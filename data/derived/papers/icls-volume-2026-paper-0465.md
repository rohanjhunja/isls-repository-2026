# From Classrooms to Communities: A Systems Framework for Social-Emotional Learning Policy

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper presents a systems-level framework for Social-Emotional Learning that extends beyond classrooms to encompass families, communities, and policy structures. Drawing on the learning sciences and socio-ecological systems theory, the study argues tha t conventional SEL initiatives often operate in isolation. Using examples from the United States and Kazakhstan, the paper highlights how differences in alignment shape children’s experiences. It introduces emotional equity to emphasize the need for more coherent and culturally responsive approaches to SEL.

Over the past two decades, Social-Emotional Learning (SEL) has become a central focus of educational research. Despite its widespread implementation, SEL initiatives frequently remain confined to classroom settings, where they are delivered as discrete interventions. In such approaches, the broader ecological contexts shaping children’s development are often treated as secondary. This fragmentation limits not only the consistency of SEL practices, but also students’ opportunities to experience coherent emotional development across contexts. This paper argues that the key challenge in SEL is not only what is taught, but where and through whom emotional development is sustained. Following perspectives that view learning as distributed across systems rather than centered solely on the individual (Hecht & Crowley, 2020), SEL is conceptualized here as a relational process co-constructed across interconnected environments. Building on ecological systems theory (Bronfenbrenner, 1979) and recent scholarship on systemic approaches to SEL (Foster et al., 2022), this perspective situates emotional development within three interdependent spheres: school, family, and community. From an ecological perspective, development is shaped through dynamic interactions across nested environments, where changes in one sphere influence processes in others (Bronfenbrenner, 1979). Similarly, systemic approaches to SEL emphasize that emotional development depends not only on individual competencies, but on the conditions created across interconnected settings (Foster et al., 2022). From this perspective, the three-sphere model is not introduced as a new structure, but as a way of making visible the relational conditions under which SEL becomes meaningful and sustainable. The three-sphere ecosystem model This framework identifies three interconnected spheres that shape children’s emotional development. While similar school–family–community models have been discussed in educational research (e.g., Epstein, 2011), this paper repositions these relationships s pecifically within the context of social-emotional learning, emphasizing their role not only as contexts of influence but as co-constructors of emotional meaning. The School Sphere. Schools serve as the primary setting for intentional SEL instruction, where educators structure learning environments and model social-emotional practices. Through formal curricula, classroom interactions, and institutional norms, schools introduce shared language around emotions, relationships, and behavior. However, when SEL remains confined to classrooms, its impact is often limited and difficult to sustain. Without reinforcement across other environments, students may experience SEL a s situational rather than transferable, limiting its long-term developmental impact. The Family Sphere. Moving beyond deficit-based perspectives, this framework draws on work that positions families as active contributors to children’s development (Ishimaru, 2019). Families shape emotional development through everyday interactions, cultura l expectations, and relational practices that define how emotions are expressed and understood. These practices often reflect deeply embedded cultural values, making the family sphere a critical site for the continuity —or disruption—of SEL. When school-based approaches are misaligned with family norms, students may experience tension between competing emotional expectations. The Community Sphere. Communities, including local organizations, peer networks, and informal social structures, provide broader contexts in which emotional competencies are practiced and reinforced. These environments contribute to what can be understood as the “moral vocabulary” through which emotional meaning is constructed. Importantly, community contexts often mediate between institutional expectations and lived cultural practices, shaping how SEL is interpreted, adapted, and sustained beyond formal sc hooling. 2824 Importantly, these spheres do not function as separate domains, but as mutually reinforcing environments. Inconsistent expectations across them may create fragmentation in children’s emotional experiences, while alignment enables the internalization of shared values and practices. When these environments operate independently, SEL remains fragmented; when they are connected, SEL functions as a relational ecosystem.

## Cross-context dialogue: United States and Kazakhstan

A comparison between the United States and Kazakhstan illustrates how this fragmentation manifests across different contexts. In the United States, SEL is supported by established frameworks such as those developed by CASEL, along with increasing attention  at the policy level (CASEL, 2020; Jones & Kahn, 2017). However, despite this institutionalization, implementation often remains uneven, and alignment across school, family, and community contexts is not consistently achieved.

In Kazakhstan, social and emotional development has historically been embedded within broader moral and cultural traditions, often conceptualized through tärbie  (value-based upbringing). This concept reflects a holistic understanding of development in which emotional growth is closely tied to moral education, collective responsibility, and everyday social relationships. While many Western SEL approaches emphasize  individual competencies and self-expression, the Kazakh context foregrounds moral harmony and collective responsibility. For example, values-based education frameworks implemented in Kazakhstani schools integrate indigenous philosophical traditions with g lobal approaches to social-emotional learning, emphasizing character formation and responsible citizenship (Makhmetov, 2025). At the same time, SEL has not been formalized as a distinct policy domain at the national level, resulting in practices that are culturally grounded but not always systematically aligned across educational contexts (Ministry of Education and Science of the Republic of Kazakhstan, 2020). This comparison does not position one system as more advanced than the other. Rather, it highlights a shared structural challenge: in both contexts, SEL remains limited when it is not supported across the environments that shape children’s daily lives. Thi s contrast suggests that both highly institutionalized and culturally embedded approaches face similar limitations when connections across contexts remain weak. Alignment, rather than structure alone, becomes the critical condition for effectiveness. Reframing SEL as a relational ecosystem suggests a shift in how SEL is conceptualized and supported. Rather than expanding SEL through additional programs, this perspective emphasizes strengthening coherence across the environments that already shape children’s lives.

Emotional equity can be better supported when policy explicitly recognizes cultural variability in emotional socialization. Children do not enter schools with a neutral or uniform emotional framework; rather, their emotional expression, regulation, and understanding are shaped by culturally embedded norms, values, and relational practices. When SEL frameworks assume a universal model of emotional development, they risk misalignment with students’ lived experiences and may inadvertently marginalize culturally grounded ways of knowing and feeling.

Drawing on work on equitable family– school partnerships, this perspective aligns with the idea that policy must move beyond “fixing children” toward transforming the systems that shape their development (Ishimaru, 2019). This shift requires recognizing families and communities not as peripheral actors, but as central contributors to the co-construction of emotional development. This shift also requires reciprocal learning across contexts. While Western systems offer structured frameworks and evidence-based approaches, collectivist traditions provide insight into relational and value-based dimensions of development. Integrating these perspectives can support more culturally responsive and sustainable SEL systems.

Ultimately, emotional equity is not achieved through standardization, but through alignment —when children encounter consistent, meaningful, and culturally responsive expectations across the environments in which they live and learn. In this sense, SEL becomes not a program to be implemented, but a shared social infrastructure that supports children’s development across contexts (Hecht & Crowley, 2020). Bronfenbrenner, U. (1979).
