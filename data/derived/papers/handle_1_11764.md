# “I Am an Engineering Person, I Just Don’t Call It That”: Rural Library Professionals’ Engineering Identities-in-Practice

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Educators’ self-perception as individuals who could engineer influences their motivation and support they provide during engineering learning. This study examines ways to support engineering identity development of library professionals and position them as educators for children’s engineering learning. Drawing from the theory of identities-in-practice and the concept of boundary crossing, this study conducts a case study to investigate how rural library professionals developed traces of emerging engineering identity. By examining nine library professionals’ engagement over two years in a three-year project to co-design and facilitate an online engineering program for children and their parents, this study contributes to the understanding of how public library professionals perform boundary crossing practices to position themselves as engineering learners and educators. The study further illuminates the potential of public libraries to serve as transformative spaces for engineering engagement and encourages public libraries to redefine library professionals’ roles.

## Introduction

To promote  diversity in STEM participation, methods of intentionally brokering STEM participation across settings and organizations is needed (Cannady, 2014). In the last decade, public libraries have emerged as out-ofschool learning institutions to strengthen STEM and maker learning (Kim et al., 2022). Aligning with broadening STEM participation efforts, this study focuses on strengthening rural libraries to support children’s engineering learning, which has been the discipline that received less attention in public library settings. This exploratory study builds on research on educators’ STEM identity development to examine ways to support engineering identity development of library professionals and position them as educators for children’s engineering learning. Educators’ self-perception as individuals who could be engineers —also referred to as the engineering identity (Capobianco, 2006) —influences the motivation and support they provide to their learners. As such, strengthening public library staff’s engineering identity would influence the educational experiences they offer and empower children and families within their comm unity to engage in engineering learning. This aligns with previous literature on teacher ’s STEM identity, which demonstrates that teachers who develop a strong professional identity in STEM are better equipped to inspire youth’s STEM interes t and engagement (Avraamidou, 2014). However, prior literature suggests that many public libraries staff experience STEM anxiety to facilitate engineering-related programs due to limited experience and knowledge in STEM (Kim & Copeland, 2021; Baek, 2013), which can inhibit their ability to support children to participate in engineering. To examine how we can support library professionals  to develop engineering identity, we present a design-based research study called Family Makers that engaged nine rural library staff to co-design and facilitate an online engineering program for elementary-aged children and their caregivers. A case study approach was utilized to address the following research question: how do rur al library staff develop traces of emerging engineering identity during their engagement in the co-design and facilitation of an online engineering program?

To address this question, we examined (1) how t heir articulation of  engineering understanding and knowledge changed, (2) how their recognition of self-efficacy in engineering shifted over time, (3) how they framed their engagement in the engineering design process, (4) how their experiences shaped their future professional practice both in the online engineering program and in their  library, and (5) how they navigated boundary cross ing to engage in engineering.  By examining nine library professionals’ engagement over two years in  a three-year project, we illuminate how public library staff performed the boundary cro ssing practices to position themselves as engineering  learners and  educators. The study findings contribute to expanding our unde rstanding of the potential of public libraries to serve as transform ative spaces for engineering engagement and encourage public libraries to redefine library professionals’ roles. We contend that this study al igns with the conference theme as the findings acknowledge library professionals as active transformative agents through boundary cr ossing practices to support children’s engineering learning.

Theoretical grounding and related literature This study draws from the theory of identities-in-practice to conceptualize identity as emergent, dynamic, and being constructed through ongoing social negotiation in the practices of a community (Lave, 1996). Identities-inpractice consider that identity development occurs as people engage in practice, further suggesting that identity and practice are mutually constitutive. As learners become a certain kind of people, their changing identities also influence their approaches to practice (Gee, 2001). In this way, it emphasizes that identities develop in situ “within and against local norms and expectations and as a p art of longer standing sociocultural and historical  narratives” (Calabrese Barton et al., 2013, p. 14).  In our study context, broader disciplinary narratives around what it means to be an engineer and who can be an engineer (Eastman et al., 2019) and cultural narratives around what it means to be a librarian can influence how librarians engage in engineering activities and recognize them selves as a particular kind of person.  Akkerman and Bakker (2011)  further assert that identity work involves  crossing boundaries between different social and cultural contexts, roles, or identities as boundaries can introduce “sociocultural differences that give rise  to discontinuities in interaction and action ” (p. 139). As such, this study adopts identities-in-practice and the concept of boundary crossing to re cognize and investigate how rural library professionals’ experiences and interactions during the co-design and facilitation of the online engineering program served as the foundation for developing the early stages of engineering identity. We also draw on literature that STEM identity is shaped by various factors within cultural and social contexts, such as recognition of self and others, self-efficacy, and situated engineering activitie s (Cribbs et al., 2015; Godwin, 2016; Simpson & Knox, 2022). In particular, we draw on teacher education literature to understand ways in which public library professionals, as educators of children’s learning, develop their engineering identity. Previous literature on teacher education indicates that recognition from students and colleagues as co mpetent in STEM or problem-solving has helped teachers to develop STEM teacher  identity (Cross Francis et al., 2022; Silvestri et al., 2019). In a study of three teachers engaged in an afterschool engineering club, Silvestri et al. (2019) demonstrated that teachers’ self-recognition evolved through interactions with stude nts, shifting their roles from traditional educators to facilitators.  As teachers negotiated between different roles and made sense of the unfamiliar STEM content, teachers developed self-efficacy in STEM by making connection to familiar practices and recognizing the broader value of STEM  (Boice et al., 2021; Cian & Brasili, 2024). Further, r esearch has consistently shown that authentic STEM learning experiences help teachers develop a stronger STEM identity by connecting theory with practice and fostering a sense of belonging in the STEM community (Avraamidou, 2014). Notably, research indicates that teachers’ STEM identities significantly impact thei r professional development and teaching practices (Zhai et al., 2024). While previous literature reports on the empirical evidence of teachers’ development of STEM identity, there is scant litera ture on public library staff’s emerging roles and i dentities in STEM. Therefore, this study explores how rural library staff develop traces of engineering i dentity during their engagement in the engineering design process and il luminates ways in which dominant engineering discou rses are negotiated through boundary crossing practices.

## Methods

As part of a three-year design-based research project, we implemented a research-practice partnership (RPP) with seven public libraries located in rural areas across seven states (two in the West; three in the Midwest; two on the East coast) in the U.S to develop and implement an online engineering program for children (5-10 years) and their caregivers (i.e., legal guardians, parents) in rural communities. We focused on the online space to expand learning from a fixed physical setting and strengthen public  libraries’ outreach programming. Through eight co-design sessions with library staff that occurred in Zoom, we developed six online engineering sessions (each 90 minutes) to develop a culturally relevant engineering progra m (Ladson-Billings & Gloria, 2014). The first iteration of th e curriculum, including these six sessions,  were implemented by library staff in Zoom within each community. While researchers joined each online session to support facilitation, library staff were positioned as the main facilitators of the online engineering program.  A case study approach was employed to explore the particular nature of a situation of interest, focusing on how rural library staff developed engineering identity (Stake, 1995)

## Participants

A total of nine rural library staff  from seven library partners as part of our RPP  participated in this study (see Table 1). While RPP started with seven library staff, two more staff (Sydney and Tamara) joined as backup staff. Backup staff recruitment was encouraged across all libraries,  but only feasible at two due to limited staff. All participants were female and held various positions, such as librariandirector (n=5), library manager (n=1), youth services and outreach coordinator (n=1), library clerk (n=1), and children’s coordinator (n=1). Many participants mentioned having prior experience in offering STEM/STEAM programs, although none indicated having experience with engineering programs  specifically. Rural libraries in our RPP served an average population of 2,431 people with an average median income of $51,920. There was an average of 5 full-time staff working at each library (the smallest library had only 1 full-time staff; the largest library had 9 full-time staff). Since two libraries faced difficulties in participant recruitment due to  mid-way dropout from schedule conflicts and hesitation from families to sign up, seven staff facilitated the engineering sessions once a week through Zoom. ## Data collection and analysis

To gain a holistic understanding of how library pro fessionals’ engineering identity developed through our program, we collected five forms of data. First, w e conducted an hour-long Zoom pre-interviews about prior experiences and perceptions of engineering at the start of the project. Then, after facilitating all six online sessions, we conducted post-interviews to understand their experience and changes in their  perception of engineering. While most participants completed both pre-and post-interviews, two backup library staff who were recru ited midway were unable to participate in the pre-interview. Second, each library staff completed Parents Engineering Awareness Survey (PEAS)  (Yun et al., 2010)  before and after the completion of six online sessions that asked participants to reflect on their understanding of e ngineering and their capacity to support children’s  engineering learning. While the original instrument was developed for par ents, it was adopted and slightly modified to understand change in library staff’s engineering aw areness. For instance, we excluded one item, “I am aware of engineering curriculum at my child(ren)’s school,” from the survey instrument since there were no en gineering curriculums in any of the rural schools attended by children in the study. Third, after facilitating each session, we engaged participants in debrief interviews (about 15-30 minutes). Furthermore, we created fieldnotes and summaries of co-design sessions to note  key discussion points and outcomes from the session s. Lastly, we collected video records of six online sessions in Zoom. From the entire session recordings, this study focused on episodes in which participants shared artifacts they created to address the engineering design prompts during each session. The following  are examples of the engineering design prompts: (1) design something  using a light component to communicate who your user is and what problems you want to solve for them, (2) design something for your user to transport two rocks over a distanc e of at least five inches and unload them using a mechanism in the device, and (3) create a place for your user by considering environmental factors in the community. All interview data, debrief interviews, and videos of participants’ sharing of artifacts were transcribed in verbatim with pictures of the artifacts. Transcriptions and co-design summaries were analyzed thematically in iterative and inductive approach to address the research questions. We initially began by adopting  the coding scheme used in Simpson and Knox (2022) that explored children’s engineering identity development with a priori codes of situational interest, recognition, self-efficacy, doing engineering, and future self. Four researchers individually applied this coding scheme to three in terview transcripts and modified the coding scheme based on new codes that each researcher observed  (i.e., perception, learning) or codes that were entirely missing from the dataset (i.e., situational interest). Based on several rounds of coding and group discussion, we established a revised coding scheme and applied it to our dataset (see Table 2). ## Results

Findings demonstrate that librarians  developed traces of engineering identity  by (1) gaining expanded understanding of engineering, (2) developing increased engineering self-efficacy through dismantling the preconceived notion of engineering,  and (3) doing engineering  by being resourceful and embracing failures. Analyses illustrate that  these traces of engineering identity emerged through boundary crossing practice that integrated content knowledge and skills in librarians’ areas of strengths with the unfamiliar content of engineering. Findings further illustrate librarians’ emerging engineering identity informed their profes sional practice. Representative examples and quotes are presented to substantiate our findings. Expanded understanding of engineering At the start of the project, the library staff understood engineering as problem-solving through trial-and-error and experimentation. Wendy’s remark demonstrates this s hared understanding: “Engineering is figuring out h ow to solve problems…There’s a lot more tinkering involve d in engineering, a lot of trial and error.” Simila rly, participants primarily viewed engineers as problem-solvers who utilize critical thinking and analytica l skills to make things easier and bring ideas together to solve problems. Aptitude in math and science were often mentioned as a prerequisite. This is well reflected through t he “face of an engineer” that Krystal had created d uring one of the co-design meetings. Krystal described her choice of materials in her engineer’s face as: A calculator cause there’s a lot of math involved i n being an engineer…I have money for eyes because if you are an engineer, I feel like you’re gonna save money because you’ll get it right the first time…I have a paper clip…and tape because they seem to take different ideas and stick them together. And then I have these gloves…They ha ve to go out like on a job site…and see the things that they thought up how it actually works.

However, through their actual doing of engineering and facil itation of engineering programs, all library staff, except for Gina who worked as a MESA (Mathematics, Engineering, and Science Achievement)  advisor, developed an expanded understanding of engineering disciplines and broadened their perspectives. Compared to their initial understanding of the engineering disciplines  that primarily focused on mechanical and civil engineering, participants recognized diverse engineering discipli nes. Winter expressed: “ So much has changed because there are so many different types of enginee ring that I honestly did not put it with engineering.” Cindy also highlights this finding: “So before this program, w hen I heard the term engineering, I immediately tho ught of principles of science and mathematics. And now m y horizon has been widened, so that I now think of engineering as creativity and community. And that this has made it more interesting to me.” By doing engineering for six sessions, library staff also began to recognize the relevance and impact of engineering in their lives. Tamara and Beatrice’s remarks exemplify this: “I guess I didn’t really give it much thought of how engineering is literally and everything around you, like literally everythin g”; “And just making myself aware of ho w much engineering is in our world...So, I definitely learned a lot ab out that.”  Consequently, library staff shared this program broadened their perspectives  and helped them connect previously unrelated information and experience  to engineering. While this was observed across participants, it was particularly evident in Winter who shared feeling terrified to offer engineering  at the beginning of the project: “So much has changed… It’s crazy because every time I look at something, I wonder how it was made and… what kind of engineer thought of this…I look at things different… I start talking about engineering and all this … so I ’m using it, like, not only at work, but in my everyday life.” Relatedly, p articipants’ understanding of engineering also encompassed an  increase in  engineering knowledge, attitude, and behavior. In a session where we asked families to communicate their user and their problem using one light component, Cindy used a little fairy gnome from her favorite book. She shared that the fairy l ived in their library but wanted to keep reading when everyone left the library after turning off the lights. In another session, the making challenge was to design a place for the user by considering environmental factors  in the community. Inspired by her love for gardenin g, Cindy created a house for her user who wanted to garden a t the library. This approach enabled her to facilitate engineering learning opportunities for children and caregivers at her library. Whether big or small,  we observed a similar pattern of boundary crossing practice reflected in librar y staff, which allowed them to leverage their strengths, interests, and cultural repertoires to navigate the unfamiliar content of engineering. For Wendy, she leveraged her background and strengt h in theater to be the confident facilitator. Gina leaned on her prior experience of being a MESA advisor and K-12 teaching experience to figure out what to teach  and how to engage children. With background in sculpture and childhood experience of watching her father work as an engineer, Sydney quickly c ame on board to co-facilitate with Cindy and/or completely lead the se ssions when Cindy was unable to attend.  Overall, there was evidence that library staff in our study were brokers of their own engineering learning by making connections between and integrating two different boundaries.

Emerging identity informs professional practice Our analysis further demonstrated that developing traces of engineering identity informed rural library staff to integrate engineering concepts into their existing work and develop commitment to sharing the engineering design process and concepts in their professional roles.  For instance, a ll participants shared specific plans for utilizing the engineering design process and/or the curriculu m as part of their future professional practice. This was expressed in several ways: “Oh, I would like to bor row the c urriculum…for probably our homeschoolers” (Krystal); “We will be starting a home school group … I can see myself using the curriculum in that way  as well and perhaps pairing older siblings as the caregiver wit h the younger siblings” (Wendy); “ We have the summer reading program happening …and I’m going to start in July doing some of these sessio ns (Gina). In one library location, Cindy and Sydney shared that they already started applying the engineering design concepts into existing library programming: “I was doing this pollinator p rogram this summer. And because of what we have don e…I asked Sydney to do it with butterflies and use circuits and that was because of this program.” Several participants also envisioned applying what they learned to other  library programming and services: “T here’s a lot of adults that can’t make it to the library…just can’t drive…So I’m thinking, okay, if we send them a kit, how can we, you know, we could do this?” (Winter); “We have a new person in our makerspace…So, I will definitely be using the engineering concepts and language and scaffolding that I learned in this program” (Wendy). One librarian, Winter, was particularly eager to share the engineering des ign process with her current and future staff as sh e clearly recognized its value in enhancing library programm ing: “I have it written right here on my wall becau se I think that could fall in many things that I’m doing and I  just really want that to stick in my head, especia lly when I’m, I’m working with either my staff or even when, I’m getting new staff… because…it’s going to improve ou r programing.” In this regard, participants’ intentional use of en gineering in library programming and their eagerness to share and apply the engineering design process signals a shift from seeing themselves solely as library staff to recognizing themselves as educators of engineering. In addition to developing traces of engineering ide ntity, participants anticipated that what they learned beyond engineering would support them to become better facilitators of learning in their community. For instance, they gained insights into scaffolding children’s le arning and designing better curriculum, as exemplified by Winter’s remark: “The component that we’re missing is that learnin g aspect… You really laid it out …It would give me the confidence to help with the designing p art.” Some experienced a reorientation towards recognizing children’s potential, as reflected through Winter: “I also love the imaginativeness of Larry’s, like completely outof-the-box, which I don’t expect from him… I, I was astonished that that came from Larry.” Others have gathered insights into community needs. Krystal stated, “What I learned that was new is …there are people that would be really interested in doing this …especially at home school …We need to fill that gap. I liked getting to know my patrons…in a different way as opposed to just checking out books.” Similarly, Wendy shared the needs of gifted individuals: “We’re in a rural area and our school district only has one gifted teacher for all 1600 students…Those [STEM] opportunities are hard…So I can see this being very, very helpful for families.”  Some have noted discovering best practices from other public libraries, as stated by Krystal: “I never noticed it before, but I see that there are a lot of public libraries that do science-y engineering, designing inventory stuff for their home schoolers.” In this regard, library staff in our RPP came to ob serve children’s creative potential and gained  new insights to better scaffold them  based on more nuanced understanding of the community needs a nd landscape of public library STEM programming, which they found to be beneficial towards their future professional practice.

## Discussion

Our case study showed that rural library profession als developed traces of engineering identity by per forming boundary crossing practices that integrate d their existing knowledge, strengths, and assets wit h unfamiliar engineering content to position themselves as engineering learners and educators. In line with past research (Cribbs et al., 2015; Simpson & Knox, 2022), our study demonst rated that factors such as recognition, self-efficacy, and doing engineering were crucial in shaping library professionals’ emerging identities as engineer s. Importantly, our work sheds light on the understand ing of the public rural library staff’s engineering identi tiesin-practice (Lave, 1996; Wenger, 1998) through detailed accounts of crossing boundaries be tween public library work and engineering.

As Akkerman and Bakker (2011)  asserted, library staff experienced “sociocultural differences ” or clashes between what is considered public librarian s’ work and who is able to participate in engineeri ng. For example, if we return to Winter’s remark— “Engineering was like, libraries should not, you know, that’s not part of their whatever ”—we can see the cultural clashes that some library s taff experienced when they signed up to facilitate the engineering program. We also see that library staff’s emerging engineeri ng identities developed as they negotiated against the dominant sociocultural narratives of engineering, mainly subscribing individuals with strong aptitude in science and math who have histor ically been the white male with easier access to technology and tools (Eastman et al., 2019). Although Gina did not exhibit further development o f her engineering identity, we hypothesize that her substantial prior experience as a MESA advisor had already established her self-efficacy in engineering, setting her apart from other librar ians. Through engaging in engineering design process, participants recognized that engineering is much mo re than applying mathematics and science knowledge and dismantled the preconceived notion of who cando engineering. In this way, recognition in our study was framed as rediscovering one’s sense of belonging by challe nging preconceived notions about engineering (e.g., “I’m not bad at math”, “I could do this”; “I am an engineering person, I just don’t call it that”). This finding is in contrast to some studies where recognition in science was fr amed through a love of science, interest in research, and/or sense of belonging (Carlone & Johnson, 2007; Godwin, 2016). We posit that while library staff began to recognize that they cando engineering and think like engineers, they would not identify themselves as professional engineers at any point in their future. As such, we hypothesize that for adult learners who are also in the position of educators, dismantling the dominant historical narratives of engineering may be more critical for developing recognition and self-efficacy in engineering rather than fostering love and interest in engineering. To form identity as a STEM person, learners need to  engage in STEM experiences that are more “augmented and personalized” to reflect upon what it means to be a STEM person (Lyon et al., 2023). Our findings shed light on how library professionals internalize d and transformed their identities through particip ation in and facilitation of our engineering program during RPP. We conjecture that taking on different roles during RPP—as a co-designer of the curriculum, a facilitator of engineering sessions, a learner to do engineering—helped library staff to gain  self-efficacy in engineering and a sense of agency that enabled to develop and define their own authoring space (Tan et al., 2013) in engineering. Notably, library staff in this study leveraged and brokered their existing interests, strengths, and assets to cross the boundaries between public library work and engineering. Areas that were personally relevant, such as art, craft, stories, history, gardening, were utilized as an important brokering anchor for boundary crossing in engineering identity work. We anticipate that public library staff’s leveraging of personally relevant interests and strengths in the doing of engineering would transform the engineering narratives that public library staff share with children and e xpand the way children think about engineering. The se findings support previous work on asset-based and culturally relevant pedagogy that value learners’ interests, strengths, and knowledge as important resources for learning (Ladson-Billings & Gloria, 2014; Yosso, 2005)  and further suggest the conjecture that embedding an asset-based approach in the training of public library staff to facilitate engineering learning could  potentially transform their perspectives, shifting  from a narrow view to expanded understanding of engineering. However, future research is needed  to explore methods for making these localized, participatory approaches more accessible to a wider population of library professionals.

## Conclusion

Our exploratory study focused on library professionals who are not traditionally regarded as STEM educators and examined their development of emerging engineering identities through co-design and facilitation of the online engineering program in research-practice partnership between the university and seven rural public libraries. Our work contributes to the scant literature on public library staff’s emerging roles and identi ties in STEM  and illuminates how public library professionals perform boundary crossing practices to become engineering learners and educators. This focus on public library staff as educators is an important distinction as public libraries address access and equity issues that differ from tradition al classroom settings or other informal STEM learni ng institutions, such as museums and afterschool progr ams that may require membership. The study further illuminates the potential of public libraries to se rve as transformative spaces for en gineering engagement and encourages public libraries to redefine library professionals’ roles.
