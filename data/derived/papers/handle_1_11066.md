# Responding to Cultural Imperialism: Introducing Culturally Relevant Pedagogies Within Egyptian Educational System

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study responds to the challenge of cultural imperialism often encountered by teachers in non-western contexts. We report on a formative intervention (Engeström et al., 2014) for a community of teachers in Egypt, and the collective expansive learning they experienced through the process of designing culturally relevant pedagogies centered on 21st-century competencies. This study was conducted as part of the Critical Action Learning Exchange (CALE), an international community of critical educators (Carvalho et al., 2021).

## Introduction

This study reports on a formative intervention to engage a community of Egyptian teachers dedicated to designing culturally relevant lesson plans that are centered on 21st-century competencies. When developing any form of pedagogical support for teachers, one must consider the teacher’s educational and cultural context. In the context of non-hegemonic countries (Schenoni, 2019), this includes  the notion of cultural imperialism, which Mains describes as "a process of disproportionate influen ce over social practices and ideologies by one soci opolitical group over a politically weaker and (frequently) le ss-wealthy group" (2009, p. 322). Believing in the not ion of “teachers as knowledge producers,” the ‘teacher res earch’ approach to 'practitioner inquiry' was us ed with the objective of developing an 'inquiry-as-stance' mindset” (Cochran-Smith & Lytle, 2009). Another aim is to develop a culturally responsive model that could be used to  support teachers in non-hegemonic cultural contexts when introducing innovations that originate in hegemonic educational contexts. This study examines how a Professional Learning Community (PLC) of teachers in Egypt can s upport them in adopting pedagogical innovations. Th is PLC was organized in the context of the Critical Ac tion Learning Exchange (CALE), which is concerned with empowering teachers to design pedagogical activitie s that incorporate critical action and allow for th e inclusion of students’ voices (Carvalho et al., 2021).

## Theoretical framework

We draw from Cultural-Historical Activity Theory (CHAT) to consider the elements of culture and history in our understanding of human activities, decisions, and values. CHAT holds that cultures are rooted in history, and that culture creates the context in which humans’ activities evolve and develop. Within CHAT, innovations prioritize socio-cultural and historical norms (Sannino et al., 2016), aiming to guide and explain the collective transformational efforts of activity systems. We also employ a pedagogical approach for Critical Action Learning (CAL; Carvalho et al., 2021), which aims to empower  students as transformative agents when facing comp lex socio-environmental issues that affect themselves and their communities.

## Methods

This project reports the formation of a teacher PLC  in Cairo, Egypt, comprising initial design worksho ps and subsequent enactment and reflection. Using participatory methods, our design process is informed by voices from several perspectives, including researchers from CALE, educators from Egypt, and administrative leadership from a non-governmental organization based in Cairo and focused on local empowerment issues. Teacher participants are engaged in a formative intervention to examine their practices thr ough the lens of practitioners' inquiry methodology, which positions practitioners as drivers of social and educational change. Practitioners in this study are guided to problematize their current practices,  interrogating the different ways knowledge and p ractice are constructed, examined, and used. The formative inte rvention that was designed for the teachers include d two phases; a workshop phase and an implementation phas e. A pre-survey and the engagement in various critical discussions and reflective writing, served as the d ouble-stimulation artifacts prescribed by the Formative Intervention methodology (Engestrom et al., 2014). Resources such as the CALE framework and pedagogica l approaches were offered as mediating artifacts. The goal of this w orkshop phase is for teachers to design their own curricular or extracurricular lesson plans usin g the frameworks and approaches presented during th e workshops. In the implementation phase, participating teachers implemented their designs in their own classrooms/schools. Participants continued to have follow-up sessions with a CALE mentor during and after the implementation of their designs.

Outcomes: The CALE-Egypt PLC The Design workshop comprised four 3-hour Zoom meetings which provided teachers with an overview of 21stcentury competencies and engaged them in discussions about their own students and teaching practices. Teachers were introduced to the idea of cultural-responsiveness and practitioners' inquiry and engaged in reflections about their own classroom practice. Teachers then explored examples of CALE lesson designs, engaged in collaborative design discussions about how those examples could fit within an Egyptian context, and reflected on their existing teaching practices in relation to the Egyptian cult ural and historical aspects. The CALE framework ser ved as a reflective artifact that allowed critical comparisons between the teachers’ status quo and their aspirational practice. The final session was dedicated to mentor and peer feedback, and discussing new ways of teaching. In the Enactment phase,  teachers implemented their designs in their classro oms as part of the CALE Egypt professional development cycle. Participants left the final workshop session with a follow-up meeting already scheduled with a CALE project mentor. CALE is committed to a car ing model, which allows for continuous follow-up, support, and guidance to participants. As participants start enacting their designs, they face challenges and obstacles, including the simple loss  of motivation or engagement, with which they can b enefit from close contact with project mentors. All teacher designs go through interactive cycles based on peer feedback, as well as teachers’ own evaluations of their students’ experiences with early versions of the lesson plans.

## Discussion and conclusion

Our formative intervention approach allowed teacher s to critically examine their current teaching prac tices in terms of what they aspire to change. Spending the time to reflect and become self-aware of their own readiness to change was insightful and provided guidance that they could resort to when it was time to implement their newly designed lesson plans. Teachers were encouraged to use the CALE framework while designing their lesson plans, but continuously challenged to add the element of c ultural-relevance. The three elements of formative interventions, which are (1) the object, (2) expans ive learning, and (3) transformative agency, were i nvestigated and experienced in this formative intervention. The  object, which is the teachers’ instructional practices and methods, was studied, investigated, and led to the teachers’ development of a vision for their future practices. The collective efforts of teacher participants resulted  in their expansive learning of a new object, a mod el of their activity that is culturally relevant and acceptable. This accounts for the third important dynamic of transformative agency (Sannino et al., 2016).  The significance of this study relies on its tackling the issue of cultural dependency of developing countries on hegemonic countries thro ugh a formative intervention that engages a profess ional development community of teacher practitioners. Tea chers in this study are supported to explore the im portance of 21st-century pedagogies without losing sight of their own cultural norms and societal values.
