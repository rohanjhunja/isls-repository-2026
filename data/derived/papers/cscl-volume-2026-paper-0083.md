# Scaffolding Knowledge Building through Ordered-Epistemic Network Analysis-Supported Reflective Assessment and Meta-Discourse

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study presents a reflective assessment approach supported by ordered-epistemic network analysis (ONA) tools, designed to enhance students’ knowledge building (KB) and discourse within the Knowledge Forum. While prior research has demonstrated that re flective assessments integrated with learning analytics can improve students’ metacognitive skills and self-regulation in knowledge-building contexts, this study uniquely incorporates ONA-based reflection and meta-discourse to elucidate and promot e the dynamics and trajectories of epistemic moves during the knowledge-building process. The participants comprised 40 Year 5 students engaged in a collective inquiry into metabolism using the Knowledge Forum. Quantitative results indicated that, followin g the intervention, students exhibited increased levels of social and community interaction, alongside more sophisticated epistemological beliefs. Qualitative analysis further illustrated how ONA-facilitated reflective assessment enriched students’ collaborative inquiry process. This work advances pedagogy in knowledge building by demonstrating that ONA-enhanced reflective assessment and meta-discourse can effectively support and deepen collaborative knowledge construction.

Knowledge building (KB) is a collaborative learning process within a community, emphasising the continuous development and refinement of ideas and theories (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2010). It centres on collective knowledge creation to enhance shared understanding. Knowledge Forum® (KF) is a computer-mediated platform designed to facilitate communal knowledge advancement through epistemic activities such as problem formulation, idea elaboration, explanation co-construction, referencing authoritative sources, theory revision, and reflective synthesis. Research shows that explanation-driven inquiry underpins scientific communities, with questions and explanations progressively advancing knowledge (Zhang et al., 2007). Sustained questioning, idea improvement, and synthesis of diverse ideas are key for fostering advanced knowledge (Chan et al., 2018). While KF supports ongoing discourse and explanatory inquiry, a challenge lies in students’ difficulty tracking different building talks, reflective portfolios (Van Aalst & Chan, 2007), and metacognitive conversations (Zhang et al., 2015). Recent methods utilise learning analytics such as word clouds (Resendes et al., 2015), knowledge connection analysers (Yang et al., 2021), and KBDeX (Oshima et al., 2012) to visualise idea progression and support reflection (Yang et al., 2022). However, research on students’ reflections on their epistemic contributions, especially their dynamics and trajectories, is limited. Ordered-epistemic network analysis (ONA) offers a promising approach by revealing the temporal sequences of epistemic activities, enabling both quantitative and qualitative analysis of collaborative interactions (Tan et al., 2020). While ONA has been utilised to analyse epistemic moves within KB (Yang et al., 2022), few studies have explored its pedagogical applications. Our prior work demonstrated ONA’s potential in scaffolding reflective assessment and improving domain understanding (Zhang et al., 2025). This study furthe r investigates classroom meta-discourse, ONA-reflective assessment, and their effects on social interaction and epistemic beliefs (EBs). The research questions are:  RQ1: Does ONA-enhanced reflective assessment support improvements in social interactions and epistemological beliefs? RQ2: How do students engage with ONA in reflective assessment to advance knowledge?

## Pedagogical design

The course lasted for 3 weeks, including 8 lessons (each lasting 45 minutes). T he first 5 lessons focus ed on introducing and engaging students in KB, and the last 3 emphasised ONA-supported reflective assessment designs. These three lessons aim  to enable students to recogni se and develop a deeper understanding of the collective knowledge development trajectory supported by classroom meta-discourse and ONA visualisation for more active KB work. We developed these lessons in progressive ways for illuminating the KB trajectory, which includes sustained questioning of existing ideas, improving ideas with information and explanations, and synthesising diverse perspectives using meta-discourse and ONA reflection. Each ONA-supported lesson comprised two main components: (1) classroom  meta-discourse on KF discourse to help students understand the KB trajectories (Figure 2), and (2) a reflective assessment activity guided by ONA visualisation (Figure 1) to help students reflect and modify the trajectory in KF discourse. Students continued to contribute to the KF after each lesson. The first lesson focused on changing the basic trajectory from “Initial Question → Idea” into “Initial Question → Idea → Sustained Question”. The second lesson extended this to “Initial Question → Idea → Sustained Question ↔ Improving Idea”. The third lesson further developed the trajectory to “Initial Question → Idea → Sustained Question ↔ Improving Idea (with Information) → Synthesis”. The “who built on whose notes” matrices derived from the KF log data were transformed into social networks to characterise students’ social interactions. In this study, weighted out-degree means how many times one student built  on others’ notes. We used the percentage of weighted out-degrees to characterise the extent to which the students engage in collective knowledge creation. Paired sample ttests were employed to compare those SNA indices between the experimental and comparison classes. Epistemological Beliefs: pre/post-test. Each student was asked to respond to the following questions: “Where do I think ideas come from?” and “Why do we need ideas? What can ideas do?” Their responses were coded according to Popper’s framework (Hong & Chiu, 2016; Popper, 1979). Responses that described beliefs as mental representations within individual minds were classified as World 2, whereas those viewing ideas as collective creations emerging from group discussions were categorised as World 3 (Zhang et al., 2026). All students’ writings on EBs were independently coded by one researcher and DeepSeek. The Cohen’s kappa coefficients for the two questions were 0.825 and 0.771, respectively. The teacher was blind to the students’ EB scores, which helped minimise potential bias or influence related to these measures. Student engagement in analytics-supported knowledge building. Students’ responses to the worksheets are analysed to examine their engagement with the ONA-supported reflective assessment. As well, an interview was conducted with several students. The interview transcript and reflection worksheets were collected to analyse their engagement in ONA-supported reflection activities. A qualitative analysis was conducted to examine how students engaged in the ONA-supported reflective assessment and KB inquiry. RQ1: Did the ONA-enhanced reflective assessment support students’ improvement of epistemological beliefs?

Social interactions The results of the percentage of weighted out-degree and the paired sample t-test are presented in Table 4. The paired sample t-test revealed that, after lesson 1 and lesson 2, the percentage of weighted out-degree increased significantly (t = -4.045, p < 0.001; t = -3.784, p = 0.001). These findings indicate that after engaging in meta-discourse and reflective assessment focused on “sustained questions” and “improving ideas,” students were more likely to build on existing ideas by proposing sustained questions and improving ideas. Epistemological beliefs For the question “Where do I think ‘ideas’ come from?”, more students adopted a World 3 perspective after the intervention (24/40, 60%) compared to before the intervention (16/40, 40%). However, the chi-square test did not indicate statistical significance (χ² (1, n = 80) = 3.20, p = 0.074). For the question “Why do we need ‘ideas? What can ‘ideas’ do?”, the number of students holding a World 3 perspective increased from 17/40 (42.5%) before the intervention to 30/40 (75%) afterwards, and this difference w as statistically significant (χ² (1, n = 80) =8.76, p = 0.003). Overall, the intervention enhanced students’ EBs, shifting from a World 2 to a World 3 perspective. For example, one student wrote before the intervention, “Ideas come from our mind; ideas are used to think,” but by the end of the course, stated, “Ideas come from questions; we need ideas because they can generate new questions; ideas enable us to solve problems.” RQ2: How did the students engage in knowledge advances and ONA-enhanced reflective assessment?

We presented the key events from students’ KB talk in the classroom, students’ responses to ONA worksheets, 516 and interviews to analyse student engagement in community knowledge advancement and in ONA-supported reflective activities are also examined.

Theme 1: Posting questions and ideas.  Before the intervention, students posted their initial questions and ideas about metabolism on the KF view. Their questions included: “What does metabolism include?” “What is the relationship between metabolism and exercise?” “What is the link between metabolism and obesity?” “Why does metabolism weaken with age?” “How is metabolism related to height?”, and “What are the advantages and disadvantages of slow metabolism?” Theme 2: Asking sustained questions. In the reflective ONA worksheets from lesson 1, most students recognised the importance of sustained questioning. They noted, “After the classroom discussion, we found that the KB trajectory ‘Question → Idea → Sustained Question’ can promote knowledge creation. In our previous KF discourse, we lacked sustained questions.” Subsequently, students began to ask sustained questions to deepen existing ideas in KF. For example, in response to “What is the relationship between m etabolism and exercise?”, one student answered, “High-intensity exercise accelerates metabolism,” while another sustained with, “Why does exercise accelerate metabolism?” Theme 3: Improving existing ideas. In the reflective ONA worksheets from lesson 2, students observed, “Sustained questions arise from ideas, and ideas come from initial questions.” They also commented, “The line between sustained questions and improving ideas is subtle,” and “Some ideas ar e too simple; we need more improved ideas.” As a result, students sought additional information from the internet or books and proposed improving ideas in response to sustained questions on KF. For instance, one student wrote: “During exercise, our bodies require a large amount of oxygen, which enters the bloodstream and circulates through the heart. This process involves metabolism, as the heart helps remove waste products like carbon dioxide, thereby accelerating blood circulation and expelling these wastes from the body.” Theme 4: Synthesising diverse ideas to answer initial questions. In the reflective ONA worksheets from lesson 3, students wrote: “From the ONA diagram, I noticed there are many connections between sustained questions and ideas, and more between sustained questions and improving ideas. We need to do more summarising of ideas and explanations, as this helps make complex thoughts more concise and understandable.” Each student selected an initial question and wrote a synthesis of improving ideas related to that  question. This study investigated how ONA-enhanced reflective assessment can support students in reflecting on their KB trajectories on KF and in adjusting their actions during subsequent phases of inquiry. The quantitative findings indicate that students shifted from a World 2 perspective (focused on personal and subjective understanding) to a World 3 perspective (centred on collective, objective knowledge), alongside demonstrating increased levels of community-collaborative interaction. These results align with our initial conjecture: through developing a deeper understanding of KB trajectories during the ONA-enhanced reflective assessment process, students gained insights into the nature of collective knowledge with richer social interactions in KF discourse. The ONA analysis also suggests that  students with higher ONA-reflection scores, demonstrating better understanding of ONA visualisation and reflection of the KB learning trajectory, demonstrate better epistemic action patterns. The qualitative analysis provides further nuance, showing that students actively examined their ONA diagrams to identify areas for improvement and to strategically refine their KF discourse. Students developed more understanding, progressing from a relatively basic trajectory (“Initial Question → Idea”) to a more sophisticated pattern (“Initial Question → Idea → Sustained Question ↔ Improving Idea (with Information) → Synthesis”). This shift reflects not only an increased awareness of KB trajectories but al so a growing recognition of the temporal and iterative nature of the KB process. By making discourse patterns visible, ONA visualisations and reflection prompts empower students to engage in the KB trajectory. These findings align with and extend prior research on using reflective assessment (Resendes et al., 2015; Yang et al., 2021) for advancing knowledge building. We pioneered the use of ONA visualisation-reflection for school-aged children, and our initial work has demonstrated that ONA interventions can enhance students’ domain understanding (Zhang et al., 2025). The present study goes further by examining the effects of ONA-based reflective assessment on students’ epistemological beliefs and social interactions, and connecting the more closely related relations of ONA reflection with epistemic action on the Knowledge Forum.
