# Collective Development of Practices and Tools to Support Culturally and Critically Ambitious Science Teaching

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Science teacher education faces significant challenges in linking pedagogy with equity and in overcoming the isolation of teacher preparation programs in the United States. Legislation opposing equity and social justice, combined with the idiosyncratic nature of isolated programs, has led to fragmented and inefficient teacher education. This conceptual paper advocates for collective practices and tool development as a means to better prepare science teachers as well as shape the growth of science teacher educators. Using the framework of Cultural and Critical Ambitious Science Teaching (C2AST) as a model, we propose a new paradigm for cross-institutional collaboration that respects local contexts while establishing a shared language and vision. Our framework for equity-focused science education aims to create common tools and practices that enhance pre-service teachers' critical consciousness and instructional skills. By integrating core instructional practices with culturally responsive pedagogy, we envision a more unified and effective approach to teacher preparation.

Problem Justice-focused science teacher education —education that empowers students with the knowledge  and skills to challenge and address social inequities (Westheimer  & Kahne, 2004) —faces two immense challenges. First, opportunities for new teachers to consider how peda gogy and social justice are inherently linked have been constrained by existing school culture, and increasingly through state legislation that constrains equity, diversity, and social justice within schools (Woo et al., 2024). Second, there is a long-standing problem of science teacher education programs working in relative isolation, w hich produces idiosyncratic and siloed knowledge, p ractices, and structures for supporting teacher learning (Grossman, 2018). These challenges illustrate that our failure to create a shared understanding of key constructs and  practices creates a teacher education knowledge de sert with small, separate oases of innovation leading to inef fective and inefficient means for the improvement o f teacher education, and by extension the development of teachers in service to our nation’s children. While attempts to solve analogous problems in other  fields, such as the Flexner Report in medical education, have been credited for more standardizat ion and a higher quality formation experience, revi ews over the past two decades have highlighted the destructi ve aspects of this report, particularly to physicia ns of Color (Laws, 2021). Given these challenges and the historical approac hes of other professions that have failed marginalized populations, we propose the field of t eacher education engage in cross-institutional research and practical collaborations that embrace the differences between programs in order to grow as a field. We propose a new paradigm involving multiple institutional collaborations with attention to recognizing the necessity of placebased decision-making, drawing on the culture and history of speci fic teacher education environments, communities, and institutional contexts while also working across sites to create a shared language and vision of professional work that is designed to be useful across programs, while remaining responsive to local needs. This paper uses science teacher education as a case  study of the possibilities of this paradigm. We fi rst describe our theoretical foundations for teacher learning and provide an overview of an equity-focused framework for science education as an example that has the potential to serve as the anchor point for developing our common language, tools, and practices across diverse sites. Second, we outline the challenges that currently prevent a shared vision of teacher preparation focused on equ ity and justice across institutions. Third, we present a vision of the possible, how we might, as a field, begin to  overcome these challenges by outlining ways in whi ch disciplinary methods courses can be a locus for dev eloping teachers’ critical consciousness simultaneo us with learning disciplinary instructional practices. Furt hermore, we outline ways in which teacher educators  across diverse institutions and programmatic structures ca n collaborate to create common experiences and lang uage augmented by programmatic diversity. We conclude by outlining critical research needs in this space as a means to prepare generations of pre-service teachers [PSTs] that remain committed to social justice in their classrooms. Challenges to a shared vision of teacher education Several challenges constrain a shared vision of teacher education. For example, a programmatic disconnect often exists between courses designed to develop PSTs’ vision of equity and justice and the methods course s designed to develop their teaching practice. Teacher educato rs frequently call for PSTs to examine issues of equity and justice in terms of access, opportunity, and achiev ement, as well as attend to power, identity, and so ciopolitical contexts as they design classroom environments (Erickson & Gutierrez, 2002). Yet such conversations are often confined to “foundations” classes and the conversations are frequently absent  from discipline-specific methods courses in which PSTs learn about the daily professional work of teaching (Calabrese Barton & Tan, 2020). PSTs are often asked to do the complex work of making co nnections across courses between equity and social justice and the way that they teach disciplinary content, which results in fragmentation and incoherence in the preparation program, specifically around cultural and critical educational issues. A second challenge is that our field does not currently have shared language, practices, and tools to guide teacher preparation programs. Individual teacher preparation programs are often is olated, and must constantly innovate in isolation. Communities and professions grow as p ractitioners and researchers develop shared languages, practices, and tools that can be used, adapted, and worked on collectively across sites of work. Without common artifacts and discourses, the field ca nnot focus on common  problems of practice, nor can we begin to think about equitable classrooms given the vastly different images of instruction across sites of teacher preparation (Grossman & Dean, 2019; Shah, 2021).

We argue that isolation is not an innate property o f teacher education. Rather, persistent myths about individual teacher education programs’ variable str uctures and needs have caused cross-site collaborations and research to be eschewed. While we know individual p rograms can work, such solitary efforts are ineffic ient and deny opportunities for collective growth across the  field. We know that designing better tea cher preparation programs has been part of national conversations fo r over 100 years (Fraser, 2007), yet there has been almost no systematic research around teacher preparation peda gogies across multiple institutional contexts that are most productive in developing complex instruction that aims to disrupt inequities in classrooms (Fraser, 2007). We also suggest a critical step involves reframing the inst itutional and structural diversity of teach er education programs as an asset to understanding how individual programs contextualize teacher education pedagogies. Therefore, we seek a conversation and research program across sit es of teacher preparation that intentionally represents variability in program structure to explore the impact of a shared pedagogical framework and set of practices. Framing critical and cultural approaches to ambitious science teaching Our work takes a sociocultural perspective on learn ing (Lave & Wenger, 1991), and views knowing as socially negotiated and constructed by the participants in a dynamic process of science (or science teaching)-in-the-making (Kelly et al., 1998). Moving the field away from less idiosyncratic and  toward more coherent language and practices that can elevate instructional practice a cross multiple contexts requires cross-institutional research and collaboration. These partnerships can create foundational tools and tasks that can be adapted to meet local needs, but only if ideas and discourse candiffuse and be taken up across institutional boundaries. To develop a critical equity perspective, PSTs must first conceptualize science classrooms as places where all students can experience science learning in ways th at are relevant to their own worlds and cultures. Critical and Cultural approaches to Ambitious Scien ce Teaching (C 2AST) is a framework designed to support PSTs in “grounding learning opportunities in studen ts’ cultures and identities and work toward cultura lly and linguistically sustaining practices” (Thompson et al., 2021, p. 59)  as a means of  “increas[ing] the capacity for everyone to participate in meaningful learning activity, and  requires wide-ranging professional skills to sustain each day” (Windschitl et al., 2018, p. 12). This framework provides a foundation for the visi on of the science teacher education practices we hope to develop.

Developing a C2AST mindset and associated practices requires well-designed learning opportunities for PSTs (Kavanagh & Danielson, 2020). We argue that PSTs’ must be provided experiences with practice and space, both before and after, tonegotiate the meaning of those practices. For example, these might include participation in approximations of practice (e.g. rehearsals) and deconstructions of practice, such as video analysis of teaching. PSTs need opportunities to examine the way students  express their thinking in the context of purposefu l instructional moves and classroom interactions. PST s’ participation in such practice-based pedagogies s upports them in developing a deeper understanding of C2AST while illuminating how instructional decisions support some students and constrain opportunities for others. Examining how C2AST tools and practices are implemented across diverse contexts provides  an opportunity for the field to see if and how prac tice-based teacher education pedagogies might help PSTs learn to notice and attend to student thinking in critical and culturally-focused ways throughout their initial licensure experiences and into their first year s of teaching. To accomplish this goal, it is necessary to address challenges that have tempered progress as a field. New opportunities for growth in the field: The case of C2AST We propose an exploration of C2AST across multiple teacher education programs as a first step toward providing more unity across teacher educators. C 2AST addresses the first challenge of teacher educat ion by integrating issues of equity and pedagogy through its inherent positioning of children as sensemakers, as well as raising questions about who science is for, who has been marginalized in science, and how participants in science exercise their agency to challenge the status quo. In particular, language exists for exploration and extension in individual sites around a set of four principles: 1) Recognizi ng our own and other’s worlds and developing critic al consciousness; 2) Learning about and prioritizing students’ communities and cultures; 3) Challenging the culture of science through social and restorative justice; and  4) Designing for each student’s full participation  in the culture of science  (Thompson et al., 2021). While teacher educators across multiple sites may v ary in their approaches, pedagogies, and assessments, these principles provide both language and a set of non-negotiables for what must be addressed in the pre-service experience.

C2AST is also well-positioned to address the second challenge of build ing commonality because  of its roots in core instructional practices (i.e. Ambitio us Science Teaching (Windschitl, et al., 2019)). To highlight what cross-institutional collaboration using C 2AST looks like we offer a glimpse into how two auth ors (Kirsten and Heather) situated in radically different contexts (Hawaiʻi  and Tennessee) co-developed place-based immersive experiences that attended to culture, history, community and institutional context: When Kirsten and Heather first started working with  AST, their shared goal was to provide their PSTs with experiences that would help them learn how to center student ideas and sensemaking in their teaching. Most of their PSTs had never experienced science in ways that treated their ideas as valuable resources for learning  so they collaboratively designed an immersive learning experience. Both Kirsten and Heather  showed their PSTs images of dew-covered grass in the early morning alongside the same grass later in the day without dew and asked them: where does the dew come from? and, where does the dew go? While they found this to be effective in terms of introducing discipline-focused teaching practices of AST, it was inadequate to help cultivate a critical equi ty lens PSTs needed to reimagine science teaching and learning i n culturally expansive ways (Barton & Tan, 2020; Thompson et al., 2021). A nalysis of the lessons PSTs develop ed and implement ed revealed that while they centered student ideas and sensemaking they prioritized canonical science and included limited opportunities for their students to draw on lived experiences as prod uctive resources during science learning. Kirsten and Heather asked themselves what they, as instructors, needed to do differently if they wanted to help PSTs make culture, place and social justice important in their teaching. They realized that the shared immersive experience about dew was not providing them an explicit model of how to operationalize that knowledge in the learning exper iences they were designing and teaching. Working collaboratively, they used the C2AST principles to design immersive experiences that were specific to their respective contexts. In Hawaiʻi that meant making space for exploring Indigenous ways of knowing and doing science, as well as examining the ongoing injustice of settler col onialism. This resulted in Kirsten and her PSTs dev eloping an immersive cultural and placed-based secondary science unit on Kaulana Mahina (pha ses of the moon through a KʻŌ lens). This unit partners KʻŌ lunar knowledge with western conceptualizations of the lunar cycle to engage students in thinking about the question: No ke aha mai, ua ʻokoʻa ka mahina ke nānā aku (Why does the moon look different ?) This desettled unit incorporates kilo, KʻŌ sense abilities (Oliveria, 2014), as well as ʻōlelo Hawaiʻi, and aloha ʻāina (care for and stewardship of place). Since beginning to use this unit, Kirsten has noticed a dramatic shift in the design of PSTs’ lesson plans to center KʻŌ ways of knowing and doing. In Nashville, Heather’s PSTs are generally not from  the Nashville area  and initially do not feel a connection to the city. Yet, the rapidly growing population is felt everywhere as new developments are constantly changing the landscape of the city. Heather’s immer sive experience started with recent news articles a bout the disappearing tree canopy in Nashville. PSTs discussed why tree canopies are important, who benefits from having them, why they are disappearing, where this is happening, and the consequences if the trend continues. As part of the experience, PSTs explored data regarding different areas of Nashvill e’s tree canopy overlayed with lenses of race, socioeconomic status, and disease where patte rns quickly emerge that surface inequities regarding where tree canopies are located and thus who benefits fro m them (Carlone et al., 2024). This led to discussions abo ut what kind of action PSTs could take as future teachers to promote environmental justice in units like this. Both AST immersive learning experiences use C 2AST principles to unpack what it means to take an equitable and ambitious approach to teaching science. Even though  Hawaiʻi and Tennessee are different, having a common focus on culture, language, and place, and a shared understanding of AST and C2AST allowed Kirsten and Heather  to support each other in this important work. Their collaboration helped them develop discourse structures and tools similar enough for them to learn with and from each other, but specific eno ugh to support PSTs in their unique contexts. Now more than ever scienc e teacher educators candraw strength in thinking together about how to navigate challenging sociopol itical contexts and cultivate rich linguistic and c ultural repertoires of practice through the design of engaging immersive learning opportunities for PSTs. Setting a research agenda for the field To understand these complex learning environments a cross contexts requires looking at research methodo logy through new eyes and also a patience for complex work that allows for the development of claims over time. We suggest that this work start with mid-sized groups that can foster productive collaborati on and identify ‘pain points’ for common language and tools that are raised by various programmatic constraints. This approach reflects the exploratory and design-based nature of this work as an alternative to the history of single institutional field-based investigations. Multi-site collaborations and studies of 5-8 institutions could capture significant diversity in program structure, geography, and culture while all owing for the iterative collection of evidence that  can lead to broader studies based on the design principles that can be developed. Perhaps more importantly, the methodological approa ch to this work is countercultural in many traditions, as we see the work necessarily reframing the diverse institutional structures and backgrounds as assets, not deficits to the methodological rigor. The work should be don e across various institutions that serve different populations, located in different geographic locati ons, and composed of different program structures. As we can see in Kirsten and Heather’s example, thi s diversity across programs can be significant, but that is what allows for the methodological richness of a cross institutional study. In the long view, these are not variables that can, or as evidenced by the historical case in medical education, should, necessarily be controlled. Understanding where language, tasks, and practices do and do not resona te across various programs can lead tonew theoreti cal understandings of what can be achieved when institu tions identify foundational design principles that can be adapted and evolve in ways that best serve PSTs and the students they teach. Advancing the field of teacher education in ways th at bring utility to collaborations and builds theor y will require a constant dialectic between research and practice. Here we propose a sample of initial q uestions whose exploration would benefit not only the field of science education, but teacher education more broadly: ● Looking across multiple university-based teacher preparation contexts, how and why do PSTs’ Critical and Cultural Ambitious Science Teaching practice shift over time? ● What are the design features of science methods courses across contexts that influence PSTs’ development of Critical and Cultural Ambitious Science Teaching practice? ● How does our understanding of well-articulated sets of teaching practice evolve as we understand their use and enactment across contexts?

● What professional learning practices are productive for supporting cross-institutional teacher education learning and developing a shared knowledge base for teacher education from the diverse enactment of common practices and tools?

To address these types of questions, we call for fu nding to provide three important trajectories for collaboration. First, funding for conferences to bring together teacher educators from a variety of program and institutional contexts to identify opportunities fo r cross-institutional collaboration. Second, building on th ose conferences, the most significant need will be taki ng the ideas to the field and engaging in the work of collaboration and associated research. In particular, we see the need for initial funding for mid-sized groups (~58 institutions) that can produce evidence of proces ses for cross-institutional collaborations to shape teacher education as well as outcomes, roadblocks, and unfo reseen successes. Finally, if we intend to see chan ges in practice that stick, this work should take the long view and allow for data collection through the teacher education experience and into the first two or more years of teaching.

## Conclusion

This paper raises questions about seemingly false assumptions that collaboration and shared language and tools will necessarily fail to create teaching practices that are rigid and unresponsive to particular needs  and contexts of different teacher preparation progra ms. Our conceptual stance recognizes that all teach ers can move toward more equitable and inclusive science education that positions all students as sensemakers. In order to do that, the field must move forward the possibility of developi ng common language, tools, and practices that can m eet the diverse contextual needs of learning at different locations. This paper identifies existing challenges and proposes a vision of the possible through the example of cro ss-site implementation of C 2AST. Additionally, we raise questions for the field and identify future funding  priorities that can provide first of its kind evid ence about effective science teacher education for equity and diversity across a community of teacher educators. Ultimately, this research program can provide a model of teacher education  practices and tools that can be shared and made available as resources to everyone engaged in teacher education.
