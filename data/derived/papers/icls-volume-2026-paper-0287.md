# A Latent Profile Analysis of Math Self-Efficacy and Anxiety in Game-Based Learning

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study investigated how math anxiety and self-efficacy shape students’ algebra learning in From Here to There! (FH2T), a digital game for algebraic reasoning. Data from 607 seventh graders were analyzed using Latent Profile Analysis, identifying five p rofiles representing unique combinations of math performance, worry, and emotional reaction. Grounded in Self-Efficacy Theory, the findings show how belief in ability shapes students’ emotional and cognitive experiences in digital learning.

In math classrooms, students often look similar on the surface but experience learning in very different ways. One student may solve algebra problems with ease and confidence, quickly recognizing patterns and enjoying the challenge. Another, with the same level of skill, may be distracted by constant worry about making mistakes, rereading each step, and second-guessing their answers. A third may respond with frustration, sighing, or shutting down when tasks become difficult.  Another may appear calm but avoidant, completing problems mechanically without much engagement. These combinations of algebra knowledge, cognitive worry, and emotional reactions shape how students approach challenges, how long they persist, and whether they see setbacks as opportunities to learn or as reasons to give up. However, when researchers rely on traditional analyses that average across all students, these important differences remain hidden.

This study draws on publicly available data from the From Here to There!  (FH2T) efficacy trial, part of a larger project evaluating three educational technologies for improving algebra understanding (Decker-Woodrow et al., 2023; Ottmar et al., 2023). The sample included 607 seventh-grade students who completed all pre-, mid-, and post-tests and participated in classroom-based algebra gameplay sessions. FH2T is an interactive algebra learning environment game that helps students learn algebra by moving and changing numbers and symbols to turn one expression into an equivalent one using gestures on the screen. Our goal was to understand how students’ affective–cognitive patterns evolve over time and how confidence in their math self-efficacy influences these changes. Conceptually, we extend prior work by identifying learner profiles that integrate algebra knowledge with both cognitive (worry) and emotional (negative reaction) dimensions of math anxiety, providing a nuanced picture of students’ learning e xperiences. We then examine how math self-efficacy predicts profile membership and change over time. This study addresses two key research questions: What profiles of algebra knowledge, cognitive worry, and emotional negative reactions emerge from the data? To what extent does math self-efficacy predict profile membership and change across time?

## Literature review

For decades, research has shown that math anxiety can impact cognitive processing and create negative feelings toward mathematics, affecting both academic performance and emotional well-being (Ashcraft & Krause, 2007; Hembree, 1990). This anxiety has two dimensions: a cognitive aspect, often called “worry,” which involves concerns about performance and failure; and an emotional aspect, characterized by “negative reactions” like physiological tension and avoidance behaviors (Dowker et al., 2016; Ganley & McGraw, 2016; Harari et al., 2013). The interplay between emotion and self-efficacy is important in math learning. Recent studies show that math-anxious students struggle to manage emotions during problem solving, while those with higher self-efficacy tend to stay engaged, viewing challenges as learning opportunities (Supriadi & Suherman, 2024; Živković et al., 2023). These findings highlight the importance of understanding how confidence and emotion shape students’ math learning experiences. Digital game-based learning environments offer a promising avenue for supporting students in a less threatening context. Resear ch suggests that a playful and developmentally appropriate design can support integrated learning, merging cognitive and emotional engagement (Zandi et al., 2024). For example, recent studies on the algebra game FH2T provide insight on how students’ gameplay behaviors relate to algebra learning. Chan et al. (2022) found that FH2T’s perceptual design improved students’ understanding of algebraic equivalence more effectively than traditional practice, suggesting that interactive feedback can enhance engagement and conceptual learning. Norum et al. (2024) used FH2T data to identify four distinct student profiles 1927 based on in-game performance and help-seeking behaviors.  Their work demonstrated that metrics like strategy efficiency, error frequency, and hint use were significant predictors of algebra knowledge, even after accounting for prior performance.

This paper extends this line of inquiry by applying a person-centered approach to identify affective-cognitive profiles of students playing FH2T. We examine how math self-efficacy predicts profile membership, connecting research on math anxiety, self-efficacy, and digital learning design. In doing so, we provide a more nuanced understanding of how students differ in their engagement and emotional experience during gameplay.

## Theoretical framework

This study is grounded in Bandura’s (1997) Self-Efficacy Theory, which posits that an individual's belief in their capability to perform a task is a primary driver of their motivation, emotional reactions, and persistence. According to this framework, a student's confidence in their math ability shapes how they interpret and manage affective states like anxiety. Recent work confirms this, showing that students with higher self-efficacy are more engaged with challenging problems, whereas those with lower sel f-efficacy struggle to manage their emotions (Živković et al., 2023). Specifically, a student with high self-efficacy may interpret feelings of worry as a normal response to a challenge, allowing them to persist. In contrast, a student with low self-efficacy is more likely to view those same feelings as a sign of impending failure, leading to avoidance (Bandura, 1997). This theory provides insight for why math self-efficacy would predict students' membership in distinct profiles, connecting their self-beliefs to their patterns of performance, worry, and emotional reaction during gameplay. Students (N = 607; 47% female; Grade 7) participated in nine 30-minute FH2T sessions during regular classroom instruction.  First, we used latent profile analysis (LPA) to identify distinct profiles of students based on algebra knowledge, cognitive worry, and emotional negative reaction at each time point. To examine motivational predictors of profile membership, we used a Bayesian multilevel categorical regression model estimated in R using the brms package (Bürkner, 2017). Because students were measured r epeatedly across time, multilevel modeling was used to account for the nested data structure. Math self-efficacy was entered as a fixed predictor of profile membership, and a random intercept for students accounted for individual differences in their basel ine patterns of profiles. This provided a more accurate and robust estimate of how students’ confidence in their math ability predicted their likelihood of belonging to different profiles across time.

## Measures

Algebra knowledge was assessed pre-, mid-, and post-gameplay using standardized algebra problem-solving tasks designed to capture procedural fluency and conceptual understanding. Cognitive worry and emotional negative reaction, the two components of math anxiety, were measured using subscales, focused on intrusive thoughts and physiological tension when engaging with mathematical tasks, adapted from the Math Anxiety Scale for Young Children–Revised (Ganley & McGraw, 2016). Math self-efficacy was measured by five items from the Academic Efficacy subscale of the Patterns of Adaptive Learning Scales (Midgley et al., 2000). For RQ1, we estimated latent profile models in R (version 4.5.1) using the tidyLPA package (van Lissa, 2018). To determine the optimal number of latent profiles, models with one to six classes were estimated and compared using the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), and sample-size adjusted BIC (SABIC). All information crit eria decreased sharply from one to four profiles and began to level off thereafter. The five-profile solution yielded the lowest BIC (4,891.43) and AIC (4,794.44) values, with only minimal improvement at six profiles. Entropy also indicated good classifica tion accuracy (≈.72), exceeding the recommended threshold of.70 for reliable class separation (Celeux & Soromenho, 1996). Following Hickendorff et al. (2018) and Nylund et al. (2007), a five-profile model was selected as the most parsimonious and theoretically meaningful solution.  A five-profile solution emerged at pretest and was replicated at mid-and post-test after aligning class labels across waves.

For RQ2, to examine whether students’ math self-efficacy predicted their likelihood of belonging to different affective–cognitive profiles, we estimated a Bayesian multilevel categorical regression model using the brms package in R (Bürkner, 2017). In this model, profile served as the dependent variable, representing students’ classification across five latent profiles that captured distinct combinations of algebra knowledge, cognitive worry, and emotional negative reaction. Math self-efficacy was included as a fixed predictor to test whether students with 1928 higher confidence in their mathematical abilities were more likely to belong to adaptive profiles. Because each student contributed multiple observations across three time points, a multilevel structure was specified to account for the nested nature of the  data. A random intercept for each student captured individual variability in baseline profile membership, ensuring that estimates reflected true effects of self-efficacy rather than repeated measures within individuals. The model used a logit link function appropriate for categorical outcomes. Posterior summaries included parameter estimates, standard errors, and 95% credible intervals (CIs) for each profile comparison, with profile 1 set as the reference category. This approach allowed for probabilistic inference regarding how self-efficacy shaped students’ likelihood of belonging to more or less adaptive profiles. We found five profiles, each representing a distinct profile of algebra knowledge, cognitive worry, and emotional negative reaction (see Figure 1).

RQ2: To what extent does math self-efficacy predict profile membership?

To address RQ2, we examined the extent to which students’ math self-efficacy predicted their likelihood of belonging to each affective– cognitive profile. As shown in Table 1, math self-efficacy significantly predicted 1929 profile membership. Students with higher math self-efficacy were 13% more likely to belong to Profile 2 (High Anxiety, High Math), b = 0.12, 95% CI [0.07, 0.17]; 63% more likely to belong to Profile 3, b = 0.49, 95% CI [0.40, 0.59]; and 12% more likely to belong to Profile 4 (Low Anxiety, Low Math), b = 0.11, 95% CI [0.06, 0.17], compared to Profile 1 (Moderate Anxiety, Low Math). In contrast, a one-unit increase in math self-efficacy made students 19% less likely to belong to Profile 5 (High Worry, Low Mat h), b = –0.21, 95% CI [–0.27, –0.15]. Overall, math self-efficacy emerged as a predictive factor: students who were more efficacious in their mathematical ability were more likely to belong to high-performance or emotionally regulated profiles, whereas those with weaker self-beliefs were more likely to remain in anxiety-dominated, low-achievement profiles. Interestingly, profile 2 combined high algebra knowledge with high math anxiety, which represents students who engaged in math learning despite emotional tension. Students in this “high-performance, high anxiety” profile challenge the traditional assumption that math anxiety always impairs math achievement. Based on Self-Efficacy Theory (Bandura, 1997), these students may perceive anxiety as a normal part of challenging tasks rather than as a sign of failure. Their high level of self-efficacy helps them persist through discomfort and turn anxiety into motivation rather than avoidance. As mentioned earlier, previous studies have shown that math self-efficacy supports continued engagement in math learning (Martin & Rimm-Kaufman, 2015; Živković et al., 2023). The present study extends these findings by showing that self-efficacy enhances math learning outcomes, even under high levels of anxiety. This effect was consistent across different time points. Math self-efficacy significantly predicted Profiles 2 and 3, both representing students with high levels of math knowledge but differing in intrusive thoughts. Students in Profile 2 experienced more negative thoughts yet showed a good level of math knowledge through strong self-efficacy. In contrast, Profiles 1 and 5 revealed that students with low math self-efficacy tended to struggle with math learning, regardless of whether their anxiety levels were low or high. The results are also consistent with the Processing Efficiency Theory (Eysenck & Calvo, 1992), which suggests that anxiety may reduce cognitive efficiency but not necessarily performance effectiveness. Some mathanxious students may still learn and perform as well as their peers if they can compensate through cognitive strategies or motivational factors such as self-efficacy. The present findings suggest that math self-efficacy is one of the key factors distinguishing high-performing from low-performing math-anxious students. Students with lower self-efficacy, in contrast, were more likely to belong to low-math, high-anxiety profiles and demonstrated hesitation and reduced persistence when facing challenges. The Bayesian model confirmed that math self-efficacy strongly predicted profile membership. Students with higher confidence were more likely to belong to adaptive profiles with stronger performance and lower emotional interference. Overall, these results show that self-efficacy shaped how cognition and emotion interacted dur ing gameplay, determining whether anxiety became a motivator or a barrier. Supporting students’ confidence may therefore be key to helping them persist and regulate emotion in digital learning environments like FH2T. This study contributes to understanding digital game-based environments by illustrating the interplay between cognition and emotion. Our identification of five learner profiles reveals meaningful and distinct patterns in how students approach algebra while managing anxiety. Furthermore, we esta blish math self-efficacy as a pivotal factor that shapes whether students perceive challenges as threats or as manageable tasks. These findings have practical implications, suggesting that interventions focused on building student confidence can enhance persistence and emotional regulation, thereby leveraging games like FH2T for both academic and personal growth.
