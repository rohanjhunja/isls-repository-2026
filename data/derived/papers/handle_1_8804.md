# “Everything That’s Hard Got Harder”: Preservice Teachers’ Attempts at Rigorous and Responsive Instruction During Online Pedagogical Rehearsals in the Covid Pandemic

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
At the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic, our teacher preparation program shifted to an online setting, disrupting a key feature of practice-based teacher preparation: Preservice science teachers’ (PSTs) approximation of rigorous and responsive instruction during extended pedagogical rehearsals called macroteaching. Given this unplanned shock to their preparation, we examined how PSTs viewed macroteaching and their evolving participation in the teaching rehearsal. Using a situative perspective, we collected multiple forms of data. We found that while PSTs wanted to enact rigorous and responsive instruction, their participation was deeply impacted by the sudden shift to an online setting. Our analysis of video-recorded lessons confirmed PSTs’ observations that their instruction became less rigorous and responsive over time. We conclude with questions about teacher preparation during the pandemic.

The title of the paper is a quote from a preservice teacher (PST) describing her experience as an instructor for an extended pedagogical rehearsal during a practice-based secondary science methods course. Noting the difficulty of teacher preparation during a typical year, this PST succinctly summaries the herculean challenge of learning to teach during the COVID-19 pandemic when the teacher preparation program necessarily and abruptly ended in-person methods courses and shifted to online instruction in March 2020. In our secondary science method course, the shift from in-person to online participation immediately disrupted a key feature of practice-based teacher preparation: PSTs’ approximation of rigorous and responsive instruction during pedagogical rehearsals (Grossman et al., 2009). In these rehearsals, dubbed “macroteaching” by a former student (Stroupe & Gotwals, 2018), PSTs learn to provide students with opportunities to examine puzzling phenomena and construct evidence-based explanations (rigor) while noticing and attending to student thinking as they build a classroom community with students (responsiveness) (Thompson et al., 2016, see Literature Review for further descriptions). At the start of the 2020 semester, macroteaching resembled previous instantiations. PSTs were divided into four content groups (biology, earth science, physics, chemistry). The first two groups – biology and earth science – each completed a round of in-person macroteaching, which included the use of 10-12 course instructional hours for the rehearsal of one unit of content instruction taught to peers in the methods course. The physics group completed one in-person lesson in March 2020. Then, as COVID-19 forced the shuttering of the university, the PSTs requested to continue macroteaching online. As teacher educators, we wanted to support PST participation, and as a class, we decided to continue macroteaching with the remining physics unit and the entire chemistry unit using the Zoom™ video teleconferencing platform. However, given this unplanned and unwanted shift in the context of the practicebased teacher education methods course, we became curious about how the PSTs viewed maroteaching and their participation when COVID-19 forced them online. In addition, we wanted to understand how PSTs’ participation around rigor and responsiveness during marcoteaching, both as instructors and students, might be shaped by the online setting. We asked: ¥ How did PSTs frame the purpose of macroteaching when COVID disrupted the practice-based methods course?

¥ How were PSTs’ participation in macroteaching, and their attempts at rigorous and responsive instruction, shaped by the sudden disruption of practice-based methods course?

## Background and framing

As Kang (in press) argues, the field of teacher education struggles to design professional learning opportunities for teachers to take up rigorous and responsive instruction. We extend this concern to teacher preparation, and claim that more research to examine the opportunities for PSTs to learn about, rehearse, and receive feedback about their attempts to enact rigorous and responsive instruction. While we hoped that macroteaching might provide such a learning opportunity for PSTs, moving the methods course online fundamentally changed the dynamic of the class and the instructional episodes that PSTs enacted during macroteaching (See table 1).

Rigor ICLS2022 Proceedings 3 © ISLS While some people may have an image of rigor as ext ensive content coverage and difficult tasks to complete, we claim rigor is an emergent property of discursive classroom interactions (Thompson et al., 2016). We frame rigor as a collaborative effort to construct and revise scientific explanations, which is a central practice across scientific disciplines (See Table 1).

Responsiveness Like rigor, defining and understanding responsivene ss in teaching is difficult. Broadly, from the perspectives of teachers, responsiveness involves noticing, attending to, interpreting, and using students’ ideas and needs to shape pedagogical actions (Kang, in press; van Es et al., 2017). For this study, we focused on two dimensions of responsiveness: building on students’ scientific ideas, and encouraging participation and building classroom community (See Tables 2 and 3).

Practice-based science teacher preparation Given the need for a better understanding of preparing teachers for rigorous and responsive instruction, some researchers have focused on supporting PSTs through practice-based teacher preparation. By practicebased teacher preparation, we mean a framing of teacher education that focuses on disrupting preservice teachers’ images of disciplinary work and teaching, reimagining teaching through a lens of equitable instruction, and learning core teaching practices through multiple opportunities to rehearse teaching, and building relationships with students and their communities (Stroupe, Hammerness, & McDonald, 2020). Through scaffolded work on interactive practices that encourage principled improvisation, preservice teachers can learn the more interactive relational judgments required by the teaching profession. Macroteaching While a practice-based approach to teacher preparation provide a framework for supporting PSTs, teacher educators must design learning opportunities for PSTs to approximate instructional practices. In our methods class, we use “macroteaching” as a learning opportunity for PSTs to approximate rigorous and responsive instruction. Macroteaching takes place at a large Midwestern university, where PSTs participate in a five-year teacher preparation program in which they major (and minor) in a science discipline while fulfilling requirements for a teaching certificate. During the PSTs’ final year of coursework, they participate in two sequential four-hour per week secondary science methods courses. Macroteaching involves groups of PSTs planning, teaching, and reflecting on eleven to twelve consecutive hours of instruction to their peers (approximating a complete unit of instruction) during methods class. During macroteaching, PSTs engage in five learning opportunities: ● In-the-moment consultations. The teaching team gathers (often in a “huddle”) to discuss their immediate and upcoming instructional decision.

● “Time-out/time-in.” A pause during instruction—called a  “time-out”—in order to engage in immediate reflection about a recent event.

● “Rewind.” At times, following the time-out/time-in or instructional coaching, a teaching team member requests an immediate opportunity to retry an interactive episode. ● Question-and-answer session at end of each lesson. During the final five to ten minutes of each lesson taught by the teaching team, the PSTs serving as students and instructors ask the teaching team to decompose pedagogical decisions they made during class, and inquire about upcoming lessons. ● Teaching team debrief at the end of the unit. The preservice teacher “students” and instructors ask the teaching team to decompose pedagogical decisions made during the unit, and to reflect on instructional opportunities they might shift in the future.

Learning to teach in virtual settings While macroteaching usually occurs in person during our methods class, the COVID-19 pandemic forced the PSTs to enter a virtual setting (Zoom™) to continue their preparation work. When investigating how to engage in teacher preparation online, we encountered a dearth of research about learning rigorous and responsive teaching practices in virtual settings (though studies do exist – see Cohen et al., 2019, and Watkins et al., 2020 for examples). This study, then, adds to literature by examining how PSTs’ participation shifted when attempting to teach in a virtual environment.

Theoretical framing We used situative theory to address the complexity of examining how and why PSTs’ participation might be shaped moving into an online setting. Situative theory is a hybrid analytical lens framing individuals’ learning through their participation in activities that occur through interactions with actors, tools, and discourses of a context (Greeno, 2006). A situative perspective on PSTs’ participation helps focus researchers’ analytical lens to make sense of why PSTs’ participation might be shaped by an online setting given the new norms and expectations for participation (see Fairbanks et al., 2010; Peressini et al., 2004; Putnam & Borko, 2000).

## Methods

ICLS2022 Proceedings 4 © ISLS Design Experiment Given the conceptual framework and dual roles as instructors and researchers, we viewed this study as a forced design experiment. By forced design experiment, we mean that outside circumstances (i.e., the COVID19 pandemic) required fundamental changes to the original design of the methods course. In turn, we rapidly and simultaneously engineered particular forms of participation for our PSTs while engaged in a systematic study of that participation within the new virtual methods class context (e.g., Penuel et al., 2011).

## Participants and Methods Class Context

The 15 participants, all undergraduate students majoring in a science field, were peers in a secondary science methods course we co-taught and framed around rigorous and responsive instruction. The participants self-identified as: 9 women (1 East Asian, 1 Iraqi-American, 7 White Americans) and 6 men (White Americans). The methods course in this study was the second in a two-course sequence. We met for four hours per week, two hours per class.

Macroteaching Importantly, the PSTs wanted to continue macroteaching online when the university shut down inperson instruction. As a classroom community, we co-designed an action plan given the impending closure, and the two macroteaching groups that did not teach in-person (physics and chemistry – see below) requested to continue macroteaching in order to have the opportunity for the extended pedagogical rehearsal. We constructed the teaching teams based on disciplinary major and teaching interests. In addition, we assigned each team a typical disciplinary topic that they would likely teach during their teaching career. ## Data Collection

We collected and analyzed multiple forms of data from four different types of interactive episodes: (1) requested or informal planning communication, (2) observations/video recordings of macroteaching, (3) participant-generated artifacts, and (4) two types of interviews. We selected these data sources for three reasons. First, we wanted to collect an array of data to better understand how and why we and the PSTs learned over time. Second, we needed multiple forms of data to examine how the PSTs’ pedagogical reasoning evolved during important episodes of professional work – planning, instructing, and reflecting. Third, given our situated theoretical framework, we thought that the data sources could better capture episodes of participation as the PSTs engaged in instructional practices.

## Data Analysis

In this section, we describe four interacting phases of data analysis: (1) developing and applying codes to the initial round of interview coding, (2), using a priori codes about rigorous and responsive instruction to analyze macroteaching instruction in the online setting, and (3) pattern finding and triangulating data. The first phase of the data analyses focused on developing a coding scheme. The coding scheme was initially informed by the literature on rigorous and responsive teaching, approximations of practice, and later by emergent themes from the data. For interviews, the coding scheme was debated and iteratively revised in weekly research meetings until we reached a consensus. We each coded a sample of data using the final coding scheme, identified areas of disagreement and refined the coding process over time. For coding instructional episodes (see below), we used observation instruments validated in a prior study (see Stroupe & Gotwals, 2018)). We examined teaching episodes, or “small, socially shared scripted pieces of behavior” recognizable across most classrooms (Leinhardt & Steele, 2005). In total we coded 77 episodes within the 18 lessons.

## Findings

In this section, we organize the findings around three assertions. First, while PSTs wanted to participate in rigorous and responsive instruction during online macroteaching, how they framed the purpose of macroteaching reflected the sudden shift in the medium of interaction and the timing of the rehearsals. Second, all participants observed that their participation was shaped by the rapid shift to a new classroom setting. The PSTs serving as instructors noticed that they altered their enactment of instruction, and the PSTs serving as students engaged with their instructors and peers differently than they hoped during macroteaching. Third, our analysis of video-recorded macroteaching observations aligned with the PSTs’ stated observations during interviews: The PSTs’ words and actions became less rigorous and responsive as macroteaching progressed. All ICLS2022 Proceedings 5 © ISLS quotes are taken from whole class debriefing conversations, planning communication, class activities, or interviews. Note that our analysis and claims focus on macroteaching that occurred in the online setting after COVID forced a drastic shift in the practice-based teacher preparation program. We do not compare in-person macroteaching to online macroteaching. However, the PSTs used their experiences during in-person macroteahing to make sense of the dramatic shift in context, and this study aims to both share their stories based on their words and actions, and to examine their attempts at rigorous and responsive instruction as they navigated a terrible situation.

In terms of rigor, the physics and chemistry instr uction decreased from focusing on how and why a phenomenon occurred, to asking students to describe what happened during a phenomenon. By the end of both units, the instructors’ became focused on students citing facts and definitions about a phenomenon, the lowest rating on our coding scheme for rigor. For example, the physics group initially pressed students to determine why radio telescopes on Earth received fast radio bursts at regular intervals from space. During the first lessons, the physics group provided students with opportunities to consider how observable features of the phenomenon related to unobservable features, and to justify their thinking with initial evidence and life experiences. However, as the unit progressed, the physics group became more concerned with covering content and ensuring that students stated correct information. By the end of the unit, the physics group shifted from a focus on ‘why” to a “what” focus in terms of rigor. The chemistry group followed the same pattern: Beginning the unit with high rigor by asking students to explain why chemicals might make people sick, but over time, decreasing the rigor and hoping that students might be able to recall information about chemical reactions. Similarly, responsiveness (in terms of “Responding  to and building on students’ scientific ideas”, BSI, and “Participation structures and the building of a community”, PART) decreased after initial attempts to create online classroom communities in which students felt safe and valued to share and build on each other’s science ideas. In both units, students’ initial science ideas that could have become valuable resources for the classroom community were elicited and publicized, but never again used by the teaching team. For example, in the chemistry unit, the instructors asked students about a phenomenon of people becoming poisoned by mixing household cleaning products. One student, Edna, eagerly shared her story of a similar experience in the university chemistry laboratory in which she mixed chemicals, caused a potentially toxic gas to be created, and was decontaminated by the university fire patrol. This story triggered memories from other students who were in the chemistry building that day, recalled the evacuation, and wondered how a chemical reaction could clear out an entire campus building. Edna’s story had the potential to shape the instructional team’s unit, and during the first lessons, the PSTs engaged in features of responsiveness that rated highly on the coding scheme. By the end of the unit, however, the chemistry team “forgot about Edna story” (Phil), and instead focused on content coverage. Thus, our analysis of classroom videos aligned with PSTs’ observations that their attempts at online macroteaching decreased in rigor and responsiveness over time. Given the decline in rigor and responsiveness during macroteaching, PSTs also noticed that student talk decreased during macroteaching. Our analysis shows that instructional teams may have contributed to the decline by enacting instructional episodes that provided fewer opportunities for student talk. Specifically, we found that episodes that might promote talk, such as whole class conversations, became less frequent over time. In addition, episodes types that might limit possibilities for talk, such as content injection and seat work, increased in frequency during macroteaching Some episodes that might promote talk, such as small group conversations, were enacted across macroteaching lessons. While such episodes remained consistent in terms of frequency of enactment, the decrease in rigor and responsiveness of the episodes limited opportunities for talk, thus shaping student participation. Importantly, the PSTs’ observations and instruction aligned: A decrease in rigor and responsiveness (especially in terms of BSI and PART) indicates that students had fewer opportunities to talk about science ideas, which is the phenomenon reported by the PSTs in interviews.

## Discussion

This study occurred in the midst of a global pandemic that forced PSTs into an online setting, requiring them to make pedagogical decisions that they never anticipated, nor as they noted, ever wanted to make. As the course instructors, we wondered how the PSTs’ participation in an extended pedagogical rehearsal – macroteaching – might be shaped by the new online setting. In this section, we make sense of the findings by noting that while the online setting constrained PSTs’ participation, in another way, the online setting helped strengthen their desire to enact rigorous and responsive instruction because they experienced teaching episodes that limited their opportunities to learn with and from each other’s ideas. Rigor and responsiveness decreased As noted in the findings, the rigor and responsiveness of instructional episodes decreased over the two macroteaching units taught online. Student talk became less frequent, teacher talk increasingly resembled Initiate-Response-Evaluate (IRE) discourse aimed at moving a conversation in the teacher’s direction, and the ICLS2022 Proceedings 6 © ISLS overall science talk became more focused on facts than on explanatory conversations about phenomena in the units. Thus, the cognitive demand of the class decreased (Michaels and O’Conner, 2012), even though the online setting was in place for half of the macroteaching units. While PSTs attempted to support conversations in which students might engage in rigorous and responsive activity, the reduction in participation constrained opportunities to hear and use students’ ideas as resources to shape instruction. Subsequently, instruction resembled “delivery pedagogy” (Stroupe, 2016), in which the teachers are the sole knowledge authority their focus remains on controlling the classroom and covering curriculum (Duschl, 2008).

Using a situative perspective, we offer a two-pron ged explanation that emerged from the data for the reduction in rigorous and responsive instruction over the course of macroteaching online. First, PSTs’ engaged in pedagogical reasoning while simultaneously making sense of a new and rapidly changing setting. While the PSTs expressed disappointment that their participation dwindled and that their instruction drifted away from rigorous and responsive instruction, they understandably made participatory decisions that focused on surviving and accepting the online teaching rehearsal. The online setting required PSTs to question their initial instructional goals, and they subsequently made decisions based on their interpretation of possibilities. PSTs could simultaneously envision how they wanted to participate based on their experiences in-person, but still chose to think and act in ways that decreased rigor and responsiveness over time citing the online medium as the cause of their dual thinking.

Second, PSTs explicitly described how the online setting limited opportunities for using students’ ideas and experiences as resources to shape instruction, which is a hallmark of rigorous and responsive instruction. PSTs lamented that the online setting limited how much time they could provide students to talk, engage in activities, and make sense of puzzling phenomena, and thus, they heard less from students over time. Without students’ ideas available PSTs resorted to information delivery in the hopes of covering content deemed important during planning sessions. Even when students shared experiences that were immediately relevant to the unit (such as Edna’s story about the chemistry lab evacuation), such ideas were rarely elevated to the public plane of talk at the whole-class level, or were lost in the chaos of online instruction. While online settings provide spaces for students’ ideas to exist, PSTs often used such spaces as storage sites rather than inscriptions to be leveraged for later lessons. Again, PSTs frequently noted they wanted to use student thinking as they encountered during macroteaching in-person, but their reasoning about survival and enduring led them to make instructional decisions that limited the elicitation and use of students’ ideas. PSTs’ participatory opportunities changed Given PSTs’ decisions to survive and accept the limitations of the context, their participatory opportunities changed for two reasons from a situative perspective. First, PSTs felt unable to use the learning opportunities embedded in macroteaching, such as time-outs and group consultations. Prior to moving online, PSTs serving as instructors witnessed the five learning opportunities embedded in macroteaching, and observed how their peers’ participation was aided by the opportunities. In the online setting, however, PSTs expressed frustration because they felt limited in their learning opportunities in the online setting, especially after watching the biology and earth science groups engage in the reflective teaching during in-person macroteaching. PSTs, then, made decisions to survive and endure online macroteaching at the expense of more time-consuming but beneficial learning opportunities they observed in-person. Second, as Philip et al. (2019) note, teaching practices are inherently relational. As the PSTs noted, the relational work of online instruction seems different than the possibilities for building and growing relationships in-person. In the online setting, PSTs serving as instructors could not engage in hallmarks of rigorous and responsive instructional practices that emphasize relational work such as providing daily opportunities for students to reason through productive talk and making student thinking public and subject to consideration by the classroom community (Engle & Conant, 2002; Lehrer & Schauble, 2006). Unlike in-person macroteaching, PSTs attempting online instruction did not prioritize relational work, which ultimately limited opportunities for students participation.

Tension between desired and enacted instruction Given the decrease in rigorous and responsive inst ruction, and the reduction in PSTs’ participatory opportunities, we worried that PSTs might begin to express an interest delivery pedagogies, or would become disenchanted with teaching. However, all PSTs reported that participating in an online setting helped strengthen their desire to enact rigorous and responsive instruction because they experienced teaching episodes that limited their opportunities to learn. Further, PSTs realized there was striking difference in their participation as instructors and students in-person or in online settings, and they vehemently expressed frustration that they could not enact instruction that aligned with their developing vision of from rigorous and responsive instruction. The tension expressed by the PSTs between the instruction they wanted to enact and the instruction that occurred during online pedagogical episodes is not unique to this study. Teachers, regardless of setting, make ICLS2022 Proceedings 7 © ISLS pedagogical decisions during planning and in-the-moment with students that reflect a constant conversation between a personal vision of instruction and learning and a context’s implicit and explicit messages about what counts as success. The PSTs in this study made the decisions they felt necessary to “survive” and “cope” given the rapidly changing participatory circumstances. They balanced immediate needs of engaging in a pedagogical rehearsal in a drastically different setting with long term goals of learning to teach. Importantly, their vision of teaching that they wanted to enact remained unchanged, even as rigor and responsiveness of the instruction declined. The PSTs recognized that they could enact better instruction, and given a different setting that allowed for in-person interactions, they felt confident that they would make different pedagogical decisions that resulted in rigorous and responsive learning opportunities.

## Conclusion

We are left with two lingering questions for the field to consider about practice-based teacher preparation and supporting PSTs as they learn about rigorous and responsive instruction. First, can online settings support practice-based teacher preparation? Second, will PSTs be more likely to push themselves to enact rigorous and responsive instruction during their first years of teaching? Will they be more attentive to student participation since they noticed how instruction and participatory structures are linked and candecline rigor and responsiveness in tandem?
