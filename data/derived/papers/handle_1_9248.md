# Towards Emotionally Intelligent Scripting: A Methodological and Multimodal Approach to Capture Emotions

**Conference:** ISLS 2023

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Evidence has emerged on the importance of emotions for students’ problem-solving. Despite the convincing evidence on the role of emotions, there has not been much discussion on how the emergence of different kinds of emotions is related to IT students’ problem-solving as well as the challenges of retention and dropout rates. We measure emotions in a multimodal way and study their role in the collaborative problem-solving (CPS) of IT students (N=50). We investigate how emotions are associated with IT students’ CPS involving scripted and non-scripted conditions. Our findings will contribute on reconstructing theories on scripting, by focusing on missing elements, namely the role of emotions.

## Theoretical background

Working life increasingly needs IT professionals with collaborative problem-sol ving (CPS) skills. Globally, the learning/teaching of IT in higher education (HE) faces the challenges of interrupted studies and delayed graduation times. Dropout rates are high: in Finland, up to 75% of students who commence s tudies in IT do not graduate within the set five-year period. One challenge for IT students exists in computer programming courses, with a failure rate of one-third of the students enrolled in introductory programming courses. The courses include diverse CPS tasks. Evidence has emerged on the importance of emotions for students’ learning and study processes, but few studies have addressed how different emotions are intertwined with I T students’ (vocational) learning. One efficient way to promote and enhance CPS is to support CPS via scripts (Vogel et al., 2017; Fischer et al., 2013). CSCL scripts (have been shown to support the development of cognitive, social, or motivational aspects  of learning (Kobbe et al., 2007). Scripts can assist in the scaffolding of specific interaction patterns. Scripts operate by sequencing activities and by assigning roles, seeking thus to enhance effective  CPS via the thoughtful use of available resources and task division (Fischer et al., 2013; Kobbe et al., 2007). Many studies have illustrated the positive effects of socio-cognitive scaffolding via scripts, especially in HE c ontexts (e.g. De Wever et al., 2015)

– all this being related to what one can term the script theory of guidance (see Fischer et al., 2013). However,

there is a missing element in the script theory of guidance, as pointed out by Fischer et al. (2013), namely that the theory does not address the emotional perspective. This poster presentation illustr ates a methodological and multimodal approach to capture emotions via the following RQ: What kinds of emotions are present in CPS under scripted and non-scripted conditions? SubQ: What kinds of valence and arousal di mensions are present within the experimental, behavioural, and physiological components of emotions in CP S activities in the short term?

## Method

The participants are 50 IT students. We investigate the emotions (RQ 1 and SubQ), in scripted and non-scripted conditions. We rely on a multiple-component, multi-dimensional understanding of emotions in vocational learning (Eteläpelto et al., 2018) as follows: Firstly, emotional responses ha ve experiential, physiological, and behavioural components that are activated by appraisal of personal significance. Secondly, emotions have both a valence dimension (contrasting states of pleasure vs. displeasure) and an arousal (or intensity) dimension (low arousal vs. high arousal). For the purposes of our study, we draw on one of the best-known scripts, i.e. the conflict script. The conflict script is rooted in Piaget’s cognitive conflict theory (involving an individual’s interaction with the concrete world); this notion was further developed to address socio-cognitive conflict via an emphasis on the importance of social interaction (Mugny & Doise, 1978). Socio-cognitive conflict will be induced in learners by offering contrary points of view (e.g. within textually and/or graphically repres ented instructional information) through scripts that specify, sequence, and distribute roles and activities. In the conflict script, we create a situation that forces students to resolve this socio-cognitive conflict. The conflict s cript has been chosen on the grounds that it may elicit a range of emotions during socio-cognitive conflict resolution. CSCL 2023 Proceedings  © ISLS 395 The RQ focuses on the identification of emotions. We analyse multimodal emotion data based on the three components of emotions (RQ, SubQ). Data are used in feature enginee ring to create variables for data analysis (e.g., Bulagang et al., 2020). To analyse the experiential component (EC) of emotions and its valence and arousal dimensions, we use EC data and the interviews (covering subjective experiences). Utilising experience sampling, participants will report their emotions by clicking emotion words on EC. We calculate the absolute and percentage frequencies of expressions related to different emotions (EC). Via deductive thematic analysis we obtain information on emotions in terms of valence (i.e., their positive or negative nature), and arousal dimensions (i.e., the intensity of the emotions). To analyse the physiological component of emotions and especially its arousal dimension, we use electrodermal activity (EDA) and Heart rate variability (HRV) data. The assumption is that the EDA tracks changes in the sympathetic nervous systems and that increased psychological arousal accompanies most emotions. We analyse HRV data for the given time spans, to determine  the intervals between consecutive heartbeats (Eteläpelto et al., 2018). HRV indicates the activity and changes in  the autonomic nervous system. EDA and HRV will be pre-processed and analysed via state-of-the-art algorit hms (e.g., time-frequency method) using Python and NeuroKit2. To analyse the behavioural component of emotions, and its valence and arousal dimension, we use face recognition data and audio recordings. We analyse the  facial expressions of individuals and groups using iMotions and the leading facial coding engine Affectiva AFFDEX. From the audio recordings, and from the HRV and EDA indicators, we analyse emotion-related speec h prosody in terms of voice quality, valence, and arousal level. We analyse the acoustic features of fundame ntal frequency (F0) and its variations (Hämäläinen et al., 2018). The acoustics pertaining to vocal expression will be  calculated using Praat software. Finally, we analyse quantitative indicators of emotions from multiple sour ces by building a structural equation model, machine learning models, and causal network models (see, Heilala, 2022).

## Expected results

Despite convincing evidence on the value of conflict scripts, the emotions actu ally present in the conflict resolution process are yet unknown. Developments in artificial intelligence (AI) systems, encompassing the ability to sort, analyse, and learn from massive amounts of data, have the potential to respond to these learning needs. The confluence of multimodal data with multimodal learning analytics will enable u s to understand complex learning processes (Sharma & Giannakos, 2020). However, technology alone will not make  education more efficient. Research is needed on how this potential can be applied ethically, to renew learning theories and develop educational practices. This ongoing research project complements previous re search by analysing emotional diversity, considering notably how emotions are associated with students’ CPS  under scripted and non-scripted conditions. Our approach encompasses ethical perspectives to ensure the inclusive and equitable captu ring of emotions. In the future, this will help on reconstructing theories on scripting.
