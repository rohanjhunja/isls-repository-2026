# Connecting Teacher Sensemaking to Instructional Practice

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
A large body of research has identified core features of effective professional development (PD). Recently, scholars called for moving beyond core features to understand the mechanisms that support teacher learning. This study contributes to this aim by tracing teachers’ sensemaking of PD to their classroom implementation. In the context of a two-year PD initiative in mathematics, this study investigates the relationship between seven teachers’ perceptions of the PD and their instructional practice. Emergin g findings suggest three profiles of teachers’ conceptualizations of the PD approach to teaching and learning. How teachers conceptualized the PD was tightly aligned with what was observed in their practice. These findings have implications for theories of action to support teacher learning during PD. Objectives A substantial body of research has identified core features of effective professional development (PD) (e.g., Desimone, 2009; Darling-Hammond et al., 2017), recognizing the need for high-quality teacher learning experiences that change teachers’ practices and, in turn, improve student outcomes. Recently, this scholarship has been critiqued for overlooking the theories of action and mechanisms that support teacher learning and the highly contextualized nature of PD programs (Asterhan & Lefstein, 2024). Yet, h ow teachers take up PD learning is informed by their individual and collective sensemaking processes, and situational factors they face (Warner et al., 2022). Moreover, impactful professional learning often requires building teachers’ situation-specific decision-making skills, which is more complex than asking teachers to implement a set of procedures with fidelity (Kennedy, 2019). This suggests that the field needs more fine-grained analyses of how teachers make sense of PD focused on developing their decision-making skills and how that sensemaking is reflected in their instructional practices. This paper aims to serve that purpose. In our context, elementary school teachers participated in PD around Cognitively Guided Instruction (CGI; Carpenter et al., 2015), which aims to increase teacher knowledge of research on children’s mathematical thinking to inform their instructional decision-making. In this study, we ask: How do teachers conceptualize the PD’s approach to teaching and learning? How are teachers’ conceptualizations connected to their instructional practice? Implications from our findings can contribute to refining theorizations of how teachers learn and inform future PD designs that move beyond core features. In this paper, participating teachers engaged in PD aimed at improving their knowledge-based reasoning and situation-specific decision-making skills. This type of PD recognizes that teachers must learn to enact particular practices contingent upon what their students understand and aims to develop teachers’ strategies and insights (Kennedy, 2019). In this study, we extend limited research that has investigated the link between teachers’ sensemaking of PD and their classroom decisions (Knapp & Peterson, 1995; Rimbey & Kucan, 2018; Warner et al., 2022), particularly around responsive mathematics teaching that builds on students’ emerging understandings.

## Theoretical framework

We draw on sensemaking theory (Weick et al., 2005), which recognizes sensemaking as an individual cognitive process and as socially situated. Teachers make sense of PD through an iterative, interpretive process of both talk and action; they begin to conceptualize PD learning within their existing understandings and practice, enact these new understandings in their teaching, which then leads tonew conceptualizations of PD learning. At the same time, their sensemaking is influenced by the broader context in which they work, including their interactions with colleagues (Spillane et al., 2018) and the guidance they receive from school leaders (Coburn, 2005). Recent research on teacher sensemaking has highlighted its culturally situated (Liang et al., 2025) and socially constructed (Daniel et al., 2023) nature, suggesting that teachers make sense of PD through mental frameworks shaped by not only their district infrastructure but also broader cultural narratives surrounding mathematics teaching and learning.

In this paper, participating teachers engaged in PD aimed at improving their knowledge-based reasoning and situation-specific decision-making skills. This type of PD recognizes that teachers must learn to enact particular practices contingent upon what their students understand and aims to develop teachers’ strategies and insights (Kennedy, 2019). In this study, we extend limited research that has investigated the link between teachers’ sensemaking of PD and their classroom decisions (Knapp & Peterson, 1995; Rimbey & Kucan, 2018; Warner et al., 2022), particularly around responsive mathematics teaching that builds on students’ emerging understandings.

## Context and participants

1207 This study takes place in the context of a research-practice partnership (RPP) between a small school district, county office of education, and university researchers on the West Coast of the United States. The RPP was established to improve mathematics instruction and student outcomes throughout the district's elementary schools. In service of that goal, the county office of education provided all teachers five days of pull-out PD on Cognitively Guided Instruction (CGI; Carpenter et al., 2015; Empson & Levi, 2011) each year for two years. Professional Development on Cognitively Guided Instruction (CGI PD) Rather than prescribing particular instructional practices, CGI PD aims to build teachers’ knowledge base of research on children’s mathematical thinking to inform their instructional decision-making (Carpenter & Franke, 2004; Jacobs et al., 2010). Teachers learn to make productive use of children’s thinking during classroom instruction by asking specific questions (Jacobs & Empson, 2016; Tyminski et al., 2021), leading whole class particular mathematical ideas tailored to students’ emerging understandings (Land et al., 2019; Empson et al., 2021). Teachers were separated into two groups based on grade band (K-2 or 3-5). During pull-out PD, teachers discussed readings, analyzed student work samples  and video clips, and participated in micro-teaching opportunities. To extend pull-out PD experiences, university researchers partnered closely with principals and teachers at Maple and Oak elementary schools (all names are pseudonyms) to co-design additional site-level learning opportunities. These site-level supports included monthly schoolwide meetings, grade-level planning meetings, and classroom co-teaching opportunities.

We investigate the experiences of seven teachers who participated in the district-wide pull-out CGI PD and a variety of additional site-level learning supports. The four Oak teachers are Tori (2nd grade), Joann  (3rd grade), Gwen (4th grade), and Mike (5th grade). The three Maple teachers are Lupe (1st grade), Diane (2nd grade), and Rachel (3rd grade). At the onset of the CGI PD initiative, all teachers had taught in the district for more than 25 years, except for Lupe, who was new to the district yet had nine years of prior teaching experience.

## Data and analytic method

Primary data include audio-recorded interviews conducted at the end of each year  of CGI PD (mean=46.6, range=40-58 minutes) and video observations of mathematics lessons conducted twice during the second year. Through semi-structured interviews, we sought to understand teachers’ perceptions of the PD initiative and district and school improvement efforts, their understanding of CGI, and how they incorporate it into their instruction. For video-recorded lessons, we asked teachers to teach a typical l esson around a topic that made sense for their students at that point in the year. transcripts of interviews and writing analytic memos to surface teachers’ perceptions of the CGI PD initiative. Drawing on this initial memo writing and  relevant literature (e.g., Carpenter et al., 20 15; Land et al., 2019), we developed a coding framework to capture teachers’ conceptualizations of CGI PD as an approach to teaching and learning, as well as how they integrate it into their instruction. The  research team collaboratively identified patterns in teachers’ conceptualizations at the end of each year of the PD initiative. We report sensemaking profiles that emerged at the end of year 1 and shifts we observed in their discourse in year 2. To answer the second research question, the first and second authors scored one lesson separately using a five-point rubric developed to measure classroom implementation along four principles of CGI: 1) problem solving plays an integral role in teaching; 2) teacher attends to details of students' mathematical thinking; 3) students engage with peers about mathematics; and 4) teachers supports students’ understanding and meaningful use of the language of mathematics (Schoen et al., 2024). The  first two authors  met to discuss scoring, reconcile differences, and establish a consensus on the meaning of the rubric indicators.  The first author scored the remaining lessons, checking with the research group as needed. We report our findings from this analysis by sensemaking profile.

Diane was the earliest to incorporate a CGI approach into her instruction, and did not draw from other curriculum resources during mathematics lessons. Tori also attempted to take a CGI approach each day, though in the first year, she still relied on prior curriculum material to structure her lessons and units. At the end of two years, both Diane and Tori shared how motivated they were by seeing their students’ growth and had specific goals they wanted to work on with their students and in their practice. In contrast, Profile 2 teachers positioned CGI as supplemental, rather than central, to their mathematics instruction in the first year. Gwen was positive about CGI as an approach from the beginning, but decided to incorporate it two days per week after teaching her core curriculum. By the end of two years, Gwen  shared, “I've learned so much that now I feel I cando more CGI than I used to.” Joann, Rachel, and Lupe all reported being initially skeptical of CGI, and like Gwen, were hesitant to incorporate CGI early on. At the end of two years, they each appeared to be less skeptical and reported incorporating CGI-aligned practices to varying degrees. Rachel still expressed the need for, at times, to “model as a teacher, like I do, we do, and then you do on your own.” Joann ended the two years in a fragile state. She expressed excitement about the growth she had seen in her students, had incorporated more of a CGI approach into her teaching, yet still had questions and wanted additional support. And we apply that manner of thinking to whatever the curriculum may be.” He saw CGI as building students’ understanding prior to the introduction of an algorithm, often by making use of diagrams to represent the quantitative relationships in a problem. Further, he described using students’ thinking as driving his instructional decisions. When planning, he shared, “whatever you do is gonna be dictated by student performance and understanding.” Additionally, in interviews, Mike  stressed the importance of students explaining their 1209 thinking, both to him and his peers, which he considered evidence of understanding. Paying attention to the details of students’ thinking to leverage toward a goal and an emphasis on explanations as evidence of understanding aligns with some aspects of CGI PD.

Our emerging findings suggest a strong connection between teachers’ conceptualizations of CGI PD and their implementation. Teachers’ conceptualizations of CGI as an approach to teaching and learning at the end of their first year were connected to the practices observed in their lessons during the second year, which potentially shaped their conceptualizations of CGI shared at the end of year 2. At the end of the second year, Profile 1 teachers were more specific when they talked about CGI. Profile 2 teachers were more positive about implementing CGI and were motivated by their students’ performance. Our findings underscore the iterative and ongoing nature of teacher learning during PD, where teacher sensemaking combines both naming and action (Weick et al., 2005). We theorize that this process is reciprocal, in which detailed descriptions of CGI shape and are shaped by teachers’ ability to enact CGI principles  in more sophisticated ways in their instruction. Further, consistent with prior research (Coburn, 2004), our findings suggest that the perceived dissonance between teachers’ existing practices and PD was related to their implementation. These findings contribute to understandings of teacher learning during PD efforts as nonlinear, iterative, and requiring integration into existing practices. These findings also suggest a variety of dimensions that might shape teacher sensemaking during PD efforts: the perception of PD as a departure from current practice, how teachers incorporate new PD learning into existing practice (e.g., core versus supplemental), the extent to which a teacher is willing to experiment or take a risk with new practices, and the challenge of the practice itself (e.g., connecting mathematical representations for conceptual understanding). In future analyses, we intend to capture variation in teachers’ sensemaking, surfaced in their discourse and practice, along these dimensions. Further, our findings suggest that teachers’ practices can be evidence of their sensemaking.  However, a limitation of this study is the use of video to capture classroom practice at two points during the year. Future work may use multiple in-person observations of teachers’ practice or video-stimulated interviews as evidence of their sensemaking over time. We also plan to capture nuances in their sensemaking around instructional improvement efforts, providing important insight into how teachers’ school and district contexts inform their sensemaking during PD efforts. 1210 Asterhan, C.
