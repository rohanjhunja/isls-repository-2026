# Exploring Computational Action: Summer Camp Influence on Computing Attitudes among Middle School Students

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Computational action, grounded in culturally responsive computing, emphasizes student reflection on their identities and communities through designing computing solutions for personally relevant social issues. Though some research exists, there is limited work on developing tools to assess its effective implementation. This mixed-methods study uses an existing validated pre/post survey of computing attitudes, observations, and interviews as a first step to effectively assessing computational action’s impact on attitudes. Quantitative results show no significant attitude changes, yet interviews and observations reveal strong interest in computing and students’ sense of computational empowerment. Convergent findings also indicate participants’ enjoyment of project-based learning and a sense of community. Results highlight the value of mixed methods approaches to capture data in complex contexts and the need for tailored instruments and methods to measure computational action’s impact. Findings also suggest computational action's potential to foster positive attitudes and engagement in computing.

## Introduction

Limited access to high-quality computer science  (CS) education and exclusionary stereotypes contribute t o the underrepresentation of Black/African American stude nts in the  CS field (Horn et al., 2020; Lachney, 2017). Strategies to address this issue include out-of-school programs (Means & Stephens, 2021) and culturally centered learning experiences (Champion et al., 2020). The Culturally Responsive Computing (CRC) framework integrates Culturally Responsive Teaching (Scott et al., 2015) in computing education to validate students' identities, empower them through technology, and foster meaning ful relationships.  As a practical avenue for CRC, computational action (Tissenbaum et al., 2019) emphasizes students’ identity and empowerment development in computing by shifting the focus from traditional computational thinking to designing technology-based solutions for societal issues. However, research on the impact and evaluation methods around computational action remain limited.

This study examines a two-week computational action summer camp for Black/African American youth, where participants used technology to design community-focused prototypes. Due to the lack of validated instruments for assessing computational action, the  study employs existing computer science attitude m easures and a mixed methods approach to evaluate shifts in computing attitudes. It also suggests future paths for improved evaluation methods. Research questions include: 1) How can computing attitude shifts be effectively measured in a computational action camp? 2) How does computatio nal action foster belonging and collaboration in underrepresented students?

## Literature review

Culturally responsive computing Diverse racial, ethnic and gender groups are contin uously underrepresented in the field of computer science. African Americans, for instance, represent only the 3.5% of CS undergraduates despite making up 13.2% of the U.S. population (Lachney, 2017). Barriers such as lack of access to quality CS education experiences (Madkins et al., 2020) and biasing stereotypes contribute to this disparity (Means & Stephens, 2021; Lyon & Green, 2021). Consequently, early exposure is crucial for fostering positive attitudes and interest in CS (Song et al., 2023). Outof-school programs, like CS camps, can play an important role in engaging underrepresented students (Means & Stephens, 2021)  by offering interactive experiences that increase interest  among younger learners (Lira et al., 2022).

Integrating cultural elements into CS education is essential for creating meaningful learning experiences among underrepresented groups. Scott et al.  (2015) presented the  Culturally Responsive Computing (CRC) approach, which integrates Culturally Responsive Teaching (CRT) in technology education, allowing students’ identity exploration and validation, and foster ing empowerment and innovation within the field  by creating relevant and contextualized learning experiences. Therefore, CRC goes beyond traditional approaches such as providing access to computing to underrepresented communities but proposes a more nuanced approach, taking into account intersectionality and social structures within the learning experiences. In addition to CRC, research has highlighted other effective strategies to engage learners in computing education. These include  project-based learning (Lira et al., 2020), mentoring (Clar ke-Midura & Pantic, 2020), creativity through multimedia (Mesiti et al., 2019), and project s showcasing through digital portfolios and community fairs to develop computing identity and engagement (Authors, 2021). Computational action: an opportunity for CRC Computational action  adapts computer science learning to focus on students’ identities, needs, and contexts (Tissenbaum et al., 2019). It empowers students to set their learning goals, create personal meaningful prototypes, and engage in community-focused projects. This fosters identity development, which is especially relevant for underrepresented groups. This approach encourages s tudents to: 1) Develop technological solutions relevant to their communities,  2) E xplore computing’s social impact, 3) Lead their own learning, and  4) D evelop their computational identity and discover their potential in computing. Despite its promise, research on computational action remains limited. Pang et al. (2023a) studied a oneday App Inventor intervention but focused only on A I perceptions. A follow-up study (Pang et al., 2023b) examined a short intervention’s effects on com putational identity and self-efficacy, finding significant gains but lacking triangulation for validity. Another study (Bawankule & Tissenbaum, 2023)  explored maker identity in a computational action camp, highlighting benefits of  project-based learning, mentorship, and collaboration but relying solely on qualitative data.

This study addresses these gaps by applying previously validated computing attitude surveys and a mixed-methods approach to triangulate quantitative and qu alitative findings, providing a more comprehensive assessment of computational action in practice.

## Context

This study took place at a computational action summer camp for Black/African American youth in central Illinois in July 2024. The camp incorporated four key components of the computational action framework: computational identity, digital empowerment, computational design  thinking, and creative self-expression. Over nine days, participants learned about computing tools and develop ed prototypes of solutions to social causes for their communities. The camp staff included mentors and instructors fro m diverse backgrounds, i ncluding African American men and women, and local community partners that were also part of  camp sessions, enhancing the culturally responsive approach of the program. ## Participants

Two local community groups supporting African American youth invited their members to join the camp and assisted with logistics. Of 36 campers, 32 had pare ntal consent and are referred to as participants. 7 1.4% selfidentified as female and 28.8% as male. Most of them aged 11, 12 and 13 (26.1% each), followed by 14-year-olds (13%). All identified as Black/African American, wi th three also identifying as Hispanic, one as Asian, and one solely as White.

## Methods

A concurrent mixed methods design (QUANT + QUAL) was employed to ensure triangulation, given the potential reliability concerns with young participants' survey and interview responses. This approach enhances the accuracy and depth of findings, providing valuable insights into capturing attitude changes effectively.

## Data collection

Pre-and post-surveys, administered via Qualtrics, measured chang es in computing attitudes. Due to the lack of validated instruments for computational action, constructs from existing surveys were adapted, including creative expression (Morales-Navarro et al., 2023), interest and belonging (Haynie & Packman, 2019), and empowerment and impact (Kong et al., 2018). Questions used the word “computing” instead of “computer science” to avoid associations with “coding”  (Tissenbaum et al., 2021). Sample items inclu ded: “I feel I belong in computing spaces” and “I can be creative with computing,”  and were rated on a 5-point Likert scale. The post-survey also collected demographic data, favorite camp activities, and feedback about the sense of belonging in the camp. Qualitative data was gathered through observations and semi-structured interviews to enhance validity. Two researchers recorded daily field notes on parti cipant interactions and project progress. Interview s occurred on Day 4 (six participants, discussing mind maps and relationships with technology) and the final two days (seventeen participants, reflecting on camp experiences, projects, and interest in computing).

## Data analysis

For the quantitative analysis, data was cleaned involving the  identification and exclusion of  responses with insufficient engagement based on survey administrat ion observations (e.g. students answering without reading) and Qualtrics response times. The final sample incl uded 23 participants with post-surveys and 21 with both pre-and post-surveys. Descriptive statistics (mean, median, SD, skewness, and kurtosis) were calculated, and normality was assessed via skewness, k urtosis, and the Shapiro-Wilk test. A paired samples t-test was employed to compare pre-and post-survey means for computing attitudes. For the qualitative analysis, data was  organized in a spreadsheet by participant and camp day, then uploaded to Dedoose, a qualitative analysis software, for thematic analysis using an inductive coding app roach (Strauss & Corbin, 1990). Initial codes were refined into broader categories, with separate coding for observations and interviews to distinguish between  students’ observed  behaviors and verbal reports. Using code frequency facilitated trend identification, while iterative analysis by the research team enhanced validity.

## Findings

RQ1: What is the impact of a summer camp using the computational action approach on participants’ attitudes toward computing?

The paired samples t-test revealed no significant differences in pre-and post-test scores across the four constructs: computing creative expression, t(21) = -0.92, p =.367; computing interest, t(21) = 0.31, p =.756; sense of belonging, t(21) = -0.68, p =.501; and computing empowerment, t(21) = -1.23, p =.230. Despite the lack of significant attitude changes in the survey, qualitative findings indicated enthusiasm for computing. In interviews, 11 of 17 participants expressed interest in engaging in future computing camps, while three preferred other pursuits, such as sports.

Based on the survey, many participants reported enj oying the camp, particularly project creation, as reflected in their rankings of preferred activities. In order of preference, their favorite activity was building their projects, followed by learning Micro:bits, learning electronics, learning App Inventor, building their mind maps. The lowest ranked was the afrofuturisim session. Even though building their projects was among the most favored activities, students reported diverse preferences for specifi c tools (e.g. some preferred Micro:bit and did not like electronics). Those survey fundings align with interviews, with 13 out of 17 participants verbally express ing positive feelings about creating their projects. They reported feeling proud of their work and enjoying the creative process.

Participants' projects emerged from reflections on personal and community challeng es, covering topics such as poverty, education, and environmental issue s. Examples included a robot providing free food, a n app for football coaches, and a device for alerting parents to potential child abductions. One participant, inspired by family experiences, created an app to educate people on le gal rights: “This is mostly for like, people who can't afford law... Because my dad, he had to fight in court multiple times for custody”. While participants found project-building engaging, observations and interviews revealed that 12 of them encountered difficulties with the tools. Some struggled to implement their ideas due to a lack of advanced training. For instance, one participant abandoned his app development after failing to find relevant tutorials. Despite these challenges, all participants completed and presente d their projects at the final showcase, demonstrati ng engagement and persistence.

RQ2: How can computational action foster a sense of belonging and collaboration in camp participants from underrepresented communities?

In the post-survey, participants rated their agreement with the  following statements on a Likert scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree): “I felt comfortable in this camp,” “I felt I belonged in this camp,” and “I felt accepted by my peers in this camp.” These ques tions aimed to assess the extent to which the camp created a comfortable and welcoming environment for participants. The tot al scores for the three questions ranged from a minimum of 3 to a maximum of 15, categorized into four levels: low (3–6), medium low (7–9), medium high (10– 12), and high (13–15). Most participants reported a medium high (47.8%) or high (39.1%) level of comfort, while only a few reported low (8.7%) or medium low (4.3%)  levels. These results indicate an overall high lev el of satisfaction with this aspect of the camp.

Observations showed  high levels of collaboration among participants, wi th at least 15 engaging in teamwork or assisting peers. This was evident in th eir project work, where participants helped each other overcome technical challenges. For instance, one  participant experienced in computing  worked to set up a breadboard circuit to later explain it to the group, who waited for her explanation. The camp’s room layout, with tables arranged for face-to-face interaction, facilitated this collaborative environment. Even when findings of RQ1 indicated that the afrofuturism session was the least favorite, qualitative data indicates that some of the culturally responsive characteristics of the camp were key for their level of comfort in the camp. During the afrofuturism s ession, s tudents explored  art, books, and music they recognized, which deepened their involvement. For example, when a slide displayed song lyrics, a participant s pontaneously sang along. Another interviewee shared that the instruct or's relatable topics helped her feel more comforta ble in the camp, stating "I just felt more comfortable... I was sitting back quiet, but the things you said, I was so highly related to that too."  These experiences, along with open reflections, created a s ense of connection and comfort that contributed to their overall engagement in the camp. The Afrofuturism section was likely rated lower bec ause it was the only lecture-based session, making it less engaging than the hands-on activities. However, despite challenges in proje ct development, collaborating with peers and being in an environment that affirme d their identities may have contributed to the high  reported sense of belonging in the camp.

## Discussion

The results for the first research question reveale d a divergence between quantitative and qualitative  findings. Although no significant changes were found in the p re-and post-test scores for computing interest, creativity, sense of belonging, and empowerment, qualitative data indicated that participants were eager to engage in future computing activities, took pride in their projects, and reflected on societal issues through technology. This divergence highlights the value of a concurren t mixed-methods approach for triangulation. The challenges faced with quantitative data collection—such as participant lack of engagement in filling out the survey and missed responses—reduced the sample size and may have influenced their responses. Observations revealed that the camp's final project fair sparked pride and satisfaction in students, which the post-survey did not capture, as it was administered in the morning before the fa ir, during a stressful moment that may have also influenced their responses. Furthermore, concerns about survey adaptability and the term "computing" raised questions about the instrument’s validity for measuring this specif ic intervention. Interviews with participants about  their understanding of "computing" and revising survey administration methods (e.g., retrospective pre-and post-tests) will be key to addressing these concerns in future research. Other findings —such as tool preferences, frustration, and challeng e— may have influenced the development of computing attitudes as measured in the survey, i ncluding the development of diverse attitudes depending on their preferred tool. This underlines the need to develop a new instrument that better aligns with the camp's focus on computational action and is adapted to participants' age and experiences. Despite these challenges, the positive outcomes of the camp—such as enjoyment, project pride, cultural reflection, and proposed technological solutions to  social issues —support the effectiveness of Culturally Responsive Computing (Scott et al., 2015). These findings align with existing literature on project-based learning (Lira et al., 2020), mentoring (Clarke-Midura & Pantic, 2020), and the integration of crea tivity and multimedia (Mesiti et al., 2019). This study further emphasize s how computationa l action can foster early exposure to computing while promoting culturally relevant environments to broaden participation in the field.
