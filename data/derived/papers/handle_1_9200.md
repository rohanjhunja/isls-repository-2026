# Collaborative Sketching in Virtual STEM Design Activities

**Conference:** ISLS 2023

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Sketching is a crucial skill of STEM and Design. Researchers argue for involving children in the design process; however, there is a lack of understanding of how children use sketching while collaborating in virtual STEM designing activities. This study examines children sketching during their participation in a virtual STEM Design Club using the ethnographic case study approach. Findings indicate multiple ways in which children collaborate during virtual sketching experiences: 1) Annotations in the form of labels, ideas, critique; and 2) Developing shared meaning of visual representations during collaborative sketches. Results imply that children’s collaboration during sketching have implications for building, improving, and sustaining virtual cycles of design with children provided with appropriate scaffolds as they participate in design-based sketching activities.

## Introduction

Sketching plays a crucial role in the field of Science, Technology, Engineer ing, and Mathematics (STEM) education (Ainsworth & Scheiter, 2021; Wu & Rau, 2019). It can improve the quality a nd novelty of ideas in design process, and it is an effective tool for engaging children in learning STEM (Wu & Rau, 2019). Prior studies examined technology’s role in supporting collaborative interactions (Toprani  & Borge, 2021) and virtual participatory design activities (Parekh et al., 2022). However, there is a la ck of understanding of how children develop their collaborative sketching skills when participating in informal design activities (Shokeen et al., 2022). To the best of our knowledge, we have found little to no empirical research that examines children’s collaboration during a virtual STEM design sketching context. For this reason, we focused on asking: How do children collaborate while sketching during virtual STEM design activities? Findings suggest that children use annotations to label their sketches, expand design ideas, and critique through questions and peer feedback. This study contributes to improving and sustaining virtual cycles of design with children.

## Theoretical framework

Drawing to learn is an active, constructive, and interactive form of engagem ent (Ainsworth & Scheiter, 2021). Researchers advocate for incorporating drawing activities throughout STEM cur ricula as it can help direct children’s attention to useful details t hrough which they develop a better understanding of scientific concepts (Schmidgall et al., 2020). For instance, when children are asked to draw wha t they see, then they pay closer attention to what they are viewing which enhances their observational skills whi ch are important in scientific practices (Quillin & Thomas, 2015). Wu and Rau (2019) argue that drawing activiti es are more effective if students use drawings to build knowledge by integrating their prior knowledge with externally presented information. Additionally, Fiorella and Kuhlmann (2020) found that encouraging students to  coordinate verbal explanations with drawings that they create can enhance their learning. Freehand sketching is critical to design practices – to explore, explain, persuade, and analyze ideas (Lewis & Sturdee, 2020). It serves multiple purposes such as a short-term memory aid, an important tool to facilitate group communication, and documentation of ideas and its implications are getting examined in collaborative design work to externa lize knowledge and viewpoints (Quan & Gu, 2018). Recently, Sung et al. (2019) suggest teaching freehand sketching techniques, particularly for younger grade students to reduce their cognitive load of drawing 3D representations.

## Method

The ethnographic case study approach (Merriam, 1998) was used to focus on how children behaved in a cultural setting of a virtual STEM design environment – STEM Design Club. It allowed us to consider the particularity and complexity of sketching and its affordances within different design activiti es. The Club sessions were organized using a Cooperative Inquiry approach (Druin, 1999). Additionally, we followed a radical constructivist perspective (von Glasersfeld, 1995) which promotes the idea that the only way of learning is by doing.

## Overview of the STEM design club

The primary aim of the STEM Design Club was to engage children in design activities. It was conducted virtually via Zoom (https://zoom.us) and Jamboard (https://jamboard.com) in partnership with a public library.  The library CSCL 2023 Proceedings  © ISLS 230 staff advertised by posting the project on their official website and interested patrons re gistered online. Once the consent and assent forms were collected, the librarian shared the participants’ contact details with the researchers. The Club consisted of eight members including 5 children aged 9 through 11 – three girls (Anna, Bella, Nora) and two boys (Aaron, Ryan) – two researchers and one librarian. The Club met for eleven 90-minute sess ions conducted weekly, and each session involved both individual sketching and collective s ketching. Although the Club was conducted virtually, the children were given the freedom to draw sketches either by pen on paper or on Jamboard, and some paper-based sketches were subsequently photographed and added to Jamboard.

## Data collection & analysis

Data was collected in the form of Zoom video recordings of all the sessio ns, researchers’ observation notes, and the artifacts developed by children during the sessions. Jamboard was use d during each session for conducting brainstorming and ideation activities, which supported data collection of the res ulting artifacts. The Jamboard were shared with the Club members, so everyone had access to make edits during the session. After each session, the Jamboard pages were converted into PDFs. All session video data were transcribed and Jamboard PDFs were added into the transcripts to combine data modalities. The transcripts were then imported into the qualitative analysis software MAXQDA with their respective video recording for coding purposes which allowed referencing the video in case of a lack of clarity. The data was coded solely by the first author using emergent open, focused, and axial coding (Charmaz, 2006), with weekly discussions with the research team. These discussions involved references to the original data to find the suitability of themes to the data. These study team reviews and discussions during the analysis process increased the reliability and transferability of results.

## Findings

Annotations In children’s sketches, annotations were not found originally, but they easily adapted to it when introduced to the relevance of annotations for communicative value of sketches in design. For exam ple: in session 1, the children were asked to sketch their favorite technology-based product. In their initial dra wings, they did not include any annotations until a discussion among the team about their usefulness in increas ing the communicative value of sketches. The evolution of children’s sketches from unlabele d to labeled ones occurred quickly and smoothly, within the first session and children were found continuing annotation in sketche s in later sessions. We further divide this theme into subthemes: labels, extending ideas, and critique. Over the course of the design sessions the children were found using annotations in multiple ways such as giving labels for description, expanding on ideas, and critiquing by asking questions and giving feedback. Labels Labels were used by the children to describe the components of their sketche s, and the use of labels seems to make sketching ideas easier for children. For instance, during session 3, Anna’ s presented her solution to deal with shyness by drawing a Stress B all with the help of labels such as ‘Stress Ball’ and ‘You Can’. The us e of labels in her sketch helped her to communicate her ideas efficiently, as dra wing a stress ball that can be easily distinguished from other kinds of balls is hard without the help of labels. It would have been difficult for the team to figure out what she had drawn if there were no labels on her sketch. Thus, the use of labels helped the children to represent and communicate their ideas with each other about the ideas depicted in the sketch. Expanding ideas Sketches provided a situated artifact for children to expand on each other’s ideas. Children engaged in expanding ideas using the sticky notes feature in Jamboard which allowed everyone to annotate the sketch simultaneously. After adding their annotations to the sketch, they verbally engaged in discussing and e xpanding on their annotations. They explained how their annotations expand the original idea depicted in the  sketch. This helped the group to connect multiple ideas and expand on the original idea from diffe rent perspectives. Once everyone added their sticky notes to the sketch, they each explained their ideas verbally, often offering new ideas as well as connecting multiple ideas. For example, during the discussion of Nora’s ske tches on solving the pr oblem of shy children, Bella added the idea of an ‘ice-breaker game’ and Anna added ‘shared habits’. Later, Bella suggested that shared habits can be combined within icebreaker games. Anna expanded this idea by suggesting that in some ice-breaking games, people asked about common stuff to “get to know each other” and find people with s imilar interests or similar favorite dishes to grouped together. Sketch critiquing CSCL 2023 Proceedings  © ISLS 231 Children were found to be frequently using annotations to critique each other’ s ideas depicted in sketches which involved asking questions and giving feedback to each other on their sketches. For exam ple, Anna was found adding a critique to a sketch of voice-controlled smart curtains collaboratively drawn by Ryan and Aaron. In her critique, she added a question “will it tranzlate?” and a suggestion “Maybe you could make it say d name.” Despite the spelling errors, she was able convey her question and suggestion. Anna’s annotations helped Aaron and Ryan to think about things that they didn’t consider while sketching their ideas, and which they then took up. Developing shared meanings Children engaged in developing a shared meaning of visual representations in ske tching while collaboratively engaged with Jamboard. Collaborative sketching refers to when two or more children members work together on a sketch. Children were found to be asking questions of each other while engaged in collaborative sketching; these questions encouraged children to think deeper about their sketches. They also engaged in brainstorming before beginning sketching to evaluate different ideas and discuss how to represent those ideas. These discussions helped the group to resolve their conflicts, differences, and come to a consensus after exploring different options. For example, in session 7, children were engaged in improving the design of existing tools using technology. During that session, Ryan and Aaron together drew the sketch of voice-controlled smart curtains. Their main idea was to have curtains that can be opened and closed with a voice command. To begin their collaborative sketch, Aaron and Ryan brainstormed different ideas for over 15 minutes in order to develo p a shared meaning of the ideas depicted in their sketch. They deliberated about different curtain styles before selecting one that opens from the center outward. They both started sketching curtains separately on the same Ja mboard, and their initial designs were still different – for example, they each drew different connections from the curtains to th e curtain rod. To reach a shared consensus on the connection style, Aaron downloaded an image from an Internet search and added it to their Jamboard to show how curtain loops look in the images, and they revised according to the images. Then they decided to show two drawings: one in which the curtains are drawn, and one in which the curtains are closed. To make their sketch more realistic, they decided to draw a window in the sketch where the curtains are drawn. Then they added annotations to their sketch and presented it to the group. This exa mple illustrates how collaborative sketching among children encourages them to think through different s cenarios to develop shared meanings of visual representations in sketches based on consensus during discussion.

## Discussion

We found that children rely on the use of annotations to communicate their des ign ideas and that during collaborative sketching they engage in developing shared meanings of visual representation of their ideas. These abilities are important for developing children’s collaboration, communication, and design skills. Our findings signify that examination of children’s sketching experiences is useful to both under stand and develop their collaborative design skills. These empirical findings extend prior researc h on children’s participation in virtual design environments by exploring how children engage in virtual collaborative sketching. Children engaged in discussions on their individual and collaborative sketches which he lped them to develop their design ideas collaboratively, extending research on drawing in ST EM (e.g., Quillin & Thomas, 2015; Schmidgall et al., 2020) to drawing in design. In particular, the presentation of and discussion on sketches in the virtual environment was both feasible and effective, and the sticky  note feature helped capture feedback and expand ideas. Although, sticky notes are useful in in-person design sessions, we found its effectiveness among children in online design environment as well. The use of sticky notes on collaborative discussion on sketches can be adapted in both in-person and virtual design session with children. Importantly, we found that children are capable of sharing their design ideas via sketches in virtual environments. Using sketches supported their communication, collaboration, and discussion of various design ideas. When collaboratively  sketching, children to engage in crucial communication and collaboration skills through weighing the pros and cons of different visual representations while sketching. This aligns with Fiorella and Kuhlmann’s suggestion to improve learning through coordinating verbal explanations with drawings. This implies that children should be enc ouraged to engage in collaborative sketches as it can improve their design skill as well as collaboration skills. Moreover, findings indicate the annotating their sketches helped children to repr esent ideas that are difficult to convey solely via visuall representations. The freedom to use text  while sketching demonstrated various uses to distinguish and specify different components of sketches, to expand on ideas depicted in sketches, and to ask question and give feedback to other which encouraged them children to think deeper on their sketches and consequently on their ideas. This implies that children should be encouraged to annotate on their sketches to increase the communicative value of their sketches during collaborative activities. These findings can be leveraged by future researchers and practitioners who aim to involve children in design or sketching. This exploratory study focused on children’s collaboration in design sketching over the duration of their CSCL 2023 Proceedings  © ISLS 232 participation in a STEM Design Club. These findings are limited in multiple ways such as giving participants the choice to use the mode of sketching (digital or on-paper) in which they feel most comfortable, a small number of participants, and the virtual nature of the Club. We did not examine the dif ferences in individual sketches and collaborative sketches, or differences in the sketches on digital platforms (such as Jamboard) or pencil sketches on paper; instead, our focus was limited to understanding ch ildren’s virtual collaboration. We call for future research to expand on children’s experiences in collaborative design sketching within different modalities.

## Conclusion

Our participants developed design, collaboration, and communication skills when they engaged in sketching during their virtual design processes. Our work expands research on the importanc e and usefulness of freehand sketching in design (e.g., Lewis & Sturdee, 2020; Quan & Gu, 2018) by offering specific aspects of sketching to support and encourage, particularly in virtual environments. In short, we should encoura ge children to sketch, annotate, and discuss their ideas as a way to engage in multiple learning opportunities such as labelling, expanding ideas, critiquing, and developing shared meaning of virtual representations. Providing technological support and scaffolding to engage in virtual individual and collaborative sketching experiences c an help in building, improving, and sustaining virtual cycles of design with children.
