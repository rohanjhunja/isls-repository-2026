# Elementary Teacher Candidates’ Lines of Flight Toward Humanizing Science Assessments

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study explores the navigation of elementary science teacher candidates (TCs) as they seek to humanize their assessments in an educational system that centers neoliberal testing practices. This paper is the first step in addressing the need for more research on supporting TCs who desire to implement humanizing pedagogy in their assessments. With rhizomatic analysis and the use of Deleuze and Guattari’s concepts of territorialization, deterritorialization, and reterritorialization, we examine the lines of flight the TCs took in their science methods course (Dimitriadis & Kamberelis, 2006; St. Pierre, 2019). Findings show deterritorialization moves with the ways that TCs approached humanizing pedagogy in their talk and implementation of assessments. However, the findings demonstrate territorialization and reterritorialization moves as TCs planned these assessments in their 5E lessons. We discuss implications for promoting humanizing pedagogy in science assessments and supporting TCs as they navigate tensions.

## Introduction

Humanizing pedagogy is a way of teaching that seeks  to center equity in the classroom by expressing students’ consciousness through their knowledge, experiences,  and perspectives (Bartolomé, 1994; Freire, 1970; S alazar, 2013). Humanizing pedagogy is an approach in the learning sciences that  has been identified as a promising method to prepare teacher candidates (TCs) for the diverse classrooms of today (Andrews et al., 2019). Practices such as standardized curricula and testing dehumanize our educational system, making it a challenge for educators attempting to implement humanizing pedagogy (Taylor, 2019). Little is known about how best to support TCs as we prepare them to enter this dehumanizing system as they attempt to  humanize their classrooms. To arrive at tangible practices to support TCs, we must first understand what tensions TCs  experience as they seek to implement humanizing pedagogy and how TCs navigate these tensions. This study focuses on elementary science TCs enroll ed in a science methods course that centered equitable science teaching and learning. We utilize artifacts from the science methods course a nd implement rhizomatic analysis (Jeong and Steele, 2023)  to aid in the exploration of the TCs as they naviga te humanizing pedagogy in a dehumanizing system. Jeong and Steele (2023) proposed looking at “how preservice teachers take lines of flight (micro or macro lines) more careful ly to possibl y resist, adjust, and negotiate their response to institutional and bureaucratic structures and curri cular norms” (p.474). The current study builds on t his idea by exploring how teacher candidates negotiate their co nceptions of humanizing pedagogy  in assessment. The Deleuzian concepts of desire, lines of flight/deter ritorialization, territorialization, and reterritor ialization were relevant as we do not consider the TCs to be in a fixed state but rather engaged in the ongoing and dynamic process of becoming humanizing educators (Andrews & Castillo, 2016). Theoretical framing This study draws on Deleuzian philosophy to explore how the elementary science TC s’ desire to be humanizing teachers helped them to navigate tensions in their journey towards humanizing science education. From a Deleuzian perspective, desire is a positive product ive force that helps to shape and transform human b eings (Dimitriadis & Kamberelis, 2006; Tuck, 2010). For m any philosophers, if we desire something it is beca use we are lacking it. Desire is a negative force. On the contrary, Deleuze and Guattari resisted the gap or hole conception of desire and rather than lacking, focused more on how desires help to shape us (Tuck, 2010). From thi s perspective not only is desire a positive productive force, but it causes change. As humans, desire helps us grow, improve ourselves, recreate ourselves, and become new and better versions of ourselves. Humanity also has a collective desire to shape, change, and organize th e world (Fouweather and Bosma, 2021). In this study, TCs sought to better themselves as science teachers and  to improve science learning for their students  by allowing their desires to shape them as they journey towards humanizing education. Deleuze and Guat tari defined territorializations as controlling and limiting spaces (Fouweather and Bosma, 2021). Territorializations are created and m aintained through the repetition of performative acts, behaviors, refrains, or routines (Fouweather and Bo sma, 2021). This creates systems in our society that are stratified, and social hierarchies are formed, wher e some are included and some are excluded in the sp ace (Fouweather and Bosma, 2021). In the process of recreation, in changing ourselves and the world, desires produce lines of flight, also known as movements of deterritorialization (Deleuze, 1997). In terms of desire, lines of flight are “a space where desire is liberated from the con straints of all overdetermined and overdetermining system: psychoanalysis, Marxism, capitalism, and so on” (Di mitriadis & Kamberelis, 2006, p.96). This is especi ally relevant for this study as we look at neoliberal practices such as standardized curricula and testing tha t were put into place by our educational system and  how TCs navigate those as they try to center equity  and humanization. In contrast to lines of flight and deterritorializa tion, “To (re)territorialize is to try to recontain  and to replace boundaries around desire, to repress it” (Dimitriad is & Kamberelis, 2006, p.96). Reterritorialization would be places where TCs adhere to the neoliberal structures put into place perhaps acting contrary to their desires. To see these concepts exemplified in educational settings, see Strom and Martin’s (2013) self-study of their classroom where they frame their acts of resistance toneoliberal educational practices using the concepts  of lines of flight and deterritorialization. For instance, a teacher balancing power dynamics in the classroom is framed as a movement of deterritorialization. This framing is beneficial as we examine the small moves TCs made and how that drove their transformation towards humanizing education. In resistance to the more linear, hierarchical West ern epistemologies, Deleuze and Guattari’s rhizomes are “nonlinear, anarchic, and nomadic” networks that “build links between preexisting gaps and between n odes that are separated by categories and orders of segmented thinking, acting, and being” (Dimitriadis & Kamberelis, 2006, p.89). The rhizome particularly grounded our analysis (Dimitriadis & Kamberelis, 2006; St. Pierre, 2019). Focusing on testing, we consider how TCs planned th eir assessments, which was a small move they made t o act on their desires, push  against the boundaries, and create lines of flight.  We consider assessment to be a traditionally controlling and limiting space, and t hus a territorialization (Fouweather and Bosma, 202 1; Taylor, 2019). All students are given the same assessment, that does not necessarily draw on their culture/backgrounds/experiences, and they are evaluated the same way.

## Literature Review

Humanizing pedagogy in teacher education In humanizing pedagogy, educators assume that stude nts have the capabilities to be active participants  when it comes to their learning (Bartolomé, 1994).  Salazar (2013) lists ten principles and practices t hat can be used to operationalize humanizing pedagogy for educators. A ndrews et al. (2019) highlight the benefits of critical consciousness, Salazar’s (2013) second principle and practice of humanizing pedagogy. Part of critical consciousness is engaging in sustained critical self-reflection and resisting binaries.  Sustained critical self-reflection helps TCs to produce lines of flight, as they liberate themselves from deficit-based perspectives as well as helps raise their awareness of  the larger system with which they are a part which privileges certain identities, practices, and dispositions. Resisting binaries “helps us expand our frames of reference to include knowledge and ways of knowing and being that are often excluded o r positioned on the periphery of mainstream sociopo litical dialogue” (Andrews et al., 2019, p.16).  This produces lines of flight as it disrupts hierarchies and includes those who would normally be excluded in a particular space (Fouweather and Bosma, 2021). In the process of creating assessments, we consider how TCs engage in critical consciousness in their assessment practices which would result in deterritorialization, and the limitations, which would be territorialization/reterritorialization. Much of the work on humanizing pedagogy remains theoretical  but some studies illuminate how humanizing pedagogy is enacted in practice (see Czop et al., 2019; Qin & Beauchemin, 2022; Tigert, 2022).  This body of work emphasi zes how the tenets of humanizing pedagogy are especiall y beneficial for bilingual/multilingual K-12 students given the balancing of power dynamics b etween teachers and students, valuing students’ backgrounds and experiences, making the curriculum relevant to students ’ lives, and encouraging critical reflection. They are examples of deterritorialization given the pushback on the structures set up by the educational system that typically results in students being bystanders  in their own learning  rather than active participants. Apart from critical consciousness, this aligns with the first principle and practice which notes that humanizing teachers bring in students’ in and out of school lives including sociohistorical, sociocultural, and sociopolitical aspects (Salazar, 2013).  Moreover, the third principle and practice emphasizes how  educators should build on these sociocultural resources. When TCs engage in these two principles and practices, particularly in terms of assessment, movements of deterritorialization  are made given how the “status quo” is to st andardize assessments which does not allow students to bring themselves into the classroom. The above work demonstrates the potential benefits of humanizing pedagogy in teache r and science education. We now turn to tensions th at have been discovered when educators try to implemen t humanizing pedagogy in a US context, which relies heavily on neoliberal educational practices.

Conflicts between humanizing pedagogy and assessment Schissel (2024) proposes an approach for humanizing assessment that  is decolonizing and communing. Decolonizing “means working with flexible, adaptable methods rather than looking toward standardized processes and procedures (p.12). Examples they provide are reimagining portfolios or self-assessments and using assessments that are unrelated to large-scale, standardized assessments. Communing involves being flexible and responsive to the needs of all  parties involved in the creation of the assessment.  Those involved must also be aware of the power dynamics between those involved in the creation of the asses sment and society as a whole. They recommend moving away from lenses that prioritize the goals and standards of a profession to one that looks at the lived realities of people. Schissel (2024) notes that this approach will be challenging given that assessment approaches currently reflect coloniality and modernity. For example, the results are used to rank people into categories and subcategories. This i s an example of territorialization given how it places students in boxes. Prioritizing an approach that relies on flexibility and adaptable methods, community, and incorporating people’s lives is an example of deterritorialization and it is important to study how this approach to humanizing assessment could look in action. It is also important to look at how in action one can be challenged by traditional notions of assessment as this article shows.

Taylor (2019) further illustrates tensions that can arise specifically looking at urban elementary teachers with humanizing pedagogical orientations in an ethnographic study. One of the tensions the teachers experienced was trying to tailor curriculum and instruction  to the students’ needs but being required to standardize by using provided materials (i.e. rigid curriculum, district planning guides, and standardized tests). The teachers navigated this by being flexible in their interactions with students. They found that it was beneficial for teachers and students to co-construct humanizing pedagogical spaces when faced with dehumanizing discourses.  Co-constructing humanizing pedagogical spaces  are movements of det erritorialization. These tensions could be further explored with TCs who are still making sense of what humaniz ing assessment looks like for them  and what deterritorialization moves they are able to make.

Lastly, Jeong and Steele (2023)  through rhizomatic analysis followed elementary TCs’ lines of flight with their pedagogical decisions, ideas, and positionalities. They illuminate how becoming an equitable teacher looks different for each and how tensions can be internal as TCs wrestle with themselves on what to do differently in future teaching.  Focusing specifically on assessment given the studies that s how that this can be an area of conflict with humanizing pedagogy, the following research question guided our inquiry:  What lines of flight do elementary science TCs take in assessing their students as they work tonegotiate what humanizing pedagogy can become?

## Context

Data from this study was collected as part of  a larger project on equitable science sense-making, which involved the design of an elementary science methods course focused on equitable science learning. The course w as designed to support TCs as they surface and challen ge deficit ideologies in science teaching and learn ing, taking on a more transformative perspective (Pierson et al., 2024). For the course design, a significant part of the transformative perspective was helping the TCs recognize the multiple and unique ways that students make sense of science. Additionally, the resources that students bring into the classroom are valued and encourage TCs to use these for sensemaking in science (Pierson et  al., 2024). This perspective to teaching and learni ng aligns with multiple principles and practices of humanizing ped agogy for instance, “students’ sociocultural resour ces are valued and extended” and “students will achieve thr ough their academic, intelle ctual, and social abilities” (Salazar, 2013).

Drawing from NASEM’s (2021) report on the four appr oaches to equity in science education (access, identity, agency, and justice), after the first iteration of the course Pierson et al. (2024) examined the elementary TCs’ equitable noticing and curriculum design. Pier son and colleagues found that after completing thei r science methods course, TCs predominantly approached equity  in line with the findings of the NASEM (2021) repo rt which says that access and identity is prioritized over agency and justice in science education. The TCs focused more on access as opposed to justice. When TCs did approach equity in terms of identity and agency, they used normative and dominant perspectives. These findings  align with research demonstrating that what counts  as knowledge in science (and STEM broadly) has been de termined by dominant groups (Benavides et al., 2023). Thus, the course syllabus and activities were re-designed to place more emphasis on appreciating mul tiple ways of knowing and what counts as science in an effort to promote agency and justice focuses from transformative perspectives. For instance, the re-design included embedded equity reflection prompts throughout assignments. Additionally, the readings and direct instruction were updated to reflect transformative approaches to equity (empowering student agency, connecting science to s ocial justice)  with activities that provided the TCs an opportunity to engage with the ideas. Another notable change was the removal of a facilitator for the video clubs. Additionally, the inclusion of a protocol to support TCs as they watched themselves teach and practiced different ways to see students as “smart” and “count” the dif ferent ways that they are scientific. This included  noticing students’ resources, interpreting them as strengths, and leveraging them in instruction.

## Methods

This study looked at one section of the 16-week elementary science methods course at a large p ublic university in the Midwest. Nineteen of the TCs enrolled in this course section agreed to participate. This section was selected because during a preliminary analysis of post-interviews after the course, multiple TCs in this s ection expressed a desire to be more humanizing in their teaching pr actices. These TCs were in their junior year of the ir teacher preparation program. During their junior year, they spent 2-3 days in their student teaching placement per week. The TCs were in a range of schools for their placements, some with more emphasis on science than others. Their schools either adopted Next Generation Science Standards (NGSS) or standards that utilize the disciplinary core ideas and practices from NGSS. We use d pseudonyms selected by the TCs after completing the ir consent forms during the first week of the course.

This study used data sources that illuminated how TCs talked about practices they wanted to implement (their desires) and the small moves they made to en act those desires (territorialization, deterritoria lization, and reterritorialization). Throughout the course, the unit plan was an opportu nity for TCs to design an elementary science unit that aligned with at least one of the NASEM (2021) equity approaches and that encouraged the multiple pathways of student science sensemaking. A s part of the unit plan, TCs designed two lesson pl ans that followed the 5E Instructional Model. This teaching model adapts a student-centered approach by encouraging students to interact with  scientific concepts, construct knowledge, and process content based on their prior knowledge. There are five phases (engagement, exploration, explanation, elaboration, and evaluation), each with its own purpose for science learning and instruction. The evaluation phase is where teachers can evaluate students’ progress in terms of the learning objectives and where students can assess their own understanding (Bybee, 2006). Data that illustrated TCs’ talk included artifacts from the course such as unit plan reflections, video club discussions, and post-interviews. During weeks 12 and 14, TCs participated in video c lub where they watched a small clip of each other teaching, commented on eac h other’s teaching practices, and highlighted their  students’ sensemaking, working to really notice their students’ strengths. Additionally, TCs reflected on how their unit plan promoted equitable teaching and learning and allow ed for an inclusive learning environment.  During post-interviews, TCs were prompted to reflect on their v ision of elementary science teaching and equitable science teaching.

Data that illustrated the small moves the TCs actually made included the 5E lesson plans in the unit they designed, particularly the assessment they planned in the evaluation phase. In addition, their actions during video club. During the course, students had the opportuni ty to teach these lessons so the lesson design is a  preview of what they would actually do. During video club, TCs highlighted their own and each other's actions, demonstrating what they valued in themselves and each other as teachers. Building on the concept of a rhizome  a s we explore d TCs’ thinking and actions in their becoming humanizing educators, employing rhizomatics  and rhizoanalysis was appropriate as this would not be a linear process for the TCs (St. Pierre, 2019). As stated, “The goal of rhizomatics, then, is not the obliteration of existing strata (or organized territorialized space) but the  discovery of the available lines of flight  within that space” (Dimitriadis & Kamberelis, 2006, p.92). Given this,  rhizomatics allow ed us to use our data sources as an entry point as we explored where TCs were in their becoming humanizing educators. Another key part of rhizoanalysis is connectivity  given that rhizomes  connect from one point to another (Masny, 2016). In our open-ended exploration, we made connections between our data and the Deleuz o-Guattarian concepts of  lines of flight/deterritorialization, and reterritorialization (see Strom & Martin, 2013 for connecting constructs). We also made connections with our  data and humanizing pedagogy/assessment.  We consider assessment to be a traditionally controlling and limiting space, and t hus a territorialization (Fouweather and Bosma, 202 1; Taylor, 2019). We explored how TCs’ desires produced lines of flight in terms of the humanizing things they said and did (small moves) that resisted the status quo (deterritorialization) in their assessme nt of students. These allowed us to draw connections between TCs ’ talk and actions with humanizing constructs based o n the literature. These connections were lines of flight and create d areas of deterritorialization. However, we also explore d territorialization/reterritorialization as we know that assessment is a stable societal construct that  is highly emphasized in our educational system.  Thus, connections were made between what TCs said and did  that relate to more traditional constructs of  assessment.  Following our post-qualitative theoretical foundations, rather than presenting “codes” or themes, in the following sect ion we present lines of flight (deterritorializatio n) as well as territorialization and reterritorialization that em erged in our open-ended exploration of the data. These are illustrated through examples of deterritorialization, territorialization, and reterritorialization moves we saw several of the TCs making in the data. These exemplars were selected because they most clearly demonstrate the connections between the TCs’ talk and moves, assessment, lines of flight, and humanizing pedagogy.

## Findings

Our findings showed that the elementary TCs expressed desires to bring humanizing pedagogy to the forefront in their teaching. This included recognizing students’ strengths, valuing students’ cultures and backgrounds, letting students have agency, and leaving space for more op en-ended responses. These practices they described connect to humanizing pedagogy given their alignment with the key principles and practices  (Bartolomé, 1994; Freire, 1970; Salazar, 2013). The TCs’ desires produced lines of flight as they discussed their vision for assessments and taught in placements. However, deterritorialization can be temporary (Str om & Martin, 2013) and thus, in the lesson plans they wrote, we saw TCs reverting back to some of the more neoliberal education patterns, particularly when it comes to assessment.

Deterritorialization in assessment/evaluation For the TCs, equity meant recognizing students’ res ources and valuing the approaches students provide.  For instance, during the post-interview one TC, Lucy, explained: I may provide assessments or whatever form of grading to really get an understanding or assess students’ understanding. But I should be OK with, l ike approaching it in ways that the student wants to give to me, so being able to hand that power back to the student while also maintaining that OK, I can still assess you and I can still com e up with, like developments for like grading and stuff like that. Here Lucy demonstrate d evidence of critical consciousness in her acknowled gement of unbalanced power relations that exist between students and teachers (Andrews et al., 2019, Qin & Beauchemin, 2022). Particularly when it comes to assessment, teachers usually have a set structure for how the assessment will look and how it will be graded (Taylor, 2019). She described empowering students to demonstrate their knowledge in a way that made sense to them (Salazar, 2013). Lucy recognize d that there are multiple wa ys for students to know and understand science, so she let students demonstrate  their ways of knowing and understanding (Andrews et al., 2019). There was also recognition that she is expected to assess and grade students (this is a structure/system she needs to “maintain”) so in that she describ ed territorialization (Taylor, 2019). However, she also describ ed how she negotiates that with her desire to give power back to her students. She could still assess and grade students in the way they provide. Thus, this is where she pushe d back and as a result, we saw deterritorialization. This is significant evidence that shows that not only are TCs comfortab le deconstructing power dynamics in their classroom, but it is also within their desires to do so which is a powerful d riving force in their transformation as educators.

Multiple TCs discussed how they valued  students' interests, culture, backgrounds, curiosit ies, ways of knowing, and understanding (Andrews et al., 2019; Czop et al., 2019; Salazar, 2013). They wanted to bring these various aspects of students into their teaching inc luding their assessment. In assessment, they sought  to incorporate student agency and open-ended tasks (NASEM, 2021)  which are constructs unrelated to large-scale standardized assessment (Schissel, 2024). For examp le, in her unit plan reflection and justification, Afia recommended offering “assessments that cater to different modal ities, such as visual, auditory, kinesthetic, and tactile. For example, allow students to choose betw een writing an essay, creating a visual infographic, or delivering an oral presentation to demonstrate thei r understanding”. Similarly, in her unit plan refle ction and justification, Lucy mention ed incorporating multiple “learning perspective opport unities and sense-making skills”. She said she will be sure to do this by being “mindful that my approaches within assessments may not be the same for one student as another. Being able to differentiate students' abilities, and connections within the lesson”. In the evaluate section of her 5E lesson plan, she had students direct her in creating a model. She reflected on how this was a lesson she later taught and how each student was eager to “add a little piece of their own work to what became a class representation of what we just learned”. This shows how Lucy expressed her desire to be more humanizing in her assessment of students and  went on to do this when she went to her placement as she used a consensus model as a way to assess student understanding instead of a traditional test or quiz. Incorporating student agency and ways of knowing and understandin g allows TCs to push back on the more traditional forms of assessment which do not traditionally allow stud ents to bring in these aspects of themselves (Schissel, 2024), so as a result there was deterritorialization. Similar to what we saw with desires to balance power dynamics, this is evidence that shows TCs actually do desire to bring more of their students’ lives into their assessments. The TCs ’ value of being more student-centered was further demonstrated during video club. For example, during video club Connie shared a clip of her teaching a lesson on the states of matter, focu sing on ice melting. She shared with her peers how she had originally intended to do a graphic organizer during the lesson as a form of formative assessment. Students were to complete this independently. However, based on feedback from her mentor teacher that her students would need mor e guidance, she decided to w alk her students through the graphic organizer, as a more direct instruction. In the clip, we saw how interactive the lesson was as Connie walked her students through various phases of the ice melt ing and elicited students’ thinking throughout, asking questions like “What do we think could be happening to the ice right now?” “What are you noticing?” “What does melting mean?”. At the beginning of the discussion, she was even able to do a review of the previous lesson where they talked about gases. A student shared about how gas is inside of a bal loon, which he remembered because it was his birthday. By making the shift from a more formal formative as sessment to an informal discussion based on her students’ needs, Connie was still able to as sess her students' understanding while making the l esson more engaging for them. In this way, we see Connie noticing and supporting student sense-making (Bang et al., 2017). It also points to her flexibility in assessing her students (Schissel, 2024, Taylor, 2019). Additionally, during video club, Elizabeth pointed out how assessment can be driven by the students and how beneficial this is for their learning. As anoth er TC shared a clip of her lesson on the needs of l iving things and her classes’ discussion on what penguins need, Elizabeth complimented the TC on how  she approached the graphic organizer the class was working on, a KWL chart. This organizer helps students organize information into what they already know about a topic (K), what they want to know about the topic (W), and what they have learned about the topic (L). The clip show ed the TC filling in the “L” portion of the KWL chart,  which was her planned evaluation from her 5E lesson plan. However, some students shared things they were still curious about. Elizabeth complimented the TC on how she still fill ed in the wonder section and did not restrict them to just sharing what they learned. Being flexible in her plans and allowing students to share in this way ended up giving the TC different information than what she planned, but that she could potentially use in upcoming lessons. During her own clip, Elizabeth showed a video on weathering and erosion and students had to fill in a Venn diagram with similarities and differences between weathering and  erosion that they learned from the video. During t he lesson, she noticed that the video was going too fast, apologized, slowed the video down, and replayed it for her students. Another TC complimented Elizabeth on this, saying i t was good how Elizabeth “positioned herself as a human, not an all-knowing teacher”. In letting students' needs and wants drive the lesson, again the TCs gave some of the power back to their students (Andrews et al., 2019;  Salazar, 2013). This may not have been possible had the planned assessment been more of a traditional form of assessment, so deterritorialization happened again. This is significant because i t shows that in action TCs are capable of acting on  their desires to be more humanizing in their assessment.

The excerpts from the post-interviews, unit plan reflections,  and video club show what the TCs valued and practiced. They valued their student s’ knowledge and needs and then used this to drive their lessons. The assessments they planned for were more open-ended, which allowed students to bring more of themselves into the assessment. When the TCs realized that a different approach would be more beneficial for their students, they were flexible and made changes accordingly. All of these moves that the TCs made were  moments of deterritorialization. These moments show what teachers wanted and were ab le to do in terms of humanizing assessment. In the next section, we describe how t hese values and practices did not always show up as  teachers planned for assessments in the unit plan.

Reterritorialization in assessment/evaluation As shown in the previous section, the TCs emphasize d student agency as they discussed their vision for  science teaching and assessment. Many of the TCs defined agency as letting their students have a choice in how they want to be assessed. While TCs did push back on more tra ditional forms of assessment by letting their stude nts have more of a voice during the assessment itself, the T Cs left limited choices for students as they planne d for the assessment. Each of the TCs only planned one form of assessm ent, they did not plan multiple ways to assess the same learning objective. Students were not allowed to choose from  different options as TCs described. For example, the Venn diagram for her weathering and erosion lesson was one that Elizabeth included in her unit plan. All students were expected to complete this V enn diagram and to do so the same way. In her unit plan, she wrote that each student would work independently on their Venn diagram. Students whoneed ed support could work with her in a small group so that she could scaffold it for them. Advanced students could do research on the topic if they finish the Venn diagram early. English Language Learners (ELLs) could fill the Venn diagram out in their first language and translate it. We saw parts of this enacted in her clip for video club. The assessment she later planned for students all has them doing the same assessment, in similar ways. The differentiation helps them to complete the assessment she has designated for t hem to do.  If Elizabeth had plans to give students choices within the assessment she described, we did not see those plans written in the lesson. A second example would be with Afia who earlier rec ommended “assessments that cater to different modalities” and providing different options for students. Afia had students answering questions from a lab as the assessment in her lesson plan on states of ma tter. As we s aw in the lesson plan, all students are assessed on th e lab worksheet they completed. In Afia’s lesson plan, there were places throughout the lesson where she was able to elicit ideas from students through discussion. I n the “Explain” portion,  students discuss their responses to the questions on the lab worksheet about physical and chemical changes, for instance. They also discuss their findings from the lab in the “Elaborate” phas e. This aligned with the other TCs who included turn and talks/thin k-pairshares, visuals, activating prior knowledge in their lesson plans. She planned to evaluate them based on whatever they wrote on the lab worksheet.  A third example would be how Connie throughout her ice melting lesson had planned that students wou ld revise a model that they created during the “Exp lain” part of her 5E lesson plan. Returning to these models and revising is her plann ed evaluation throughout the entire lesson. ELLs were encouraged to explain the reasoning in the models i n the language they felt most comfortable with. However, all students were expected to complete this model. She mentioned how she would provide a rubric for students with expectations on how to create their models.

At each point when these various modes of assessmen t showed up, all students were doing the same thing. All the students did a turn and talk. All students filled in a graphic organizer. All students drew a picture. All students completed a weather log. In the differentiation sections of each lesson plan, we saw supports the TCs put in place to help students complete the form of assessment they were expected to do. For example, ways that TCs scaffold activities and include translations for English Language L earners (Tigert et al., 2022). These are moves that TCs made to push back on the status quo (deterritorialization), as these are not supports that are always offered to students during more traditional assessm ents. However, by only allowing their students one way to showcase their knowledge at each point, the TCs t ook back some of the power they provide d. By limiting the choices at each stage in the lesson the TCs decide d how students w ould be assessed, and this was an area of reterritorialization. This limit in choice connects to more standardized forms of assessment and illuminates a place of challenge as TCs negotiate what humanizing pedagogy could become. There was evidence that TCs did not always follow through on their limited plans for assessmen t. An example of this can be found with Lucy who was aske d during the post-interview if there was ever a time when she allowed students to demonstrate their knowledge differently than how she planned to assess them. My approach was to have students create like, create an object that produces sound either using the geoboard or the tissue blocks to really see if students are really like grasping that understanding of vibrations and things like that. And students took the approach of like why do we have to use either or, can we connect our geoboard to our tissue box to sh ow you a bigger version of that? So excuse me, they definitely found an approach to show me differently or they used different parts of their body to vibrate to show me OK, this is what it's doing. Even though I can't hear the sound I'm showing you that it's vi brating this so you can't see it, but I'm going to show you. So, I guess showing it in a way that makes sense to them. Thus, originally there was territorialization in Lucy’s lesson plan. She planned a performance assessment for her students with certain guidelines for them t o show their knowledge of the concept. She did not origina lly plan to give students options for how they can show mastery of the concept. We do acknowledge the mome nt of deterritorialization when Lucy was actually teachin g the lesson. This is similar to the mome nts we saw during video club where the TCs were very open and flexibl e to the ways of knowing and learning that the stud ents provided. The point we make here is the disconnect between what the TCs said and did regarding their teaching and what they plan out in the 5E lesson plans. TCs are expected to assess their students and even with more openended assessments, they are still expected to have clear guidelines on how they will be assessing the students (as specified in the rubric for how they will be assess ed on the unit plan). With this, TCs go back to “bu siness as usual” to make sure they are meeting their expectations as teachers.

## Discussion & Implications

In conclusion, when TCs were planning there was some disconnect between their desires and what they wrote down. We do note that because TCs were flexible in the moment when they were teaching (as evidenced by video club) they could have made changes based o n their students' needs. From what we have seen, in  their planning TCs fell into structures that have been put into place for them. In their actual teaching, they pushed back more.

## Discussion and implications

We set out to explore the lines of flight of elementary science teacher candidates as they assess their students and negotiate what humanizing pedagogy looks like for them. In their talk, TCs expressed desires to balance the power dynamics between themselves and their students, valuing their ways of knowin g and understanding science and allowing their students to guide the way the assess ment will go. TCs expressed their desires during po stinterviews. Our findings suggest that the TCs’ desi res led to several small moves that pushed back on the status quo of testing in their teaching practice and plann ing. This deterritorialization occurred during unit  planning and video clubs. We saw TCs implementing more open-ended assessments, and student-led. As a result, students were able to bring more of themselves into the assessmen t. Instead of being rigid in their plans for the as sessment, we saw TCs being more flexible based on their students ’ needs. However, we also saw places where reterritorialization occurred and TCs fell into more typical educational structures and expectations. This occurred primarily in the lesson plans of TCs’ where student  choice in the type of assessment was limited. Though TCs used different strategies throughout the lesson pla n, the official assessments that TCs planned for di d not leave much room for students to choose between different formats of assessment, which would allow studen ts to demonstrate their knowledge in a way that made sense to them. Instead, the TCs planned one form of assessment for all of their students in their lesson plans. Gi ven how much TCs emphasized student choice in their  talk, for example during their post-interviews, this discrepancy points to a roadblock somewhere along the way as they try to implement student choice.

One of the primary contributions of this paper has been demonstrating the places where deterritorialization can and does happen for elemen tary TCs as well as where there can be reterritoria lization. Showing these areas of strength and challenge is beneficial for teacher educators who wish to center equity in the form of humanizing pedagogy  as they attempt to  anticipate what supports the TCs  may need. Our findings are promising given how their desires led TCs to create  assessments that are not the kind we would see in a standardized test. Much of what they created aligned with their talk. Based on our findings, a challenge is for TCs to incorporate student choice in their assessment. Agency is one of the 4 approaches to equity and is one of the most difficult for educators to implement (NASEM, 2 021). Our TCs’ moves that allowed for more student voice in their assessments is a move towards incorporatin g more agency. However, part of agency is empowering students to make consequential decisions about what they study and how they study it (NASEM, 2021). In terms of assessment, this would include how they are assessed. Students would be able to decide how they demonstrate their knowledge.

The next steps in the field should be the exploration of  specific supports to put in place as TCs plan for assessment. This could include exploring TCs’ definitions of agency in terms of assessment. Do they consider the assessments they have planned for to be an implementation of student agency? In what ways? Additionally, future directions could explore what structures could be p ut into place to support TCs as they seek to incorp orate more student agency. We will explore what ways we can en courage TCs to think of multiple options for studen t assessment in their unit plans. A last direction co uld be exploring the productive tensions that arise  as TCs plan for assessment (Jeong & Steele, 2023). Some TCs recognize activities like turn and talks and co-creating a model as a form of assessment. Some reverted back to more  traditional independent pen-to-paper (answering questions on a worksheet). We would explore whether TCs notic e these differences in assessment compared to peer s and whether they notice difference s in their talk, their plans, and their actions. Using the lens of diffracting noticing would be  useful here (Jeong and Steele, 2023). This would be  beneficial in the journey to being  humanizing educators given the importance of critical consciousness (Bartolomé, 1994; Freire, 1970; Salazar, 2013).
