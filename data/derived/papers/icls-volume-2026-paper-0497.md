# An Exploratory Factor Analysis of K–12 Teachers’ Perceptions of Artificial Intelligence in the Classroom

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study examines K– 12 teachers’ perceptions of AI in education using Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) on survey data from 139 participants. Four interrelated factors were identified: Barriers & Anxiety, Professional Engagement & Instructional Readiness, Ethical & Responsi ble AI Use, and Resource Support & Conceptual Awareness. These findings contribute to understanding how K-12 teachers perceive AI integration.

Teachers have a pivotal role in effectively integrating AI-based technologies, such as personal tutoring systems, into the classroom. Recent research (Nazaretsky et al., 2022) identified eight underlying dimensions (e.g., Required Shift in Pedagogy) that influence K–12 teachers’ trust in AI-based educational technologies. Similarly, Depaepe et al.

(2025) revealed four factors related to pre-service teachers’ trust of AI-based educational technologies (e.g., Trust

and Confidence). However, despite growing interest in this topic, there remains a need to examine the underlying structure of K –12 teachers’ perceptions of AI —particularly how the latent dimensions of these perceptions interrelate to shape adoption in real classroom settings. To address this gap, the current study surveyed K–12 New York teachers to explore their beliefs, experiences, and concerns regarding AI in education to identify latent dimensions that underlie teachers’ perceptions of AI integration. The objectives of this study were to identify the latent factors underlying teachers’ perceptions of AI in education (RQ1) and to examine how these factors interrelate (RQ2).

## Method

This study employed an online survey via Qualtrics to examine New York State public school teachers’ perceptions of AI in education. The survey was developed and verified by two experienced researchers, following a pilot study conducted in December 2023 to  ensure its face validity with target group. The survey included 30 Likert-scale items assessing teachers’ AI use, trust, preparedness, barriers, ethical concerns, and professional development needs. The reliability of the retained 20-items was calculated using Cronbach’s alpha (α =.60). The survey was distributed in Spring 2025 to approximately 10,000 New York State teachers, yielding 168 responses. Of the respondents, 66% were women. Most taught at the high school (45%) or middle school (36%) level, with 42% having over 20 years of teaching experience, and Humanities & Social Studies (46%) as the most common certification area. After excluding 29 missing value responses, 139 participants were retained for analysis.

To address RQ1, an EFA was conducted to identify the latent structure underlying teachers’ perceptions of AI in education. Principal Axis Factoring (Henson & Roberts, 2006) was used, accounting for measurement error, and recovering weak factors in perception-based data. Given that the factors were expected to be correlated, an oblique (Oblimin) rotation was applied. To determine the number of factors to retain, multiple criteria were considered, including Kaiser’s criterion (eigenvalues > 1; Kaiser, 1960), the scree test (Cattell, 1966), and parallel outperform the Kaiser and scree methods (Horn, 1965), supported a four-factor solution. Items with loadings below.40 (yielding  7 items) were excluded from further analysis, as loadings greater than or equal to.40 are considered meaningful indicators of factor contribution (Henson & Roberts, 2006). To address RQ2, we examined the correlations between factors.

Item responses were collected on a 5-point Likert scale and met normality assumptions, with skewness (– 1.31 to 0.71) and kurtosis (– 1.15 to 1.95) within acceptable thresholds (West et al., 1995). Sampling adequacy was confirmed by a Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) value of.78, and Bartlett’s test of sphericity was significant, χ²(435) = 2854.70, p <.001,  indicating that the data were suitable for factor analysis. Parallel analysis supported a four-2919 factor solution. After removing items with loadings below.40, the analysis was rerun with the remaining 22 items, resulting in four factors: (1) Barriers & Anxiety, (2) Professional Engagement & Instructional Readiness, (3) Ethical & Responsible AI Use, a nd (4) Resource Support & Conceptual Awareness, which together explained 62% of the total variance.

To address RQ2, factor correlations were examined as shown in Figure 1. The analysis revealed a moderate positive relationship between Barriers & Anxiety and Resource Support & Conceptual Awareness (r =.33), suggesting that teachers who experience challenges and anxieties in using AI also tend to perceive the importance of resource support and conceptual understanding. A positive relationship was found between Professional Engagement & Instructional Readiness and Resource Support & Conceptual Awareness (r =.14), as well as between Barriers & Anxiety and Professional Engagement & Instructional Readiness (r =.60), indicating that teachers who feel prepared to use AI tend to be comparatively aware of the conceptual and resource supports available to them, even as they encounter barriers and anxiety about  AI use. A n otable finding is that Ethical & Responsible AI Use was negatively correlated with the other dimensions, suggesting that teachers’ perceptions of ethical considerations of AI use may be at odds with other considerations among teachers. For instance, teachers expressing more instructional readiness with AI also  tended to be less concerned with issues of responsible AI use and ethics (r = -.33).

This study identified four distinct dimensions shaping K –12 teachers’ perceptions of AI use in the classroom: Barrier & Anxiety, Instructional Readiness & Professional Engagement, Ethical & Responsible AI Use, and Resource Support & Conceptual Awareness. Barriers and anxiety emerged together, suggesting that difficulties in using AI may increase teachers’ anxiety. Regarding the interrelationships among these dimensions, most factors showed positive associations, except for Ethical & Responsible AI Use, echoing earlier studies that emphasize the need for educators to prioritize ethical considerations in AI adoption and use (Holmes et al., 2022). However, the findings should be interpreted with caution due to the relatively low response rate, which may introduce bias and limit generalizability. Additionally, the sample was limited to teachers from New York State. Future studies should explore how teachers’ perceptions evolve over time as AI-based systems become more prevalent in schools.. Longitudinal research could also examine whether increased ethical awareness coincide s with other factors such as instructional readiness and access to resources.
