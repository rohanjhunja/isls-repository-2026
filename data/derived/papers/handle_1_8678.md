# Preparation for Future Learning and Disposition Development in a Social Studies-Themed Digital Simulation Game: A Quantitative Ethnography

### Abstract
We use quantitative ethnography and Epistemic Network Analysis to examine how the game mechanics of a social studies-themed digital simulation game may facilitate disposition development and preparation for future learning.

## Introduction

Previous research indicates that Offworld Trading Company  (OTC), a digital simulation game thematically aligned to social studies invites players to practi ce using disciplinary knowledge, skills, and concep ts (DKSCs) associated with social studies to plan, take, and reflect on goal-mediated actions (Kessner, 2021). We are interested in how OTC might be leveraged towards two ends: (a) preparati on for future learning; and (b) the ability and sensitivity elements of disposition development. We  are particularly interested in identifying how gam e mechanics generate problem spaces in which these two goals might be facilitated. Good games are excellent learning environments. The y privilege experience before text, performance before competence, identity play, and systems thinking; and they present players with interesting, well-structured, and well-sequenced problems players solve by master ing and applying game-valued skills (Gee, 2003). Go od games often align better than formal education environments to long-standing contentions of situativity theorists that what we learn cannot be disentangled from how and why we learn it (Gresalfi et al., 2009). For th ese and other reasons, games have maintained a level of popularity among both educators and researchers. In the context of social studies education, however, a mismatch ex ists between this interest and the available eviden ce of effectiveness, as well as conceptual guidance around what (kinds of) games are useful and why. Use of games in social studies education contexts may indeed represent a unique challenge. Good social studies education prepares students for active participation within a pluralist representative democracy (Banks, 2006; Barton & Levstik, 2004). Ci vic preparation, Kessner and colleagues (2020) argu ed, should include citizens coming to see the world as designed and redesignable, and themselves as posses sing agency over those designs in the form of designerly  agency. Economics, the heart of which comprises pr oblem framing and decision making, offers a particularly potent disciplinary toolkit for identifying, consid ering, and planning, taking, and reflecting on such design problems. Nevertheless, research on economics education remains decidedly scant relative to its fellows under the social studies umbrella; and while scholars have attended to the economics of the videogame industry as well actual in-game economies, we are unaware of serious schola rly attention to videogames as sites in which economics education may present. We use quantitative ethnography (QE) (Shaffer, 2017) to examine small-group play of OTC to explore the following research questions:

1. To what extent might playing OTC influence learners’ ability, as measured by the ap propriate use of

disciplinary discourse, to use economics concepts t o attend to real-world problems, and what game mechanics might be associated with such development?

2. To what extent does playing OTC develop learners’ sensitivity to economics in the real world, and what

game mechanics might be associated with evidence of changes in this sensitivity?

3. To what extent does playing OTC prepare students for future learning in the domain of economics, and

what game mechanics might be associated with positive evidence of preparation for future learning?

## Theoretical framework

By dispositions we mean the bridging of the ability to do something and actually doing it. Dispositions comprise three elements: ability, sensitivity, and inclinati on (Perkins et al. 2000). Dispositions are also par t of the fastthinking system of the brain, which relies heavily on prior experiences for decision making (Kahneman 2011; Seligman et al. 2016), experiences which occur as embodied interactions with the world mediated by tools. Gee

(2015) described this interaction as conversations with the world, cycles of thinking and action in which people

identify goals, plan actions, and gather feedback on those actions from the world. Learners probe the world with actions, and the world “talks back”. In videogames,  players are embodied in the game world as avatars,  which ICLS2022 Proceedings 1990 © ISLS come with toolkits (Gee, 2015). It is with these to ols and through these avatars that players (are abl e to) take actions in games.  Thus, games provide a solid theoretical foundation for disposition development. The ways in which OTC presents core deep-structure problems relevant to social studies in myriad surface-feature configurations also, we contend, supports transfer of knowledge to novel real-world scenarios, which is essential to disposition development (Bransford & Schwartz 19 99). Furthermore, we suggest that engaging with the se problem spaces prepares students for future learning in the domain (Schwartz & Arena 2013).

## Methods

QE leverages quantitative designs and analyses to m ake sense of complex qualitative discourse data (Sh affer, 2017). Recent games scholarship leveraging QE has explored themes from how an online game community forum facilitated participants’ identity exploration processes (Barany & Foster, 2019) to the correlation between facial expressions and understanding of biological and complex systems concepts (Martin et al., 2019). QE represents particular affordances for the present study, as we ll. Games are immensely complex systems, making pur ely qualitative accountings of the connections between many diverse variables difficult if not untenable. Participants for this study are a class of high sch ool students enrolled in an Academic Decathlon (AD) elective course in a public school in the American South. This year, AD has an economics theme. Specif ically, we are working with underclassmen enrolled in the course but who did not make the competition team. Students come from diverse backgrounds, and their varied lev els of academic achievement, from “low” to “high,” as measured by school-calculated grade point averages,  indicates a wide and potentially representative sa mple of academic interest and engagement with school. While prior learning would be a concern in a traditional economics classroom, the English Language Arts teachers spons oring this implementation of AD are trained neither  as economists nor as economics educators, and they his torically rely on senior students who already posse ss the requisite knowledge. The students who did not make the competition team, then, by definition are unlik ely to possess significant prior knowledge.

The intervention consists of students playing OTC in diads during class time for one week. Instructi on on relevant economics concepts will be taught by th e researchers in a just-in-time fashion (Squire, 20 11). Gameplay will be screencaptured, and student discourse data will be audio recorded. At the end of each  lesson, students will be tasked with skimming news headlines for relevant social studies concepts, which they will bring to class the next day to discuss. Transcribed disco urse data, gameplay data, and student work will be analyzed through Epistemic Network Analysis to identify conn ections between gameplay and student talk that eith er directly leverages disciplinary discourse or otherwise implicates discipline-relevant thinking.
