# What Makes a Competent Outsider?

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Fifteen years ago, Noah Feinstein introduced the idea of the competent outsider to argue for a comprehensive re-thinking of the purposes of science education. The idea orients competence away from science disciplines, per se, and toward the goals that everyday people might frame for themselves for which science may be useful. Despite its increasing use as a goal, however, answers to the question of what sorts of knowledge and skill renders anyone a competent outsider remain elusive. As the concept of the competent outsider has been taken up in recent arguments for how to organize science education in a post-truth era, it is a good time to reconsider the idea and to interrogate both of its components: what renders one competent with respect to science and what is a competent outsider actually outside of?

## Overview

There has been a quiet revolution in the last decade or so concerning what should be the proper purpose of science education through high school. This revolution has to do with what it means to consider someone scient ifically literate, as scientific literacy has been a longstanding goal of science education for decades. It also has to do with how scholars are trying to answer the question, “Ho w should the average citizen be able to engage with  science in everyday life?” These are not the same thing, although it might seem like they are, mostly because formulations of scientific literacy have not always been clearly  grounded in the practical utility such literacy mi ght have. The question of the public’s engagement with science ha s taken center stage over the last decade as social media and other factors have greatly expanded the availabilit y of information, and misinformation, to the averag e person (Barzilai & Chinn, 2020; Bromme & Goldman, 2014). Concerns about how to prepare youth to navigate a “posttruth” era have only been inflamed by the intransigence of discourse around climate change and the confusion of the COVID-19 pandemic. Available evidence suggests pretty cle arly that current approaches to teaching science are not serving people well as adults (Lederman et al., 2024). The emerging consensus in science education is that  it is probably more important that young people learn about science, especially how the practices a nd institutions of science work to produce reliable  claims to knowledge that are “true enough”  (Elgin, 2017). The history of this emergence is tra ced comprehensively by Osborne (2023), who advances the idea that scientifically literate people are competent outsiders to science. The notion of the competent outsider was introduced by Feinstein, and he defined it as someone who has “le arned to recognize the moments when science has some be aring on their needs and interests and to interact with sources of scientific expertise in ways that help them achi eve their own goals”  (Feinstein, 2011, p. 180). Crucial to Feinstein’s notion of outsider here is the view tha t scientific expertise can be used to achieve one’s  own goals, which are almost certainly not scientific for most people. From an educational perspective, Feinstein locates two kinds of compet ence: being able to recognize when science has some  bearing on one’s needs and interests and being able to interact with science in ways that help one achieve one’s goals. The idea of the competent outsider is helpful for reconsidering the purposes of science education because it orients competence away from the disciplines, per se, and toward the goals that everyday people might fra me for themselves for which science may be useful. Des pite its increasing use as a goal, however, the que stion of what sorts of knowledge and skill renders anyone a competent outsider remains elusive. Further, the distinction between insiders and outsiders to science can be cr itiqued as maintaining ideas that have been damagin g to historically marginalized groups that their knowled ge and competence is unwelcome in, or even inferior  to, scientific knowledge and competence.

The goal of this symposium is to interrogate the concept of the competent outsider and its potential value as a target outcome for pre-college science education. What roles do or should scientific knowledge and practice play in people’s everyday life? What forms of scientific competence might contribute to civic engagement? What does it mean to construe learners as insiders or ou tsiders to science practice, both in school and out ? How does science instruction need to change to develop compe tent outsiders, or an alternative version of compet ence still focused on engaging with science for non-science ends? These questions require grappling wit h distinctions between school science and professional science, each of which can take many forms. They require distinguishing epistemic features of knowledge and reasoning that might be considered “scientific” from the instituti onal organization of the fields that are broadly conside red “the sciences.” Not least, they also require examination of the role of science education in the democratic project of working toward a more just and equitable society. Each presentation in this session takes on some sub set of these and related questions, as each conside rs whether or how the notion of competent outsider ser ves as a useful goal for science education. The ran ge of presentations highlights that within a broad agreement on the value of individual and public competence to engage in science, there are many perspectives and points of contention. Our collective aim is to engage each  other and the audience in a consideration of both how science education may be oriented toward legitimately civic purposes and how the field might actually accomplish such purposes. Becoming a “competent outsider” Jonathan Osborne Our modern culture divides its intellectual labor. Specialized knowledge is distributed across a diver se array of experts (Nichols, 2017). As Ryder has shown, many of the contemporary issues of a science-related nature require a knowledge of aspects of science that school science does not afford (Ryder, 2001). Evaluating complex scientific evidence and explanations  is, therefore, the activity of scientific experts. For those without expertise, however, the only rational path when confronted by science-based claims is to evaluate the source (Sperber et al., 2010). As non-experts they simply do not have sufficient expertis e or training to spot experimental flaws, to recogn ize cherry-picked data, to detect unsound statistical analyses, to consider unstated alternative hypotheses, and more. What science education cando to improve students’ ability to make good critical evaluations of this flood of information that pervades daily life?  As studen ts and the public are epistemically dependent on th e expertise of others, the issue for the non-expert is making an informed decision about who to trust (Hardwig, 1985; Norris, 1997). Yet, the standard focus of all forms of contempor ary science education is on the cognitive tools req uired to evaluate whether the conclusions are justified b y the data and the evidence. Yet, few of us have th e ability or time to read the evidence and eva luate its merits. For instance, the majority of the  public believe that climate change is happening – yet very few have read the substantive IPCC reports. Thus, the belief that a formal science education can attain the necessary knowledge and sk ills to bec ome “a critical consumer” (National Research Council, 2012) of scientific knowledge is at best misplaced and, a t worst, naïve. Rather, what is needed is the competencies and knowledge required to be a ‘competent outsider’ (Feinstein, 2011). But what might these be? Drawing on a body of contemporary scholarship condu cted by myself and others (Osborne & Allchin, 2024; Osborne & Pimentel, 2022; Osborne et al., 202 2), this presentation will argue that science educa tion must relinquish the myth that it can enable its students  to achieve intellectual independence. Rather, it must recognize that everybody, including future scientists, requir es the foundational competencies to evaluate expert ise.  These are a combination of specific digital media literacy strategies coupled with a knowledge of the social practices of science that enable the production of reliable know ledge (Höttecke & Allchin, 2020; Ziman, 1968). The latter requires an understanding that science depends on the work of a collaborative community for which peer review, in its broadest sense, is a fundamental requirement; a knowledge of the criteria that define scientifi c expertise; and the significance of consensus as a primary goal  of science. More fundamentally, it must seek to de velop a sense of epistemic humility helping students to recognize the limits of their own knowledge (Osborne & Pimentel, submitted). Research shows how these outcomes can be achieved  and provides evidence of significant positive outcomes (Pimentel, 2024; Wineburg et al., 2022). What the implications for science education are w ill be explored.

Approaching the notion of competent outsider from a meta-perspective Frauke Voitle, Kerstin Kremer, William Sandoval Science education has to deal with  today’s knowledge-driven society by educating individuals to become competent outsiders  who are capable of engaging with scientific informa tion critically and responsibly  in everyday life (Feinstein, 2011; Osborne, 2023; Skilling & Park, 2 024). However, what characterizes a person as a competent outsider has yet to be defined and science education researchers from different fields of research have promoted different suggestions and foci. For this discussion to bypass longstanding controve rsies between researchers from different research fields, a fresh  and integrative impulse that allows for fruitful d iscussion is needed. Therefore, we propose an integrative framework that aims to facilitate productive discourse by moving debates to a meta-level and call for science education researchers to collaboratively focus on their shared goal of educating responsible citizens capable and willing to make informed decisions in science-related issues. This framework integrates two continua: (1) the purview or scope of analysis (individual versus collective perspective) and (2) the context of science (school science versus professional science). These two dimensions are mapped as continua that intersect to  form a two-dimensional grid, resulting in four quadrants. Each quadrant represents a field of interest within science education, offering a structured way to position research perspectives within the field of science education. The framework’s structure outlines a meta-perspective towards issues discussed in science education. Application of this meta-perspective prompts  researchers to either seek consensus along the framework’s continua or identify divergent foundations of their approaches. Thereby, the framework centers discussions on establishing a shared perspective towards science education or on considering the complementary value of differing approaches in terms of a holistic picture. Consequently, instead of arguing about specific competences or content knowledge, the meta-perspective encourages constructive and inclusive dialogue and promotes a holistic framing of debates.

In this presentation, the framework is used regarding the question of what defines a competent outsider. In positioning current debates in science education  along the framework’s two continua, it becomes visi ble that there is an emphasis on normative approaches that deductively suggest characteristics of a comp etent outsider. Available approaches to characterize qualities of a competent outsider reasonably derive suggestions from existing work of differing theoretical bases in science education (Feinstein et al., 2013; Osborne & Allchin, 2024; Skilling & Park, 2024). Unfortunately, this also bears the risk for longsta nding controversies in science education  to impede fruitful discussion within the research community and thus, may hamper much needed progress in science education. To counteract this risk, the analysis of the research landscape based on the proposed framework is used to suggest different options for science education to proceed. For instance, it is demonstrated, how the application of the framework can help to identify  white spots of yet under-represented research fields within the debate and their possible contribution to characterizing a competent outsider will be discussed. In what sense an outsider? The organizational and institutional perspective on science Noah Weeth Feinstein Feinstein’s 2011 definition of the competent outsider seems straightforward: competent outsiders are “people who have learned to recognize the moments when science has some bearing on their needs and interests and to interact with sources of scientific expertise in ways that help them achieve their own goals.” (Feinstein, 2011, p. 180). Yet as science education has focused more and more on notions of epistemic community, the portrayal of students as outsiders has been criticized by scholars who argue that we should center the knowledge-making work of students, pedagogically and rhetorically positioning them as insiders. Using frameworks such as rightful presenc e (Calabrese Barton & Tan, 2019) and epistemic agency (Miller et al., 2018), these scholars argue that positioning diverse young people as outsiders reinforces their sense of exclusion from science and their lack of confidence in their own individual and collective sensemaking cap acity. Under what circumstances is it educationally  useful and ethically defensible to position students in this way? This conceptual paper argues that it is important t o distinguish between being two senses of being a scientific insider/outsider. Science education (e.g., NRC, 2012; OECD, 2022) tends to focus on understanding the abstracted epistemic character of scientific work  rather than the institutional character of scientific work, emphasizing the activities that distinguish the int ellectual work of science from other sorts of human  activity. In contrast, focusing on the organizational and instit utional character of scientific work would mean emp hasizing the institutions through wh ich this work is done, as well as the activities of  scientists in these institutions (e.g., obtaining funding, negotiating research priorities,  and publishing peer-reviewed articles) (Gieryn, 1999; Knorr-Cetina, 1999). Science education addresses these activities only i n the loosest analogical sense, yet it is difficult to imagine contemporary science without them.

If we think of “insiders” as people who are members  of a particular community, participate in the practices of that community, and whose participatio n is accepted by other participants  (Lave & Wenger, 1991; Longino, 2002), this distinction makes it easier to see how students who are insiders from an abstracted epistemic perspective—who participate in the epistemic practices of scien ce in classroom settings, for example —are still outsiders from an institutional perspective. For in stitutional outsiders, understanding how scientific  institutions work is essential to public engagement with science  across many different contexts (e.g., Epstein, 199 6; Ryder, 2001). This is why declining public trust in institutions has serious implications for science education and public engagement, and why science education that neglects  the institutional perspective on science is unlike ly to solve the problem.

In this context, is “trust” really a useful goal fo r science education? Projects that encourage “trust  in science” rarely capture the relational quality of t rust that characterizes public engagement with scie ntific institutions (Wynne, 1992), and fail to consider the implication s of science’s real institutional shortcomings, as well as its historical wrongdoing and its complicit y in global crises. In place of trust, I advance an  institutionally grounded, sociologically informed framework of “appropriate respect for science.” Being a competent outsider entails critical sociopolitical awareness Ravit Golanduncan, Rishi Krishnamoorthy, Edna Tan Feinstein and Waddington (2020) argue for “… pedagogies that help people work toget her to make appropriate use of science in social context.” (pg. 2) given th at “people encounter science as members of social and cultural groups, interpreting  and responding  to it in ways that reflect the attitudes, values,  and propensities of those groups.” (pg.5). We build on Feinstein and Waddington’s sociocultural perspective and nuance it by emphasizing that the social contexts in which science is used are always and invariably historically and politically laden. Being a competent outsider therefore also entails critical sociopolitical awareness of how socially constructed hierarchies of power and oppression impact both the representation and use of science. Consequently, science education needs to foster critical sociopolitical awareness of how scientific phenomena, and the scientific enterprise, are shaped by power and oppression.

Our argument is grounded in the perspective that sc ience and science education are inherently politica l (Morales-Doyle et al., 2020) and that equitable science education involves expanding who can engage in science, whose knowledge and practices are valued, and towar ds what ends (Calabrese Barton & Tan, 2020; Tan et al., 2019). Science education has a role to play in the struggle for a humanizing and just science and society (NASEM,

2024) and our ideas about what it means to be a competent outsider ought to reflect this role.

Fostering critical sociopolitical awareness of scie ntific practice and science phenomena is a tall ord er. We share our ongoing efforts, part of the Bio4Community project (Duncan et al., 2023), in which we attempted to promote such awareness in the context of two biolog y units at the middle and high school  levels. The middle school unit engaged students in examining the ways in which social forces shape individuals’ and commu nities’ experiences of stress. Instruction emphasized the e ntanglement of systemic oppressions in the environm ent and our health—i.e., how racism is making us sick (Adelman, 2008).  The high school unit similarly engaged students in understanding racial health disparities of diabetes (gene-environment interactions) and also emphasized the political nature of science and its use. For exampl e, the problematic attribution of racial health dispar ities to genetic (or physiological) differences between racial groups (Doucet-Battle, 2021; Graves & Goodman, 2022). In both units, students examined how the environments in which they live, affect their own and their community’s health, making visible how oppressive rules, expectations, and structures produce environments harmful  to minoritized communities. Students’ growing critical awareness subsequently informed their ideas about how these problems can be best addressed. They collaboratively developed science-informed advocacy efforts to transform their local environments into more just and caring spaces. Our work builds on and extends other efforts in the  field that offer additional examples  (Kotler et al., 2024; Morales-Doyle, 2017; Tan et al., 2019, and others). We beli eve that expanding our definition of what it means to be a competent outsider can support a more expansive and equitable science education and a public that can better use science towards a more just future.

Toward a civically transformative science education William Sandoval Since Feinstein put forward the idea of the competent outsider fifteen years ago, the world has seen dramatic shifts in how people get and make sense of information. Wh ile the internet and social media have made access to information completely widespread,  so too has this enabled the proliferation of misinf ormation and disinformation. Trust in institutions has declined, as has trust in expertise (Nichols, 2017). Trust in science, which has been in decline for decades in the United State s (Gauchat, 2012), was very publicly questioned durin g the COVID-19 pandemic (National Science Board, 2022)  and has long been discounted in public debates abou t climate change and other issues. The state of thing s led to cries that science education has failed in  its decadeslong effort to develop scientific literacy (Lederman et al., 2024). The notion of the competent outsider presaged the e merging consensus within science education of the need to focus on helping students understand the ep istemic bases of science, the kinds of reliable process (Chinn et al., 2011)that can support judgments of the trus tworthiness of particular claims (Osborne, 2023). This is consistent with calls for “epistemic education” bro adly (Barzilai & Chinn, 2018), which is an educatio n that prepares people to evaluate all sorts of claims to knowledge, not just scientific ones. An assumption in this reformulation of the goals of science education, and indeed of all education efforts in the United States, at least, is that by improvin g individual students’ abilities to engage with sci ence then we will inevitably improve the public’s engagement with science. This assumption seems unwarranted, both because (as Feinstein argued fifteen years ago about versions of science literacy) we have no evidence this is the case, and because sociocultural accounts of learning suggest that practices of thinking and acting are tightly bound to the contexts in and goals for which they are learned (Sandoval, 2023). Unless science learning is oriented explicitly toward public engagement, there seems little reason to believe that engagement will be improved. This presentation synthesizes a range of emerging trends in science education to propose a set of features of a science education that is not just civically o riented but can contribute to the broader democrati c project of a more just and equitable society.  Such a transformative science education must frame collective learning goals (Feinstein & Waddington, 2020), should localize ins truction to the communities in which people live (Buxton, 2010; Calabrese Barton & Tan, 2010), and support critical social action (Morales-Doyle, 2017). To date, proposals grounded in this sort of community orientation to science instruction have mainly emerged from efforts to engage and support learners from historically marginalized communities. The proposal here is that all pre-college science education, for everyone, requires these features if scienc e education is to truly participate in the democrat ic project. This entails profound changes for science education. The presentation will address one partic ularly important area of implication: science teacher preparation.
