# Athletic Tinkering: Playful Computing Learning at the Intersection of Sports and Technology

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In this work, we implement computing tools in athletic spaces where youth leverage their pre-existing interests and expertises in sports to make sense of technology. Through this, we aim to create culturally sustaining computing experiences. We analyze young learners and facilitators making sense of a volleyball themed iPad-based training game through play and embodied interactions. We find participants leveraging a variety of athletic resources to tinker with and make sense of how this technology works. We call this embodied and athletic engagement with technology athletic tinkering. This presents an example of culturally sustaining conceptualizations of STEM and computing learning, and it offers heuristics on how to design for similar activity-linked embodied interaction experiences.

## Introduction and background

Culturally sustaining pedagogy (CSP) reframes tradi tional approaches to instruction by centering the c ultural identities, values, and practices of learners withi n the learning experience (Alim & Paris, 2017; Inst itute of Education Sciences, 2023). CSP is an invaluable too l to engage people from diverse backgrounds and to ignite deep learning and can be leveraged in computing education. The relationship between identity and learn ing resources take form in practice-linked identities, where the practice and ut ilization of ideational, relational, and material resources are the building stones to ident ity development (Nasir & Cooks, 2009). By supportin g access to these learning resources, it creates space for l earners to bring in their cultural knowledge. We ad apt this framework toward our athletic computational STEM learning environment. We refer to the learning experiences in our study as tinkering, “fiddling around with” or “sensemaking.” Tinkering is an engaging way for people to learn th rough the process, rather than aiming for a single result (Timotheou & Ioannou, 2019). It supports the exploration of the world often through embodied interaction, where the physical body and tangible interactions signifi cantly impact learning (Lyons et al., 2015). The so cial dimensions of tinkering allow for emerging collabor ations when designed for multiple groups to meaning fully participate and support one another (Brahms, 2014; Halverson et al., 2018). Such support must be equit ably designed to come in different forms and across mult iple individuals in the space (Allen & Gutwill, 200 4; Roque et al., 2023). These considerations are key for researching the intersection of tinkering and CSP. Collectively, these works suggest that utilizing ti nkering as a lens for CSP in computing education ca n leverage youth’s pre-existing knowledge and spark their interest. This p aper delves deeper into this topic, analyzing and drawing design heuristics from  how youth draw upon various spatial, ideational, re lational, and material resources while engaging in athletics as a culturally sustaining pedagogy.

## Methods

Within an after-school community space in a large midwestern city in the US, we visited and facilitated a sportssituated computer science learning experience with youth. Three undergraduate members of our research project assisted in facilitating this act ivity. The data presented in this study focuses on an activity centered on an iPad-based training game called HomeCourt.AI designed fo r sports performance training. Through the iPad app, gameplay videos are automatically created with the game animations ov erlayed. In the session, our five young learners – between ages 10 to 12 – played the volleyball lateral pass drill with the s upport of the facilitators. The volleyball drill was new to all of the participants. As such, we analyze the 20 minutes of play ma de across all of the participants for their embodied interactions and talk as they all learn how to play the volleyball drill. We chose to apply the Tinkering Learning Dimensions Framework (Bevan et al., 2015) as codes for our videos due to how closely this experience resembles  other tinkering-based learning environments. The learning indicators include: Engagement, Initiative and  Intentionality, Social Scaffolding, and Development  of Understanding. With this coding scheme, we highligh t how the participant’s embodied and athletic engag ement is a form of tinkering that utilizes a multitude of resources that the group applies to their actions.

## Findings

Throughout the videos, we noted 16 instances of Eng agement codes, 21 instances of Social Scaffolding, 16 instances of Initiative and Intentionality, 36 inst ances of Development of Understanding. In the follo wing paragraphs, we highlight 2 vignettes.

The first vignette highlights how situating tinkering in an athletic context expands the conception of work and physical connections. The facilitators, Audrey and Antonio, are off-screen and offer suggestions, while Georgia, Ellie, and Iris follow suggestions and try  their own ideas. For example, while Georgia attemp ted to get the point with her head, Ellie tried getting the po int with her legs. Iris joins in, attempting a typi cal volleyball bump. The different approaches pursued by the youth  reflect a unique shape of how their work makes phy sical connections with each other. This combination is a result of the preceding sequence of successes, fail ures, and constant attempts to leverage movements belonging t o traditional volleyball play within the new contex t. While we do not see the specific moves Audrey and Antonio  are describing, their revisions (e.g., “Oh, you know what? It might be like this.”) reflect a dialogue between youth movements and the group’s thinking indicating that some of the movements are in response to their suggestio ns in addition to their own experimentation. This v ignette exemplifies a tight interlinking of almost all aspects of the Learning Dimensions Framework. We see Engagement as well as Initiative and Intentionality reflected in the continued testing of different approaches by the pl ayers, Social Scaffolding in how they are building on faci litators’ feedback and instructions and engaging in complementary approaches, and Development of Unders tanding in their revising of moves as well as the realizations in dialogue from Audrey. By building off each other's movements, the youth were able to further their understanding of the game. Additionally, the facilitators kept offering suggestions but evidently have made space for youth to pursue their own approaches as well. This offers a diversi ty of pathways towards sustained participation – allowing for those willing to experiment to continu e, and for others to join the activity by guided tinkering. This form of social scaffolding created a supportive and nurturing learning environment. The second vignette highlights how this work is cou pled with explicit athletic knowledge – visible in players’ movements, talk, and attempts at making se nse of the technology. In this vignette, Audrey enc ourages the youth to play the game with two people facing each other, similar to how volleyball is played. Audrey suggests that the pair pass the ball back and forth between each other. The group assumes that the game is for two players, bringing their understanding of volleyball passing as a drill that involves two people to practice. Wh en passing the volleyball, Ellie’s and Georgia’s movements use postures common to the sport. Georgia gets the ball first and uses her forearms to pass the ball to Ellie – a common practice in volleyball called bumping the ball. Ellie takes a few steps to get the ball Georgia bumps and catch es it to walk back close to the screen. This excerp t contrasts their other strategies, where the players focus on understanding how to score points over continuously passing the ball – underscoring how their volleyball knowledge and how it  shapes their tinkering approach. The nature of volleyball as a two team game tightly links this ap proach to also activating relational resources, suc h that the players are tinkering together by practicing group engagement and distributed sensemaking.

## Discussion and conclusion

The vignettes highlight the interactions between youth, facilitators, and the app as they make sense of how to play the game. We call this embodied and athletic engagement with technology athletic tinkering. Ideational resources were an integral part in dr iving the youth and facilitator’s athletic tinkerin g. Almost all of their experimentation was based upon their previous experiences and knowl edge of volleyball. We observed the use of ideation al resources in every vignette through bumping, settin g, coaching, and trying to play the game as two tea ms. This demonstrates the significance of designing programs  that build off pre-existing knowledge and experiences in order to teach new skills. It also highlights a uni que strength of activities like sports, which afford play within these structures in generative and non-restrictive ways. We also observed youth approaching this technology with agency and autonomy, often trying out-of-the-box, exploratory movements – in dynamic dialogue with the facilitators and each other. This invitation and space to support persist ence, authorship, and resourcefulness – in social groups including friends, teachers, and fami ly members – is often articulated as a strength of learning through making and tinkering (Brahms, 2014), and is  an important consideration for designing similar a ctivitylinked experiences (Nasir & Cooks, 2009). Our paper focuses on conceptualizing the unique kin d of learning experience that can be fostered when presenting technological sensemaking opportunities that are richly embedded in culturally situated act ivities and spaces. Offering unfamiliar technological tools to enhance athletic play experiences can foster what w e call athletic tinkering  – a new perspective on activity-linked experimentation with technology and computin g concepts. This poses a powerful design and analytical thrust for work that aims to offer identity expanding inroads into complex computing concepts and learning.
