## Introduction

Design education encompasses many fields of study i n higher education from architecture and urbandesi gn to product and graphic design. A central feature of design education is the design studio along with a range of courses that build creativity, technica l skills, attitudes and competencies necessary for the design field. These courses reflect the individual outcome focused nature of higher education which is in dissonance with professional design practice where there is rarely, if ever an individual designer (Faerm, 2023). There is a growing interest in play in higher education in t he past few decades (Whitton, 2022) with increasing research on its benefits with many case studies on ways to incorporate play into the higher  education classroom. Prior work that integrates play and collaboration h as focused on games and gaming and how that can support collaboration. For example, Wang et al (2010) used a ‘knowledge trading game’ to encourage sharing of knowledge and resources in the architecture design studio which they believed to be key to collaborati ve design learning. Multiple studies on play in design educat ion or more broadly design thinking have focused on  game design (Arnab et al., 2019; Cassim, 2020; Lee et al., 2023; Mercer et al., 2017). Play and creativity and its possible links have been well-studied making play a suitable addition to design curriculum (Tsai, 2012) This research involves a case study of an undergraduate Art History course on Museum Marketing with 16 students ranging from freshman to seniors, mostly Arts and Architecture or Liberal Arts majo rs (9 students). The class meets three times a week for an hour. The  typical format of the class involves introduction to various creative tools on Monday followed by weekly or bi-weekly creative assignments due on Friday related to the tool introduced during the lecture-based classes. The play activities were carried out on Wednesday where the activity is linked to the content of the week  for 6 weeks between Week 3 and Week 12. The activities were designed to reflect both the pedagogical structures of playful learning as well as to be problem-solving tasks involving creative making.

Playful learning and collaboration Playful learning emerged in response to the perform ative, risk-averse culture of higher education, emphasizing the “emergence of knowledge through failing, risking, experimenting, wondering, constru cting, and critically reflecting” (Nørgård et al., 2017). Whitton (2018) outlines tools, techniques, and tactics for playful  learning by extending the ‘signature pedagogy’ for playful lear ning which includes surface (game) structures, deep  (play) structures and implicit (playful) structures (Nørgård et al., 2017). Whitton (2022)  describes playfulness as an attitude that includes behaviors like spontaneity, humor, cheerfulness, being uninhibited, open and ex pressive; intellectual creativity, and a willingness to take risk, accept failure and try new experiences.  Collaboration is defined as the process that leads to the ‘convergence of meaning’ (Roschelle, 1992). Schürmann et al. (2024) lists four facets of peer collaboration including “managi ng task and progress, constructing shared knowledge  and solutions, maintaining a functional team, and indiv idual and joint participation” (p. 112).  In this study we focus on playful behaviors and collaborative processes. W e ask, how does play and playful activities influen ce peer collaboration within design education?

## Methods

This paper uses a case study methodology. Case stud ies are useful in revealing the decisions behind an  action, how and why they were implemented and what were the results of the actions (Yin, 2018). Case studies also allow for multiple sources of data and their convergence.  This study collected multiple sources of data incl uding a student interview and instructor interview, student surveys, field no tes, photographs of classroom activities and artifacts, and audio recordings of group discussions during playful activities. Data analysis focuses on one of the playful activities, the ‘Museum of Anything’ activity as all students mentioned it as a playful activity in the surveys a nd interviews. In this activity the students, in groups, create an Instagram promotional handle for a fictional museum of their imagination using OuiSi (1) picture cards as posts. The field notes, photographs and audio recordings of group discussions were analyzed to understand specifically how playfulness overlapped with collaborative processes during the activities.

## Findings

Collaborative processes and playful behavior Audio recordings of discussions of two groups durin g the ‘Museum of Anything’ activity was analyzed for overlaps between collaborative processes and playfu l behavior. Of the four collaborative processes out lined by (Schürmann et al., 2024) the most common process in this activity was constructing shared knowledge and within this facet the collaborative actions were developin g and proposing ideas, expansion on each other’s id eas, deepening and broadening ideas, asking clarifying q uestions as well as making decisions about the solu tions. Playful behaviors that overlapped with this collabo rative process involved spontaneity, sense of humor, being uninhibited and enjoying silliness, being open and expressive, displaying intellectual creativity and willingness to take risks. For example, students spontaneously playing in a VR space they entered together or staying after class to jam together or visit art exhibitions together. However, there is an interesting tension that emerges between creative freedoms that are an essen tial facet of play-facilitated by the absence of norm s for student practice, and rules and classroom level reg ulations that are necessary for collaborative practice that in turn can foster these creative freedoms. This di fficulty was highlighted by the instructor during the interview describing how some of the open-ended play was a struggle as the students didn’t yet know each other as a team o r what roles they would play. This points to the need to move past superficial considerations of play as merely t he absence of rules, and to think of it as a refram ing of the constraints and affordances of specific forms of pe dagogical practice. The interplay of collaboration and play requires a careful consideration of how to pedagogically structure it.
