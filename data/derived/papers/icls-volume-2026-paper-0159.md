# Reflections on Hybrid History and Designs at ISLS Annual Meetings

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper presents a brief history of the hybrid design efforts across recent Annual Meetings of the International Society of the Learning Sciences. As such, this is a non-traditional conference paper. I first report on society history le ading to a commitment from leadership to support slow design innovation through the Hybrid Engagement subCommitte ee. I then share committee guiding purposes that shape virtual and hybrid design efforts. This paper is meant to make hybrid design efforts more transparent and to invite members to contribute to these efforts via committees, volunteer roles, and innovation in their own hybrid symposia design.

This paper is meant to make hybrid design efforts more transparent and to invite members to contribute to these efforts via committees, volunteer roles, and innovation in their own hybrid symposia design. The present paper is a brief history on the development of hybrid designs at the International Society of the Learning Sciences (ISLS) Annual Meetings (AMs). This history captures various  calls for the society to engage with hybridity, and the responses to these calls. As good learning scientists, we are never without our design-eye on our interactions, and the affordances of new designs often lead to rapid spread across LS conferencing events. After several years of inaction prior to the ongoing COVID pandemic, ISLS followed calls from many corners of the society to address the need to reconsider learnings from the virtual-only era of Annual Meetings (2021, 2022) after the vaccine rollout in ways that might inform the return to  in-person meetings that have come s ince. The result was the establishment of a new initiative, embodied in the work of the Hybrid Engagement subCommittee, liaising between the Annual Meeting Committee and the Local Organizing Committees (LOCs) of each Annual Meeting. This is the story of how that work came to happen, and what we have learned in these first few years. The present paper has three goals. (1) T ell a story about needs and concerns, collective action, and iterative design that has shaped developing hybrid approaches at our Annual Meetings. (2) S hare this story to ensure our society has a transparent look at the efforts that impact how we gather to look at our work together. (3) I share this story in the hopes of inviting more members of the society, new and old, to join this important work. A brief history of ISLS virtual and hybrid designs The International Society of the Learning Sciences is a leader in the design and study of learning environment s. The ISLS AMs  are a primary venue for engaging with this research. As such, the design of the meeting itself structures how society members engage with emergent research (i.e., knowledge).  AMs have included combined International Conference of the Learning Sciences (ICLS) and the International Conference on Computer-Supported Collaborative Learning (CSCL) since 2021. AMs are a s ite for presenting  research on learning, opportunities for community building, and seeding innovations that feedsour community in the coming years. The shift from alternating years of ICLS and CSCL to a combined Annual Meeting was  the first major innovation shaping the design of ISLS conferences in some time. Guided by the Annual Meeting Committee (AMC), the shift to AMs allowed for more routine engagement of society members and for the potential for crosscommunity collaboration by bringing together scholars who might otherwise only attend one conference or the other. It should be named that as ISLS continues to grow, the burden of organizing AMs has become untenable, leading to many and diverse behind-the-scenes approaches to distributing organizational efforts across LOCs and ISLS committees. The onset of the COVID-19 pandemic brought yet more  opportunities for innovation (and additional design burdens), shifting AMs to fully-virtual in Bochum 2021 and Hiroshima 2022. These shifts were necessary due to the rampant spread of a disabling virus, offering new challenges (e.g., offering “chance meetings” without being in person, managing time zone challenges across the globe). Yet it also introduced new innovations to attend to those challenges such as Hiroshima’s okonomiyaki competition where ISLS members across the globe were able to try out Japanese recipes and connect with other foodies across the society. Perhaps most importantly during virtual AMs, marginalized scholars who struggled to attend in-person conferences were able to engage in ways similar to those whose needs have always been centered. For example, scholars with caregiving duties did not have to wrestle to find alternative care for days on end, scholars who face harassment and/or assault from TSA agents (e.g., queer, trans, racialized people) did not have to do so to share their research, and disabled/chronically ill scholars did not have to risk their lives to further their careers. Moreover, the society unintentionally addressed prior calls from scholars such as Suárez (June, 2023) to attend to the needs of Muslim researchers  in the face of U.S.-based discussions of a potential “Muslim Ban” (needs that were dismissed as “too expensive” to solve). 1277 COVID left the society with few alternatives but to solve these problems. Early pandemic  gains are important for ISLS as a community to continue developing despite the rush to “return to normal” since the 2021 vaccine rollout. Scholars in  Equitable Participation Working Group put out a call to the ISLS Board and Equity & Justice Committee to resist this inequitable, unjust, unneeded, and largely unexamined “return to normal.” In response to a disastrous AERA 2022 (accommodation to present for which the computer with zoom was left without turning on the speaker, presenters not being given their  links to participate until minutes before or even during their presentation slot, inadequate in-room technology arrangements to support digital participation across modalities, etc.), the Equitable Participation Working Group letter included the following message: We hope to persuade our community to resist the draw to “return to normal” when “normal” has always been deeply inequitable for people with disabilities, marginalized identities, and those navigating persistent circumstances at the intersection of structur al racism and homophobia/transphobia. We take seriously that attending to the most marginalized members of our community is integral to the mission of ISLS and serves all of us. Attendees to AERA 2022 learned quickly that virtual attendance, without attention to design for equity and inclusion, resulted in second-class participation and status; hybrid sessions limited engagement for virtual attendees to listening only (no chat functions in Zoom webinars), and even specially approved “disabled” access often times did not allow conference presenters to speak (microphones were not set up before sessions started) and all special access required “approval” of individuals’ disabilities by AERA administrators. We believe that ISLS cando and must do better in supporting equitable virtual engagement, and that this will require deliberate attention and effort in collaboration with our full diverse community of scholars.  (2022, May) The authors included disabled, chronically ill, queer, trans, environmentally-informed scholars and allies. Montreal 2023 was an AM of firsts. First in-person combined ICLS + CSCL, first back to in-person after quarantine-era, introduction of an accommodations team, etc. This placed a burden on ISLS and the Montreal LOC, one that led ISLS to hire a part-time employee to institutionalize knowledge of how to design and run AMs and support future LOCs. However, Montreal did not have this support and was left to wrestle with extraordinary challenges such as difficulty recruiting committee members to help with collaborative planning during the rush to “return to normal” and associated burnout, the need for new systems and innovation for the first-ever in-person combined AM, ever-increasing conference registration (a 30% increase in Montreal alone), complex navigation of multiple committees and designers’ needs (e.g., career workshops, pre-conference workshops, program chair efforts), a first ever LOC Accessibility & Inclusion Advisory team, and many other challenges. In the face of these challenges, Monreal 2023 drew on earlier in-person design and some fledgling hybrid features. Many aspects of the in-person design efforts were born of an honest desire to bring people together again after the collective trauma of the early pandemic. As LOC co-chair Liz Charles (personal communication, 9/30/25) explained, “We hoped being in-person would catalyze renewed connection, trust, and a shared sense of belonging.” Montreal also introduced limited hybrid options including a $75 registration option to livestream the keynotes. However, for presentations, expectations were set that if scholars could not attend in person, they were to ask a colleague to present on their behalf or send a video-recording to be played by the session chair. Thus, there were early steps that did not yet meet the complexity of needs voiced by the community. The AM in 2023 also brought to the forefront a series of challenges that make hybrid AM features particularly difficult to design.  For instance, there are real challenges due to  the immense work necessary to organize technological arrangements years in advance to properly ensure smooth hybrid access. This adds to the extensive burden already placed on LOCs to attend to a rapidly growing society. Additional challenges were shared from the community such as those that stemmed from the ISLS AM calendar, with the timing of acceptances presenting challenges for those needing visas to visit Canda. D’Angelo (2023, June) shared that ¾ of presenters in her session was unable to attend due to visa processing delays and that many others had shared similar challenges. Other a ttendees highlighted disabled/chronic illness challenges, caregiving challenges, and travel-related safety challenges faced by queer/trans scholars and scholars of color in vibrant social media debates before and during the conference. Organizers rightfully named the complexity and demands of hybrid design, something that was perhaps too much to ask of the first ever in-person combined CSCL and ICLS meeting. In addition to this transitional time, there were calls for innovation grounded in LS perspectives. Requests were made to draw on ISLS extensive expertise in CSCL and ICLS communities. Scipio (2023, June) wrote “why no COMPUTER supported COLLABARATIVE learning” and Vakil (2023, June) wrote “We have to also name the deep irony that a conference that's all about computer-supported collaborative learning apparently wasn’t able to create an acceptable hybrid environment.” I would argue that it was simply a matter of not YET having found 1278 our way to hybrid design. Working towards hybrid innovation requires significant effort, starting with an understanding of our purposes for AMs. Vakhil (2023, June) wrote, “the question of thoughtfully designed hybrid learning environments is a matter of equity and justice” noticing that from the standpoints of both disability and climate justice “there is a clear moral imperative to be significantly more intentional in when, where, and how often we convene in person, and how to authentically include everyone in our community.” I see Vakhil’s call, along with the letter from the Equitable Participation Working Group, as calls for anticipatory design (Doyle et al., 2025) that places the burden of design on the society, not individuals (Evans et al., 2017). Anticipatory designs are those “where learning environments, materials, or systems are created in a way that predicts and addresses potential barriers before learners encounter them ” (Doyle et al., 2025, p. 4). Suárez (2023, June) noted that anticipatory design through centering the needs of Muslim scholars as far back as 2019 might have lowered the burden on Bochum 2021 and Hiroshima 2022 by beginning the work to develop hybrid designs, designs that would have also lowered the burden on Montreal 2023. Despite a slow response from ISLS in the early years of calls for hybrid options, Scipio (2022, October) emphasized that ISLS had  already gained significant knowledge and practice that could inform the work: Yes! We're learning scientists-designers for equity & inclusion. Let's take what we've learned from th e past few years & develop hybrid sessions that maximize affordances & minimize constraints of hybrid technology-rich learning & collaborative environments. This call was taken up that very fall.

In the Fall of 2022, The Hybrid Engagement subCommittee (HEsC) was founded by D Teo Keifert with the aid of the then-AMC chair, and current ISLS president, Yotam Hod. Keifert and Hod, supported by then-president Joshua Danish and the ISLS Board, were responding to these myriad concerns and the reality that many conferences were dropping their virtual and hybrid designs thereby relegating many marginalized scholars once again to the periphery despite having developed adequate capacity to continue innovating. Thus, u nlike conferences that have refused disability access or other hybrid design features (AERA) or have segregated disabled scholars through a virtual-day separate from in-person events (NARST), ISLS chose to explore rich virtual access via an accommodations process while maintaining traditional in-person commitments.  Through HEsC the society is ensuring iterative design work continues to better meet the needs of learning scientists. Hybrid engagement subcommittee purpose and charges The Hybrid Engagement subCommittee was created to attend to several needs of the ISLS society, specifically within the context Annual Meetings. The founding documenting includes four society needs that HEsC meetings (Keifert & Hod, Charge for the Subcommittee on Hybrid Engagement, December 12, 2022). For access to the full document, please email the author. I present an abbreviated form of these charges here:

1) Need for increased capacity: The society needs support to adequately attend to the challenges of

sustainable, equitable annual meeting design.  Adding this burden on to the expectations for Local Organizing Committees (LOC) may make it more difficult to recruit hosts in future years. Therefore the sub-committee will assist and partially offload LOCs with hybrid engagement of the annual meeting and serve as institutional memory.

2) Need for sustainability: The hybrid engagement sub-committee will serve as infrastructure that ensures

sustainability across LOC s. Sustainability requires the society develop stable knowledge systems that can be iterated upon and passed along through a group of people that are stable over several annual meeting designs.

3) Need for wider expertise: We are proposing a structural shift towards iterative new design for meaningful

hybrid engagement. Therefore, including a broader pool of innovators to jumpstart creative reimagination of annual meeting design is vital for the design of annual meetings that  are responsive to the times and the needs of historically/continually underrepresented members of our community.

4) Need for institutionalization: T his sub-committee institutionalizes informal and short-term

collaborations in the ISLS community in a way that can make real, measurable outcomes for the society. This charter speaks to range of challenges and instructional structures to address those challenges. Across the full charter, needs related to supporting ISLS AM increased capacity while maintaining a sustainable work effort on the part of LOCs was a primary concern. Those primary concerns requires an  increasing the pool of innovators shaping these efforts and systems of institutionalization to ensure continued knowledge development across LOCs from year-to-year. The result as a list of charges for the HEsC that include: providing input to the Annual Meeting Committee and LOC s, developing new strategies, generating and maintaining guidelines, coordinating with LOCs, gathering feedback, and contributing to annual reports towards “establishing new practices for cultivating 1279 innovation in hybrid design” (HEsC Charter). These charges have led tonew foundational designs for AMs, grounded in the history of 2021-2022 innovations and drawing from the vast expertise and knowledge within our Learning Sciences community to iteratively design for justice, equity, diversity, and inclusion (IslandWood, n.d.). ISLS annual meeting standard hybrid infrastructure The Hybrid Engagement subCommittee, liaising with the Annual Meeting Committee and each year’s Local Organizing Committee, have slowly expanded our hybrid competencies and imagination. ISLS maintains an in-person gathering to ensure all scholars can leverage their travel funds to attend. HEsC, meanwhile, has coordinated to ensure an easy accommodations process for presenting scholars to engage virtually as needed. The resulting base-line infrastructure for AMs has thus evolved significantly in just the past few years. Centrally, our initial responsive accommodation designs in Buffalo 2024 were based on responding to accommodation requests after the fact, while our current model, successfully piloted in Helsinki 2025, is an anticipatory accommodation process that establishes baseline access as a planned process well before attendees arrive. I present now the current infrastructure to support subsequent exploration of innovative designs from virtual-only years (Bochum 2021 and Hiroshima 2022) through in-person with hybrid accommodations AMs years (Buffalo 2023 and Helsinki 2025). An easy and straightforward accommodations process is at the core of ISLS AM designs. Those in need of accommodations to present remotely only need indicate a broad category for their need. This means that no members of ISLS or LOCs are in a position to “evaluate” those needs, which would be inappropriate and unethical (c.f. AERA 2022 which insisted on reviewing my disabled status through a black-boxed process of evaluation). Instead, ISLS gathers categorical data on society needs (e.g., disability, illness, financial, safety, political/visa) to help track needs over time. Tracking these data also allows ISLS to learn which needs led to remote presentation requests most often and to consider whether alternative designs might better serve those members. For instance, when someone requests a remote presenting accommodation request due to financial concerns, their information is shared with a financial need committee to determine of that scholar might qualify for financial support. Additional designs have been implemented in routine ways to ensure that remote presenters and those registering for virtual attendee access have opportunities for fluid engagement with in-person attendees. Rather than relegate virtual attendees to a “watch and listen” position, these designs have created opportunities for facilitated interactions via community chat features, in-room turn-taking support for online attendees, and opportunities for virtual-focused and hybrid community building events. This includes the incredible efforts of Hybrid Ambassadors (HA), volunteers who sign up prior to the conference help presenters and attendees to join the Zoom presentation space. Furthermore, these HAs serve a critical bridge to ensure that virtual attendees are able to engage via chat with in-person attendees, and that virtual attendees are able to gain the floor during Q&As. Another key shift is the use of Zoom for all presenting, regardless of the local technological arrangement. In Helsinki, this meant setting up host computers in some rooms each day to serve as the local Zoom host, while in other rooms resident computers already served this purpose. The affordances of this shift in norm are vast. First, attendees no longer must  bring an array of dongles or  adjust their screen mirroring settings last-minute to achieve their preferred presentation format (e.g., sharing slides while viewing presenting notes). This reduces the time-burden of switching physical computers and dongles between presenters, instead only needing a few moments to share a screen from a new computer. Finally, the consistent Zoom-presenting format relieves challenges faced by both virtual and in-person attendees who may have vision challenges that make it difficult to view slides from the back of a room. Preliminary, informal reactions from Helsinki attendees indicate relief in having consistent technological arrangements for presentations. The above benefits from the standard hybrid design, however, remain dependent on the available technology infrastructure at the LOC host location.
