# Learning through Chatbot Design: Examining Children’s AI Understanding and Hallucination Awareness

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Young learners increasingly interact with AI chatbots in classrooms and daily life, as educators explore their potential to support learning. While chatbots can personalize instruction and enhance engagement, they also produce hallucinations, which are plausible but incorrect responses. Because such errors arise from the probabilistic nature of generative AI, it is essential to help students develop literacy in recognizing and addressing them. Grounded in constructionist approaches to AI literacy, this study engaged 47 middle school students in designing and testing their own chatbots using a LUMI environment equipped with hallucination detection tools. Through hands-on creation and peer evaluation, students developed a deeper understanding of how AI systems function and where their limitations emerge. Findings show significant gains in AI understanding, hallucination awareness, and students’ ability to propose concrete detection and mitigation strategies. The study demonstrates how we can cultivate critical and reflective engagement with youth through emerging AI technologies.

Children now regularly interact with chatbots in both educational settings and daily life. These systems have shown potential to engage students, personalize learning, and support educators (Kuhail et al., 2022, Riahi et al., 2026). However, they also present a critical challenge, the tendency to generate plausible but incorrect information known as “hallucinations” (Huang et al., 2025). Since these hallucinations may create confusion or spread misinformation (Solyst et al., 2024, Danry et al., 2025), it is essential to equip young learners with skills to detect and address them. Grounded in constructionist learning theory, participatory design offers a promising approach, enabling learners to develop critical perspectives by making design decisions and observing their consequences (Papert, 1980; Hourcade et al., 2023). While recent studies have demonstrated the promise of engaging youth in designing AI applications (Morales-Navarro et al., 2025, Tseng et al., 2023), there is a critical need to examine how this approach can specifically teach about AI hallucinations as a core AI literacy skill. To address this necessity, we engaged 47 middle school students in designing and testing their own chatbots using LUMI  with built-in hallucination detection features. This hands-on approach allowed students to explore how design decisions shape AI behavior and to develop deeper understanding of hallucinations and mitigation strategies. Specifically, this study investigate s the following research question:  To what extent does the Chatbot Building Workshop influence students’ AI knowledge and understanding, awareness of hallucinations, and ability to build trustworthy chatbots? Findings show that combining chatbot design with structured scaffolding significantly improved students’ AI understanding, critical thinking, and awareness of AI limitations.

## Participants

We conducted the IRB-approved study with 47 assenting middle school students with parental consent to participate at two computing summer camps covering block-based programming and data modeling topics hosted by a large public university in the southeastern United States. Participants (N = 47, ages 11-14, M=12.2, SD=1.16) were 54% male and 46% female, and their racial/ethnic composition was 38% Black, 23% White, 19% Asian, 6% Multiracial, 4% Hispanic/Latino, 4% other, and 4% preferred not to say. Chatbot building workshop We conducted a two-session workshop (1.5 hours per session, 3 hours total) to examine how constructionist chatbot design influences students' AI literacy and understanding of hallucinations. We conceptualize AI literacy as a set of competencies for critically understanding, using, and evaluating AI technologies (Long & 1647 Magerko, 2020), aligning with the AI4K12 Big Ideas framework (Touretzky et al., 2023), as understanding hallucinations connects to how AI represents and reasons with data (Big Idea #2), learns from data (Big Idea

#3), and raises ethical accountability ques tions (Big Idea #5). Participants first completed a pre-survey, then

engaged in a 30-minute introductory lesson covering core AI concepts, including how chatbots generate responses and why hallucinations occur, illustrated with real-world examples. The main activity involved a 60-minute chatbot design task using LUMI (Figure 1), a custom development environment that allows students to construct and test their own chatbots. The interface is divided into two panels: construction and testing. In the construction panel, students define their chatbot's identity by specifying its name, role, personality traits, behavioral rules, and information sources (e.g., uploaded documents). In the testing panel, students select an AI model and interact with their chatbot to evaluate its responses. To support critical evaluation of AI outputs, LUMI includes five hallucination awareness features: (H1) a response confidence indicator showing the model's certainty level, (H2) a fact-checking tool that crossuploaded sources informed each response, (H4) a model comparison utility enabling side-by-side testing across different AI models, and (H5) a repeat questioning function to assess response consistency. D uring Session 1 (chatbot creation), student groups were randomly assigned to use LUMI either with or without the hallucination awareness features. In Session 2 (chatbot testing), all students used the full version of the tool while evaluating their own and  peers’ chatbots. Since every participant engaged with these features at some point, no significant differences were found between those who used them from the beginning and those who used them later. Therefore, all data were analyzed together to assess overall learning outcomes and address the research question. Through structured peer testing, students evaluated and refined one another’s chatbots, gaining exposure to diverse design approaches and reflecting on how design choices influence AI behavior. Figure 1 LUMI Environment with Construction and Testing Panels We collected pre-and post-surveys from 47 participants to measure changes in students' AI understanding, hallucination awareness, and confidence in building trustworthy chatbots. The survey was adapted from a validated AI self-efficacy instrument (Wang & Chuang, 2024) and assessed three primary constructs: (1) AI knowledge and understanding (six items, e.g., “I understand how chatbots generate responses”), (2) hallucination awareness (five items, e.g., “I can recognize when a chatbot might be giving incorr ect information”), and (3) confidence in building trustworthy chatbots (five items, e.g., “I candesign a chatbot that provides reliable information”). Each construct used 5-point Likert-scale items (1 = Strongly Disagree to 5 = Strongly Agree) with adequate internal consistency (average Cronbach’s α = 0.72). We conducted pairedsample t-tests to analyze pre-post changes on these constructs. 1648 Beyond L ikert-scale items, we analyzed students’ open-ended responses to assess their ability to articulate concrete hallucination detection and mitigation strategies. For hallucination detection strategies (prompt: “What would you do to check if a chatbot's answer is correct or not?”), we developed a 4-point rubric grounded in prior research on AI literacy (Cohen et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2025): Score 1 (poor) = no strategy or non-response; Score 2 (basic) = generic methods without specificity (e.g., “Google it”); Score 3 (good) = a single, actionable strategy with clear steps; Score 4 (excellent) = multiple actionable strategies demonstrating sophisticated thinking. Two independent coders achieved substantial inter-rater reliability for detection strategies (Cohen's κ = 0.79), with disagreements resolved through discussion. For hallucination mitigation strategies (prompt: “If a chatbot gives wrong responses, what do you think could help it give better ones?”), we conducted thematic analysis to identify and categorize the types of strategies students proposed rather than scoring correctness. Recognizing that hallucinations stem from multiple sources including data quality, training processes, and inference mechanisms (Huang et al., 2025; Chakraborty et al., 2025), we adopted a framework from machine learning literature to code four strategy types: (1) data & source improvement (e.g., using reliable sources, improving training data), (2) system design & training (e.g., adjusting model parameters, retraining), (3) prompt engineering (e.g., refining user questions, adding constraints), and (4) feedback & correction (e.g., user corrections, iterative refinement). Because students could mention multiple strategy types in a single response, we us ed multi-label coding. Two coders achieved substantial inter-rater reliability on the four mitigation strategy types (Krippendorff’s α = 0.77). Paired-sample t-tests revealed significant improvements across all three Likert-scale item-based constructs from pre-to post-survey and hallucination detection strategy scores (Table 1). Before the workshop, 32% of participants (15 out of 47) provided detection strategies categorized as Poor level, which represented 23% of all pre-survey responses. These were responses indicating no strategic thinking (e.g., “I don’t know”, “I use my brain”, “I would google it”), suggesting these students had not yet developed a critical framework for evaluating chatbot responses. After the workshop, none of the students reported having "Poor" AI hallucination detection strategies.  For example, one participant turned their initial response of “I don’t know”, to “I would crosscheck on different sites and compare the answers” in the post-survey. We also observed a noticeable shift towards more advanced level strategies: the proportion of students providing responses rated as Good or Excellent level increased 12.77%, from 63.8% to 76.6%. One participant initially said: “I would find a few online sources of where it gets its information (usually listed) and skim the page to get a general consensus of what info is true and what isn't." This strategy, while recognizing the need for verification and source-checking, was vague and lacked an actionable method for evaluating source quality. Post-study, the same participant’s detection strategy became significantly more specific and actionable, moving beyond “online sources” to name specific, reputable organizations that serve as mental models for authoritative information: “I would look at the sources that the chatbot got its answers from, i.e. Wikipedia, NASA, or Britannica.” The study also demonstrated a noticeable conceptual shift in participants’ open-ended responses on proposed hallucination mitigation strategies. This shift manifested in two ways. First, participants moved from vague to concrete mitigation responses: non-specific responses dropped from 23% to 5%, demonstrating that nearly all participants could articulate actionable solutions for addressing AI limitations after the learning activity. Pre-study suggestions were often brief and positioned the user as the sole agent of change (e.g., “Solve it", “Ask it a different question"). Some responses reflected human-like learning assumptions, sugges ting participants viewed AI systems as capable of learning from user correction similar to human learning (e.g., 1649 “by telling it the right answer that it learns what the correct answer is”). Post-study, learners proposed specific technical modifications, such as “I would adjust the constraints to stop it from hallucinating”. They identified core issues with knowledge sources, suggesting “you could train it to only use reliable sources”. Some synthesized multiple concepts, suggesting to “Ask better questions, input better data, make the pattern more recognizable”. Second, participants shifted from user-focused to system-focused solutions. System Design & Training strategies more thandoubled from 26% to 45%. This indicates that participants increasingly attributed the responsibility for chatbot errors to developer intervention and system design (e.g., “re-program it”, “update its system”, “train it”). Other strategy categories remained relatively stable: user-side approaches (Feedback & Correction and Prompt Engineering) decreased slightly from 36% to 33%, and Data & Source Improvement increased slightly from 15% to 17%. This shift reflects a foundational step toward developing accountability and the role of stakeholders values in AI design (i.e., AI4K12 Big Idea #5; Touretzky et al., 2023). Additionally, participants proposed more diverse mitigation solutions post-workshop. The total number of distinct strategies increased from 54 to 64, suggesting that participants developed a broader perspective for potential mitigation. This reflects stude nts' developing an understanding that AI systems are complex and that hallucinations may stem from multiple underlying causes, consistent with frameworks identifying diverse sources of AI hallucinations (Huang et al., 2025). This pattern suggests that part icipants developed a new understanding of system-level mechanisms while maintaining their existing knowledge about user interaction, recognizing AI systems as modifiable technologies under developer control rather than fixed tools that users must work around or tools that have human-like learning abilities. Individual participant trajectories demonstrate this conceptual development as well. For example, one participant progressed from a non-response “Idk” to explaining that chatbots “give hallucinated answers when they weren't program[med] to know the question if many people [want] to know the answer to the question you can add a topic to that to make the bot right.” Another participant moved from user level suggestion (“I think feedback could help a lot”) to suggesting technical intervention (“I would adjust the constraints to stop it from hallucinating”). These progressions demonstrate participants’ growing understanding of how AI chatbots function, framing hallucinations as technical challenges addressable through system refinements. This study demonstrates that middle school students candevelop sophisticated understanding of AI systems and their limitations through constructionist design activities. By engaging 47 students in designing and testing their own chatbots using LUMI, we observed significant gains in AI understanding, hallucination awareness, and confidence in building trustworthy chatbots. Students progressed from vague detection and mitigation suggestions (e.g., “Google it”) to articulating specific technical modifications, identifying data quality issues, and proposing multiple strategies that reflected accurate mental models of AI.

Our findings provide evidence that constructionist, design-based approaches effectively foster AI literacy. By positioning students as creators rather than passive users, the LUMI bot builder approach transforms abstract AI limitations into tangible problems that students can analyze, debug, and refine through critical thinking. The hands-on design process made visible the trade-offs inherent in AI systems, fostering reflective engagement with how design decisions shape chatbot behavior and trustworthiness. Several limitations highlight opportunities for future research. The lack of differences between interface conditions raises questions about which specific hallucination detection features most effectively support learning. Comparative studies could inform iterative feature design and integration. Additionally, the short workshop duration (three hours) limits our understanding of retention and transfer. Longitudinal studies could examine whether students apply these skills to real-world AI interactions. Gathering more systematic feedback on design affordances and usability will also guide refinement. Finally, gathering systematic student feedback on the hallucination detection and mitigation features would inform iterative design improvements. As young learners increasingly interact with AI systems in educational and everyday contexts, equipping them not only to use these tools but to understand their mechanisms, recognize their limitations, and engage critically with their outputs becomes essential. This study suggests that constructionist learning environments can empower youth to move beyond surface-level use toward critical and creative participation in the design of AI technologies.
