# A Study of the Development of Evaluation Methods for Dialogical Reports in PBL Classes

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study developed methods of evaluating students’ outcomes in remotely conducted project-based learning. “Dialogical reports” which used a hypothetical interview were introduced to encourage reflection on learning outcomes from others’ perspectives. As a result of qualitatively analyzing the questions of dialogical reports, it was found that dialogical reports have the potential to encourage reflection on courses from the perspectives of others.

## Background and Research Purpose

Higher education is adopting project-based learning (PBL), including inquiry-based learning. While the process of project-based learning gives rich experience to students, evaluating the process of what students have learned and the way they learned is difficult (Shirouzu, 2013). In project-based learning, it is important that students inquire a lot of questions. Also, when engaging in problem-solving projects, it is important to see things from the perspectives of others. Ordinary report assignments, however, pose only one question, and are therefore not suitable for evaluating the process of project-based learning. It is necessary to promote the creation of interactive deliverables. We propose “A dialogical report using a hypothetical interview” adopted as the final assignment for the classes in this study. The aim of this study was to  verify whether such reports encourage learners to effectively review their learning  from the perspective of others. To this end, we foc us here upon the questions used in hypothetical interviews, analyzing the types of questions in the dialogical report.

## Method and Analysis

The summer quarter classes of 2021 at Y Faculty of X University were conducted over 7 weeks. They were PBL classes related to learning and design, consisting of lectures, practical activities, and group work (Kolodner, 2002). The classes were inquiry-based, and involved paper prototyping, project activities, and review. About 90 students participated in the classes via Zoom. Six teaching assistants joined, facilitating students’ discussions. A dialogical report with hypothetical characters was used for the evaluation. Writing an account of one’s own learning outcomes, via dialogue with the characters in a hypothetical interview, is called a dialogical report in this study. Such reports are backed by the multi-vocality concept proposed by Mikhail Bakhtin (1981), in which a variety of opinions from differing positions are presented. Three important conditions underpin dialogical repo rts: 1) they are written in a question-and-answer format; 2) the target audience that is expected to read the article is clearly determined; 3) they contain hypothetical characters like interviewer, student, professors, and teaching assistants. The reports were required t o be around 2,000 characters in Japanese. In this study, 82 reports were analyzed based on other assignment submissions. Based on the questions and answers in the hypothetical interview, we analyzed whether they were from the perspective of others (Shirouzu, 2013). The questions in the report were classified as “Facts” or “Recognition”. “Facts” refers to questions addressing the facts of course content, and “Recognition” refers to questions addressing the recognition of the author of the report.

## Results

The average number of characters in the interview s ection of the analyzed reports was 2224.4 (SD = 335.2). In hypothetical interviews, the dialogue of one character is defined as a “unit,” and one conversation is taken as one unit. This definition of a unit is based on the concept of multi-vocality of Shirouzu (2013). The average number of units per report was 22.5 (SD = 8.7). In this study, these units were first categorized as “questions” or “answers.” Also, units that did not fit into the “questions” or “answers” categories, such as filler words and sounds given during a conversation to show comprehension, were categorized as “other.” From a total of 82 reports, 1,848 units were extra cted. The breakdown of the units is as follows: 677 questions, 697 answers, and 474 other units. Among the 677 units categorized as questions, tendencies in 522 questions posed by the interviewer were investigated. ## Discussion

We have focused on the possibility of the dialogical reports encouraging reflection on their learning. Creating the hypothetical interview can help reflection on their  role and viewpoints in the project-based learning.  The dialogical reports acted as a tool that allowed learners to review learning outcomes, while themselves assuming a monitoring role (Miyake 1986) and switching viewpoints with others. However, this study leaves some issues unresolved. We will analyze data such as the discussion process to reveal a relationship between the reports and the learners’ learning processes in the PBL.
