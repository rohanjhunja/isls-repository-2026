# Towards Playful Computational Empowerment - Teenagers Designing Their Learning Environment

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study explores play and computational empowerment, focusing on how design activities support teenagers' computational skills. By integrating play into participatory design workshops, teenagers can ideate, create and test game environments in an age-appropriate and engaging manner. Findings highlight the role of directed and free play, as well as individual and social play. The paper advocates the use of playful computational methods in education to better prepare students for a technology-driven future.

## Introduction and background

Children and teenagers have been included as an imp ortant user group for decades (Druin, 2002). This study explores the role of play in the computational empo werment of teenagers, examining how play emerges in participatory design and how it can be effectively utilized. While computational empowerment emphasizes critical engagement with technology and active participation in its development (Dindler et al., 2020; Iivari et al., 2023), the role of play in this process has remained under explored, particularly among teenagers. Exploring f rom sociological and psychological perspectives, we investigate how play fosters creativity, self-expression, and social interaction in design contexts. Prior research highlights the developmental significance of play (Huizinga, 1955; Vygotsky, 1967). Theorists like Fr oebel, Piaget, and Vygotsky offer key insights  seeing play as central to development,linking it to cognitive processes, and highlighting its role in learning within the Zone of Proximal Development (Froebel, 1887; Piaget, 1962; Vygotsky, 1967). While often applied to young children, these ideas also relate to teenagers. Yet teenage play differs from both childhood and adult play in its performative, exploratory, and identity-forming aspects (Johnston et al., 2023; Tieben, 2015). Johnston  (2023) found that playful learning fosters independence and imagination in teenagers., although imaginative play is considered central to young children’s learning. In childhood play is an active pursuit that stems from children’s natural curiosity and imagination (Erikson, 1977; Froebel, 1887). In the study of Tieben (2015), teenagers used words like 'creativity' and 'imagi nation' to describe the benefits they experienced w hen their teachers used a playful approach to learning,  suggesting that their experience of playful learni ng promotes their creativity. Play can be adult-directed or free, supporting creativity across ages  (Brooker, 2011; Yarnal & Qian, 2011).  Bruce (2012) and Hirsh-Pasek & Golinkoff (2008) argue that adult-led play may hinder spontaneity and imagination. Play is partly an individual, pers onal experience, and it has an individual dimension  (Barnett, 1991, 1991; Lieberman, 1967; Staempfi, 2007). According to Johnston et al. (2023) play is a context for learning” that promotes well-being and relationships, where children express individuality and make connections with their existing knowledge (Proyer, 2011). When talking about social play in t he context of teenagers, it is at least clear that teenagers are social beings forming cliques and crowds and having a strong peer conformity (Tieben, 2015). Children enjoy themselves while being socially acti ve (Goldstein, 2012; Tieben, 2015). Social play, involving peer interactions, enhances well-being, creativity, and skill development (Piaget, 1 962; vygotsky, 1980). It also includes performative activities, fostering engagement and motivation (Kort & IJsselsteijn, 2008; Márquez Segura et al., 2019).  By integrating playful methods int o participatory design, we aim to understand how pl ay supports teenagers’ agencies in shaping technology and devel oping computational thinking. As our research quest ion we ask: “ How play can be utilized in in computational empower ment of teenagers?” So far, play has not been scrutinized in computational empowerment endeavors with teenagers during design, while its value for computational empowerment of young children has already been pointed out  (Tikkanen et al., 2023).

## Research design and empirical findings

This study is part of a multidisciplinary game design project which aims to design and implement a virtual learning environment for teenagers. We organized participatory design and evaluation workshops in st andard classroom environments at three different school and analysed data from eight design activities in the g ame design process to gain insights from teenagers from three differen t classes (7, 8 and 9 graders) aged 13 to 16. When analyzing our data (pre and post-interviews, video recordings, field notes, research diaries, photographs, children’s artworks, crafts and writings) collected from these sessions, we utilized abductive theme analysis (), and combined activities to four main tasks. The first three were carried ou t without the use of technology (unplugged). During  task four, students needed their mobile or used school tablets. ## Conclusion

This study highlights the versatile ways play can enhance computational empowerment in teenagers, highlighting both structured (e.g., paper prototyping) and free play (e.g., sensitizing tasks), as well as individual (e.g., element and character design) and social play (e.g., gr oup design and evaluation tasks). Despite some cont ention around adult-led play   (Bruce, 2012; Hirsh-Pasek & Golinkof, 2008; Johnston et al., 2023), our  study shows that intentional play integration in the design process is beneficial. Free play naturally emerges among te enagers, offering significant benefits like improved psychol ogical well-being and increased creativity  (Staempfi, 2007). Playful activities are effective, hands-on, and foster independence  (Johnston et al., 2023)  and social interaction (Bruce, 2012; Hirsh-Pasek & Golinkof, 2008; Kort & IJsselsteijn, 2008; Tieben, 2015). We propose a framework for incorporating play into participatory design wi th teenagers, emphasizing the value of individual a nd social play in both structured and free forms. This framework can guide designers in engaging teenagers through playful activities, ultimately enhancing learning and design outcomes. Overall, based on the results of our study, we wish to remind that also teenagers are children and that children’s ideas in design need to be valued, their solutions can be more playful, funny and hilarious than adult gen erated. For fostering of play, explorative and hand s-on activities are suggested for the design sessions as well as warm-up games playful activities to awaken imagination and sensitizing tasks which support free play. Base d on our workshops, we also propose forgetting adults' sedentary and inactive design, and add playful movement and embodiment to the design, stating as well that high expectations like supporting teenagers to be active designers leading to better learning and design outcomes
