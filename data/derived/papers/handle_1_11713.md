# Spirituality and Linguistic Plurality in Learning: Centering Wholeness on the Land with Refugee Learners

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Children’s wholeness, particularly for racialized, multilingual refugee learners, is often fragmented in institutionalized educational spaces. This work highlights spirituality as a vital, yet often silenced, dimension of learning. Within a co-designed land-based program, educators and children collaboratively created a multilingual poster that honored diverse spiritual and ecological connections to soil. This pedagogical moment illuminates the transformative potential of centering children’s languages, spiritual knowledges, and relational ties to land in educational spaces.

## Introduction

Children embody a wholeness that is often silenced,  fragmented, or stripped away within institutionali zed education systems grounded in Eurocentric epistemol ogy, ontology, and axiology. For racialized, multilingual refugee learners, their complex and layered relatio nal selves—deserving of recognition and care—are frequently left at the margins of institutional learning space s (Harper & Kayumova, 202 2). In our work, we understand honoring wholeness as an act of pedagogical care that embraces all facets of a child: their languages, knowledges (spiritual, ecological, and cultural), and connecti ons to the land. This poster focuses on one entangled dimension of such wholeness: spirituality. Spirituality, a key aspect of the ways we come to know, see, and interact with the world, is often silenced in public educational spac es (Oraby et al., 2024). Nested in fears of disrupting the perceived neutrality of learning spaces, spiritual connections are often, based on our observations, to be left at the door of learning spaces, especially conversations related to “science.” This practice ultimately strips wholeness in both implicit and explicit ways. Aligning with justice-oriented pedagogies in teacher education, our work offers an episodic glimpse into what emerges when children’s voices and spiritual knowledges are honoured along with facilitators’ voices and spirituality  (Kayumova & Dou, 2022;  Morales-Doyle, 2017; Vossoughi et al., 2023). Situated within a co-designed land-based program, Soil Camp (https://www.soilcamp.ca), we selected an episode wherein Sophia (Author 1) as a teacher/researcher facilitated conversations around linguistic wholeness and the enactment of what she named “whole languaging hearts ” pedagogy. The whole languaging hearts pedagogy sought to disrupt the dominance of English monoling ualism by centering children’s and educators’ subju gated languages, intergenerational ecological knowledges, and spiritual ways of knowing. Foregrounding hearts reflects a shift toward understanding language as living, relational, and spiritually held—carrying affective, ancestral, and sacred ties to land, on e another, and what we hold most dear. This pedagogy illuminated the deeply intertwined nature of spirituality in how we come to know, see, and be with soil and its entanglement with language and body. This transformative moment unfolded as Sophia shared the Arabic words, أرضand ﺗﺮابand some Islamic spiritual connections with soil—words and teachings that she holds close to her heart. The pedagogy was sparked by the offerings of Liana Wolf Leg, a Blackfoot knowledge keeper and educator in Soil Camp team when she shared her ways of languaging connected to the land. Moving into a whole group sharing, educators and children collaboratively co-created a multilingual whole language heart poster that invited their sharing and honored their diverse ways of knowing, seeing, and being with soi l (see Figure 1). This poster asks: How can educators attend and engage in the spiritual dimensions of learning, honoring the wholeness of themselves and children?

## Methodology

Aligned within the methodological orientations of t he larger Soil Camp  project, we draw from social design research methodology (Gutiérrez & Jurow, 2016) and are also guided by Indigenous ways of knowing and decolonizing methodologies (Tuhiwai Smith, 2012). T he greater project centers ecological resilience and linguistic equ ity through our multi-year collaborations at a 30-acre urban farm where refugee children, transdisciplinary educators, and researchers co-design learning spaces to (re)connect and learn together about soil, plants, and animals along with key concepts in Science, Technology, Engineering and Mathematics (STEM) and socio-environmental justice (Takeuchi et al., 2021; 2024). Our work seeks to see how we can create opportunities for rich, relational moments and learning to emerge, grounded in the lives and worlds of children (Balla rd et al., 2023; Tan & Faircloth, 2023; McDaid Barry et al., 2023). Out of the larger data set, we selected a smaller sub-set of data (totalling 30 hours  of video data) connected to the design and pedagogy related to lin guistic wholeness. Drawing from the perspective of desettling interact ional analyses (Krishnamoorthy et al., 2021), w e focused on how embodied and linguistic practices supported such (re) connection while challenging dominant linguistic norms. Through iterative, collective video analysis, we examined how the larger pedagogical design exp anded opportunities for the sharing of relational and spiritual ways of knowing. For the purpose of this poster, we selected one illustrative episode that highlights the emerge nce of the whole languaging hearts pedagogy through  crossreligious and linguistic connections to soil.

## Findings and contributions

The following  interaction arose as part of co-creating a poster that showcased diverse languages for the soil (Figure 1). Two friends, Hana, a Yazidi girl-identifying child, and Amal, a Muslim Syrian girl-identifying child, shared with Sophia and Miwa (Author 2) on how embracing their whole languaging heart has supported them in (re)connecting spiritually on the land. Hana explained to Sophia and Miwa that the word for soil from her heart directly translated to God in Kurmanji. Amal followed, sharing her word ﺻﻼة,meaning prayer in Arabic. When asked about the significance of these words and their connection to soil, Amal described how prayer provided her with feelings of security, grounding and connection to n ature across time and place. Hana also spoke of feelings of grounding, turning to and connecting with God especially during periods of loss, with soil and trees playing a crucial role in this (re)connection. This moment highlights a cross-religious bond rooted in the spiritually meaningful words these children associated with soil.

Motivated by a commitment to unsettle the privilegi ng of Western science and challenge the “anthropocentrism in Western knowledge systems” (Ba ng et al., 2012, p. 312), and grounded in the commu nity garden space, this poster illustrates how Sophia, driven by a “deep yearning for wholeness” (Alexander, 2002, p. 99), engaged pedagogically with the spiritual dimen sions of learning —within herself and with racialized, multilingual refugee learners  and facilitators. Anchored in a vision of restoring connection and wholeness disrupted by forced displacement, faith-based persecution, and Eurocentric education system s (Battiste, 2013), this work centers the “full-humanities, rights, dignities, self-determination, and self-expression” of all participants in the land-based space (Harper & Kayumova, 2022, p. 11). The emergent moment of “whole languaging hearts” sought to embrace the onto-epistemological multiplicity of all in the space, showcasing the collective possibilities of inviting children to bring their often-silenced languages and spiritual connections to the soil. This work disrupts the dominant disciplinary view of soil, instead illustrating the relational dimensions lovingly and caringly held by these children, rooted in their lives and worlds. T he findings highlighted a poignant moment of cross-religious and cultural connection as Hana and Amal shared the ir words, stories, and connections. This raises the  question of what potential lies in cente ring these subjugated onto-epistemologies and what larger pedagogical work is needed to embrace this dimension of knowing, learni ng, and being —toward eco-and socially-just educational realities. Honoring children’s voices and spiritual knowledges  in social design research highlights its transformational potential—not only for its participants but also in terms of the axiology guiding such research. This approach prompts important questions about the type of learning such work aims to support and its ultimate purposes, grounding it as relational, emergent, and nurturing of wholeness through the land.
