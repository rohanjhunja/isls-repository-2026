# Culturally and linguistically “Blind” or Biased? Challenges for AI Assessment of Models with Multiple Language Students

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Investigating AI's role in educational assessments, this study compares AI-provided and teacher scores of hand-drawn scientific models by Multilingual Language Learners (MLLs) in elementary classrooms. Using Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN) for scoring, we aligned AI assessments with those of experienced teachers. The results show moderate agreement (Kappa = 0.326), with AI favoring mid-range scores, while teachers provided a broader score spectrum. This suggests AI's consistency may miss the interpretive nuances teachers offer. The study emphasizes careful AI integration to support the diverse assessments of MLLs, though it notes the limitations of a small sample size and the opaque AI scoring rationale. Our findings advocate for combining AI's analytical strengths with teacher expertise to enhance equitable, effective educational assessments.

Rationale Today's science education landscape is increasingly  characterized by an emphasis on collaborative and communicative practices. This evolution signifies a profound shift towards viewing learning as a dynam ic social process, deeply intertwined with scientific exploration itself. Central to this transformation is the role of language as a fundamental tool for meaning-making and learning. As we embrace these promising pedagogical shifts, the rise of Artificial Intelligence (AI) in educational  settings presents new challenges and possibilities. AI tools are increasingly used in classrooms, primarily as tutor ing systems to assist with grading and other repeti tive tasks, thereby reducing teachers' workloads (Li et al., 2023a). However, the application of AI in education, particularly for assessing student work, raises concerns about p erpetuating existing educational disparities. Studi es indicate that AI assessments can inadvertently introduce bia ses, particularly affecting MLLs (Li et al., 2023b). Empirical research shows that machine learning algorithms may not be as accurate in scoring responses from MLL students compared to their English-dominant peers. This leads us to investigate the congruence between AI and teacher assessments of hand-drawn scientific models from MLL students. We postulate that teachers, informed by their understanding of the classroom's linguistic dynamic s and students' cultural backgrounds, may offer  analysis that differ from those provided by AI. We aim to understand the potential biases in AI grading models and the crucial role of teachers in interpreting student work, part icularly in the context of language and cultural di versity in the classroom. This comparison is vital in assessing the equitable deployment of AI in education and ensuring that its integration complements rather than compromises the inclusivity and effectiveness of science education.

## Theoretical framework

Science education has shifted to develop student knowledge-in-use proficiency  through doing meaningful scientific endeavors, by trying out emerging ideas to solve problems with others. Scientific modeling is a highleverage practice to engage students in science lea rning (Li et al., 2024). Despite its’ importance, constructing scientific models challenges young students and tea chers (Li et al., 2021). This is partially due to the  challenges of analyzing complex and diverse student models, which is time-consuming. A parallel shift of science education has occurred with understanding of how students learn language by using meaningful language to do science with others. In this way, students, including those labeled MLLs, develop language for the purpose of creating meaning in situ, as they work with others to solve problems and ex plain phenomena. The teachers and students that developed the models are best positioned to make se nse of the models.  However, teachers, mostly English dominant speakers, are likely to view the students who are labeled as MLLs through a deficit lens. Teachers may expect less of students labeled MLLs and assess students according to those expectations. Although it is possible AI reflects similar biases of the programmer, an algorithm may be able to interpret student models without being biased by prior information and expectations of individual students, irrespective of past scores, and home language. This research explores: Do AI and teachers analyzing of MLL students’ hand-drawn scientific models consistently? If not, how are teachers scores of MLL students’ hand-drawn scientific models different from AI’s scores? ML-PBL curriculum materials integrate 3D learning and the features of PBL to meet a set of NGSS performance expectations, which results in a critical generaliz able approach for knowledge-in-use. Holistic rubrics were also designed to measure students’ level of knowledge-in-use objectively and reliably through a 4-point scale. Item response theory was used to validate the quality of  all items (Li et al., 20 24). In this study, we focus on th e modeling task sq_v3 for 3rd graders. We employed data augmentation techniques on training i mages during the algorithm training process, which means randomly erasing and changing input images' brightness, saturation, and so on. Our analytic process commenced with the retrieval of machine-generated scores for the students' models within our testing sample, which consisted of 207 i ndividuals. From this cohort, we identified a subse t of 24 students who were designated as MLLs. Utili zing the models created by these MLL students, we e stablished a focused dataset intended for further analysis. We then engaged three expert teachers, each possessing exte nsive experience in delivering the relevant unit to MLL students, to undertake the scoring of these models. Prior to the scoring process, these teachers underwent a rigorou s training program to ensure understanding of the s coring criteria. The scoring commenced only after achievin g a satisfactory level of interrater reliability. To address the RQ, we apply a weighted Cohen’s Kappa to evaluate the cons istency of the scores between the teachers and the AI for MLL students’ models. Then, we closely examined and reported the score distributions for both the teachers and the machine across the 4-point scoring scale. This was done to discern and d etail the scoring patterns and to pinpoint any discernible differences between the two scoring entities.

## Findings

We calculated the accuracy of human-machine scoring agreement (HMA) for both the training and testing stage. The averaged validation accuracies across folds in the training stage range between 38.31% and 64.12%. The data augmentation technique significantly improves the scoring performance,  resulting in 62.32% +/ -1.63% scoring accuracy. To evaluate the consistency between AI and teacher s coring of MLL students’ hand-drawn scientific models, the agreement was measured using the weighted Kappa statistic, yielding a value of 0.326. This indicates only a fair level of consistency between AI and tea cher scoring methods. While this suggests that ther e is some agreement, it is not strong enough to concl ude that the scores are entirely consistent. The me an scores from AI were slightly lower (M = 1.71, SD = 0.75) compared to those from teachers (M = 1.75, SD = 0.99), thoug h the difference was not statistically significant as sho wn by the paired samples t-test (p =.824). AI scoring showed a concentration of 62.5% at the mid-range score of 2, indicating a potential tendency t owards a central tendency bias. T eacher scores were more evenly spread across the sc oring scale, which may reflect a more nuanced interpretation of the models (see Figure 5).

## Discussion

This study contributes to a critical examination of  the role of AI for education, questioning the status quo and encouraging a progressive dialogue on the development of equitable and effective assessment tools. By continuing to scrutinize and refine the AI assessment of compl ex constructs, we can foster an educational environ ment that not only recognizes but also amplifies the diverse voices, language practices and abilities of all students. In these environments, instead of students learning to use m odalities in ways that AI can be trained to interpret as knowledge, AI training is analyzed and improved by input from knowledgeable local language users, such  as teachers, parents, and students, to better accommod ate diverse knowers. This study echoes the need for a careful approach to integrating AI in educational assessmen t, particularly for the diverse and complex respons es characteristic of MLL students (Li et al., 2023b). The findings suggests that while AI scoring can offer consistency, it may lack the depth of interpretation that experi enced teachers have. Such disparities underscore the crucial consideration required in rubric design, as it can influence the analysis of MLL students' work.  This study has several limitations, including the relatively small sample size and require more transparency in the AI scoring process. O ur results highlight the importance of incorporatin g experienced educators in the development and training of AI systems. Teachers with rich backgrou nds in working with MLL students can provide AI with the contextual understanding needed to better reflect the diverse performances of students. This collaboration between human expertise and machine efficiency could yield scoring systems that are not only aligned with teac her expectations but also conducive to timely instructional decision-making.
