# Organizing Outreach for Cultural Transformation: The Design of a STEM Education Learning Pathway

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This case study leverages the Nasir et al. (2020) learning pathways framework to examine the relationship between learning environments, activities, roles and responsibilities, as well as social interactions taking place in a collegiate STEM outreach program. Using observational, survey, and interview data collected from three groups of instructors and learners in both university and high school settings, we identify how the course participant structure and distributed system of activities helped to engender university students’ participation, identification, and transformation. We highlight how students embarked on a transformative identification process with STEM fields and broader intellectual identities as they undertook multiple roles and responsibilities in different learning sites and timescales. Our study suggests an innovative model to enhance the design and enactment of university-based outreach efforts to support alternative, culturally transformative pathways in STEM.

## Overview and significance

Like many STEM fields, engineering seeks to attract a diverse population into the field. But becoming an engineer is complicated and dehumanizing endeavor for many i dentity groups, who experience being marginalized a nd pushed out (Adams et al., 2011; Stevens et al., 200 8). Cultural change is needed to create more just, equitable, and humane learning environments. In this paper we examine two linked environments—one in higher education and STEM education settings in rural high schools. Rural US settings are under-resourced and under-researched (Lavalley, 2018), and have challenges in recruiting teaching staff (DeFeo & Tran, 2019), and in preparing students for engineering (DeUrquidi, 2019). We describe the organization of a university outreach program based  in a college of engineering, who partners with high scho ol STEM teachers. This program has tools and cultur al practices to support learning pathways (Nasir et al., 2020) for two groups: (1) higher education gradu ate and undergraduate engineering students (whom we call “mentors”) interested in educational outreach; (2) high school students taking STEM education courses (whom we call “students”). This paper focuses on the university mentors.

## Literature review and conceptual framing

The program utilizes a project-based learning (PBL) approach that seeks to promote students learning by doing as they collaboratively pursue endeavors that are a uthentic to them and to disciplinary practices, whi le teachers facilitate students’ investigations (Boardman et al., 2021; Miller & Krajcik, 2019). We employed Nasir et al.’s (2020) learning pathways conceptual framework to guide our analysis. Learning pathways are culturally organized trajecto ries of positioning, participation, and identificat ion. The framework advances two key ideas: a) how access, resources, and experiences of learning, while multidimensional and intersectional, are socially and institutionall y driven and b) how learning as a process of naviga ting and interacting with cultural practices and values alwa ys evolves in relation to identities. The framework  takes as a unit of analysis cultural activities, encompassing practices, interaction, artifacts, and objects that support how an individual is “socialized into navigating and understanding” their learning and development (p. 205). We applied three characteristics of learning from the framework: (1) how identification is based on relationality, affect, and motivation, (2) how learning is accomplished through cultural practices and routines distributed across persons, time, and space, and (3) how privilege and marginalization are enacted within structures. To understand how learning is accomplished through cultural practices and rou tines distributed across persons, materials, time, and space, we utilize the  concepts of participant structures, activity struc tures, and hybridization. Philip (1972) described participant structure as the organization of roles and responsibilities in a learning environment, which is one aspect of divisi on of labor (Engeström, 2001) widely taken up in le arning sciences (Sandoval, 2014). Within a participant structure, role assignment influences learners’ social, linguistic, and cognitive development (Herrenkohl & Guerra, 1998). Distributed activity structures delineate sequences of activities, milestones, and artifacts, all tying to disciplinary goals (Polman, 2004); this perspective describes how students appropriate the tools they use during activities as their own, thus learning and developing new identities, under the guidance of teachers (Polman, 2004). In hybridized learning, people carry out hybrid cultural (Gutiérrez et al., 2019) and boundary-spanning (Buxton et al., 2005) practices. Examples of successful hybridized learning ICLS2022 Proceedings 1157 © ISLS space include the Fifth Dimension (Cole & The Distr ibuted Literacy Consortium, 2006) and related effor ts (DiGiacomo & Gutiérrez, 2017) which have bridged un iversity teacher education and learning sciences coursework, and many more in museums, libraries, and communities.

## Context and Methods

This analysis is drawn from a university-based science and engineering outreach program, AirProject (all proper names are pseudonyms), in a mountain west US state. The collegiate course is in an engineering department at a large US public university which serves almost 33,000 students in its graduate and undergraduate programs; 70% identify as white, 13% receive federal Pell grants, and 30% take out loans (NCES, 2020). The high school students served by this program are in 9 rural schools and are 71% white and 52% economically disadvantaged; 40% would be first generation college students (Colorado Department of Education, 2020). Mentors’ learning is structured into a yearlong course, offered to both undergraduates and graduates. The fall focuses on (1) technical objectives around air quality science and engineering, (2) scientific communication, and (3) mentoring skills. Specifically, mentors con duct a small research project of their choice, in w hich they pursue air quality questions in their local environment; collect data with a monitoring device (yPod); analyze the data and communicate results. They also practice teaching. In the spring, with support from university instructors and partner teachers, mentors guide HS students through a three-month air quality monitoring project. They meet once a week with instructors and classmates to reflect, discuss, and strategize teaching tips and problem solving. Study participants include 12 mentors enrolled in the AirProject course, 3 university course instructors, and 3 high school teachers. 7 mentors identified as female and 5 male; 10 mentors identified as white, 1 Hispanic or Latinx, and 1 white and Asian American. High sch ool classroom activities involved 86 students. Our data is drawn from three sources: 1) classroom observation notes; 2) mentor surveys; and 3) a focus group interview with 12 mentors at the end of the spring. We coded these data using the conceptual framework described above, along with axial emergent sub-themes within each category (Corbin & Strauss, 2019).

## Findings

The AirProject participant and distributed activity structure prompted mentors to conduct air quality research throughout the fall and then teach these content and skills to their high school students during the s pring. Thus, mentors have opportunities to rotate among differen t conventional and unconventional roles within a fa miliar curriculum. We identified two key themes: a) how th e AirProject learning ecology shaped mentors’ cultu rally transformational learning pathways and b) how mentors enacted learning as identification and becoming. The learning ecology shapes culturally transformational learning pathways Learning with multiple, responsive instructors and supportive high school teachers. In this small-size, learnerfocused university course, the instructors worked together and supported one another in facilitating the lessons as well as responding to student inquiries. Ava appreciated “different angles that each instructor brought to the table.” Ava’s impression was concurred by all mentors. Participant structures incorporating multiple instructors are not novel, but it was notable how the three instructors, each with unique expertise, worked together to fa cilitate a multi-level scientific and engineering learning environment. In addition to appreciating the learning experiences in their university-based class, mentors reflected on their interactions with the high school teacher.  Due to COVID-19, mentors had to call in for remote teaching while their partner teachers were in the room with students. Teachers skillfully helped mentors to stimulate stu dents’ learning through compliments, encouragement,  and lighthearted jokes. Lucas said: “Our teacher was ph enomenal… knowing the students, he would say … ‘Thi s person is probably going to have good work, we can volunteer them to go as an example.” In between les sons, teachers were the main source of immediate assistance for students. They coordinated a schedule to lend out yPods and helped answer questions and troubleshoot. While  leaving mentors autonomy to execute their lesson p lans, teachers modeled productive, engaging communication.

Learning with multiple purposes. What was unique in the design of AirProject was tha t as mentors participated in the course, they had just-in-time l earning to facilitate teaching HS students. Mia not ed, “I didn't know very much about [air quality engineering] going into the course, so a lot of what we ended up tea ching to the students I'd learned like a month or so beforehand … [I] immediately transferred that knowledge to someone else.” Positioned as a mentor, Mia not only noted informat ion relevant for lesson plans, but also handpicked materials that she believed to be engaging for students, drawing from her personal experience with those materials. Ben, Lucas, and Sophie recognized how they took on facilitating, scaffolding, and mentoring roles the ways that mirrored what their instructors did in the fall. In our observations, we frequently corroborated the synergy between the university-based instructors’ guidance and how mentors positioned themselves as facilitators in high school ICLS2022 Proceedings 1158 © ISLS classrooms. Mentors carried themselves more like “k nowledgeable partners” as opposed to merely claimin g a position as authoritative sources of knowledge (Tabak & Baumgartner, 2004). Mentors’ participation in the AirProject reflected how they motivationally, relationally, and affectively (Nasir et al., 2020) operated in a system of learning pathways generative to their identity development. Like many of her peers who came to the course without much fa miliarity with air quality, Amelia pushed herself t o study unfamiliar concepts and terminologies. Similarly, Noah was motivated to further his curiosity; he explained how he appropriated previous knowledge of scientific re asoning and sensemaking to his AirProject experienc e. Mentors also noticed generative relational patterns  in their participation. Noah described how the col laborative mindset and the “peer community” learning experience rippled into the HS classrooms; he detailed how mentors rotated responsibilities, so they had one person focusing on the main curriculum and the other more “focused on managing time and interactions” and served as “the backup on curriculum topics and content.” As Ava ta ught, she related more to her professors’ work, and found herself willing to pay more attention in class to reciprocate the professor’s effort.  Mentors were transitioning from positions of novice learners to more knowledgeable peers; they appropriated relevant skills to advance disciplinary proficiency and at the same time actively moderated their own participation. Finally, at the end of the year mentors discussed emotion and affect. Emma described her overwhelming “imposter syndrome” and “lack of confi dence” had made her feel the experience was at firs t “terrifying.” Emma then noted improvements in how she handled questions and facilitated learning each time she came back to visit her students, which was “affirmi ng,” as was seeing her students skillfully presenti ng their projects. Other mentors concurred with “joy” and “pride” in seeing students present their research. Learning as identification and becoming Eleven of 12 mentors self-identify and are socially  positioned as white, but the focus group interview  revealed their non-monolithic identity positions. Working alongside peers and mentees prompted mentors to revisit their personal and social awareness. Liam recalled that as a “first-generation student, … without my middle school and high school teachers finding me to be excelling in math and science, I probably would never [have] bec ome interested in engineering.” Emma noted that “as a h igh school student I didn't think engineering was a n option due to [my] lack of confidence.” A student from a rural part of the state and a student whose pathway was through the community college system shared similar sentime nts. These examples characterize how mentors’ learn ing pathways included enactment of both privileges, for  having racially privileged backgrounds and enrolli ng in a predominantly white institution and discipline—ones  that persistently favored their racial identity—and marginalization, ones defined by their first-generation status, gender, geographic location, and age. Mentors’ self-identifications situated how they ide ntified with mentoring and role modeling. Mia was adamant, “I do see myself as a role model because I came from a similar background as many of these kids, and now as a college student, I feel like I could be an example of where hard work can get you.” Meanwhile, Emma saw mentoring and role modeling in her students connecting with her through the ex perience of “someone who doesn’t have all the answers, because I think that feeling of not knowing all the answers is relatable,” and referred to her interactions with students as a way to toget her disrupt stereotyping in their STEM pathways. Mi a’s identification reveals how notions of relationality  can be blended in the dominant discourse of merito cracy. Emma’s identification reflects a more holistic and reflexive imagination, emphasizing that mentoring is as much about building relationships and connection with students as sharing domain-specific knowledge. Mentors’ transition of roles, engendered by the Air Project activity structure, contributed to their emergent identities. We noted changes in how mentor s saw themselves as STEM persons. Reflecting on the impacts of the AirProject experience on their STEM identities, some mentors shared an enhanced sense o f confidence in educational and career trajectories i n becoming an engineer. Participation in the AirPro ject prompted mentors to reconsider commitment for becom ing a STEM educator, which for many of them is a renewed learning pathway. Charlotte is “thinking a lot more creatively about what I could do with my degree that might involve education and STEM together.” As ment ors participated in learning and teaching air-quali ty subjects in a collaborative, transformative context, they forged new identities, focusing on being abl e to “build shared understanding of what is happening and what will happen next” (Nasir et al., 2020, p. 198).

## Discussion

Despite the strong interest that universities, faculty, and schools have in outreach and engagement, it is not simple to organize or to effectively implement. Our study has implications for how educators could employ innovative participant and activity structures in designing for productive STEM learning pathways, and consequently, ways to explicitly enhance the design and enactment of u niversity-based educational outreach efforts. Our r esearch contributes to describing how such systems can be m utually supportive of the various participants and the roles they need to play. The AirProject participant-activity structure provides an ecology that supports the learning and ICLS2022 Proceedings 1159 © ISLS development of participants along multiple pathways across roles and sites. University students learned air quality science and monitoring technology engineering; they  also had the opportunity to learn how to teach wit hin a context highly supported by university instructors and high school teachers. Mentors’ learning pathway s were situated in multiple sites, as they moved across university classes, self-selected sites for data collection, and high school settings. This ecology provides the time, place, context, scientific tools, and milestone events for evolving interactions, inquiries, and practices, all of whic h facilitated mentors’ developmental pathways. Besi des its benefits in concurrently scaffolding STEM learning for two communities of learners, the AirProject also prepares for a rippling effect of role modeling (Hinojosa, 2018). University instructors acted as role models to mentors as more advanced engineers and experienced educators; mentors acted as role models to high school students; and teachers acted as role models to mentors as educato rs. The design of this distributed activity system across university and rural high school settings provides one example of how learning pathways can be support ed to provide more equitable access and participation in engineering, a field that has historically marginalized many.
