## Overview

Grounded in the conference theme of "partnering with purpose", this full-day workshop responds to a growing need among Learning Scientists engaged in Research–Practice Partnerships (RPPs) to better understand how to capture and scale the local, practice-based knowledge that drives meaningful innovation. Many RPP teams at ICLS and beyond have been grappling with how to document, analyze, and share such knowledge in ways that recognize teachers’ voices and support collective learning. This workshop invites participants to engage in a hands-on exploration of how stories of practice can serve as a bridge between local knowledge and scalable, crosspartnership learning. Prior to the conference, participants will be invited to collect identity-oriented stories of practice from teachers in their partnerships, using a dedicated, ethically approved AI-based chatbot that we will provide. This chatbot, previously validated as an effective elicitation tool, guides teachers in crafting reflective narratives that surface their experiences, challenges, and insights about implementing or scaling innovations. A short pre-conference online session will be offered to introduce the tool and demonstrate its use. During the workshop, about 10 to 30 participants will join the organizing team to analyze these stories through collaborative reflection supported by another ethically approved AI-based chatbot. This second chatbot is designed to assist in linking stories of practice with pedagogical design principles within INSIGHTS, a digital environment that supports bottom-up knowledge building and serves as a shared repository for reification artifacts emerging across partnerships (Kali et al., 2024). Working in thematic groups led by organizing team-members, participants will first examine how such stories reveal, refine, or extend design principles and how this process deepens understanding within their own partnerships. A collective discussion will then address how linking stories and design principles, through AI-supported tools, can promote upscaling of educational innovation across and within RPPs. The discussion will also address the curation of the design principle repository within INSIGHTS, considering issues of selection, representation, and ethical authorship. Discussants will offer integrative commentary connecting workshop insights to the broader legacy of design-based research within the Learning Sciences. Our ultimate goal is to create a scholarly community around this practice-one that may later develop a s ymposium and/or a special journal issue on how stories of practice that are connected with research-based design principles can foster inclusive learning within and across RPPs aiming to deepen and scale educational innovation. Importantly, the AI tools used in this process, particularly the chatbot for story elicitation, were developed and implemented under approved ethical protocols. These tools ensure full privacy and transparency: no conversational data are stored, shared, or used for training large language models. Teachers retain full ownership of their narratives and explicitly approve which stories may be shared. Stories may be written in participants’ native languages and, when needed, translated through a secure, locally managed process that ensures data privacy and fidelity of meaning. The stories themselves are not anonymized, allowing teachers’ professional contributions to be acknowledged, while any pre-approval exchanges are permanently deleted and remain inaccessible to researchers or third parties. This design reflects our ethical commitment to participant agency, informed consent, and responsible data stewardship within RPP-based research.

## Theoretical background and significance

Efforts to upscale educational innovation—to move beyond isolated projects and generate sustainable impact— require more than replicating successful interventions. Research identifies five interrelated dimensions of upscaling: spread, depth, sustainability, shift in ownership, and evolution (Coburn, 2003; Clarke & Dede, 2009; Law et al., 2015; Law et al., 2021). Spread refers to expanding the use of an innovation across classrooms or institutions; depth involves changes in pedagogical beliefs and practices; sustainability concerns maintaining the innovation over time; shift in ownership emphasizes practitioners’ leadership; and evolution highlights adaptation and refinement across contexts. These dimensions underscore that upscaling is not only about growth in quantity but also about the quality and nature of educational change. Together, these dimensions show that educational change depends not only on transferring tools or practices but on building shared understanding, agency, and capacity among diverse actors. Within the Learning Sciences, such dynamics increasingly take shape through Research–Practice Partnerships (RPPs) that foster long-term collaboration between researchers, practitioners, and community members (Coburn & Penuel, 2016; Fishman et al., 2013; McKenney, 2016). Yet even as RPPs aim to democratize innovation, many RPPs still face challenges of knowledge asymmetry and unequal voice—particularly when teachers’ experiential knowledge remains implicit or underrepresented in the collective learning process (Datnow, 2020). To achieve meaningful upscaling, local insights must not only be surfaced but also translated into shared knowledge artifacts that circulate across partnerships and contexts. One promising approach lies in connecting stories of practice—teachers’ reflective narratives of implementing innovations—with design principles—conceptual formulations that abstract and communicate lessons from practice (Bell et al. 2004; Kali, 2006; Kali & Linn, 2008). We suggest that stories of practice (Sfard & Prusak, 2005; Luehmann, 2008) may help address this challenge, as they encapsulate tacit, situated knowledge about pedagogical enactment and identity. When elicited and shared within RPPs, such stories may function as “boundary objects” that invite multiple interpretations and foster dialogue among teachers, researchers, and other stakeholders (Akkerman & Bakker, 2011). Our previous work demonstrated that AI-based chatbots can scaffold the elicitation of identity stories, supporting teachers in articulating meaningful experiences that surface professional insights and shifts in identity. When collectively examined and linked to design principles, these stories become reified knowledge elements within a broader digital network, connecting diverse projects, partners, and perspectives (Aridor et al., 2022). Building on this framework, this workshop proposes story–principle linking as a novel methodology for networked learning and reflection within and across RPPs. By engaging researchers in hands-on work with stories of practice from their own partnerships, elicited through AI-supported tools, we aim to collaboratively explore how such narrative-design interactions can: (1) Empower teachers’ voices as co-constructors of knowledge; (2) Provide RPP leaders with a structured, research-based methods and tools for capturing and organizing local insights; and (3) Offer a mechanism for scaling innovations through conceptual connectivity and cross-partnership learning rather than replication (Kali et al., 2025). By positioning stories of practice as both reflective and epistemic artifacts, we propose a pathway toward upscaling educational innovation through relational, narrative, and principled learning.

Workshop goals and intended audience The overarching goal of this workshop is to explore how stories of practice, elicited and analyzed through design principles and supported with AI tools, can advance the upscaling of educational innovation within and across Research–Practice Partnerships (RPPs). Participants will engage in hands-on collaboration linking stories of practice to pedagogical design principles, transforming local insights into shareable conceptual knowledge. The workshop will encourage critical reflection on the design principles within INSIGHTS—its curation, authorship, and evolving nature—and foster dialogue on how this process strengthens teachers’ voices, enhances ownership of innovation, and supports sustainable change across partnerships. Beyond the session itself, the workshop aims to lay the groundwork for continued collaboration, through a joint symposium and future publication on inclusive, narrative-based approaches to scaling. By the end of the day, participants will have experienced and theorized a concrete methodology for connecting stories, design principles, and upscaling processes, gaining conceptual and practical tools adaptable to their own RPPs.

This workshop is designed for 10–30 participants, including educational researchers and practitioners who are actively involved in, or lead RPPs seeking to scale educational innovations. Participants are encouraged to draw on experiences from their own partnerships and, when possible, to bring stories of practice elicited in advance using the provided chatbot tool. Sharing stories is optional; others may participate as commentators and collaborators. The session will be most relevant for those interested in making teachers’ tacit knowledge visible and actionable, experimenting with AI tools for reflection and knowledge building, discussing the curation of shared design principle repositories, and co-developing frameworks for scaling innovation across diverse educational networks. We expect a diverse group of participants representing multiple RPP contexts, from STEM and digital learning to community-based innovations, committed to advancing equitable, knowledge-rich researcher–practitioner collaboration. While most participants will come from the Learning Sciences community, we also welcome educational practitioners engaged in RPPs who wish to share their experiences and perspectives. Workshop theme and goals The workshop centers around the need to bridge the persistent gap between research and practice, which strongly relates to the 2026 ISLS theme of Partnering with Purpose. In our session, we will consider collaborative approaches that cross the boundaries between research and practice. We present a vision for bidirectional professional learning in which both researchers and practitioners learn from each other, challenge traditional hierarchies, and foster a dynamic exchange of knowledge to bring the worlds of research and practice together. Through this exchange, we aim to support participants to produce new research that is usable and actionable and fosters mutual learning.

This workshop provides a unique opportunity to gain both theoretical and practical insights to create new possibilities for research and practice. While frameworks such as design-based research (DBR), improvement science, design-based implementation research, and Research-Practice Partnerships (RPPs) have strengthened collaboration and provided models for working together, the field continues to struggle with impact and sustainability after formal studies conclude (Brown, 1992; Coburn et al., 2013; Levin, 2013; Penuel & Gallagher, 2017). There are few places where researchers and practitioners have opportunities to interact in sustained ways (Dragnić-Cindrić & Fusco, 2025). The purpose of this workshop is grounded in theoretical perspectives that advocate for explaining how researchers and practitioners engage in sustained collaboration. Theoretical framing and background Our workshop is grounded in expansive learning (Engeström & Sannino, 2017), which emphasizes collective activity, reflection, and the creation of new knowledge across boundaries. In this case, collaboration between researchers and practitioners becomes a site for mutual learning and innovation. The workshop interleaves three complementary perspectives that ground the HEOV project: 1) the relational and affective dimensions of collaboration, 2) design principles for equitable partnerships, and 3) knowledge mobilization and boundary crossing. Together, these frameworks guide how we conceptualize and facilitate learning across research and practice.

Relational and affective dimensions of collaboration We build on scholarship highlighting the affective, social, and relational dimensions of sustaining partnerships (Potvin et al., 2024; Schneider & vander Linden, 2023; Walsh et al., 2021). This work also emphasizes the importance of capacity building and mutual ownership (Foster-Fishman et al., 2001; Franco, 2019), as well as r esearch on sustained partnerships between teachers, STEM coaches, and researchers that foster shared learning and long-term impact (Arastoopour Irgens et al., 2023; Borowczak et al., 2025; Datnow et al., 2025; Farrell et al., 2021; Giamellaro & Siegel, 2023; Susin et al., 2023). Within co-design processes, researchers and practitioners do more than just design curriculum or tools. Their relationships support deeper understanding and more meaningful impact, especially when the practitioner’s context is centered in the collaboration. These relational and contextual elements are foundational for equitable collaboration and help teams intentionally co-construct conditions for collaboration (Victor et al., 2025). We also emphasize the importance of beginning with practitioners’ needs, questions, and ideas when shaping collaborative work (Victor, 2026). Design principles for equitable partnerships Our model includes three conjectures that we present as design principles for equitable collaboration. First, ensuring every voice is heard requires intentionally disrupting power structures. Second, research articles serve as boundary objects that support dialogue between researchers and practitioners. Third, sustained spaces and activities for conversation help partnerships adapt ideas productively across contexts (McClain et al., 2024; Miller et al., 2025).

Knowledge mobilization and boundary crossing The workshop will also discuss the important perspective of Knowledge Mobilization, the process of moving research knowledge into practical use. We examine how this process can be supported between the systems of research and practice. In this work, both researchers and practitioners act as brokers who help translate ideas to bridge research and classroom practice (Levin, 2013; Rycroft-Smith, 2022). Expansive learning provides a framework for understanding how this boundary crossing can generate shared understanding and new possibilities for collaboration between the two fields (Engeström & Sannino, 2017). Workshop structure and activities This four-hour interactive workshop is structured to share and extend the Hearing Each Other’s Voices (HEOV) approach to equitable researcher–practitioner collaboration. Taking an expansive learning approach, we focus on a reciprocal process that enriches both domains, inviting participants to share, learn, and create together toward new theory and practice. Participants will engage in a progression of activities that move from reflection to enactment to co-design, mirroring the principles of expansive learning that guide the HEOV project. The design ensures that participants first ground their learning in their own “problems of practice,” then experience an authentic researcher–practitioner exchange through role play and finally collaborate to develop strategies and tools for use in their own contexts. This structure balances clarity and flexibility, supporting active engagement while allowing participants to contribute new insights and adaptations that strengthen the HEOV model. Wendy Walter is a former ELA, social studies, and multi-age teacher with over 25 years in teacher education. Her research focus is on Universal Design for Learning and curriculum design. Jessica McClain is a former middle and high school science teacher whose research centers on strengthening researcher–practitioner relationships and advancing asset-based pedagogies in P–20 educational spaces. Her work focuses on how educators and researchers can collaborate to design learning environments that recognize and build upon the diverse strengths students bring to the classroom. Linette Victor is a former chemistry professor and 6-12 STEM educator whose research focuses on connecting theory and practice through collaborative design. Her research explores how the design and implementation of professional learning experiences shape, support, and constrain the translation of evidence-based ideas into practical tools and strategies that can be readily adopted in real-world settings. Jerika Miller was a secondary English Language Arts teacher and has research interests in teacher learning and practitioner expertise, helping the team think critically about the practitioner perspective. Danielle Murphy is a former elementary teacher and an early-career learning scientist, who studies ways to foster teachers’ epistemic agency in science education. Together, the team’s expertise and scholarship provide the foundation for this workshop’s emphasis on equitable, sustained collaboration between researchers and practitioners.

The HEOV research team, consisting of seven former practitioners, has been working together to develop a sustained community approach that provides a foundation for building trusting research-practitioner partnerships. For almost three years, the project members have matched researchers and practitioners in the United States to study the conditions necessary for developing equitable bidirectional partnerships. Through these partnerships, the team hopes to solve local problems, bring in new possibilities for learning, and highlight the importance of connecting practice to research (McClain et al., 2024; Victor et al., 2025). Over the life of the project, the team has worked with and learned from 21 practitioner-researcher teams. Five practitioners from the project researcher-practitioner pairs will be present to facilitate the workshop. The breadth of experience and expertise among the workshop facilitators demonstrates the relational infrastructure we advocate for in closing the gap between research and practice.
