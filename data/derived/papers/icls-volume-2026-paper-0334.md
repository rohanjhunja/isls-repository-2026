# The Innovation Hub: A Laboratory School Model to Provide Equitable Computational Experiences for Middle School Youth

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This design paper describes the University of Virginia Innovation Hub, a laboratory school that is based upon a research-practice partnership (RPP) between the University of Virginia and Charlottesville City Schools. The Innovation Hub is committed to providing computationally-rich, community-driven, and project-based instruction to every middle school student in the school division. Instead of pulling students out of existing schools to form a separate laboratory school, the Innovation Hub uses a push-in model to provide experiences for all students across grade levels in a school district. Using principles of effective design-based implementation research as guiding principles, this paper describes the history of the RPP, the iterative refinement of the Innovation Hub, as well as current and future directions of the effort.

The University of Virginia Innovation Hub is a state-funded laboratory school in partnership with Charlottesville City Public Schools (CCS). The Virginia Department of Education defines college partnership schools as public, nonsectarian, and nonreligious schools that are created out of partnerships with public institutions of higher education with the intent of bringing innovation into K-12 education by leveraging the resources and expertise of higher education institutions (Virginia Department of Education, n.d.). The Innovation Hub builds upon a rich history of collaboration with the UVA School of Education and Humandevelopment (EHD). The Innovation Hub leverages UVA’s expertise in positive youth development, science, technology, engineering, mathematics, and computer science (STEM+CS) education, and teacher education to empower students to use and create computing technologies to solve relevant, authentic, and community-based problems. The mission of the UVA Innovation Hub at Charlottesville Middle School is to engage all students in personally meaningful, computationally-rich, and interdisciplinary learning experiences. The vision of the UVA Innovation Hub at Charlottesville Middle School is to provide educational opportunities that inspire curiosity, fuel innovation, and empower all students to solve problems and pursue lifelong learning. The lab school is founded on three guiding principles to improve student outcomes: (1) Developing a community of practice around education that incorporates interdisciplinary, pr oject-based, computationally-rich learning experiences; (2) Engaging students by incorporating their voices and choices throughout the learning process; and

(3) Making meaningful community connections to engage students, teachers, and families in real-world problem-solving, mentorship, and career exploration.

This paper describes the development of the Innovation Hub using the framework of design-based implementation research (DBIR) principles (Fishman et al., 2013). Specifically, we frame this paper using the two principles of deciding on a joint focus of work and co-designing processes and artifacts. The Innovation Hub is currently in year two of operation. By describing the design process and current approach, we aim to disseminate our model and share our successes and challenges.

Research across different educational disciplines and domains underscores the importance of school environments that: actively engage students in learning; connect to the local community with involvement from parents and guardians; provide personalized learning in spaces that are physically and emotionally safe; challenge each student to reach their full potential; and prepare students for success in college or future employment in the global workforce (Lewallen et al., 2015). School environments should: (1) engage the learner to direct their own learning through setting goals and challenges that are personally meaningful; (2) use asset-based approaches that support all learners to connect out-of-school experiences and build upon learners’ prior knowledge, i nterests, and experiences in their social and cultural contexts; and (3) purposefully engage students in disciplinary practices to help students develop a deep understanding of subject areas instead of rote memorization of concepts (National Academies of Science, Engineering, and Medicine, 2018). Prior research on embedding computational thinking 2162 in mathematics and science classrooms suggests that integration is most effective when computational practices are meaningfully connected to disciplinary sense-making rather than treated as isolated skills (e.g., Weintrop et al., 2016). These efforts also highlight the practical challenges of aligning computational work with disciplinary goals and existing classroom structures.

Middle school years mark a pivotal phase in adolescent development. Research describes early to midadolescence – youth aged 10-15 in grades 4-9 – as the most consequential developmental period outside early childhood (Blakemore & Choudhury, 2006). Choices  made during adolescence significantly influence future performance and overall well-being (Nagaoka et al., 2015). Improving the quality of the middle school experience, especially for underrepresented youth, can significantly shift the educational trajectory for those students who are most at-risk. At this pivotal stage, young adolescents value autonomy, strive for competence, and seek a sense of belonging. Taking charge of their learning, setting definitive goals, and cultivating metacognitive and self-regulating skills become essential. However, many educational structures remain rooted in outdated industrial models characterized by rigid schedules, compartmentalized subject teachings, and teacher-centric learning environments.

Deciding on a joint focus of work In 2022, the Virginia Governor announced plans to fund and open laboratory schools. When the original request for proposals was provided, representatives from UVA and CCS met to discuss the opening of a laboratory school. CCS administrators decided that middle school was both an area of need and an area of expertise for UVA, and a lab school effort could build on an existing RPP focused on integrating CS into STEM. Thus, UVA and CCS applied for and were awarded a one-year planning grant to develop processes and structures for a full laboratory school proposal. During the planning year, UVA and CCS representatives met biweekly to develop the laboratory school structure, processes, and function. With iterative refinement, the RPP worked to decide on a joint focus for work. This emerged as the UVA Innovation Hub,  a learning environment where students have agency and voice to pursue questions and problems of consequence to their lives. The Hub created guiding principles of: developing a community of practice around education that incorporates interdisciplinary, project-based, computationally-rich learning experiences; engaging students by incorporating their voices and choices throughout the learning process; and making meaningful community connections to engage students, teachers, and families in real-world problemsolving, mentorship, and career exploration. Consistent with DBIR principles, several key design decisions emerged through negotiation with school-based partners. For example, teachers expressed concern about standalone computational thinking lessons and advocated instead for science-first instruction with computational tools embedded in service of disciplinary learning. In response, the Hub refined its approach to integrate coding and computational practices within existing science units rather than positioning them as separate instructional goals. Similarly, the co-teaching model evolved across implementation cycles. In Year 1, Hub staff primarily led instruction; by Year 2, planning and instructional responsibilities were more collabor atively distributed in response to teacher feedback. These refinements reflect the iterative, jointly negotiated design process central to DBIR.

The Hub also agreed upon long-term outcome goals, including increased student performance on state standardized tests, increased teacher retention, reduced student absenteeism, and increased awareness of different careers. These goals emerged in response to historically low science standardized test performance in the division, alongside research emphasizing the importance of student agency and positive youth development during middle school. The RPP worked to balance accountability demands with a commitment to innovation, asking how computationally-rich, project-based learning could support both improved academic outcomes and more equitable, engaging experiences. Throughout these negotiations, equity remained a central priority, particularly in ensuring that emerging multilinguals and other historically marginalized students had access to meaningful computational learning opportunities. By transforming school experiences to elevate the developmental needs of young adolescents, the Hub aimed  to increase the voice and agency of middle school students in ways that align with research-based practices for positive youth development and asset-based pedagogy (Ryoo et al., 2013). The Hub realized the need to support teacher learning of computational concepts and tools, as well as helping educators shift from information dispensers to learning facilitators (e.g., Goode et al., 2014). Thus, another joint focus of the Innovation Hub was to create an innovative professional learning environment that extends into the community and provides a model for pre-service and in-service teacher education. The Hub aims to help existing teachers: (1) incorporate relevant and authentic classroom experiences for their students; (2) integrate computationally-rich activities that address Virginia Computer Science and other content standards of learning; and (3) leverage and sustain student and community resources in their classrooms. The Hub also aims to support the development of pre-service teachers by (1) embedding them in rich professional learning experiences that are grounded in student and community contexts; (2) providing opportunities to learn and apply research-based, 2163 effective pedagogical strategies with novel, computationally rich practices; and (3) providing explicit opportunities to co-develop classroom materials that privilege and sustain students’ funds of knowledge. The Hub agreed on a guiding principle of providing access to all students in the school district, as opposed to a more traditional lab school model of serving a smaller population. However, the RPP struggled with operationalizing how to reach every student  in the division. The RPP agreed on a model where every Charlottesville Middle School student would rotate into the Lab School Innovation Hub once every three science classes. Innovation Hub staff would co-teach and work closely with the classroom teachers  to facilitate PBL experiences. Given that every student is enrolled in science, every middle school student in the school division would have access to the Innovation Hub.

Co-designing processes and artifacts During the planning year, the RPP co-designed and piloted activities and structures to be included in the Innovation Hub. For example, the RPP launched a Computer Science (CS) Pathway as part of an existing summer program that helped youth learn CS concepts by solving real-world problems and providing CS-related career exposure through local industry partners. In addition, the RPP launched a Curriculum Design Accelerator experience for both in-service and pre-service middle school teachers to engage in the same projects alongside the youth in the summer program. By combining the Accelerator with the summer youth programming, the RPP aimed for teachers to see the kinds of assets and interests that students bring to their classrooms, what students are capable of doing in informal learning settings, and help teachers deepen their understanding of CS concepts and pedagogy. During the school year, science teacher candidates from UVA completed their field placements with the same teachers in CCS. The RPP worked wit h the middle school science teachers to support them in the development and implementation of two computationally-rich PBL activities in their classrooms, and held quarterly professional learning workshops with in-service and pre-service teachers. In collaboration with the Boys and Girls Clubs of Central Virginia, the RPP also led a hackathon event where youth learned new technology creation skills to solve real-world problems from community members. These activities served as initial design prototypes for the kinds of experiences the RPP envisioned for the lab school. The Hub also decided upon providing wrap-around professional learning experiences for teachers to support their development and use of interdisciplinary, project-based, and computationally rich projects through an Innovation Academy Fellowship. The Innovat ion Academy Fellowship was conceptualized as a year-long commitment available to CCS middle school educators each year, recruited by CCS administration. Participation in the Innovation Academy Fellowship includes: a significant stipend; classes regularly scheduled in the UVA Innovation Hub throughout the school year; coaching and mentoring with the UVA Innovation Hub, the UVA Innovation Hub Director, and EHD faculty; Support for designing and developing PBL units; Substitute coverage provided by UVA Center for Community Partnerships for additional professional learning opportunities such as conferences and externships; and a focus on growing their PBL and STEM+CS competencies. Year 2: Pilot launch of the innovation hub Year Two was the pilot year of the UVA Innovation Hub at Charlottesville Middle School, serving around 300 7th-grade students. The  RPP worked with the teachers to create PBL experiences for the 7th-grade students, including creating models of mitosis in Scratch and simulating pollination with the micro:bit microcontroller. Working together with the UVA School of Engineering and Applied Science, the RPP created a “BioBot” project, where students investigated animal adaptations and used the Hummingbird roboti cs kit to create robots that mimicked animal adaptations (Figure 2). The culmination of the project involved students presenting their BioBots to the community in the Rotunda  on the UVA campus. After presentations, students took tours of UVA engineering labs conducting similar research with biomimicry. Six teachers participated in the Innovation Academy, including two 7th-grade science teachers, two 8thgrade science teachers, one engineering teacher, and one mathematics/science instructional coach. Professional learning opportunities included participating in a three-day project-based learning workshop led by PBLWorks, where teachers created computationally-rich projects to implement in their classroom s. The RPP created a workbook for teachers to set goals and intentions for the year, asynchronously plan and reflect upon their practice, and get feedback from the RPP project team. The RPP also provided space for reflection and professional learning in biweekly PLC meetings.  Innovation Fellows created and implemented their own PBLs in their classrooms, which included a 7th-grade mathematics unit-Design a Home Project-where students designed a model home that could be used to increase the stock of affordable housing in Char lottesville, using ratios and ratio tables to create scale calculations of real-life measurements. Another project that was created by the teachers included a sound machine project for 8th-grade physical science, where students designed their own musical instrument using the micro:bit to understand pitch, volume, frequency, vibration, a nd similar concepts. 2164 Current and future plans We are currently in Year three of the project. Year three included the hiring of an additional Innovation Specialist to support 300 8th-grade students for a total of 600 students being served by the Lab School Innovation Hub. Over the summer, the RPP revised and refined the units from the previous year to implement with 7th-grade students, and we are currently working with the 8th-grade science teachers to create PBL experiences for physical science. The Innovation Academy was extended to non-STEM teachers, including four science teachers, one 7thgrade English Language Arts teacher, one 7th-grade social studies teacher, and one 8th-grade mathematics teacher. The RPP hopes that by branching out with other teachers in different content areas, this effort can organically grow from teacher demand and interest. Plans for subsequent years anticipate cohorts of up to twelve teachers. While the structure continues to evolve in response to school needs and staffing realities, the long-term goal is to build distributed instructional capacity across the middle school f aculty rather than maintain a small, fixed cohort. Year Four (2026-2027) will see the hiring of a second Innovation Specialist to support an additional 300 6th-grade students for a total of 900 students being served by the UVA Innovation Hub. Year Five will continue the work with all three grade levels.

This paper discussed the formation of joint foci or work and co-design of processes and activities to create a lab school to provide computationally-rich, project-based experiences for all middle school youth in a school division. Evidence from Year 2 implementation, including PLC notes, classroom observations during BioBot  builds, and student artifacts, suggests that the push-in model of the Innovation Hub and co-teaching with science teachers can provide computationally-rich, project-based experiences for every student across a grade  The push-in model did require a very close partnership and collaboration of the Hub staff with the science teachers to have time and space given the constraints of covering material for the standardized tests. Notes from classroom observations and post-build discussions with teachers and students indicated that students engaged in iterative redesign and troubleshooting, often collaborating to refine both their robotic models and their explanations of biological adaptations. The RPP team and teachers met to debrief after each build day, using those reflections to adjust facilitation strategies and pacing. Student artifacts reflected growing complexity in both design and reasoning across iterations.

Reflecting upon Year 2, the Hub does seem to have created a community of practice among Innovation Fellows. However, although all the teachers and Innovation Hub staff want to incorporate student voice and choice throughout the learning process, many of the projects from Year 2 were tightly linked to state learning standards and not as tightly linked to student resources and choice. The process of co-designing curricular artifacts also surfaced several structural and pedagogical challenges. Teachers’ planning periods are largely devoted to covering required content, making it difficult to create time for deeper project development and iteration. Balancing inquiry-based learning with the pressure to address all Standards of Learning and assess for mastery required ongoing negotiation. There was also a learning curve associated with integrating coding tools, as many teachers were learning alongside their students. Staffing transitions, scheduling constraints, and occasional absences of Hub personnel further complicated continuity. At times, lessons felt rushed, limiting opportunities for iterative refinement, an element that often produce d the richest learning moments. These tensions highlight the practical realities of implementing computationally-rich PBL within accountability-driven systems. Similarly, although some projects had community connections (like the BioBots project), many of the projects did not center real-world problem solving and career exploration. Future revisions to the Innovation Hub curricular materials and professional learning opportunities can help refine materials and activities to provide more student choice and voice as well as real-world problem solving.

Based on early implementation, several considerations may support districts pursuing similar models. First, embed computational learning within core disciplinary instruction rather than as standalone lessons. Second, co-design curricular materials with classroom teachers to ensure alignment with standards and classroom realities. Third, build structures for shared instructional ownership that evolve, rather than assuming immediate co-teaching balance. Finally, intentionally design for authentic student choice and community connection from the outset, rather than layering these elements on after initial implementation. Blakemore, S.
