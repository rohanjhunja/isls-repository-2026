# Leveraging Generative AI for Iteration in Undergraduate Design

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Training designers requires not only technical skills, but also the ability to think critically, creatively, and strategically. Design applications powered by Generative Artificial Intelligence (GenAI) are emerging every day, hence educators are concerned about the development of students’ creative abilities. The project is a pedagogical experience mediated by a GenAI tool, the ‘DIAgent’. Through a dynamic interaction between initial sketches, guiding questions, and automated feedback provided by the tool, students iterate their proposals, considering key variables such as form, usability, ergonomics, materials, and technology. This study is conducted with a mixed-method approach as an exploratory study to assess the creative abilities of a sample of 103 undergraduate students after the intervention. This paper presents the tool and the application protocol to be applied in six different design classes as the study’s first phase results, leading to a better understanding of how to redefine the students’ desi gn process.

Training designers demand more than technical proficiency; it requires cultivating critical, creative, and strategic thinking to address complex social, technological, and environmental transformations (Aldoseri et al., 2024). While there are some concerns regarding the use of  emerging technologies as professional tools, younger designers might perceive them as essential. Therefore, the is an increasing demand for a redesigned framework of educational strategies mediated by technology. In this sense, it bec omes key to integrating diverse reasoning modes and promoting high value-added innovation (Büler et al., 2022; Marrone et al., 2022). This mixed-methods study examines a Generative Artificial Intelligence (GenAI) -mediated approach in design education, focusing on its role in enhancing student engagement and divergent thinking during ideation— an ability strongly supported in creativity research (De Bono, 2009; Runco et al., 2010; Palmiero et al., 2022; Saretzki et al., 2024). Through structured interaction with sketches and automated feedback, students iteratively refine proposals while applying deductive, inductive, and abductive reasonin g (Kennedy & Thornberg, 2018).

The study hypothesizes that GenAI strengthens conceptual development and competencies such as Reasoning for Complexity and Design Concept (Tecnologico de Monterrey, 2019). Ongoing validation evaluates whether AI improves both idea quantity and quality while fostering ethical technological integration.

## Theoretical framework

Assessing creative potential has been a longstanding priority in education, recognized as a fundamental competence alongside critical thinking and collaboration (Thornhill-Miller et al., 2023; Zheng et al., 2025). Early frameworks developed by Torrance (1966) and Guilford (1967) laid the foundation for creativity assessment, particularly through the Torrance Test of Creative Thinking (TTCT), which measures Flexibility, Fluency, Originality, and Elaboration as essential cognitive dimensions (Almeida et al., 2008). In the context of Generative Artificial Intelligence (GenAI), new concerns arise regarding the preservation of students’ ability to produce original and meaningful outcomes (Chiodo, 2026), especially given qualities linked to authentic creativity such as c onsciousness, emotional depth, and lived experience (Da Pelo, 2025). In design education, GenAI is commonly approached as a tool to accelerate processes, generate AI-based products, or enhance creative and critical thinking. This study prioritizes the latter, emphasizing divergent thinking (De Bono, 2009; Álvarez, 2010; Wadinambiarachchi et al., 2024), creative fluency (Fernandez-Abascal & Martín Díaz, 2013), and requirement-based decision-making (Watkins et al., 2021). Although GenAI may strengthen TTCT-related factors (Habib et al., 2024), concerns about dependency persist. Balanced models such as co-creativity (McKayle,

2025) and AI-enhanced human abilities (Agboola & Yassin, 2025) advocate augmentation rather than replacement.

In this sense, Kosmyna et al. (2025), observed and recognized cognitive load during an essay writing task with a small sample of participants, showing that “Brain-only participants” had more distributed networks, while Large 419 Language Models (LLM) users displayed the weakest connectivity, which in the long term, translated to a poorer performance at neural, linguistic, and behavioral levels.

## Method

This exploratory mixed-methods study (Creswell & Creswell, 2005) analyzed 103 design students at Tecnologico de Monterrey, Mexico, during the 2025 academic year. The objective was to implement the "DIAgent: Design with AI" tool to foster divergent thinking and deep iteration in sketching. Traditionally, teacher pressure to iterate generates tension in the studio. DIAgent acts as a guide in this critical process, allowing AI to direct the evolution of initial ideas while the professor focuses on the final evaluation. The intervention centered on the fifth semester (out of eight) but included groups from the fourth and sixth semesters, a control group, and a sample from another city to compare student disposition and the impact of context. This structure seeks to redefine the creative process, reduce interpersonal conflicts, and enhance the fluidity of proposals through generative technology.

## Collecting initial data on participants

Each student developed a design concept from an initial sketch and iteratively refined it through guiding questions and automated feedback, addressing form, usability, ergonomics, materials, and technology. The experience aimed to strengthen critical thinking, continuous improvement, and informed decision-making.   Assessment relied on the Creative Behavior Assessment Instrument (CBAI) (López Caudana et al., 2024), applied in week five to establish a baseline for four groups in this initial phase, with later stages expanding the sample and analysis. The CBAI, validated in Spanish within Mexican educational institutions, demonstrates high reliability (CVC = 0.971; Spearman-Brown reliability = 0.870). Results enable baseline comparison with post-intervention outcomes. Each student may obtain a maximum score of 100 points.  For the evaluation of results in this application, the values for scoring on the Torrance test (1969) are considered, where:

• High percentiles (≥ 75) → High creative ability in the dimension evaluated.

• Middle percentiles (25-74) → Average creative development, with areas that can be improved.

• Low percentiles (< 25) → Need for creative stimulation interventions or strategies.

Measuring the impact of the intervention The CBAI measures levels of creative behavior in students in the dimensions defined by Torrance (1966). These dimensions will be assessed in an evaluation conducted after the educational intervention, in where students' how scores are established for each dimension. To this point, the CBAI has been applied to almost half of the sample (N=43).

To assess the impact that the evaluation had on each student, a questionnaire must be administered and completed by each participant. The maximum total score is 10 points for the three sections (Fluency = 4 points, Flexibility = 3 points, and Originality = 3 points). 420 The tool The DIAgent is an interface designed to guide students’ interaction with a Large Language Model (LLM) -based tool. Its development began with a simulator built in ChatGPT, which faculty members tested to refine the pedagogical strategy and adjust the application protocol. Once the student journey and sequence were optimized to support the design process and incorporate generic requirements adaptable across courses, full tool construction started. Although specialized product-visualization software was considered, the team prioritized accessibility by using institutional resources, such as a standard Google account. The experience promotes critical thinking and creative exploration in sketching. After producing 40 initial sketches, students use DIAgent to overcome creative blocks. They input project data, upload a sketch with contextual details, and connect to the Gem ini-2.0-flash model. The system generates prompts addressing aesthetics, ergonomics, usability, and materials. Students refine their sketches and receive final evaluations (1– 5 scale) per dimension. A downloadable PDF documents the process, transforming AI  interaction into reflective academic dialogue.

Creative capacity score Preliminary CBAI findings establish a quantitative baseline of creative performance and qualitative implementation insights. The partial sample (N = 43) included Industrial Design students from Monterrey (72%) and Guadalajara (28%), primarily fifth semester (74%) and fourth semester (26%). Gender distribution was 77% female, six male, and six undisclosed (excluded from gender analyses). The mean creative score was 67.68/100, indicating moderate creativity. One-way ANOV A revealed no significant differences b y gender, campus, or semester. Although males showed slightly higher descriptive means, differences were not statistically significant, and semester level did not predict outcomes. Overall, the findings indicate a homogeneous creative baseline across demographic groups, supporting standardized intervention across campuses while prioritizing individualized creative development strategies over demographic distinctions. Semantic trends Qualitative analysis of four teachers’ testimonials showed predominantly positive results (74%), with 18% neutral and 8% negative. Key findings indicate that students improved technical language for better tool interaction, engaged in critical decision-making through feedback, and found motivation by viewing the AI as a challenge to "beat." Quantitative and sentiment findings indicate cultural readiness to integrate GenAI into creative development. Moderate creativity levels suggest opportunities to enhance divergent thinking. The study recommends variabilitybased feedback, adaptive pacing w ith micro-breaks, and structured prompts to reduce cognitive overload, supporting institutional adoption, and continuous technical improvement of the tool. Therefore, implementing the AI tool for divergent thinking should aim not just to develop a GenAI tool for design education, also to catalyse qualitative leaps in creative fluency, originality, and reflective reasoning. As this study is ongoing, the analysis is not yet conclusive, although relevant insights have been gained regarding the impact of the Generative AI tool on the creative stage. Initial findings show no significant differences between student groups, establishing a consistent baseline for further research and formulating hypotheses without concern about the impact of context on the results. Several limitations are acknowledged: the sample size is small and not representative of the tool's full potential; furthermore, usability and technological acceptance have not been evaluated, nor is there evidence to determine whether the experience improved students' skills. Nevertheless, the qualitative analysis of the teachers' feedback is valuable in highlighting the importance of promoting deep reasoning in creative activities that consider aesthetic, ergonomic, and technical factors. Finally, the preliminary interactions, integrating learning analytics would be of benefit. Further evaluations are also required to strengthen the robustness of the changes in fluency, flexibility, and originality, considering the ethical and pedagogical implications of long-term use.
