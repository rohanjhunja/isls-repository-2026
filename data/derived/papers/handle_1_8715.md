# Supporting Elementary School Teachers in Their Learning of Programming and Robotics: A Case Study

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
We examined elementary school teachers’ learning of block-based programming and robotics under the guidance of an instructor in a professional learning (PL) course. Data consisted of teacher artifacts and videos recorded in the PL course. Data analysis revealed that the type of guidance and support the instructor provided helped teachers develop their programming skills and shaped their attitudes toward programming integration in elementary schools. The instructor’s guidance also posed a few barriers to teachers’ learning.

## Introduction and background

Many scholars recognize the importance of teaching programming in elementary schools to develop studen ts’ computational thinking skills and increase their interests toward computer science and programming starting from early ages (e.g., Lye & Koh, 2018). The literature has some preliminary answers that propose ways to p repare current elementary school teachers to teach programming. These answers generally come from end of program or training teacher evaluations (Cooper et al., 2011),  teacher artifacts such as lesson plans (Savard & H ighfield,

2015) or programs/scripts of programs (Gabriele et al., 2019). However, more studies are necessary to reflect the

complex processes of teacher learning. As Cochran-Smith et al. (2014) claimed, teacher education research should “resist simplification, and account more fully for teacher education’s contexts and processes” (p. 105). Therefore, we undertook a case study of how to support teachers in their learning of block-based programming and robotics. We pursued this research to reflect the complexities involved in teachers’ learning of programming and robotics and to present ways to support teachers closely in professional learning (PL) contexts. In this qualit ative case study, our research question is: How do instructors facilitate K-5 teachers’ learning of programming and robotics or pose learning barriers to their learning in a professional learning course?

## Context and methods

As a part of a nationally funded research project, a rural school district and a university in the southeastern United States collaborated and organized a professional le arning (PL) course, Robotics for Teachers, for third through fifth grade elementary school teachers. Nineteen teachers participated in the PL course in the spring semester of

2019. The course composed of four face-to-face class meetings (approximately fourteen hours of training in total)

and weekly assignments. Two robotics kits and their  programming platforms were used in the course: Ozobot/OzoBlockly and RoboRobo/Rogic. When the teachers worked on in-class tasks in these four face-fa ceface class sessions, the instructors supported these teachers’ learning during their engagement with in-class group or individual tasks. In this paper, we focused on t he case of one instructor, Dr Z (not one of the aut hors of this paper) as he worked with the same group of four teachers from the beginning of the course to the end. We could track these teachers’ progress under Dr Z’s guidanc e throughout the course. The teachers he worked wit h were Isabel, Malia, Neal, and Hope (all names are pseudonyms). Data consisted of the videos recorded in the PL course during which Dr Z worked with this group of four teachers and teacher artifacts (teachers’ Rogic and OzoBlockly programs/weekly assignments). We used the systemati c whole-to-part inductive approach and part-to-whol e narrative evolving in sampling and analyzing video data (Erickson, 1982). The patterns in the phases of interests, facilitators and barriers in Dr Z’s guidance, were revealed and reported.

## Findings

We observed that Dr Z supported teachers’ learning of programming and shaped their attitudes toward programming integration in elementary schools. He also posed a few barriers to teachers’ learning. Dr Z helped the four teachers develop their progra mming skills and debug their programs through providing support and direct instruction on the ess ential blocks to have RoboRobo robots move and turn.  For example, when Malia encountered a problem with her program, in which her robot completed half the task  and stopped, Dr Z guided her and explained the logic be hind setting the delay (duration) blocks in her program. Although the PL course instructor earlier instructed teachers in using the delay blocks to move their RoboRobo robots, teachers still needed further guidance when they were actively working with their robots to complete class activities.

In the first face-to-face meeting after the course instructor directed teachers to use level 4 out of 5 levels on OzoBlockly, Isabel and Olivia told Dr Z that their students would not be able to use level 4 (manipulating the wheel speeds to have robots turn: one wheel goes faster/slower than the other to make the turn) rather than level 2. Isabel then asked Dr Z why they had been asked to use level 4 instead of level 2 (using degrees in turns: turn 45 degrees). Dr Z r eplied, “With one of the teachers I was working wit h last semester, she was using the routing with the wheel speed function (in level 4). This was fourth grade,  ESOL (English for Speakers of Other Languages) class. And they were able to do it.” The teachers looked surprised to hear it; however, Isabel still remained skeptical of using wheel speeds with elementary school students. She said: “Maybe it was a different task.” She also asked whether that teacher was from their school district. Dr Z confirmed she was from their school district and added that the teacher initially set the wheel speeds and then let her students reset them to figure out the rotations. At the end of the discussion, Isabel and Olivia were more receptive of the idea of using wheel speeds on level 4. Olivia added that she would use whatever level her students wanted to use with Ozobots.

Dr Z also posed a few learning barriers with his guidance. The barriers occurred when Dr Z directly told the teachers which blocks to use without providing much context. For example, during the second face-t o-face meeting, Dr Z assisted Olivia with debugging her program. She was working on the third task. She needed to use if then block with this task, but she was not aware. Dr Z directly told her to use if then block and Olivia just followed whatever block he asked her to use. They d id not discuss what those blocks meant. Although Ol ivia’s programs worked on her robot, it was observed that she generalized the use of the if then block to every program she developed possibly thinking that if then block was essential in every program.

## Discussion and Conclusion

Our study showed that Dr Z’s systematic guidance and direct instruction helped the teachers with their learning of programming. However, when he directly told which blocks to use without providing much context for the use of those blocks, teachers developed misconceptions around the use of those blocks. Teachers’ attitudes  toward using a particular task and level with their students changed when Dr Z shared that students in another teacher’s classroom was able to work on the same task and the  level. We concluded that it is important to suppor t and closely guide teachers in many aspects of learning programming and robotics such as in debugging, developing understandings of key programming concepts and idea s. Teacher educators need to provide real life clas sroom examples on programming integration in elementary classrooms. Additional research can be performed to advance our understanding of how to effectively support teachers’ or students’ needs in learning about programming.
