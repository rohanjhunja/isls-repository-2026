# Third Space Extensions of Sociopolitical Development: Unlearning through Sensory, Discursive, and Collective Spaces

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper answers calls toward critical and decolonial shifts in learning through conceptualizing sociopolitical development as an unlearning process within the learning sciences. By distinguishing the unlearning process as continuous, recursive, endeavors rooted in reflexivity, I distinguish unlearning as a dynamic process towards resisting the many ever-changing reifications of oppression. Through excerpts from life histories from alumni of community arts programs, I demonstrate the process through which participants unlearn dominant ways of being and relating through means of deciphering the encoded fabric of macro sociopolitical realities within passing, quotidian moments. I present and instantiate three metaphysical expansions of Third Spaces in which participants’ unlearning occurred: sensory spaces, discursive spaces, and collective spaces.

## Introduction and related work

Work in the learning sciences towards equity have c alled for shifts in what determines meaningful lear ning, through the opportunities to cultivate sociocritical literacies  and critical consciousness in nondominant learners to combat oppression (Gutierrez, 2008). However, the process that embalms oppression is one of what legal scholar Reva Siegel calls “preservation through tra nsformation”—insidiously disguised narratives that pervade the minds of oppressors and oppressed alike, and uphold, through feigned neutrality and normalization, injustice throughout every aspect of our society (as cited in Alexander, 2010, p. 27). The corresponding educational process required to address social justice is then one of unlearning (McLeod et al., 2020), through which individuals continually interrogate and defamiliarize the perva sive, fungible, and oppressive dominant narratives in which they are socially inducted. Unlearning is a recursi ve and reflexive endeavor —a continual attunement to one’s innersubjectivities in relation to daily interactions, and subsequent re-evaluations of the dominant narratives and ways of being that one subscribes to in the present (Papastephanou & Drousioti, 2023). I distinguish this process from the sociopolitical learning component of Watts et al. 's (2003) semina l theory of sociopolitical development to emphasize the nature of oppression and the type of learning necessary to resist it. Sociopolitical development presents a five stage-based developmental process of learning and action— implying a linear progression through which an indi vidual accumulates increased knowledge surrounding the sociopolitical realities around them, as well as increased urgency to act upon them, banked from external sources (Watts et al., 2003). However, this approach does n ot consider the recursive internal nature of the task, nor the many manifestations, rebrands, and entanglements of  oppression through time and different contexts. Distinguishing unlearning emphasizes the dynamism of  the endeavor —asserting then, that those committed to social justice can never be unlearnt, only in continual process and (re)commitment to the endeavor of unlearning. In the following study, I examine unlearning within the “collective Third Space” of community arts spaces for nondominant youth, that subvert norms of relationality and present possibilities towards alternate ways of being and relating (Gutierrez, 2008). The role o f community arts in this unlearning process present s a selfexcavational space for historically marginalized youth in community to attune to dominant ways of being and relating that perpetuate narratives of oppression i n their lives. I show the fluid ways youth can unlearn  through out-of-school arts: by using art to attune to sensory moments through the body outside of conventionally delineated educational spaces, decipher the encoded  sociopolitical dynamics inherent in  ways of being and relating embedded in everyday interactions, and disrupt them. I expand on Gutierrez’s (2008) idea of Third Spaces as a zone of proximal development towards sociocritical literacies to reveal the metaphysical spaces accessed and traversed by participants in their unlearning. Spec ifically, I present sensory, discursive, and collective spaces as sites of unlearning. The work is guided by the following research question: Through what metaphysical divisions of Third Spaces did participants traverse in unlearning dominant narratives around ways of being and relating?

## Methods

This case study was one of 102 retrospective life history interviews, conducted ten years after concluding program participation, as part of a larger project around the long-term effects of participating in community arts programs. Given its holistic, refl ective nature, the life history approach lends itse lf to understanding how learning —and subsequently, any unlearning—was experienced and understood by a participant ove r the life course, across a variety of Third Spaces.

Case selection To study historically marginalized youth unlearning across community arts experiences, I chose two interviews through the following criteria: life histories from participants who sit at intersecting axes of marginalization (i.e., in terms of gender, race/ethnicity, socioeconomic c ircumstances, immigration status, etc.), who have a depth of experience with the program (which tends to result in more time spent in the program, as well as invol vement in leadership roles within the organization), whose narration makes explicit the centrality of sociopolitical dynamics in relation to their story. I looked for cases where participants followed a trajectory of unlearning—of attuning to a sociopolitical tension in their immediate environment, and then reflecting and acting to subvert it through art. From this process, we focus on the life histories o f Sofia and Dani (pseudonymized). These two cases were chosen among BIPOC participants for their foregroun ding of the unlearning process through community arts and life experiences —through which participants recognized a dominant na rrative or way of being and pivoted towards an alternative, using art as a sociopolitical tool. Sofia grew up in an urban setting within Southern California and attended a community visual arts pro gram as a youth. Dani grew up in  Northern California and attended a community poetry organization. Both iden tify as Black/African American women, and were aged  27 and 25, respectively, at the time of speaking. In t wo-hour interviews, the lead author asked Sofia and Da ni reflective questions around various domains (i.e., home life, childhood, academic experiences, experiences in the arts program, current life and perspectives) to surface unlearning moments throughout various Third Spaces. Analytic procedures Drawing from narrative analysis strategies concerne d with turning points as pivotal spaces of realizat ion and learning within life histories (Cole & Knowles, 200 1), I utilized a deductive coding scheme (codes: attunement, dominant narrative, alternate narrative) to understand the various milieu to which participants attuned and were deciphering during the unlearning process, the inherent dominant narratives that participants were unlearning, and the alternate narratives adopted in lieu of the dom inant narratives (Bingham & Witkowsky, 2021). From the deductively coded excerpts, I wrote reflective memo s to surface the turning points and processes throu gh which participants were undergoing this process and  utilized an inductive open coding process on the me mos to understand the metaphysical spaces in which these p rocesses occurred. This process generated codes for  three spaces, which I will instantiate in the findings section: sensory, discursive, and collective space.

## Findings

In this finding, I present narrative excerpts from Sofia and Dani’s life history interviews to conceptualize and instantiate sensory, discursive, and collective spaces as spaces youth moved through in their various unlearning processes.

Sensory space In this section, I illustrate the role of sensory s pace in catalyzing Sofia’s unlearning process. I co nceptualize the sensory space as both material and bodily experienc es to which one attunes in accessing moments of unl earning. Sofia describes the way her reflection around the normalization of sexual violence was tied to the sensory space— both through her proximity to a group of boys’ blasé recount of harrowing sexual violence a group of boys inflicted upon a teenage girl, and her attunement to her resulting bodily response: “I told you I spent a lot of time on the bus. So I talked about this experience where I was on the bus. And there were a bunch of teen boys who were picked up, ‘cos we stopped by a high school. And I was sitting in front of them, and I overhear them basically talking about raping this girl. And how I felt very guilty because I was just so frozen and so overwhelmed. But it’s, like, what can I…? You know what I mean? I just felt tight and  as I’m listening to them talking, and as they got off, I felt complicit in a way, but also just, like, shaking.” As is the nature of the origins of language, abstra ct ideas and milieu are accessed first through atte mpts at describing the material realm. In this case, Sof ia’s social analysis was accessed through her exper ience in sensory spaces—on the bus, a routine physical space on which she “spent a lot of time,” through hearing a group of boys talking behind her, and through the ways sh e senses her body respond. She highlights how she “ just felt tight” and was “shaking,” as physiological responses perceptible through sensory attunement. Her feeling “guilty” and “complicit in a way” stems from being “frozen”—from not confronting the group of boys within the sensory space, the same space in which she was attuning to her body’s intense physiological responses. Nonethe less, Sofia’s embodied experiences in the sensory space c atalyze her process of unlearning and push her to continue pursuing reflection within the discursive space, as mentioned in the following section. Discursive space In this section, I conceptualize the discursive spa ce as one which centers meaning-making around the communication of language, topics, and concepts wit h dialectic intent —with themselves and/or others, and including modes of articulation outside of the verbal—extending, for example, to visual, auditory, and embodied expressions through art. We continue along Sofia’s experience, as the way she turns to the discursive space as a way of coping and unpacking the experience on the b us: “The way that I handled th at was, like, write out everything I heard and, like, my thoughts about it. Put it in a book. Print it out, [laughs], and you know, like, just talk about it.” Sofia’s move to write out everything she heard and her thoughts about it speaks to the importance and potential of language in processing difficult events which embody atrocities of power and oppression—to put it in discursive space outside of their own minds, unpack their thoughts around it, and respond to it. As previously mentioned, the discursive space exten ds beyond the bounds of verbal or written communication, to include mediums of expression and  communication emblematic of art making. Sofia spea ks to the way the visual arts program served as a cross-modal discursive space to explore on structural and personal levels how dominant narratives around sexual violence and misogyny impacted teenage girls: “I did a project and I ended up talking about the sexual violence that is kind of normalized, like, as a teen girl around here, right? So I had a lot o f self-portraits and then I also had a lot of quotes of things that I had overheard and written i t down. [...] One of my mom’s friends, she saw my project and she’d tell my mom, ‘Your daughte r is really dark and weird.’ And I remember at the time it being like, ‘dark and weird ?’ [...] So afterwards I took my book and I hid it under my mattress. [...] And at that point, I was still a little bit malleable towards others’ impressions. I thought some things like that, but, like, as I continued working on these long-term projects, it’s, like, I wouldn’t give a shit, if someone was, like, ‘your dark daughter.’ Yes, and it’s important to talk about it, [laughs], you know what I mean? I think  just being a girl in general, a teen girl [...] there’s just so much. Li ke you can’t like this. You can’t like that. And just, the self-confidence to not have your spirit dampened by other people’s opinions.” The excerpt above demonstrates how Sofia raised dis cussions around issues of sexual violence and misogyny through her visual arts project, and the w ays it allowed her to recursively reflect on ways t hese issues affected her own ways of being and moving through t he world. She describes how she brought “quotes of things [she] had overheard” from sensory spaces in relatio n to sexual violence —macro, dominant narratives rooted in oppression and imposed onto girls —into discursive spaces connected to herself (“through self-portraits”). A response to the project from one of her mom’s frien ds (“Your daughter is really dark and weird.”) —provided through the dialectic nature of the discursive spac e—was at first disparaging to Sofia, as it negatively  conflated the “dark” nature of the sociopolitical issues Sofia was grappling with in the discursive space with her identity as a teenage girl, and represents a break in what othe rwise would be seen as a trajectory of learning. Bu t true to the recursive nature of unlearning, as Sofia continued to participate in the discursive space through art,  she began to see how such a response reified the sociopolitical issues around gender identity and expression she was grappling with. Discursive participation led to the cultivati on of s elf-confidence— “to not have your spirit dampened by other people’s opinions''—as a way of being as a teenage girl that ran in res istance to dominant narratives which serve to restrict girls’ multitudes, towards patriarchal projects of subjugation. Collective space The collective space refers to spaces of shared pre sence and practice, which provide opportunities for  surfacing, interrogating, and dreaming beyond dominant narrati ves of being and relating in participation with oth ers. We pivot to the reflections of Dani, an alumni from a community poetry program, to see how participating within the collective space of spoken word poetry prompted her to attune to and move through sensory and discursive spaces to dismantle dominant hypercapitalistic comportments rooted in the commodification of youth trauma: Sometimes slam poetry people dive so deep into themselves in a competition. Which sometimes feels—you know how in most situations you want to leave f eeling whole, and I feel like people leave themselves on the stage just to get a score or a number. And even though they facilitated the space for it to be like you know scores don’t m atter, numbers don’t matter but they’re judging your poem, they’re judging your wording, the way you spit it, all of that. And sometimes I just, I felt disgusted.

But what I helped them to do, I was like, ‘give peo ple who are in the crowd an opportunity to write as well.’ And so that they can not only process what they just heard, right, because it’s like sometimes people are giving you stuff that’s a bit triggering. [...] And so I said, give people a pen and paper so they can process wha t they’re going through as well as write notes and love letters to the people who have perfo rmed. And so that was something that they had implemented for slam poetry. I don’t know if they still do it or not but I thought it was really awesome for us to take care of those people, the au dience. To take care of the people on the stage too.

In this excerpt, Dani’s unlearning occurs through the collective space—centering on the commodification of youth suffering for performance within the spoken word pr actice as an extractive practice irrespective of pe rsonal collateral. Through attunement to her body within a  sensory space —the way she “want[ed] to leave feeling whole,” and instead “felt disgusted” at the ways “people leave themselves on stage,” only to have their expression of what they have lived through be evaluated by “a score or a number.” Dani recognized the ways in the structure of the art as competition was giving way to capitalistic practices that perpetuated cycles of extracting suffering as a resource to be taken from youth, detached from communion and collective responsibility. Her attunement within the sensory space points her to recognize the weigh t of sharing trauma through the collective space and pushes her to propose a discursive practice —in which audience members can write notes to proces s what they hear and respond to performers, as a way “to take care of [...] the audience” a nd “to take care of the people on the stage too.” Dani’s reflections present the ways she intentionally pivoted from a narrative of extraction, toward ways of relating centered on caretaking.

## Conclusion

Sofia and Dani’s reflections demonstrate the ways c ommunity arts can provide opportunities for self-excavation and collective dialogue through art for youth to interrogate prepackaged narratives and surface understandings of sociopolitical identities and power. These emergent findings extend understandings of the Third Spaces in which important sociocritical literacie s are cultivated —through understanding the roles embodiment, dialogu e, and community can play in the attunement, reflection, and response to local sociopolitical dynamics rooted in national and global histories. Such insights surface the “critical repertoires [...] alive in the varied experiences of youth”— and the resulting importance of continual work to u nderstand the ways marginalized youth c reate alternate possibilities to ever-changing reifications of oppression (Warren et al., 2020, p. 284).
