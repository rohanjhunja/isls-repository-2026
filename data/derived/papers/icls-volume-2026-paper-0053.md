In the field of learning sciences, Design-Based Research (DBR) is an important part of designing technologyenhanced learning activities (Anderson & Shattuck, 2012; Puntambekar, 2018). It provides theoretical frameworks for empirical study with theory-driven design and refinement across multiple cycles to meet the learners’ goals and improve the learning experience (Cobb et al., 2003; Puntambekar, 2018). While DBR focuses on refining the initial design and/or design conjectures, there’s still a need to understand users’ perspectives at the earliest phase of design prior to the formulation of design conjectures or further refinement. Tools for doing so from instructional design might include needs analysis and related techniques, but these tend to focus on analysis with respect to preordained competencies or learning outcomes (Murphy et al., 2014; She et al., 2022). Participatory or co-design techniques have been used to elicit learner goals and needs with students as young as 10 (Knudtzon et al., 2003), but these techniques still need adaptation to apply into research with young children who are better characterized as informants rather than Co-Designer (Scaife et al., 1997). Since 2010, researchers have started to apply UX research methods to investigate young children’s experience (Véronique et al., 2013; Salonen, 2018). However, young children often struggle to articulate their preferences, needs and reasoning explicitly. This creates the methodological gap in uncovering why children value certain features of designs of target learning tools/environment.

In the past several years, there is a significant increase in studies on applications of Artificial Intelligence (AI). These tools have the potential to fundamentally transform how children engage with reading, particularly through new forms of user interfaces and adaptive learning supports. As part of a larger project developing AI-informed tools for early literacy, our goal is to design child-centered reading platforms that not only respond to children’s actions but also reflect their perspectives and needs. To achieve this, our team seeks a deeper understanding of how young children experience and make sense of AI-assisted reading environments. While we reviewed ways to apply for user study for young children, we applied the laddering interview as a method to inform our design. Our paper first reviews existing methods used in child-centered design, including co-design and user experience research, and situates our work within this methodological landscape. We then introduce laddering interviews as a novel approach for understanding young children’s perceptions of AI tools, and present two design studies that apply this method in practice. Our two interviews were designed to explore young children’s perspectives on (1) AI-generated avatars in reading apps and (2) overall function on reading apps. Through the discussion of these two cases, we aim to answer the guiding question: What unique value did laddering provide in uncovering children’s perspectives and informing design within these two contexts, and how could this prove useful more generally in the learning sciences?

## Literature review

Bridging user study to Design-Based Research (DBR) 437 Design-Based Research (DBR) emerged as a methodological bridge between theory and practice, enabling iterative design, implementation, and refinement of educational innovations situated in authentic learning contexts (Anderson & Shattuck, 2012). Within the  Learning Sciences community, DBR has inspired related approaches that foreground user participation in the design process. Participatory Design (PD) and Co-Design than recipients of innovation (Bonsignore et al., 2013). These approaches have proven particularly valuable in educational contexts that engage participants as technology users and designers to better design tools or learning projects meeting participants’ needs.

However, when designing tools for young children, prior research on co-design with children has demonstrated both its potential and its challenges. For example, in Vaajakallio, Mattelmäki, and Lee’s research (2010), they noted children’s limited ability to  reason abstractly about user needs. Similarly, in Salonen’s work (2018), they found that Finnish elementary students engaged meaningfully in long-term co-design projects yet required adult facilitation to connect making activities to human-centered design principles. These studies indicate that while children can contribute to the generation of ideas and prototypes, there's a need for researchers to prompt young children to share their own needs and key values of expected functions. Laddering interview in design for young children The laddering interview method originated in the field of marketing research through the means–end chain theory proposed by Gutman (1982), which explained that people’s choices are guided by a hierarchical linkage between product attributes, the consequences of using those attributes, and the personal values they fulfill. This framework was later adopted into a qualitative interview technique called laddering. In a laddering interview, participants are repeatedly asked “why” questions to uncover increasingly abstract reasoning that connects observable features to underlying goals and values (Reynolds & Gutman, 1988). In the early 2000s, scholars began to apply laddering beyond marketing, particularly in Human Computer Interaction (HCI) and User eXperience Re search (UXR), to better understand why users prefer certain technologies and how interface features relate to emotional and social experiences (Subramony, 2002). Building on this work, Zaman (2008) further examined whether laddering interviews could be applied to Child-Computer Interaction. Zaman’s work pointed out that young children at 5 years old might be able to share attributes but may not complete the ladder and share deeper values. Zaman also addressed the importance of conducting interviews in familiar environments and using contextual cues such as software or toys to support expression. Zaman’s later work further refined the technique for preschoolers by incorporating playful elements such as puppets or paired interviews, showing that even young chi ldren can produce simple Attribute Consequence Value (ACV) chains linking concrete experiences to early value expressions such as friendship or curiosity (Abeele et al., 2012). In 2019, Wang, Xu, and Wu extended this approach to early childhood education by using laddering interviews to examine preschoolers’ preferences for library activities in China (Wang et al., 2019). Their study demonstrated that laddering could uncover the connections between familiar or novel attributes of activities and children’s intrinsic values like enjoyment and social connection.

Therefore, laddering offers a promising methodology for bridging design-based research and user experience approaches, as it provides a systematic means of eliciting learners’ implicit beliefs about the relationship between design features and their perceived learning value. By revealing how young learners interpret the features of educational technologies, laddering can inform iterative design decisions that align design features with children’s authentic experiences and motivations.

## Method

Design of the laddering interview While leveraging a ladder interview as the method to study users’ value, the key is to build “ladder” from their experience to their inner motivation and value toward the target design (Zaman, 2008). The three important contents of the “ladder” are Attribu te, Consequence, and Value (ACV, Abeele et al., 2012; Zaman, 2008). Attribute refers to the specific, observable feature or characteristic of a product or a system. Consequence describes participants' immediate experience feelings, or the outcomes and eff ect results from the attribute. Then Value represents the deeper, abstract meaning or personal goal that underlies participants’ preference or experience (Zaman, 2008). The ladder interview should be designed around the ACV framework. Therefore, we abstract the design guideline from previous empiri cal studies (Abeele et al., 2012; Wang et al., 2019; Zaman, 2008). First, the interviewer should provide young children with experience with a target/sample prototype.  For young children, providing a concrete prototype helps ground their reflections in direct interaction rather than abstract imagination. In the analysis phase, the research team can generate Attribute and Consequence based on 438 young children's interaction with the target prototype.  Second, the research team should ask iterative “Why” to prompt participants to share from their initial perception and move towards abstract value (Zaman, 2008). For example, the interviewer should identify what attribute (e.g., direct experience) in the conversation and ask “why” to uncover what consequence it leads to and why the consequence matters to the child. We present two studies to illustrate how a laddering interview method could be applied. The two studies took place in the context of the larger projects National AI Institute for Exceptional Education and Center for Early Literacy and Responsible AI (CELaR AI) to design AI tools for early literacy development. We aim to use the two laddering interviews to generate insight for the design team. Study 1 focused on a specific feature: AI-generated avatars that read for young children. Study 2 focuses on interviewing children’s overall perspective of functions and needs towards AI literacy apps. For both laddering cases, the interview protocol was designed by the first author. Then all the co-authors gave feedback, and the lead author revised with iterations. The lead author has piloted two protocol cases with target age children to complete within 30 minutes so children could be patient and engaged. We recruited the participating children through the institute’s collaborating early learning center, local elementar y school, and informal learning programs. All participants were individually interviewed in a conference room in the host university. Both studies were approved by the Institutional Review Board, obtaining consent forms from parents of all participating children and verbal assent from children 7 years old and above. Laddering interview 1: Young children’s perspective towards AI-generated Avatars Study 1 examines young children’s perceptions of different AI-generated avatars that read sentences aloud to them: (1) a self-avatar, generated from each child’s photo and voice recording; (2) a teacher avatar, based on a photo and voice sample of a female  professor affiliated with the research team; and (3) a dog avatar, produced using the prompt “create a lovely dog avatar that looks directly at the camera." ## Data analysis

For both studies, the study team conducted the data analysis in Atlas.ti 25 and transcribed all video data (verbatim transcript). We took the Attribute-Consequence-Value framework from laddering study (Zaman, 2008) and started with creating quotations for each interview question. Then we generated attributes from the transcript, and two authors rewatched and read the transcript to find the quotes for consequence and value. When we identified how each attribute led to its corresponding consequence, we then generated the overall hierarchical map. To present how the ladder is developed through analysis, we choose two cases (one from each study) with a transcript to illustrate the ACV framework. We selected the case through locating one attribute and went through children that share about the same attribute. We identified children for a case study, based on whose transcript clearly reflected 440 both attributes and consequences; retrieved the full transcript; and developed a detailed case study to illustrate the ACV ladder.

Young children’s perception towards learning with AI-generated avatars. For study 1, we have analyzed videos of 43 young children who completed the entire interview. Then the lead author generates the overall Attribute, Consequence, and Value. Then we listed key attributes by each avatar, we found the attributes mentioned by young children were mainly about appearance and voice. When young children talked about their experiences interacting with the AI-generated avatar, the consequences they described mainly centered on how the avatar’s appearance or voice aligned with their prior learning experiences or expectations. These consequences lead to the value of whether they would like to continue to learn with/from an AI-generated Avatar. Synthesizing insights across cases: Uncovering value structures through laddering Across both cases, we present how young children complete the ladder and the key features (attribute) they care most about. With the Hierarchy Value Map (HVM), we identify the key attribute and consequence linkage. Then we showcase how researchers identify A-C link or A-A link through detailed transcript, and ways to prompt young children to complete the ladder. In Corinne’s case, the focus was on the app’s avatar feature. Her responses began with attributes to the similarity of the avatar to real character. Corrine was able to connect the similarity to social and imaginative meanings. In Dominic’s case, he identified the ASR function as the most impressive attribute but 443 also expressed frustration when the system misrecognized his pronunciation. This led to an underlying value related to maintaining a sense of control and need for accurate pronunciation recognition. Dominic’s insight highlights a central design implication  regarding the balance between assistance and independence in AI-supported learning environments.

Taken together, the two cases illustrate three distinct forms of links within the ACV framework: Attribute-to-Consequence (A –C), Attribute-to-Attribute (A –A), and the full Attribute –Consequence–Value (ACV) ladder. In both complete ladders (A–C and ACV), the researchers’ iterative “why” questioning encouraged children to articulate deeper layers of reasoning. We also observed that in simpler tasks, such as Corinne’s the ladder, leading them to remain at the level of describing attributes rather than expressing underlying consequences or values. These patterns suggest that the depth and completeness of children’s ladders depend not only on the interviewer’s iterative “why” scaffolding but also on the child’s familiarity and comfort with the task or artifact, indicating that meaningful value reasoning is more likely to emerge when the stimulus aligns with children’s lived experiences and recognizable attributes. Exploring the possibilities of brand-new technologies for learning has somewhat of a chicken and egg problem, in that empirical methods for refining designs may not be as useful for informing initial designs. Techniques such as design-based research or co-design face unique challenges when the target users are young children. Child involvement requires creating an environment that is open, interactive, and responsive to children’s ideas, allowing them to engage meaningfully with researchers and design mater ials (Fails et al., 2013, Nousiainen, 2009). We have found that laddering interviews provide valuable tools for insight from young children to inform the design features and further design refinement. Laddering offers a valuable methodological tool for understanding how children interpret design features (including features that precede full prototypes of new technologies) and connect them to personal meanings. As this approach is still at an early stage of application in the Learning Sciences, further exploration is needed to refine how laddering can be used with young participants in learning tasks. In previous studies, researchers have used descriptive statistics to summarize attributes and map connections among conditions, attributes, consequences, and key values (Zaman 2008; Wang et al., 2019). However, in our studies, children’s limited familiarity with AI-generated avatars and automatic speech recognition made it challenging for some children to describe functional consequences or articulate the underlying values behind their preferences. These “incomplete” ladders highlight both the developmental and contextual factors that shape how young children express reasoning.

In addition, in our work, we present three excerpts from two individual interviews. From our pilot, we found in one-on-one settings, young children may not be willing to share much with researchers they saw for the first time. We also tried to invite their  siblings and parents to stay together. In the future, we are also seeking alternative opportunities to get young children engaged in laddering interviews, such as small groups. As we hope to integrate the laddering interview with other established methods  in the Learning Sciences, we consider 1) applying interaction analysis with the laddering interview or 2) designing laddering interview into design-based classroom observations. Combining these approaches may help capture not only what children say but al so how they act, collaborate, and make sense of technology in real time. Such integration can strengthen co-design processes by revealing the layered relationships among design features, user experiences, and children’s emerging value hierarchies.

Our work demonstrates how laddering can enrich learning sciences research with young children by uncovering the underlying values, goals, and reasoning that drive their interactions with AI-mediated reading tools. In the two laddering cases we studied, we present the Hierarchy Value Map, and detailed transcript to show how we uncover the ACV links. We hope this paper encourages scholars to adopt the laddering method within design research with young children.
