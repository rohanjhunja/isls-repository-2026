# Telling the Spiders’ Stories: Relationship Building as Essential Infrastructuring for Climate Justice Learning

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
There is growing attention to the need to design climate and environmental learning opportunities that support collective action towards more just futures. In this symposium, we explore the many kinds of connections required for collective work: connecting diverse knowledge traditions and cultural paradigms, connecting histories to futures, and connecting youth and teachers with community-based actors who are advancing environmental justice. We use the metaphor of spiderwebs to describe the networks of knowledges, worldviews, and stakeholders that foster visions of more just futures. Moreover, we draw attention to the work of spiders, referring to the human actors who build and sustain these webs. Our studies explore the complexity of maintaining spiderwebs across disciplinary and institutional boundaries and underscore the relational work needed to address the associated power differentials. We argue that this web-weaving constitutes critical infrastructuring for climate justice teaching, learning, and action, and advocate for recognition and support of this indispensable form of labor. Symposium theme and overview Although climate change has typically been treated as a technical problem in science (e.g., Clark, 2024), there is growing recognition that it is a sociopolitical issue that has been driven by capitalism (Oreskes & Conway, 2010), colonialism (Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change, 2022), and other forms of environmental racism (Bullard, 1993). As a result, climate change exacerbates inequities that already exist for marginalized communities around the world, who face substantial barriers to protection and recovery. Young people today understand the severity of this issue; more than 85% of young people in the US experience climate anxiety (e.g., Lewandowski et al., 2024). While there is a need to address climate change and the attendant inequities, teaching that is focused solely on histories of oppression can be disheartening and deepen students’ sense of despair about the future. Recent studies have pointed to the power of collective action to help youth develop a sense of critical hope for the future (Schwartz et al., 2023), an approach to “partnering with purpose” that warrants increased attention in science learning spaces. Through engaging in collective action, students develop their own identities as knowers, visionaries, organizers, and builders who can affect social and environmental change. Rather than framing climate action narrowly in terms of mitigation and adaptation, we echo the stance of Isha Clarke, a youth activist from Oakland who explained, “Climate justice is about completely reimagining the way that we live on the planet... It is about collective liberation and dismantling systems of oppression in every way that they exist” (Milagro Henriquez, 2021). For models of this kind of work, we look to the “lifeworlds 2387 of Black, Indigenous, and Third World peoples” who have repeatedly survived world-ending events and bring long histories of collective struggle against injustice to “[build] the world anew” (Jadallah et al., 2025, pp. 9–11). By learning from and acting in solidarity with these communities who are again on the frontlines of injustice, we can work together to imagine new social systems. We aim to create opportunities for climate learning that are consequential (Hall & Jurow, 2015; Jurow et al., 2016), meaning that they move beyond the acquisition of knowledge and skills by considering the implications for students' lives. Climate and environmental learning are made consequential through spiderwebs, or constellations of people, institutions, and geographies. These spiderwebs bridge sites of formal learning and spaces of community-based action, fostering collaboration across different worldviews, knowledge-building practices, and timescales in ways that promote social transformation. We also shine a light on the importance of spiders, or the individuals who build and sustain relationships across learning ecologies to foster collective action. Sometimes the spiders are the youth themselves. Other times, the spiders are community organizers, parents, teachers, or school district administrators. Spiders do the complex work of navigating institutional norms, constraints, and power dynamics within and across learning environments, working both within and outside of existing institutional structures. The webs that they construct are dynamic and precarious; they are constantly torn, reattached, mended, and reformed, requiring substantial time, skill, and emotional labor to maintain. We position the spiderwebs describe d across our work as critical infrastructure for climate justice teaching, learning, and research. “Infrastructuring” reflects the activity required to “redesign components, relations, and routines of schools” in order to sustain learning innovations (Penuel, 2019, p. 1). Much of the current work on infrastructuring focuses on aspects of the schooling system, such as national standards, district policies, and instructional practices, as well as the people who enact them, such as teachers and administrators. In contrast, we build on the work of scholars who have suggested that infrastructuring for justice-focused innovation in schools requires the inclusion of a wider range of stakeholders, including those whose voices are not typically heard in formal schooling spaces (e.g., Calabrese Barton & Tan, 2022). Similarly, our papers argue that infrastructuring for climate justice requires designing for the inclusion of the experiences and worldviews of communities on the frontlines of climate change. In this symposium, we explore how climate justice learning can be made consequential by creating spiderwebs that bring diverse stakeholders together to work towards social change. Each paper offers a perspective on building these relational webs. Across papers, we present theoretical tools (paper 2), design principles (paper 3), instructional activities and practices (papers 1 and 4), and relational practices (paper 5). We demonstrate how these approaches support learners in building connections across timescales (papers 1 and 2), knowledge systems (papers 1, 2, 3), and school-and community-based learning sites (papers 3, 4, and 5). In this symposium, we position infrastructuring for climate and environmental justice as an essential aspect of “partnering with purpose.” Collectively, we will consider the extent to which the learning sciences community—as individuals, within institutions, and as a field —contributes to the work of web-building and how we can and should deepen that work. 2388 Paper 1: Weaving relational webs across generations and geographies Chris Jadallah, Michelle Hernandez Romero, Lauren Daus

## Theoretical framework

Building climate justice necessitates “looking back to look forward” (Winn & Tottenham, 2025, p. 70), where we weave relational webs that connect past, present, and future and that bring together human beings and the rest of the natural world in ways that recognize our mutually connected destinies (Simpson, 2017). Looking back provides an opportunity to  re-member the ancestral knowledge and practices from which we have been dis-membered via colonial histories (wa Thiong’o, 2009), which provide a compass for living in better relation with lands and waters (Takeuchi et al., 2024). We theorize learning as a  process of re-membering and weaving relational webs (Jurow et al., 2025; Meixi, 2022). In doing so, we draw on conceptualizations of learning as movement across home, school, and other community settings through which learners weave across these spaces, and across time, to “become conscious historical actors who begin to understand their present and future selves in relation to broader historical forces and spatial architectures” (Gutiérrez, 2020, p. 2). These perspectives support our work to design and study new forms of participation, learning, and relational becoming (Meixi, 2022) that are culturally sustaining and revitalizing (McCarty & Lee, 2014). In partnership with a community-based, urban farm in Los Angeles, we engaged in a process of social design experimentation (Gutiérrez & Jurow, 2016) with a team of young people, urban farmers, educators, and researchers. Our collective co-designed a series  of activities intended to support young people in weaving relational webs and re-membering family and community histories vis -à-vis their participation in farming and gardening practices. These activities took the format of a workshop series connected to an out-of-school gardening program for teenagers. During these activities-grounded in Ethnic Studies frameworks (e.g., Tintiangco-Cubales & Duncan-Andrade, 2021) - fourteen youth participants examined the legacies of colonialism and resistance with regard to land relations. With this framing, youth participants conducted oral history interviews with family members to uncover land-based relationships in their family histories. Youth participants were then invited to reflect on whether and how their work in  the gardening program was linked to these relationships and histories, and what it meant for climate futures.

To examine the contours of youth learning and re-membering as they participated in the practice of oral history, we collected ethnographic data focused on youth participation and sensemaking. Data sources include 1. Participant observations of oral history  workshops that were compiled into fieldnotes, 2. Reflective interviews with youth participants, and 3. Artifacts produced by young people over the course of the project. Data analysis occurred iteratively and recursively with data collection as part of th e broader co-design process. After the workshop series culminated, we engaged in a more formal pass of inductive coding to identify the different types of relationships present in our data-relationships that stretched across time and space, and to focus on the concept of re-membering. These include youth-family relationships, family-land relationships, and youth-land relationships, among others. Drawing on our coded data, we produced narrative portraits to understand how youth were situated in, a nd how they made sense of, these complex and intersecting social-ecological relationships in which they were participants.

Through their oral history interviews with parents, aunts, uncles, grandparents, and other family members, the youth participants in the workshop series simultaneously surfaced and strengthened relational webs. Participants reported learning for the first time about their familial relationships with specific plants, farming and gardening practices, and land, thus surfacing connections that were always present in their histories, even if buried. Most of the family members interviewed by  the youth had immigrated to the United States, and so many of these stories were about their homelands in places like Mexico, El Salvador, and the Philippines. After having surfaced these histories, participants also reported feeling closer to their family members and gaining new perspectives on familial dynamics. They also assigned new value to land-based practices such as gardening, with many articulating 2389 how they now felt that they were continuing the legacies of their families and communities through their work at the farm.

To illustrate these findings, we highlight the case of Diego, a 17-year-old Mexican-American teenager who lives in Los Angeles. Diego conducted an oral history with his mother, who was born in Mexico and immigrated to the United States. Prior to the oral history, Diego did not know that his mother’s family grew corn, squash, and peanuts in Michoacán, where they are from. Diego connected her story to his work in the urban agriculture program, saying, “It is important to know how to farm and how to grow vegetables, plants, and fruits yourself, especially right now, since temperatures are rising  … we’re going to have to go back to our roots, basically, we have to grow stuff ourselves so we can sustain ourself.” He further shared, “It’s also a good way to honor our ancestors because even my parents, my mom, her and her family, they owned land in Mexico and they used to grow stuff and they used to sell it and then used to eat it themselves. And I feel like that’s a good way to honor your ancestors, your parents, and your grandparents.” He tied this to themes of land and legacy introduced by facilitators during the workshop series.

## Scholarly significance

Diego’s story, and our findings more broadly, highlight the affordances of historically responsive and culturally revitalizing approaches to climate change education, where learning to live with climate change can be accomplished through re-membering the land-based knowledge and practices that have long allowed communities to navigate and survive times of rupture (Jadallah et al., 2025). Diego saw honoring ancestors as being important for learning to navigate a changing climate, illustrating the role of community-based knowledge in climate change education and adaptation. Such work also opens possibilities for generative conversations about how to support learners in interweaving  relational webs with one another to  build solidarities across places, communities, and contexts, and how to do so in ways that are accountable to the local histories of the places where they live and work.

Paper 2: Weaving webs of nature-culture relations across disciplines through a Histories of Places Framework Veronica Cassone McGowan, Symone Gyles, Elizabeth Starks, Carrie Tzou

## Theoretical framework

Justice-centered approaches to climate teaching and learning recognize the intersection of social theories and constructs in the study of natural systems (Graddy-Lovelace, 2017; Schell et al, 2020). As such, work within and across educational research projects that support s justice-centered learning around the climate crisis and its impacts requires not only examining the scientific aspects of phenomena, but also the complex socio-ecological components. When projects are composed of multiple stakeholders, including researchers, teachers, families, and community members, uncertainty about how to encompass all of these components in practice can be difficult as a result of different positionalities and lenses through which stakeholders come to the work. Developing an understanding of the relational webs that connect and situate climate and environmental learning within its scientific and socio-ecological context, including across human and more-than-human communities, is necessary (Kimmerer, 2013).

Within research projects, the spiders that build these relational webs and create infrastructures that advance climate justice learning are often positioned as individuals who support the work. In this paper, we examine how the History and Futures of Places framework (HFP; Learning in Places Collaborative, 2022), which guides individuals to consider a plurality of perspectives and approaches to justice-centered learning around the climate crisis, served as a spider that weaves foundations for approaches to climate change teaching and learning across settings, spanning eight timescales and multiple communities. These timescales include: 1) hydrogeologic time, 2) plant, animal, and soil time, 3) Indigenous peoples’ time, 4) nation-state time, 5) global time, 6) living ethical responsibilities and possibilities time, 7) movement, migration, and diaspora time, and 8) celestial time. Histories span lands, waters, plants, animals, and human communities over time, yet climate education in the U.S. is often taught in ways that r ender the powered and historical intersections of social and ecological systems invisible to learners and researchers (Learning in Places Collaborative, 2022). Here we ask: how does the Histories and Futures of Places Framework support and/or expand STEM faculty in weaving together multiple perspectives and timescales to guide ethical decision-making with and for the land, and to what ends, in their climate change teaching and learning?

2390 We use a case study method (Yin, 2018) to understand the learning trajectories of two STEM faculty partnered across several large-scale co-design efforts to create expansive and justice-centered models that weave relations between timescales and communities in climate change teaching and learning across settings. We collected multiple sources of evidence that included collaborative discussions about the nature and use of HFP in their teaching and research, artifacts of their adoption of HFP for their work across contexts, including multigenerational citizen science programs, public events, high school field-based programs, and undergraduate STEM course design.

Our case study faculty showed how leveraging the HFP framework infrastructured and operationalized timescales to guide ethical decision-making for human and more-than-human communities amid the climate crisis in their own contexts. We describe how each faculty conceptualized emergent actors that served as spiders for making visible plurality and power to guide ethical decision-making across diverse stakeholders. In our first case study, Alex, an interdisciplinary arts and sciences professor, integrated multiple timescales from the HFP framework to describe how carbon moves across time, surfacing shifts in how we situate humans as a part of large-scale biogeochemical cycles. In an undergraduate STEM course, Alex noted that humans are not just emitters of excess carbon dioxide, but also embodiments of the carbon cycle in which material passes from air to body to land for all species and entities. In this way, Alex weaved together relational stories of carbon that connect across socioecological time and communities and push us to reconceptualize the role of humans in climate cycles. In our second case study, Greg, a fisheries biologist, shared how the HFP framework enabled him to articulate a relational ontology that he called Species Time to support multispecies perspective taking as an embodied practice of socio-ecological decision-making. In a talk with local community members about the future of Kokanee salmon, Greg shared that, “Native Americans and Kokanee have lived here together since the retreat of the glacier over 10,000 years ago … in 2021, we found middle-run Kokanee in a l ocal stream. These salmon are historically important and culturally important and are still with us, which brings up ethical responsibilities and possibilities for how to [sustain populations and relationships] into the future.”

## Scholarly significance

Our work suggests that tools and frameworks can be designed as spiders that support climate justice work that upholds plurality and multispecies perspective taking both within and across communities. The HFP framework served as a tool that wove together just teaching about socio-ecological systems with more just approaches to engaging in research with communities amid the climate crisis in order to expand the networks of decision-making in places.

Paper 3: Worlding as spiders: Weaving partnerships to teach towards environmental and climate justice in a large urban school district Alejandra Frausto Aceves, Daniel Morales-Doyle, Kaleb Germinaro, Fabio Miranda, Michelle Rabkin

## Theoretical framework

Ginwright (2022) cautions against critical approaches that slip into ‘problem-loving,’ where more emphasis is placed on critique than hope, joy, and change-making. This slippage often begins by failing to account for theories of learning (Vossoughi & Gutiérrez, 2016). Pedagogical approaches that overemphasize disciplinary rigor are especially vulnerable in this regard because they often limit imagination (Torres Olave et al., 2025). In contrast, transdisciplinary STEM education supersedes disciplinary rigidity, making room for possible alternatives (Takeuchi et al., 2020). In climate change education, pedagogies of hope involve learning with perspectives of Black, Indigenous, and Third World c ommunities to ‘build the world anew’ (Jadallah et al., 2025).  Similarly, collective learning pushes past individualistic notions of learning to conceive scientific literacy as distributed across communities (Jadallah & Ballard, 2025). For infrastructures to support these kinds of educational efforts, we find it helpful to think with organic life-giving structures of the natural world, in addition to the concrete and steel infrastructures of built urban environments. Spiderweb anthropologies examine spiderworlds and spiderwebs as beautiful and precarious traps that set in motion recursive operations of double environmentalization, that is, “weaving worlds into existence at the same time as it recaptures existing worlds” (Jiménez, 2018, p. 54). In this paper, we examine the spiders weaving and consuming metaphorical spiderw ebs and their ecological, 2391 infrastructural, and entangled traps. We argue their threading and traps are materially and epistemologically consequential for new openings and possibilities for equitable trajectories of worlds. Urgently, and cautiously, we approach developing solidarities across district-community-university Research-Practice Partnerships (RPPs) with professional learning that can support grassroots projects operating in Chicago’s sacrifice zones. Alejandra and Daniel have been building networks through the Youth Participatory Science (YPS) collective that include teachers, out-of-school educators, school and district leaders, scientists, and community organizers (Morales-Doyle & Frausto, 2021; Morales-Doyle et al., 2025). Similarly, Kaleb, through his work with spatial geographies, Fabio, through his work with computer science visualizations, and Michelle, through her work with environmental science education, have been contributing tonetwork building in and beyond Chicago. We argue that these networks are soft infrastructures in Pinkard’s (2019) framing of healthy STEAM learning ecosystems, or threads in metaphorical spiderwebs in our conceptualization. We address how we act as metaphorical spiders threading webs and navigating relations hips across entities – school districts, community organizations, and academic institutions – to teach toward environmental  and climate justice. In this paper, we use participatory design research (Bang & Vossoughi, 2016) to explore different types of threads and tensions in the traps, such as entangled equity perspectives that emphasize access to disciplinary knowledge versus more expansive and transdisciplinary perspectives or individual achievement versus collective community-based learning.

## Scholarly significance

Cautiously urgent work requires taking the long path (Wallach, 2022). What spiders in the natural world may create or undo in hours or days, we conceive of and enact in a timescale of years. The process of looking back (recapturing) to move forward (weaving) allows us to enact new openings and possibilities influenced by our deep relations with each other as individuals and a collective. In this paper, we reflect on the weaving that will be recaptured and woven anew in the future. Paper 4: Youth learning to weave across formal and informal contexts Helen L. Fitzmaurice, Xochitl Cortez, Joseph D. Senn

## Theoretical framework

Working in community with others provides youth with a context to develop their epistemologies of action, a group of people with whom to engage, and an  opportunity to think through what it means to be  part of  a community. If climate justice necessitates a pedagogy that enables students to “[reimagine] the way that we live on the planet” and “[dismantle] systems of oppression in every way that they exist” (Milagro Henriquez, 2021), learning how to weave webs that both deepen connections (within and across people, histories, and philosophies) and move others to action is an important learning goal. Web-weaving is learned through practice. In our lived experience, and in published accounts of environmental justice activists (e.g., Ms. Margaret’s story from Madrigal, 2025), the first step in learning to engage in community-focused action is becoming organized oneself, often by being pulled in by an organizer to  an ongoing community struggle working towards collective change. Here, we tell the story of two educator-spiders who engage students in learning to weave. 2393 The authors of this contribution have collaborated  for multiple years through Oakland Teachers Advancing Climate Action (led by Helen, with Joe serving as key advisor, participant, and workshop facilitator), a collective of teachers committed to engaging students in local environmental justice action projects, and its collaboration with Frontline Catalysts (led by Xochitl), an organization committed to building power and leadership among Oakland youth who live on the frontlines of environmental racism. We compare designs aimed at engaging youth in learning to weave in different contexts: an AP Environmental Science classroom taught by Joe and an explicitly political youth leadership program led by Xochitl, both based in  Oakland, California. Across these settings, we compare (1) motives and stances behind instructional design, (2) instructional design itself, and (3) its impact on students, drawing on data from conversations and recorded interviews among authors, author field notes, meeting notes, co-authored artifacts (e.g., grant proposals, workshop slides), instructional materials, and student interviews.

Both Joe and Xochitl engage students in community action that requires students to act as spiders (building connections with each other and their community), but each frames their work differently. Joe’s framing of his goals is relational. Pushing back against W estern philosophies of individualism, Joe believes that classrooms should be deeply connected to  students’ communities and that the opportunity and challenge of a classroom teacher is to bring in as much of their students’ community as possible to help students learn and grow. Joe wants students to understand that they are part of, and can create change within, something greater than themselves. Xochitl’s framing of her goals is more explicitly political, informed by her experience engaging in organizing as a youth in East Oakland. Frontline Catalysts’ mission is to build the power and elevate the voices of youth living on the front lines of environmental racism  in Oakland. The organization’s theory of change is that by engaging youth in analyzing and telling their own stories through an ethnic studies framework emphasizing the intersectional nature of environmental and climate injustices, taking action alongside their comm unities through ongoing campaigns, and collectively reflecting on their experiences, students grow into advocates and organizers who understand how to build and sustain power.

Despite differences in framing, we see several parallels across Joe and Xochitl’s teaching. First, both Xochitl and Joe have deep connections across Oakland and the broader East Bay community, built over decades, enabling them to connect youth with members of their own communities working for environmental justice. Second, both engage students in telling their own stories and those of their families and attend to the development of community within the youth groups with whom they work. Third, while both car e deeply about youth power and agency, they do not ask youth to invent action from scratch. Instead, they embed youth, in ways that still afford agency, within ongoing community projects or campaigns where they learn the tactics of change. Joe does this through a semester-long project through which students co-design and implement an action with a community partner, requiring students to map, build on, and make new community connections. For example, as shown in Figure 2, Joe’s students mapped out their existing connections to elementary and middle schools they attended and those attended by friends or family members. Xochitl does this through campaign-based learning in which students engage in more traditional organizing tactics (e.g., canvassing neighbors, agitating peers) in the context of ongoing community struggles (e.g., related to utility rates, l ead contamination). Finally, we see parallels in student learning and affect: both Frontline Catalysts youth and Joe’s students report feeling closer to their community as a whole and gaining context about histories of oppression and ongoing struggles for environmental justice that shape our city.

## Scholarly significance

Starting from the premise that strengthening students’ ability to build and maintain community in order to create a more just future should be a learning goal, our work has implications for educators engaging youth in climate and environmental justice action. Educator-spiders take up the work of not just building curricular learning from students’ funds of knowledge (Moll et al., 1992) or highlighting students’ cultural community wealth (Yosso,

2005) in their teaching, but also using their webs and web-weaving practices to connect students more deeply

with their own communities and families. Second, educator-spiders build their webs over time. Finally, the organizing-as-pedagogy that educator-spiders engage in is responsive, not just to youth, but to a broader community context, which shifts with community priorities, partner capacity, and the larger (e.g., municipal, state, national) political context. This makes their work fundamentally incompatible with the current educational paradigm of encouraging teachers to engage in learning based in tested practices or “teacher-proof[ed]” curricula (Philip et al., 2025, p. 2505).

Paper 5: Tending the web: Sustaining relational infrastructures for climate justice Samuel Burmester, Emily V. Reigh, Helen L. Fitzmaurice

## Theoretical framework

Many studies (e.g., Schwartz, 2023) highlight the power of engaging students in climate and environmental action projects. These projects may be particularly impactful when they support collective action among a broad range of stakeholders, including community members engaged  in environmental advocacy and action.  Building spiderwebs of school-based and community-based actors working in solidarity is thus a  central aspect of infrastructuring (Penuel, 2019) for transformative climate action. Infrastructural web-weaving between diverse stakeholders is time-consuming and challenging work. Importantly, building relational and political infrastructures in the face of structural inequities in schools and communities requires explicit attention to power and positionality (Ba ng & Vossoughi, 2016; Calabrese Barton & Tan, 2022). Transformative climate action requires attention to epistemic hierarchies, the different goals and priorities that communities hold for themselves, and inequities in access to resources.  In this contribution, we explore the everyday labor of spiders building and tending webs that connect schools and communities, work that carries the weight of sustaining fragile infrastructures in inequitable systems. We draw on interviews with five teachers and five community partners who have participated in collaborations in K-12 classrooms to enact justice-focused climate action projects in the Greater San Francisco Bay Area. Participants represented a wide range of racial, ethnic, and socioeconomic backgrounds. Most teachers worked in schools that serve minoritized students. Interviews with teachers and partners addressed the range of collaborations they had engaged in, how they conceptualized their work, the nature of the collaborations, and the successes and challenges they encountered.  Our analysis was iterative and inductive (Saldaña, 2016). First, we developed a preliminary coding scheme that identified the different activities, or “infrastructuring” work, that participants engaged in.  Then, we analyzed the multiple purposes of these activities, ultimately identifying four modes of webtending: spinning, sustaining, leveraging, and repairing. Here, we highlight sustaining, the most frequently mentioned mode.  We present representative examples of both teacher and partner perspectives on the actions that sustain webs and the challenges that they face in their sustaining work. Participants described several practices that helped sustain collaboration s, including building trust, creating new connections, coordinating logistics, sharing expertise, and working within and around institutional constraints. Partners described trying to ease burdens on teachers, with one explaining, “I want to take something off [teachers’] plate by making it a little bit easier for them to do what they’re doing.” Both teachers and partners also emphasized the importance of connections  that could expand opportunities and stabilize collaboration s. As one teacher explained, “Once we talked to one organization, then they were like, oh, like, you should talk to this person … we just need this, like, connector piece.” Participants further framed this work as a way to address  inequitable access to resources and power. One partner described their organization’s mission as “providing [frontline communities] resources, training … in being able to address these systems of harm.” 2395 At the same time, participants underscored challenges related to the instability of school-based collaboration. Teachers described high staff turnover, shifting program priorities, limited time, and weak or inconsistent administrative support as persistent barriers to sustaining partnership work. One teacher stated, “It’s taxing every year, trying to search for the next collaborator … you finally have someone good and then they leave, and you have to start over.” Another commented, “It’s hard because we don’t really get time for this … on top of everything else … I’m lucky I can build it into my elective, but most teachers can’t. So it ends up being, like, whoever cares enough does it. And that’s not sustainable.” Several also underscored that administrators valued the work in principle but provided little support in practice. These accounts suggest that sustaining collaboration often depends on teachers taking on additional labor in the face of structural barriers, increasing demands, and ongoing burnout.

Taken together, these accounts suggest that sustaining collaborations can involve rerouting expertise, resources, and political attention toward communities that have long been marginalized from these resources. The fact that both schools and community-based organizations are insufficiently resourced makes sustaining this work especially complex, yet participants’ accounts suggest it remains critical to advancing collective action toward more just futures.

## Scholarly significance

Sustaining partnerships is a continuously negotiated practice at the intersection of trust building, resource redistribution, and logistical coordination. Its effects are tangible —new air filters in a school, new data for civic debate, new habits of partnership—yet remain vulnerable to institutional churn and policy shifts. In an inequitable urban area shaped by decades of redlining, industrial pollution, and uneven investment, sustaining webs of partnership is an act of repairing structural exclusions. The  seemingly mundane acts of providing buses for fieldwork, securing stipends, or teaching data-collection methods redistribute the means of participation — material, relational, and epistemic —so that frontline youth can help shape their climate futures. From this view, justice-oriented climate education does not depend on breakthroughs or new policies; it can be advanced by deliberately valuing and supporting the everyday connective work that already exists in classrooms and community partnerships.

We underscore the need to recognize  and support the relational labor that creates transformative and enduring climate justice infrastructures. In doing so, we extend conceptualizations of infrastructuring as an ongoing and justice-oriented activity (Calabrese Barton & Tan, 2022; Penuel, 2019) by foregrounding the relational, material, and political practices that too often remain invisible (Bang & Vossoughi, 2016). Without recognition and redistribution of this labor, climate justice partnerships risk repro ducing structural barriers in schools even as they seek to transform them. Recognizing that such work is central, not peripheral, can guide districts, funders, and researchers to invest in the infrastructures of participation: stable partnerships, local knowledge, time for coordination, and capacity-building for both teachers and youth. Session overview The late sociologist Bruno Latour’s (1987) concept of the black box has had significant purchase beyond the field of science and technology studies. In essence, the term refers to how complex actors and networks become so effectively automated into a simple input-output process that the dense ecology of artifacts, labor and relations “act as one”—a durable, undisputed whole (p. 131). As video has become commonplace within learning sciences methodologies, we argue there is an urgent need to revisit analysts’ assumptions, particularly as the technological advances that have made the practices of recording and sharing video so ubiquitous have also made large-scale surveillance and social repression possible. Informed by these contexts, this symposium aims to consider the 2399 politics of the sociotechnical—a term that acknowledges the social and technological as interdependent systems — of contemporary video-analytic methodologies.

When video analysis was first established as a significant part of the methodology of the learning sciences, time and intellectual care was invested in accounting for the labor and logistics of producing video records of learning settings, as well as in re-animating those records for analysis and representation (e.g., Jordan & Henderson, 1995; Ochs, 1979). While these methods are now commonplace in learning sciences’ scholarship— effectively black boxed—both video technology and the cultural landscape of video viewing have changed significantly since they were established. The capabilities afforded by new video recording technologies have changed how cameras co-exist within learning settings, including mobile and wearable technologies, higher datastorage capabilities, and affordances of 360 o or multi-camera views. These capabilities create opportunities to examine learning in novel ways, such as the role of movement and bodies and learning within ambulatory sequences (Marin, 2020), learning on the move (Hall et al., 2020; Marin et al., 2020), and ensemble learning (Ma & Hall, 2018). The proliferation of cameras within consumer products such as phones, personal computers and laptops, as well as the accessibility of video-conferencing technologies have also created expanded opportunities to connect with different populations of learners, such as Keifert’s partnerships with COVID-cautious families (e.g., Gentry et al., 2024).

At the same time, emerging tools such as Artificial Intelligence (AI) are entering the video analytic workflow (e.g., Davidsen & Steier, 2025). Ruha Benjamin’s (2019) Race after Technology  offers tools for the learning sciences to critique emergent processes—automatic transcription and forms of tracking (eye -, emotion-, or gesture-tracking)—as well as more established assumptions —that video viewing provides privileged access to knowledge about phenomena or that the analyst can be separated from the analysis. This symposium further argues for explicit methodological attention to the sociotechnical politics of AI-use with video analysis. AI-automation is in many ways the ultimate black boxing, further limiting scrutiny into already opaque procedures. We argue that alongside the momentum within the learning sciences to acknowledge how our analytic practices operate within asymmetries of power (The Politics of Learning Writing Collective, 2017), the field of learning sciences must engage with the assumptions that AI tools embed in their automations, in addition to examining video-based methodologies that do not use AI tools. We aim to contribute to video-analytic methodologies, using this term to emphasize the interrelated nature of theory, methods, and ontology, or what is taken as empirical reality. With this in mind, symposium authors address different foci, or different assumptions related to these analytic layers. Lastly, as video analysis has become a staple of learning sciences’ methodology, we call attention to video analytic traditions and the production of academic canons. For the learning sciences to aspire towards equitable and justice-centered learning, as a field we must resist the concentration of status and resources in academia, including citational practices that conserve a “core” of the learning sciences (Learning Towards Justice Team, 2025). To resist this, we aim to highlight the heterogeneity of perspectives, the long-established genealogies of power-explicit forms of video analysis across traditions, and how this might inform the future of video analysis in light of shifting sociotechnological ecosystems. This emphasis on diverse perspectives not only enriches our understanding but also inspires us to continue learning and growing in our field. Our symposium aims to surface the different ways video-analytic methodologies are being tinkered with in the learning sciences, with significant implications for the types of learning, partnering, and knowledge that our field advances. Layers of analysis, layers of ideology: A multi-layered approach to IA Andria Ellis, D. Teo Keifert 2402 Video analytic practices are becoming a common-place tool within the Learning Sciences, particularly through Interaction Analysis (IA, [Jordan & Henderson, 1995]). The ability to record and re-visit interactions as well as replay moments for multiple viewers is vital for video-centered analytical methods. Additionally, by analyzing data beyond spoken content, a video-data centered methodology can further capture context and cues that may be missed in traditional methodologies, such as through taking aspects  of the interaction such as tone, gestures, and the pacing and/or tone of speech into account, providing essential insights into the ways individuals are navigating situations and perceptions. These affordances make video a vital methodological resource. Yet, video is often preconceived as a “neutral” record, ignoring ideological positions and assumptions that are engrained within the record. This paper argues that layering multiple forms of video-centered analysis helps uncover ideological dimensions of interaction and identity in ways that traditional video-analysis methods alone may not provide. In this poster, we present three such layers.

The first layer, traditional Interaction Analysis, emphasizes interaction across modes of talk, gesture and other artifacts, assuming that learning and meaning-making are locally co-constructed in the moment (Goodwin, 2018; Jordan & Henderson, 1995; Erickson, 2004). Building from this, the next layer we utilized is relevance through a collection of related points of talk or interaction. Critically, this layer of analysis moves beyond single moments, to collections of moments across space and time. The third layer was a scaled analysis (e.g., Daniel et al., 2023), used to better understand how individuals’ perceptions of broader identity notions and paradigms are shaped by broader sociocultural ideologies and discourses. The sequential layering of analytical methods aided in surfacing distinct ideological dimensions. Through traditional IA, we illuminate the moment-to-moment construction of meaning. The implementation of the relevance-based analysis underscored how participants themselves shaped meaning throughout different points in time, while the scaled analysis highlighted how these meanings mesh within broader sociocultural discourses. Toget her, complementary layers demonstrate how methodological actions are not neutral, but instead carry assumptions about what underlying ideologies are at play as meaning-making and identity-discussions take place.

Critical Interaction Analysis (Philip & Gupta, 2020) provides further orientation for this work, situating interaction within claims of power and learning as co-constructed through various dynamics (Fanghanel et al., 2016; Potter, 2023). By layering tradit ional IA, relevance-based analysis, and scaled analysis, this paper demonstrates how layered methodologies can act to both provide light while also highlighting the complexity of ideological dimensions within video-based research. This approach works to provide a building block on top of current video-research methods, moving to observe how analytic practices enact particular views of identity and meaning-making. Through this, this paper emphasizes the importance of methodological reflexivity and layered analytical processes within video research. Through analytical layering, these methods act to surface potentially embedded assumptions, paradigms and beliefs.

Charting the theoretical terrain of 360° video: Revisiting the politics of recording and viewing Elizabeth Metts, Candice Love Video analysis has long been central to the learning sciences. Foundational texts remind us that video is never a neutral mirror. Jordan and Henderson (1995) described video as a resource for interaction analysis, enabling repeated inspection of social int eraction, while emphasizing its partiality: analytic practice is structured by what and how we record. Hall (2012) argued that video-recording itself is theory-laden. Technical arrangements delete and restore aspects of activity, shaping what becomes analy zable. Derry et al. (2010) extended this work, highlighting reflexivity and transparency in camera placement, selection, and circulation. We revisit these foundations in light of 360° video, drawing on two research –practice partnerships that capture rich, multi-perspective video records in mathematics classrooms. The Playful Learning Project works with elementary teachers and students to study children’s mathematical engagement. Teaching Amidst Uncertainty 2405 partners with secondary teachers to document instructional dynamics in complex classrooms. Both projects share a commitment to producing rich records of children’s mathematical activity and to supporting teacher reflection. In both projects, 360° cameras were placed at student tables to capture small-group mathematical activity. Rather than seeking a panoramic record of the whole classroom, 360° video was used to make peer interactions visible in spaces often outside a teacher’s or researcher’s view. Placement remained consequential. A camera centered within a group provides access to gesture, material use, and collaboration, but backgrounds interactions across groups or teacher –student exchanges elsewhere. Following Hall (20 12), deletion and restoration persist: 360° video-recording reorganizes what is visible but does not erase the constitutive role of technical arrangements.

With 360° video, analysis is shaped not only by what is recorded but by how footage is rendered. Analysts may use equirectangular projections, pannable interfaces, or VR headsets—each producing a distinct experience. This complicates Jordan and Henderson’s  (1995) account of collaborative replay: rather than a single record around which a group can gather, 360° requires negotiating where to direct attention and how to orient to simultaneous perspectives. In our work, immersive viewing heightened awareness of  complexity but often overwhelmed viewers, while pannable interfaces afforded more targeted entry points. “Viewing” itself becomes a methodological choice with theoretical implications.

Across both projects, 360° video has been valuable for capturing simultaneous small-group activity and distributed interactions that escape a single vantage point. Yet technical costs—data size, rendering, timeliness — remain significant. More importantly, immersive visibility raises heightened responsibilities. Following Derry et al. (2010), we emphasize transparency and care in decisions about consent, circulation, and storage. Expanding what can be seen risks reinscribing surveillance logics, particularly in schools serving historically marginalized communities. Adopting 360° video requires explicit commitments to equity, participant dignity, and codetermination with teachers and schools. By revisiting foundational theories of video through the lens of 360° technologies, our work shows how immersive recording both extends and unsettles assumptions about placement, viewing, and analysis.

Power, standpoint, and the limits of automated video analysis in LS Dylan Paré Interaction analysis of video data is a central method in the learning sciences for examining participants’ momentto-moment, emergent meaning-making (Jordan & Henderson, 1995). In recent years, artificial intelligence (AI) tools have garnered attention as  ways to automate and expedite this labor-intensive process. Machine learning algorithms candetect patterns in large datasets, enabling identification and categorization that direct researchers toward “hot spots” of data. Generative AI takes it a step fur ther by “explaining” its categorizations, presenting outputs that appear knowledgeable and neutral. Yet these tools are not neutral, and their use raises questions of power, standpoint, and accountability.

Feminist standpoint theorists (Collins, 1986; Haraway, 1988; Harding, 1991) showed that claims to objectivity often align with systems of power, reproducing dominant ways of knowing under the guise of neutrality. Their critiques exposed how academic resear ch privileged white, masculinist epistemologies. Today, dominant epistemologies are being embedded in automated tools (Benjamin, 2019; Buolamwini & Gebru, 2018; Costanza-Chock, 2020; Eubanks, 2018; Williams, 2024). The veneer of objectivity in generative AI conceals that these tools are pre-trained on datasets that overrepresent dominant perspectives and bodies while underrepresenting and discriminating against marginalized groups. Opacity compounds the problem. AI tools function as black boxes, outputting sentiment analyses or thematic clusters without revealing how results are produced. Human researchers, by contrast, are accountable to peers and must explain their analytic decisions to establish credibility. Absent such transparency, the use of AI risks narrowing analysis toward interpretations that reinforce dominant knowledge. Further, because training data already encodes bias, outputs may diminish or erase the perspectives of participants whose experiences fall outside dominant patterns.

Large language models add another concern. As Bender et al. (2021) argue, these models are “stochastic parrots,” producing (or parroting) convincing text without genuine understanding. When applied to qualitative data, the results may look rigorous but lack the interpretive depth that comes from the labor of human-led analysis, characterized by noticing, questioning, and reflexively engaging with data. Some advocate “human-in-the-loop” approaches, where AI produces a first-pass analysis for researchers to refine. Yet this too risks reconfiguring how we see. Goodwin (1994) demonstrated that “professional vision” shapes how experts notice and categorize ph enomena. Introducing AI into analysis alters that vision, channeling interpretation along narrower paths structured by the tool’s training. This narrowing is also evident in machine 2406 learning’s preprocessing of large datasets, where the machine’s initial categorizations shape what the researcher examines further.

For the learning sciences, the stakes are high. What happens to our commitments to professional vision and credibility when analytic labor is outsourced to tools that conceal their workings? What does it mean for our field if we accept outputs that perform  efficiency but subtly reinscribe inequity? And what alternative tools or practices might we imagine that support, rather thandisplace, careful, situated, and accountable research? Reconstituting video through choreographic practices Lauren Vogelstein Jordan & Henderson (1995) suggested video for analyzing learning as an interactional achievement because they saw minimally edited video as the most naturalistic way to reconstitute an event. Since then, video recordings have become a commonly used immutable mobile (Latour, 1987) in the learning sciences—they can be played in almost any location, replayed over and over at multiple speeds, and frozen at any point. Reconstituting events in video records entails “learning how to look and listen” (Erickson, Dorn, & Artiles, 2017) alone and in groups, since people look and listen differently (Goodwin, 1994; Stevens & Hall, 1998). Ultimately, what analysts learn to see and hear is wrestled down into a two-dimensional, static representation. Looking and listening, however, are not the only consequential ways events can (or should) be reconstituted. As a dancer and learning scientist myself, I have spent the past seven  years collaborating with artists and scholars experimenting with choreographic practices to inform the design and theories of embodied learning (e.g., Ma & Hall, 2018) can support new ways of engaging with STEM concepts (e.g., developing movement rules for human and computational dancers/agents to perform [Wagh, Vogelstein, & Champion, 2024]), then they also might be needed to make sense of these new forms of participation. It was exciting and opened up so many new possibilities. This symposium brings together a contrasting set of empirical contexts and theoretic al lenses onto the study of teacher transitional moments with the goal of pinning down some core characteristics of teachers’ transitions and boundary crossings, how teachers negotiate and learn in those moments, and how to better support them in this work. Across their career lifespan, teachers experience a variety of boundary crossings and transitions (e.g. Hutchinson et al., 2023; la Velle, 2020) that are consequential for their learning and practice of the profession. Preservice teachers (PST), for exampl e, find themselves firmly positioned as students and apprentices, but transition back-and-forth to teaching positions as they engage in field-teach experiences. Subsequently, as they move into their induction year, novice teachers are called to join a new community of practice and must figure out how to fashion their own professional practices in a newly gained position as newcomer teachers (Lave & Wenger, 1991). As they move through their careers, teachers participate in various professional development activities that “throw” them into positions and roles that are ever changing and dependent on the communities of practice in which they engage. Whichever transition and/or boundary crossing we might consider, it is undoubtedly a site of substantive teacher learning. Many other examples and scenarios can be arrayed, and they are easy to imagine when we attend to teachers’ everyday professional life in various spaces and places, and across timescales of participation (e.g., Bastian et al., 2022; Lilach, 2020; T wyford et al., 2017).

Across all the scenarios above, the transitions and boundary crossings that teachers experience show somewhat different character and operate in somewhat different ways. This is because for each of these transitions across places, positions and/or communit ies, different norms, values, practices, and relationships of power must be negotiated and learned. To understand these transitions, their similarities and differences, is to develop a better understanding of teacher learning and professional growth. The studies in this symposium offer windows into different transitionary moments in teacher career lifespan, and each contribution demonstrates a different way that future and in-service teachers negotiate times of career and personal transition, with a particular eye toward the role of power, more equitable and just practices, and intentional design. We rely on different theoretical and methodological tools as a means of painting a nuanced picture of how transitions within the lifecycle of a teaching caree r shape teacher learning and how leaning into transitionary periods and contexts can make the profession more comprehensible and better prepare participants for the work of teaching. Similarly, we apply these lenses and tools to investigate a wealth of empirical contexts: final presentations in a capstone teacher education course, discussion-based workshops for preservice teachers, debriefs of a community of practice of responsive and equity-centered science educators, and the design and execution of a novel curricular unit with experienced classroom teachers. Collectively, by bringing together somewhat contrasting empirical contexts and theoretical lenses onto the study of transitional moments, we ask: What are some core characteristics of teachers’ transit ions and 2412 boundary crossings? How do teachers navigate those moments and how/what do they learn in these processes? How can teacher educators better support them in this work? The first piece (by Novelli and Azevedo) highlights the way that preservice teachers engage in sensemaking during the time of constant boundary crossing represented by PSTs engaging in field placements as part of teacher preparation classes. The authors use discourse analysis to reveal the way that an actor was taken from the context of PST-as-teacher into PST-as-student as a way to grapple with contradictions between the two contexts.

The second study (by Rubin) focuses on outcomes of a program intended to smooth the transition from PST to novice teacher. Through summer professional development activities that involve viewing video of expert teachers, PSTs are guided through sensemaking  activities intended to expose them to issues and reflective skills that ordinarily would not be developed until later in their career lifespan. These skills are particularly important as they have been shown to be key in educators designing for equity in their classroom. The third contribution (by Jaber et al.) also focuses on the PST to novice teacher transition. The researchers describe the ways that educators negotiate entering the teaching profession from university-based teacher education programs in a particularly politically charged context. They describe the ways that creativity, small acts of insubordination, and staying focused on critical-care focused pedagogies are essential for teacher persistence in a teaching environment the study participants describe as “brutal.” The final piece (by Luehmann and Wilson) describes the experience of expert educators engaging in pedagogical transitions—moving from a teacher-oriented pedagogical focus to one that forefronts student-led sense making. These educators experienced tensions during this first foray into transitioning to a novel placeand community-based teaching model as their own ideologies of their role as a teacher were challenged. The study reveals the ways that experienced teachers negotiate their self-authored identity as subject matter experts in order to embrace more student-focused responsive pedagogies. Azevedo Teacher education programs are filled with a multitude of small scale transitions and boundary crossing events as the PSTs move between the university classroom into K-12 classrooms. Teacher education often focuses on supporting PST sensemaking by linking the events of the K-12 classroom to the teacher preparation program. In the literature we see a variety of collaborative conversations where teachers at all career stages learn through instances when an actor who is directly not part of the teacher-as-learner community plays a central role in teacher sensemaking and reflection. These non-present actors are subjected to the same social authoring and meaning making practices as other participants with one crucial difference: the actor is not physically present. In our study, this actor is a teacher who hosted four different groups of PSTs (Ms. R), allowing them to teach a practice lesson in her room. While she was a large part of the teaching experience for the six PSTs, she never entered the university classroom physically. We seek to understand by what processes and to what extent Ms. R was communally constructed as a discursive object used for PST sensemaking and ideological negotiation. We situate this work in a socio-cultural view of learning fore fronting the importance of identity as prismatic and changing (Gholson and Martin, 2014). We use Holland et al. (1998) idea of figured worlds as culturally and socially constructed communities defined by the activities, powered structures and norms enacted by the members-to guide our understanding of how the primary PST identities of student  and teacher are connected. Horn et al. (2008) applies the figured worlds framework through the conceptualization of two figured worlds in which preservice teachers (PSTs) operate in-PST as educator and PST as student. Horn et al. (2008) theorize that key knowledge acquisition and negotiation happens when these two figured worlds come into contact with each other. In the educator figured world, when fore fronting their identity as educators, PSTs hold a position of authority, able to determine what knowledge is disseminated and how. In the learning figured world, where PSTs forefront their identity of student, they interpret the events of the teaching figured 2413 world through interactions with other players within the broader school community including other educators, mentors, administrators, and teacher educators/ professional development educators. Our data comes from the final reflections in the capstone course for the teacher education program. Through the cohort model of a teacher preparation program, our preservice teachers developed shared norms and perspectives. The teaching figured world was one in which the PSTs temporarily taught the 3-day lesson as a guest in an experienced teacher’s (cooperating teacher) middle school or high school classroom. The learning figured world for the PSTs was characterized by participation in a semester-long capstone course at a large public university in the southwestern United States. The specific learning figured world this study occurs in is the four days during which PST presentations on their perceived successes and failures while teaching. When sharing their experiences, PSTs brought physical representations of their classroom teaching in the form of sharing videos, as well as discursive representations by sharing student and cooperating teacher names and stories of individual interactions. Video recordings and transcripts of the presentations and discussions that followed were analyzed to map the substrate transformations of a single discursive object used by the community of practice for collective sensemaking. Using methods from discourse analysis (Johnstone, 2018) and interaction analysis (Jordan & Henderson, 1995) we trace the way that Ms. R was successively brought from the teacher-figured world of teaching into the learning-figured world, and then transformed and constructed into a tool for sensemaking beyond her physical self. We focus on a reflective assignment that acted as an intersection point for PST identities as students and teachers. During these presentations and subsequent discussions, PSTs brought events from the teaching figured world into the learning figured world.  Four groups (2 independent and 2 pairs) of PSTs in the course were working under the same cooperating teacher-Ms. R-at City High, teaching different sections of freshman biology. Over the course of the four presentations, Ms. R was given, by the communi ty of practice (PSTs and teacher educators), an identity that extended beyond her actual self, as signified by phrases such as “she sounds like the kind of person who…”. As PSTs and teacher educators heard more stories of Ms. R, they began to chime in about their own experiences in classrooms and interpretations of events described. We call this constructed identity Ms. R`. Ms. R` functioned as a discursive object (Foucault, 1966) that was repeatedly activated and built as separate from the physical Ms. R that went through several transformations as elements of her teaching ideologies were communally authored.

For example, the discursive object of Ms. R goes through a series of transformations with regards to her values around classroom management. One PST who taught in her classroom begins by describing Ms. R as someone who is “very much like no downtime” (Abby, 4/3/2023). This is reiterated the next class period by Morgan, who also taught with Ms. R, stating “But then at the end Ms. R was like, you have five minutes left over, that's too much. They should really go up to the bell” (4/10/2023). This aspect of Ms. R is taken up by other PSTs who did not teach in Ms. R’s class during the discussion portion of the presentations: “ Based on what Oak said and what I've heard about Ms. R, [it] sounds like she has these very strict expectations [about] how she likes her classrooms specifically” (Mary, 4/10/2023). This generalization marks the beginning of the discursive attribution of characteristics to what is now Ms. R’. This statement of Ms. R` as someone who is particular about her classroom then goes through several additional transformations. One of note moves Ms. R` further away from Ms. R when her identity is adjusted by another PST to read: Ms. R` is the kind of teacher who values her classroom expectations over her students. This occurs when Cliffe states “ If a teacher would say, ‘we just want you working into the bell’, that would devalue my thought of the work I'm doing. [I would think] I'm not actually doing anything useful. I'm just fulfilling Ms. R’s wishes and therefore I resent her. Basically” (4/10/2023). Ms. R` then undergoes additional transformations by a teacher educator to an amended: Ms. R` is the kind of teacher who values her classroom expectations over her students because of the socio-political power dynamics at play in schools: “Some administrators think a great classroom is one where everybody's seated and quiet. And that may be the vibe that she got from whoever is doing her observations and deciding whether she gets promotions or not….And so sometimes I think teachers are kind of caught between a rock and a hard place and that may not be where they would demonstrate that on their own.” (Dr. MT, 4/10/2023). 2414 Ms. R’ is at various times discursively described as “scary”, “safety oriented”, “Someone who doesn’t care about her students”, “Someone who is disliked by her students”, “a bad communicator” and many other things. She is used by PSTs to reason about their  own challenges with student-teacher relationships, the challenges of not being a fully qualified teacher, peer-to-peer support and advice, and teacher educator reflection.

Narratives involving Ms. R became not just a unifying experience for the preservice teachers who taught in her classroom, but an important element in the sensemaking processes of other preservice teachers who had never met her. Understanding the implicatio ns and processes of the transference and discursive construction of actors from the teaching figured world to the learning figured world by PSTs is important for guiding teacher educators in ways to best support PSTs at the end of their certification journey. The way that Ms. R is brought from the teaching figured world into the learning figured world and transformed into Ms. R’ provides key insights into the importance of group discussion, storytelling, and embracing community of practice based perspectives of teacher education programs. Further, purposeful investigation into the creation and transformations of such discursive objects could reveal the ways the socio-political nature of teaching is taken up through affect within PST communities of practice (Avraamidou, & Torres Olave, 2025) and offer guidance for teacher educator use of principled improvisation to support teacher learning (Philip, 2019). Exploring how preservice teachers’ framing of teaching practice shapes their interpretations of events Ethan Rubin The field of teacher education continues to grapple with the essential skills and dispositions that novice teachers need to make the transition from preservice teacher (PST) to novice teacher. How teacher educators frame the work of teaching, the manner in which they make practice visible and accessible, and the abilities they prompt PSTs to develop, all reflect theories and commitments about the nature of teaching. Teacher educators use a variety of tools to provide PSTs with opportunities to access the intricacies of teaching practice, such as videos (Gaudin & Chalies, 2015), rehearsals (Kazemi et al., 2015), and artifact analysis (Darling-Hammond, 2014). These approaches have particularly been shown to support preservice teachers in developing practices of responsive and equitable instruction (e.g. Kang & van Es, 2019) and therefore hold promise in terms of preparing teachers to view their work as intimately bound up with social justice concerns in schooling (Kavanagh & Danielson, 2020; Philip, 2019). By engaging PSTs with these topic before they have their own classrooms, teacher educators can help smooth the transition into the classroom.

At the same time, however, preservice teachers enter their credential programs with preexisting ideas about the nature of teaching and the practices it entails. Their conceptions of teaching practice, which frame their interpretations of classroom events, are shaped by their experiences as students and by social or cultural narratives about the teaching profession (e.g. Stenberg et al., 2014). Research has found that these frames are persistent (Rubin & van Es, 2023), even among in-service teachers (Louie et al., 2021). Moreover, PSTs’ frames for understanding teaching are further shaped by implicit messages in their field placements that may conflict with the model of teaching promoted in university programs (e.g. Ronfeldt, 2021). These tensions suggest tha t in the process of preparing preservice teachers to enter the profession, teacher educators must attend to the frames that PSTs apply to interpret teaching practice and consider how they change and develop as PSTs proceed through their credential program. To better understand this aspect of teacher learning, we ask two research questions: how do PSTs frame the work of teaching when interpreting classroom events? How, if at all, does engaging in reflection and analysis of teaching change PSTs’ conceptions of teaching practice? This study is situated in a project called the Responsive Teaching Summer Fellowship, in which six PSTs participated in a series of five 3-hour workshops involving group discussions and reflective activities. Between these workshops, participants completed four web-based modules consisting of video case studies of experienced teachers. Data consist of video recordings and transcripts of the group discussions that debriefed each module, which comprise 6 discussions averaging 20 minutes each. This study employs inductive qualitative coding (Locke 2415 et al, 2016) to examine how participants interpreted the representations contained in the modules and drew connections to experiences in their field placements. Using methods of rhetorical analysis (Feldman et al., 2016), I developed enthymemes, or implied syllogism statements, that illustrated how participants expressed their conceptions of teaching practice through the way they interpreted events. Comparing patterns in these enthymemes revealed that each participant employed particular forms of framing to interpret events. Based on these patterns, I created profiles of participants’ framing tendencies and examined points of confluence and contrast.

Though each participant demonstrated their own ways of framing the work of teaching, their tendencies fell into two broad categories. First, Fatima, Brian, and Melanie demonstrated a similar tendency to frame the work of teaching as navigating ambiguity and considering potential effects of their actions. For example, Fatima reflected on an incident in her classroom by asking herself, “what message did I unconsciously send?” In another instance, she noted the nuance of interpreting classroom activity, commen ting that “you have to notice what's happening within it, not just ‘are they working, or are they not.’ It's not binary, there's more to it.” Brian also emphasized the importance of navigating ambiguities in the moment of instruction. In response to an example from the video case studies, he narrated the teacher’s decisions as a series of dilemmas: “she wants to make him valued and respected, but also has to make sure that others aren’t being disrupted. Also, not tempt him to be distracted too much, but then how do you accommodate his specific needs?” In the same vein, he commented on his student teaching experiences by saying, “sometimes [students] look like they're doing fine, but they're not. But sometimes they are doing fine and you can almost disrupt it by popping in.” Melanie was especially focused on ensuring that students had positive socio-emotional experiences, but like Fatima and Brian, she noted that “there's no one strategy, there's no one-size-fits-all.” Overall, these three participants appear to frame teaching as interpretive work that requires self-reflection and attention to how students experience interactions in the classroom. Ellen, Ariana, and Kristen, on the other hand, were more focused on efficiency and effectiveness as the measure of a “good” teacher, and they made efforts to pursue those traits as they developed their own teaching practice. Ellen summarized her approach b y saying, “you'll see a student struggling and be like, okay, let me try to solve that. That solve might not work. Then you try another solve and that solve works. I think it's just building a toolkit of quick solves that worked in the past.” Ariana made similar points when she expressed her admiration for a teacher in one of the video case studies: “She's able to notice what they're doing and help them focus on their skill building rather than getting caught up in details. She's like, this isn't important,  let's redirect, let's focus. Within 30 seconds, she's like, problem? Fixed. Problem? Fixed.” Kristen also prioritized effectiveness, with a pronounced emphasis on content learning. In response to a video of a teacher providing a visual aid to a student, she commented, “the obstacle of not being able to multiply the numbers correctly interferes with their understanding of surface area.  So by providing that resource to bridge the gap, she enhances their understanding of a concept.” That focus on teaching content in an organized and structured manner appeared in her reflections on her student teaching as well, shown in statements like “if I provide students a guideline of what content I expect them to focus on, then I could expect that they're going to focus on that.” These three PSTs illustrate a more structured and purpose-driven framing of teaching practice; while more flexible than a “banking model” approach (see Freire, 2017), these comments imply a greater degree of control on the teacher’s part than the interpretive framing applied by the other group of PSTs.

Across the program, participants developed their ability to make sense of teaching events through video and artifact analysis and reflecting on their student teaching experiences. In other work, we demonstrate that this group of PSTs cultivated more advanced sensemaking practices over the course of their experience in the program, moving from direct comparisons at the start to later infer principles that emerged across multiple examples and contexts. Although these participants developed more nuanced and complex interpretations of teaching events over the course of the program, in many instances they continued applying the same framing across their sensemaking. For instance, Ellen reacted to the first module as a source of concrete teacher moves she could reproduce, saying that “it’s good watching teachers’ tricks and then watching them reflect on what worked or didn't.” After module 3, she was making more nuanced observations about “how cumulative it is building off your knowledge of your students.” Nonetheless, in the last session she maintained her frame that the goal of teaching is to “home in on what works.” Maintaining this frame of efficiency and “what works” indicates that the conceptions of teaching that PSTs begin with may persist in their framing of  events, even as they are introduced to alternative framings of teaching and their sensemaking increases in complexity and nuance. 2416 These findings suggest that while some PSTs begin their credential program already primed to frame teaching as a responsive and relational discipline, others prioritize effectiveness in accomplishing predetermined goals-a framing that has been associated  with efforts in practice-based teacher education. While not intrinsically

problematic, this framing of teaching practice has been theorized as a potential barrier to prioritizing social justice in the classroom (Kavanagh & Danielsen, 2020; Philip et al., 2019). This study provides an exploratory look at a small group of PSTs, and indicates the utility of rhetorical analysis as a method for examining the implicit framing in PSTs’ interpretations of events. Due to the small scale and preliminary nature of this study, however, more research is needed to explore the range of possibilities for PSTs’ framing of teaching practice. Future work could address the predominant framings that PSTs bring into their credential programs and whether they correspond to partic ular subject areas or PSTs characteristics. Moreover, studies like this one could support teacher education by further examining how PSTs’ frames change or resist change over time as they engage with teaching practice through representations and field placements in preparation for transitioning into their own classrooms. This line of research thereby has important implications for how teacher educators understand the conceptions that PSTs already hold about the teaching profession, and how they engage with PSTs to cultivate responsive and equitable approaches to teaching. Novice teachers as political cartographers: Mapping boundary crossings and affective labor in the contested K-12 landscape Lama Z Jaber, Allison Metcalf, Sierra Morandi, Sherry Southerland Teaching is a process of continuous learning that involves constant shifts between theoretical ideals and the lived realities of the classroom. For novice educators entering the K-12 system, particularly in highly politicized environments like Florida, the  magnitude of these shifts is intensified as teachers are confronted with tensions between the values and practices cultivated within teacher education and the constraints and expectations of their new workplace (e.g. Horn & Garner, 2022; Lilach, 2020; McKay, 2016). This paper draws on the narratives of two white cisgender women first-year science teachers in Florida, one middle and one high school teacher, transitioning from a university-based teacher education context after having completed terminal degrees in education. We examine how their transitions —from graduate studies to K-12 schooling—were negotiated, highlighting the intense emotional labor and the nuanced, often subversive, and creatively insubordinate work they had to undertake to navigate politically contested educational contexts.

## Framework and context

We draw on theoretical perspectives of teacher learning that frame teaching and learning as inherently affective and political (e.g. Avraamidou & Torres Olave, 2025; Horn et al., 2025; Moore, 2008; Morales-Doyle et al., 2021). Framing teaching as affective  and political becomes more important as we consider the contemporary educational landscape of states like Florida, where education is a ground for socio-political contestation marked by intense top-down administrative demands, curriculum and legislative r estrictions, and heightened parental control. Importantly, in 2025, Florida's public education system faces significant challenges. As described by Florida Education Press (FEA staff, 2025), 64% of teacher vacancies (approximately 3,000 positions) remain unfilled months into the school year, alongside a rise in out-of-field teachers, including longterm substitutes (FEA Staff, 2025). The decline in graduates from teacher education programs, worsened by the state’s ranking of 50th in average teacher pay, is a major factor  (FEA Staff, 2025; Learning Policy Institute, 2024). Many teachers are compelled to take second jobs, while legislative measures such as restrictions on severe budget cuts in the 2025-26 school year, fueled by the expansion of private scholarship programs, occurred in the wake of 51% of students leaving public schools, leading to a loss of over $4 billion for state public schools (Solochek, 2025). These budget cuts have resulted in larger class sizes, reduced programs, loss of planning time, and insufficient teaching supplies.

This backdrop becomes crucial for understanding the experiences of novice teachers, who are expected to learn how to navigate an under-resourced and overwhelmed system. At the same time, they must adhere to legislative regulations to minimize personal risk  as they strive to teach effectively, turning their 2417 induction into a period of stress and surveillance that requires intense labor. Such labor becomes even more intense for critically conscious teachers who are committed to a vision of teaching and learning as a tool for understanding and transforming the w orld, and of classrooms as spaces where all students, especially those from marginalized backgrounds, can grow and thrive (Freire, 2017; Ladson-Billings, 1995; Paris & Alim, 2017). With these considerations in mind, we ask: How do two critically conscious novice teachers navigate their entry into the K-12 classroom amidst the institutional constraints of a socio-politically contested K-12 educational system?

We draw on biweekly debriefs within an informal community of practice, a space that nurtured our growing friendship and solidarity, comprising four educators (two K-12 teachers and two university-based teacher educators) committed to rigorous, responsive, and equity-centered science education. These debrief sessions were recorded over meals we’ve had together every other week, aimed at creating what we came to refer to as “therapeutic” space that the teachers described as helping them “unwind" and "offload" stress. In this space, the teachers shared about their experiences and emotions related to teaching in K-12 settings, with the university educators offering validation, acknowledging challenges, and brainstorming solutions. This space served to counter the isolation that the teachers felt within their institutional contexts. Here, we analyze transcripts from our debriefs, focusing on moments where the two teachers reflected on the challenge of transitioning from a university teacher education context to K-12, as well as on tensions and related emotions associated with their work as novice teachers. We engaged in an iterative analytic process, involving multiple readings of the transcripts to identify recurring tensions (e.g., navigating competing expectations between equity-oriented teaching commitments and school-imposed curricular pacing), emotions (e.g., expressions of frustration, self-doubt, and moments of affirmation), and instances of boundary crossing (e.g., attempts to translate or adapt practices learned in teacher education into K–12 classroom contexts). We then refined these into themes that captured shared patterns across experiences, which we describe in the next section. Navigating institutional challenges as first-year teachers For the two teachers, their first year of teaching in the public school systems was marked by overwhelming institutional demands that tested their capacity as novice educators. Both teachers noted that the transition from graduate school into K-12 settings was “brutal,” with K–12 teaching leaving little room for the reflective space they once had within the university setting. One teacher, while initially promised to be responsible for just one course preparation at the time of her hire, was abruptly assigned three very different courses just before the school year began. This sudden shift significantly increased her workload, leaving little time for thoughtful planning. Both teachers noted that they were required to submit detailed weekly lesson plans to include standards, objectives, activities for all their subjects, and a lengthy list of student accommodations. This requirement, enforced only for novice teachers, added to their already heavy workload and created a sense of constant scrutiny. In addition, their already limited designated planning time was frequently interrupted, as they were often “pulled” to cover other classes due to a shortage of substitute teachers or to undergo additional training and peer teaching observations as part of their required professional development. Resource scarcity added to their frustration. As one of them shared, basic supplies, such as scissors, were unavailable, and she had to either borrow materials, wait until classroom funds became available, or buy needed supplies  herself. Creative insubordination within curricular pressures and surveillance As the teachers reflected on the curricular challenges they faced across their different contexts, they noted the institutional push to achieve high standardized test scores, leading to what one of the teachers termed “cooking the stats,” prioritizing metrics and institutional reputation over learners’ developmental growth. Faced with this focus on metrics alongside a restrictive curriculum, the two teachers developed creative strategies to maintain their pedagogical integrity and commitment to student-centered, responsive instruction. As these instructional practices often clashed with institutional expectations, the teachers found themselves having to reorganize state standards to incorporate reform-based visions for teaching in contextually relevant ways. However, this reimagining of the curriculum came with risks. To avoid administrative surveillance and scrutiny, one of the teachers intentionally stored her materials on a personal cloud platform instead of the 2418 school’s shared drive. She also removed identifying logos from non-state-approved resources, framing these actions as necessary to protect her efforts to implement rigorous, research-based instruction. At the same time, that teacher found herself always un der scrutiny, positioned as a vulnerable novice, and required to devote limited out-of-class time to attend additional trainings, despite holding a terminal degree and a strong foundation in education research and pedagogy.

Emotional labor of critical care within politically contested terrains Both teachers expressed a tension between their commitments to critical care (Antrop‐González & De Jesús,

2006) in their efforts to support children as whole people and the need to comply with state-mandated

regulations that restricted their ways of caring  for their students. For one of the teachers, this tension reached a peak when she was threatened with the loss of her teaching license for calling a student by their preferred nickname that had not been officially approved by the student’s parent. This incident highlights how institutional policies and state laws, shaped by conservative political agendas mechanized by parental control, can weaponize acts of affirmation and care for students. In another instance, one teacher recalled facing scrutiny from pa rents and administrators after a parent challenged her teaching philosophy of “caring” for students and supporting them to “feel like they cando science” during a class-wide parent-teacher conference. Afterwards, a school administrator conducted an unannounced classroom observation, which the teacher interpreted as a disciplinary response to her emphasis on student well-being rather than compliance with school rules and curriculum. Another instance the teachers discussed was the mental health training they were asked to conduct in their classrooms, which they found disturbing because it tended to frame students in deficit terms rather than centering on empathy and affirming learners’ dignity and strengths. Despite such challenges, both teachers found deep joy, strength, and meaning in working with their students. They both described the best part of teaching as being with the children and derived great fulfillment in seeing their students become more engaged and empowered in their science learning. These “po ckets of joy” reaffirmed their commitments to critical care and student-centered teaching.

These findings suggest that novice teachers’ entry into K-12 teaching is less a straightforward career step and more an extended exercise in political agency, involving an ongoing and laborious navigation of a highly charged political landscape. In essence, these teachers often take on the role of cartographers, learning to map this landscape as they move from teacher education into the K-12 world. As the analysis shows, the two teachers’ journeys illustrate this critical work of mapping as they move from a n academic space where research-based findings and critical reflection are prioritized to a restrictive K-12 context where professional practice must be exercised in creatively insubordinate ways to maintain integrity. The challenges the two teachers grappled with were not mere obstacles; they were active sites where their identity as critically minded educators was being forged. Their ability to rely on their previous scholarly work and network of peers and allies outside their immediate school environment (including our own professional learning community) supported their navigation of institutional pressures. Ultimately, their experiences demonstrate that for critically conscious novice teachers, survival in contested contexts requires the agile mastery of boundary crossing, a continuous, emotionally taxing process of contextualizing theory through subtle acts of resistance and care to implement robust, responsive, and equitable approaches to teaching, while meticulously minimizing personal vulnerability. These findings have important implications for teacher education, highlighting the need to support novice teachers during this transitional stage in ways that allow them to feel heard but also, and more importantly, to feel empowered as they navigate their  career paths and make decisions about staying within the K-12 system. We suggest that our teacher education programs become better equipped not only with innovative, equitable pedagogies but also with the tools to help novices navigate the complexities of doing this work in a challenging, socio-politically contested context. In other words, to better bridge research and practice, teacher education must support novice teachers in developing what we conceptualize as political cartography of teaching—that is,  the capacity to map and navigate the emotional, relational, and institutional terrains of teaching in ways that allow them to read, interpret, and act within these complex terrains as they take up equity-oriented practices.

Lastly, an important consideration for our field, particularly as we strive to establish closer and more supportive partnerships between academia and K-12 schools, is grappling with the risk involved in conducting 2419 research with teachers in socio-politically contested environments. As we reflect on our commitment to protecting teachers, we also recognize that their participation could potentially lead to professional repercussions and expose them to additional vulner abilities and conflicts, both within and outside their workplace. As a field, we must continue to take responsibility for our moral and ethical duties to advocate for and ensure the safety of teachers and their students, first and foremost. Navigating pedagogical risk-taking to center sensemaking practices April Leuhmann, Elizabeth Wilson The Framework for K-12 Science Education (2012) and accompanying standards that 49 U.S. states have adopted in some form (NSTA, n.d.) prioritize student sensemaking, representing a significant transformation for teachers who are used to and good at teacher-centered, content-focused science teaching common in many classrooms (Windschitl & Stroupe, 2017). As teachers, and by extension their students, transition tonew ways of relating, interacting, and doing science education in collaboration with their stude nts, they are required to take up new roles that are fundamentally different from their past experiences and the status quo (Alzen et al., 2025). Thus, engaging with this transition necessarily involves pedagogical risks (Cooke, 2024) as teachers find their identities as competent educators made vulnerable (Le Fevre, 2014; Twyford, 2025). This case study explores ways two secondary science teachers engaged in knowledge-building as new practices with their students. The research question guiding our work is,  “How did science teachers new to teaching for student sensemaking navigate pedagogical risk-taking inherent in transitioning to student-centered practice?”

## Theoretical framework

We ground our study in pedagogical risk-taking (PRT), defined by Cooke (2024) as “deliberately courageous practices with uncertain and possibly negative outcomes, intended to achieve benefit(s) for an individual, group, or the environment” (p. 6). When confronted with multiple options, a teacher must choose a path forward without knowing the outcome of their choice (Howard et al., 2018), assessing how vulnerable the choice may make them and ultimately creating boundaries around the level of risk that is acceptable for them (Le Fevre, 2014; Twyford, 2025). Only if the net outcome is deemed to be positive are teachers likely to take on the risk and attempt the novel practice (Twyford et al., 2017). The risk-taking of transitioning involved in teachers working toward a transformation in teaching can involve powerful sites of possibility: “Acceptance of the in-between moments of struggle, doubt, and fear, may allow teachers to welcome the moment of risk, of feeling lost without a clear path forward” (Howard et al., 2018, p. 862). In addition, PRT is not a one and done decision; each lesson, each activity and each interaction requires a commitment in one direction or the other (Howard et al., 2018), a decision to risk, to trust, or to revert to the more comfortable  and safer. Two science teachers partnered with the two authors to develop a place-based and community-based final curricular unit. The unit asked students to generate and answer their own questions about the impact of plastics pollution at a local beach by their school. Teacher A, an eighth-grade teacher, had 20 years of teaching experience while Teacher B, a twelfth-grade teacher, is a third-year teacher. The unit was novel for these teachers in many ways: it was initiated by drawing out student questions, involved a sequence of investigations, and centered on a local, real-life phenomenon (i.e., plastics pollution). This study explores how the two teachers navigated these uncertainties.

To capture the myriad of choices and subsequent risks the teachers navigated, audio recordings of each teacher’s reflections during unit planning, during implementation, and after the unit were recorded and transcribed. These reflections, supplemented by field notes, pictures, and audio recordings became our dataset. Content analysis was used to identify the teachers’ perceptions of their roles within the unit and identify moments of confidence and uncertainty that either agreed with or conflicted with their perception of their role. Teacher A’s sense of adventure was seen from the beginning of her involvement with the unit. During the planning process, her initial reaction when asked to identify an emotion around incorporation of place-based phenomena was that “it sounds like fun.” She initially identified her role as being a liaison between students 2420 and the research team, saying “I'm not the knowledge person and I'm not the designer person, so yeah, I'm like a helper hand.” She saw this as an opportunity to step back from her existing role as classroom lead while simultaneously stepping into a new coordinator role. The shift in roles created tension: as A navigated the transition between her typical method of teaching and the novel practices promoted in the unit, uncertainties in how to bridge the two contexts were uncovered. On the first day of the unit, A initially took the lead in introducing the phenomenon as part of her typical role as classroom lead but paused and looked to a researcher as she encountered each novel practice, waiting for the researcher to introduce the practice. Halfway through the period she remarked, “Oh, I get it now” as she observed a way to elicit students’ questions that would be used to drive the unit. The second class period then saw her taking on a stronger lead role and implementing the strategies she had just observed. Throughout the unit, when A encountered new practices, she often stepped back and watched the research team implement the practice. Once she had experienced the practice’s success, she would then implement the practice, supported by the research team, in the second class period. In contrast to A’s perception of herself as “not the knowledge person”, B stated in his initial reflection that he felt himself to be “the face of the content” and “lead…the content discussions.” This drive for B to be seen and recognized as an expert, in contrast to A’s excitement in releasing the need to be a content expert, may have led to his rejection of a shift in his role. Both A and B led opening and closing whole-class strategy, B prepared for and led the discussions on his own. During small-group work B tended to isolate himself, retreating to his desk at the other side of the room from where the students worked and watching from a distance. The exceptions to this pattern were seen when equipment and skills familiar to B were introduced.

When students analyzed microscope slides, B helped the students to use the equipment and make observations. Likewise, when students created spreadsheets of dat a in order to create visuals, B helped them to organize their dataset and choose appropriate analysis strategies. The familiarity of the procedures may have supported B in maintaining his perceived role as expert within the classroom and minimizing the risk he took in interacting with students during their novel exploration. The uncertainty that came from students’ studying a novel phenomenon created a tension for both teachers because they did not know the answers in advance and therefore did not feel their typical sense of expertise. Teacher A leaned into the uncertainty whi le Teacher B leaned away, attempting to minimize risk. The roles they took on at the outset of the unit may offer some insight: Where A was excited to relinquish her role as lead, acknowledging her lack of content expertise, B felt pressure to retain a role as content expert. This dichotomy may have allowed A to navigate uncertainty with less perception of risk. While 21 days is not enough time to transform long-held teaching practices, A became more willing to value student ideas as she gained confidence i n novel knowledge-building practices, demonstrating receptiveness to exploring this pedagogical transition. As she began to navigate PRT, her initial successes may have served as an important resource for further PRT (Twyford et al., 2017). The risk of dep rivatizing her practice (Le Fevre, 2014; Twyford, 2025) as she wrestled with “moments of struggle” (Howard et al., 2018, p. 862) was mitigated by the authors’ ability to act as trusted peers and support her in exploring new practices and attempting her own implementation (Le Fevre, 2014; Radloff et al., 2019). A’s desire for student excitement and the enthusiasm with which she took up a role as co-learner may have demonstrated her belief in the purpose of novel practices (Harris, 2021) and perception of herself to be the type of teacher who would implement this type of practice (Le Fevre, 2014). Future studies may consider the ways in which pedagogical humility contributes to a teacher’s opportunities to learn through risk-taking.
