# Exclusionary and Emancipatory Learning Praxes of Gender Nonconforming Noise Musicians

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Building on the recent growth of explicitly queer approaches to learning sciences scholarship, this paper explores the learning praxes of gender nonconforming noise musicians. The community of practice (CoP) surrounding noise music, a caustic subgenre of experimental music, provides a fruitful site of research because of its history of tensions related to marginalized gender identities. Through this study, I address the following research questions: What exclusionary practices do gender nonconforming musicians recognize in noise music scenes? And how do gender nonconforming noise musicians challenge those issues to produce a safer and more just informal learning environment? In analyzing interviews with 36 globally dispersed musicians, I discuss a series of exclusionary and liberatory learning praxes that create tensions and opportunities for marginalized participants. These findings hold implications for other studies of power within CoPs and informal learning contexts more broadly.

## Introduction

This paper stems from a simple assertion: queerness  matters in the learning sciences.  As Shrodes & Paré (2022) argue, queerness provides a valuable lens to both unveil and challenge notions of power within education because the politics of gender and sexuality influence all learning contexts and praxes. Incorporating queer theory into our field thus provides an opportunity to revisit and further refine long standing theories of learning (McWilliams & Penuel, 2016) while centering the experiences of LGBTQ+ learners  expands our understanding of the embodied learning praxes that queer identifying individuals engage (Renold & Timperly, 2024; Uttamchandi, 2021). Doing so also contributes to the intertwined projects of excavating mechanisms of systemic queer oppressi on (via institutions such as education, prisons, healthcare, and more) and amplifying processes of queer resistance and joy that exist beyond (and even within) marginalization (Shuster & Westbrook, 2022). Taking up this call to action, I use this paper to explore the experiences of gender nonconforming musicians as they participate in the informal music education  contexts of noise music scenes. Here, noise music refers to a caustic subset of experimental music where musicians create dissonant and arrhythmic mus ical works from seemingly “nonmusical” sounds  as they  draw influence from punk, industrial, and Avant G arde jazz (Graham, 2023). Previous research shows that noise music scenes represent potent learning environments where participants construct idiosyncratic musical knowledges and develop shared and individual artistic identities (Woods, 2022). These scenes also provide an especially valuable site for exploring learning through a queer lens because of the longstanding influence of transgende r musicians within the genre  (Rodgers, 2010; Tigers, 2021). Yet participants regularly describe noise music as an outwardly heteronormative and hypermasculine space (Reed, 2013; Woods, 2023). This unresolved tension positions noise music as a potent space to address the repeated calls to research power dynamics within the situated learning processes of informal education (Curnow, 2022). Through this paper, I  address two research questions: What exclusionary p ractices do gender nonconforming musicians recognize in noise music scenes? And how do gender nonconforming noise musicians challenge those issues to produce a safer and more just informal learning environment?  In doing so, I consider barriers to learning and participation while also amplifying liberatory educational praxes. Through my analysis of interviews with 36 globally dispersed gender nonconforming noise musicians, I argue that participants recognize exclusionary social practices and genre tropes as barriers to their full participation. But these musicians also respond to these barriers by retheorizing the genre through the production of counterhistories and engaging in the work of defining their community of practice (CoP). This study therefore contributes to a wider understanding of queer reimaginings of informal learning contexts and their emancipatory education processes.

## Background

To situate this study, I begin by presenting an overview of gender-focused research into CoPs and experimental music education. In doing so, I argue that noise mu sic scenes exist as musical CoPs that intersect with gender in multiple ways, providing a queer-forward foundation for this study. Gendered communities of practice Originally theorized by Lave and Wenger (1991), CoPs represent learning contexts where individuals construct situated or localized knowledges through regular interaction. This learning occurs at all levels of participation as members construct both communal and individual identities related to the CoP (Jones, 2021). Engaging with CoPs begins with legitimate peripheral participation (LPP), as new members contribute in small but meaningful ways, before some participants transition into veteran st atus where they shape community boundaries and involvement more directly (Lave & Wenger, 1991). According to Wenger (1998), CoPs rely on three foundational dimensions: mutual engagement, joint enterprise, and shared repertoire. In his theorization  of musical CoPs, Kenny (20 16) defines these elements as follows: mutual engagement  includes “the music-making group interactions such as rehearsals, workshops and performances”; joint enterprise represents the “stated and negotiated aims of the music communities”; and shared repertoire involves the “practices or built-up communal resources that distinctly belong to each musical community” (p. 18).  Learning within CoPs (both musically and otherwise)  thus involves participating in, developing an understanding of, and shaping these elements. Wenger (1998) also argues that the development of one’s individual identity within a CoP represents a key part of situated learning. Subsequent scholarship builds on this assertion by showing that gender identity also forms through an individual’s participation in CoPs and their embodied discourses (Jones, 2011, 2021; Paetcher, 2003). L ocalized understandings of gender  exist within all CoPs and the process of developing one’s gender identity specifically involves the development of gendered elements within mutual engagement, shared repertoire, and joint enterprise (Paechter & Marguerite, 2021). The embodiment of gender within CoPs then infuses t hese learning contexts with specific power relations, as globalized forms of gender shape localized  or situated understandings of this social institution (Curnow, 2013). These power relations become tangible when individuals (primarily, cisgender women and gender non conforming people) cannot access certain gendered actions  due to their identity, thus barring them from full participation  in the CoP  and restricting access to certain situated identities and knowledge s (Atencio & Koca, 2011; Curnow, 2013). The embodiment of power relations within CoPs thus creates unintentional mechanisms for flattening difference, homogenizing possible identities and forms of participation that exist as legitimate (Hodges, 1998). This issue becomes exacerbated when CoPs replicate intersectional forms of oppression that incorporate gender, racialization and other social relations (Curnow, 2022). Yet despite the ever-present influence of oppressive gender norms, CoPs also rep resent crucial sites for liberatory forms of queer gender identity development. But queer liberation in this instance does not necessarily represent a process of achieving fuller participation within a CoP, instead existing as the movement out of one CoP where only marginalized participation could occur and towards full participation in gendered activities and performance in another (Paetcher, 2003). Challenging gender norms involves the creation of alternate CoPs where new identities and concepts of gender  can form through the negotiation of meaning by participants (Curnow, 2013; Gerrard, 2011). Given a flexible enough understanding of communal identit y and situated knowledge or meaning, CoPs become places of imagining alternate realities that materialize through a refusal to embody extant ways of being (Bessey et al., 2023). Gender identity development within CoP’s can then become a targeted and active process, one where participants actively create identities in opposition to transphobia and heteronormativity as they adopt these same identities over time (Bessey et al., 2023; Jones, 2021). Learning and identity in noise music While research into learning within noise music scenes remains relatively sparse, researchers have begun to explore the educative praxes of similar experimental music genres. Experimental music, in this case, refers to any new Western musical genre that explicitly breaks from the foundational tenets of the Western canon (e.g., rhythm, melody, repetitive structure, standardized musical notation). The idiosyncratic nature of experimental  music engenders an approach to learning where musicians e ngage in open ended explorations of their instrumen ts as they develop new performance techniques and musical meaning (Sordahl, 2013; Veloso, 2020). This positions learning within experimental music as highly agentic  with musicians (regardless of experience or pre-existing knowledge) routinely constructing new kinds of meaning and ways of making music in collaboration with other performers and their audiences (Tinkle, 2015; Woods, 202 2). Teaching and learning within this musical space thus embodies a democratic approach to education, one where the voices of all participants contribute to the production of new sociocultural relations and knowledges (Kanellopoulos, 2021; Wright, 2019). Turning towards the learning praxes of noise scenes  as CoPs, th e process of constructing situated knowledge through participation involves the constant reimagining of the aesthetic boundaries of the genre and the meanings that come from unique approaches to making music (Graham, 2023). R ather thandeveloping what Wegner (1998) would define as a reified musical practice, one where a specific way of making music has a settled meaning associated with it, learning within the CoP of noise music involves the development of an artistic identity built around unique techniques, compositional practices, and tools that do not necessarily align with those of other participants (Woods, 2022). This leads to a wildly diverse set of aesthetics th at all fit within the genre, ranging from the overwhelming walls of sound created by artists like Merzbow to the nearly silent performances of Sachiko M. The positioning of identity development as a learni ng process within noise music also aligns with Wenger’s (1998) foundational theorization of CoPs and research into other arts-based contexts, where participants use the practice of making art to represent themselves and further explore their own emergent identities (Halverson et al., 2023; Pinkard & Austin, 2014; Renold & Timp erley, 2024). Within noise music, t his learning expands beyond the construction of a community-specific artistic identity and includes the production of both individual and communal sociopolitical subjectivities (Niknafs, 2018). To this end, noise musicians position the maintenance of boundaries, both in terms of inviting in new par ticipants and exiling others, as a  potentially liberatory pedagogical process wherein methods of community maintenance actively shape the situated knowledges of these musical CoPs as they amplify marginalized voices and ways of knowing and being (Woods, 2019). T he intersections of identity within noise music scenes, like all CoPs,  also include intersections with local and globalized forms of gender (Jones, 2011; Paechter, 2003; Woods, 2023). And while musical discourses often portray noise music as hypermasculine, scholars have repeatedly challenged this claim and shown that noise as both a genre and a sonic material have been repeatedly gendered as feminine and queer (Rodgers, 2010; Sundell & Bergland, 2021; Tigers, 2021). How this theorization intersects with the lived reality of those in the CoP of the noise music scene, however, remains underexplored. I now turn tonew empirical data to begin this exploration.

## Methods

To further investigate how gender nonconforming noise musicians conceptual ize and experience noise music scenes as informal learning spaces, I conducted an interview-based study with musicians from noise scenes in Australian, North American, and  the United Kingdom. Through social media posts and emai led newsletters, I recruited 36 gender nonconforming noise musicians t o participate in the study. The amount of time participants had spent in noise music scenes varied from 1 year to 30, thus representing a wide spectrum of experience within these translocal communities. In terms of gender, 2 0 participants identified as non-binary, 15 participants identified as transgender, and 1 identified as agen der. 23 participants lived in the USA, 6 lived in A ustralia, 5 lived in the UK, and 2 lived in Canada. 30 of the participants identified as white while 2 participants identified as Native American and Hispanic, 1 identified as White  and Hispanic, 1 identified as Hispanic only, 1  identified as African American, 1 identified as North American In digenous, and 1 identified as Ashkenazi Jewish. This racial imbalance mirrors the historical demographic makeup of the genre (Graham, 2023), thus representing the relative lack of racial diversity within these translocal sc enes. While this study design does lead to certain limita tions (racial diversity bias, limited data sources, and l ack of analysis of musical practice among them), th e focus on perceptions of lived experiences from representative participants still creates valuable insight into the learning praxes of this informal learning context.

To collect data for this study, I recorded a single 60–75-minute semi-structured interview with each participant via online conferencing software. The interview pro tocol incorporated two broad categories: conceptualizations of artistic identity development as a noise artist and theorized connections between gender and noise music (including the genre as a whole, noise music scenes, and their own practice). I phrased the questions in order to create open-ended opportunities for participants to describe th eir engagement with noise music and gender on their terms while also allowing them to challenge the assumption that connections between noise music and gender  must exist. After I transcribed all the interviews, I analyzed the data using an open and iterative approach to descriptive coding (Saldaña, 2015), explicitly looking for moments where parti cipants articulated their conceptions of noise music scenes, participat ion in these communities, artistic identity develop ment, and relationships between the genre and gender expression. I then conducted a second round of analysis that drew on pattern coding techniques (Miles et al., 2020) by organizing my initial codes into broader themes, looking for narrative and conceptual connections between the codes I generated from the data. Positionality statement As the sole author and researcher behind this study, I want to openly acknowledge that I am approaching this work as a cisgender man who does not have the same lived experience as my participants. This proves especially important since, as Jones (202 1) acknowledges, cisgender authors far too often wri te about the lives of gender nonconforming individuals, thus taking up space tha t they themselves should occupy. While I am conscious that this study may inadvertently reinforce this trend, I take up this work as a means to advocate for people of marginalized gender identities by amplifying their voice in a context that participan ts may not have access too. To ensure this outcome, I have been in continuous communication with the participants since conducting my research, discussing the process of writing academic scholarship and sharing every iteration of  analysis while eliciting feedback via member checks.

I also approach this work as a member of the transglobal noise scene, having spent nearly 20 years as a performer, concert promoter, venue organizer, and label head. In recent years, I have noticed a growing presence from outwardly queer noise projects and music scenes which led to my initial interest in this project. Although I had never met the vast majority of participants bef ore conducting this study, I have played shows alongside a small number of interviewees and consider some of t hem to be close friends. While my extant relationship with these participants does raise a few methodological concerns, my familiarity with noise music and its translocal scenes also created a sense of comfort and familiarity that allowed unfamiliar participants to open up to me as the interviewer. My perspective as a participant also provided important background knowledge, allowing me to ask in depth and informed questions that those without this lived experience may not have access to.

## Findings

Through the analysis of these interviews, I produced eight themes related to my two research questions: What exclusionary practices do gender nonconforming musicians recognize in noise music scenes? And how do gender nonconforming noise musicians challenge those issue s to produce a safer and more just informal learnin g environment? These themes split evenly between both research questions, with an equal number of themes relating to exclusionary practices and liberatory ones. I define and analyze each theme below. Exclusionary practices In conceptualizing the exclusionary practices that exist within the CoP of the noise scene, the participants discussed the exclusionary nature of cultural homogeneity and using fascism as a thematic component within the shared repertoire of these scenes. They also designated tokenism and scenic violence as community practices that excluded gender nonconforming musicians. Each of these components of the community produced a gendered barrier to participation in the CoP.

Cultural homogeneity Despite extant conceptions of noise music often relying on a (queer) lack of categorization (Sundell & Bergland, 2020; Tigers 2021), participants regularly experienced a sense of cultural homogeneity that included all forms of expression within the community (artistic, dress, speech, etc.). As one participant explained, “What was irksome about the noise community  was that it was very white, very cis, and very straight. It was a lot of straight guys making screeching noise, very aggressive and macho. I kind of was always a little bit more of an outsider.” While the exclusion felt in this quote does refer to the racial and gender dynamics of the scene that lead to intersectional forms of marginalization, the participant also explicitly connects these identities to the aesthetics (“aggressive and macho”) and music making practices  (“making screeching noise”)  of their largely white and heteronormative understanding of the genre.

But even within explicitly queer formations of noise music scenes, similar forms of cultural homogeneity can emerge. One participant stated as much when they said the following: “I started seeing the same patterns that I was seeing when I was looking at [the broader noise scene] in these queer noise music communities. It becomes a code of conduct, cosigning on this call to be more exclusive in queer noise spaces. You have to be either 100% in the culture or you're not part of the culture at  all. That to me is very straight, to be like, ‘it is either this or nothing.’” In describing queer noise scenes in this way, the i nterviewee connects to the kinds of exclusionary practices that can exist within gendered CoPs acknowledged by Jones (2011). In this  sense, members of this particular noise scene reify the kinds of actions a nd expression that count as queer, a process that t he participant recognizes as being heteronormative.

Fascism as thematic In line with the re ification of expression that occurred in some of th e music scenes that the interviewees participated in, the noise musicians in this study also recognized the common t hemes of fascism and objective violence (including sexual and gender-based violence) that have historically existed in the genre  as furthering gendered exclusion (Reed, 2013). One participant described the role that this thematic played within noise music as follows: “There's noise music which comes from the inner, the  personal, and then there's noise music that sort of attacks. Aside from the album title and the use of swastikas,  there's something about that music that just feels off or stale. There's a significant enough difference that I can k ind of separate myself from that and feel good about that.” While the presence of fascist imagery within some musical works did not exclude this participant entirely, it did push them to limit their own participation. However, some participants also discussed the incorporation of transgressive thematics as being equal parts alluring and off-putting. As one participant explains,  “most of the time those people are being edgy and reactionary in a way that I don't think they truly believe. That expression came about more from a sense of immediacy on  what it was like, especially in Britain, at that ti me. I don't take that personally  because of my identity, but I don't find it important that noise enjoyers of color or different gender identities listen to it.” But this thematic can also extend beyond an artistic choice to represent one’s ideology. Another musician connects to this in the following quote:  “I carry myself in a musical environment full of hor rible people and I hate that.  It's a horrible position to be put in and looking for queer artists almost redeems that. I found queer artists whose music I love, but extreme music is for extreme people and unfortunately a lot of extreme people are skinheads and Nazis. But they make really good music.” To this end, the incorporation of this theme within noise music leads to artists dedicated to intersecting forms of transphobia, racism, and fascism infiltrating the scene and (sometimes violently) excluding marginalized performers.

Tokenism As alluded to in the section on cultural homogeneit y, the barriers to participation in noise music exp and beyond the aesthetics of the genre to include the actions of participants. While the participants did recognize some broad actions that made them feel not welcome, they also identified more subtle and nuanced forms of exclusion such as tokenism. Under this theme, the musicians in the  study recognized that people from dominant identit y formations only valued othered musicians if they could exploit their identity for personal gain. As one participant explains, “I don't want to be identified as a woman experimental musician or a trans musician. But that is also an important part of my identity. It's this complicated thing because that sort of form of strategic essentialism can be really vital in terms of getting my work surfaced, but it also feels very ungenuine to leverage that. ” Key to understanding the challenge to tokenism presented b y this participant is the rejection of being labeled as a trans noise musician  or woman noise musician. But r ather than writing that aspect of her identity off entirely, the musician focuses on the community process of distributing her work as opposed to considering this identification process through the lens of their artistic practice or gender identity. Additionally, the participants in this study also r ecognized the toll that tokenism plays on other gender nonconforming scene members. A participant describe d this aspect of the theme in the following way:  “There were times  where I could see myself being booked as the token queer, the token female, or whoever. After transitioning, I would still get requests for female and trans performers, but I was turning down shows that have a woman and trans focus. I'm kind of just a white g uy and I pass perfectly fine. I have plenty of opportunities. I don't need extra places set aside for me. ” In this quote, the musician sees himself as having a certain level of privilege within the noise music scene. A s a whit e transgender person who passes as a man within society’s broader conception of gender, he does not face the same discrimination as other musicians who do not fit as easily within the gender binary. Playing on shows dedicated to trans musicians would then take opportunities away from other gender nonconforming musicians who may need them more. Scenic violence Where tokenism may represent a more subtle form of gender ed exclusion, the participants also acknowledged more direct and violent experiences they had within  these spaces. One musician shared a very clear example of this violence in the following story: “I had some rather unpopular songs expressing the fact that I had been rejected by women because I was too boyish. A women’s meeting group organized an event. They wanted me to do all the work and wouldn't even have me play. We forced ourselves on the bill. These women spat at  me because they didn't like my view. It wasn't the standard feminist view. They actually told me I wasn't a real woman.” A specific, possibly reified understanding of gender held by th is group clashed with the participant’s own understanding of their identity. Members of the CoP then responded with violence to further reify this situated definition of gender. Just as CoPs intersect with broader community formations (P aechter, 2002; Jones, 2011), the threat of violence against gender nonconforming noise musicia ns also comes from the surrounding social context. A participant described this as follows:  “I do worry that us making outsider music will be us ed to justify further things against us. There was a mass shooting at a v ery queer punk show in Minneapolis. That's the other aspect, this sense of danger. We're making art in the middle of a genocide.” While queer noise scenes may provide some sanctuary for participants, this quote also speaks to the ways that participation may lead to an even greater threat of violence. The promotion of queer spaces draws at tention to these communities, making them visible to  those who want to commit violence against and further opp ress gender nonconforming people. This analysis therefore speaks to both the subtle and overwhelmingly obviou s barriers to participation for gender nonconformin g noise musicians, emerging from both the actions of the participants and the music itself. Liberatory practices Alongside the exclusionary practices listed above, participants also described how the situated practices and knowledges of this community amplified their full h umanity. The musicians  recognized this liberatory praxis existing in the music itself (historical queerness, rejecting canon) and the surrounding scene (defining community, existence as opposition), all of which allowed participants to imagine new ways of being. Historical queerness While the discourse surrounding noise music has regularly positioned this genre as hypermasculine (Reed, 2013; Woods, 2023), the musicians in this study strongly disagreed with this sentiment and aligned their historical understanding of the genre with those writers highlighting the ever-present queerness of noise music (Sundell & Berglund, 2020; Tigers, 2021). As one musician explained, “when you think about the origins of noise music, you have transgressive artists like Genesis P-Orridge and Cosey Fanni Tutti of Throbbing Gristle, and of course big inspirations like Wendy Carlos. What they were doin g was an inherently political and transgressive act, and I wanted to incorporate myself in that lineage.” As shown in this quote, the participants regularly pointed to foundational musicians as evidence of the genre being historically queer. The recognition of noise music’s queer history also uplifts their queer identity through this contextualization. Beyond merely reclaiming the history of noise music, the participants also actively positioned themselves within that history. The previous quote acknowledge s this when they describe a desire to “incorporate  myself in that lineage.” A different participant expanded on this thought in the followi ng way: “A lot of people, myself included, are definitely just like, ‘Oh my God, here is a history. I'm actually a part of  it. This is me. This is the line that I'm on.’ These women that came before me and did all this gr eat work, I understand myself as a part of that. And I think noise gives you an opportunity to  engage directly with that history. ” This embrace of one’s position within the queer history of noise music po sitions the CoP surrounding the genre as a communit y where participants can explore new manifestations of gender and situated practices. Rejecting canon In line with the queer reclamation of the histo ry of noise music, the participants also challenged  the canonical positioning of influential albums or discographies from cisgender male artists that espoused themes of fascism or gendered violence. One participant exemplified this theme when they said,  “[When] I started listening to [noise, I] didn't go back and say, ‘oh, I'm getting into noise. I should start with the  classics. Let me listen to Whitehouse and Prurient.’ I don't give a fuck. I want to listen to the stuff made by angry people that are like me.” In rejecting the highly influential artists mentioned above, the interviewee not only refuses to consider the importance of these canonical artists but also asserts the importance of queer representation within the genre. In a sense, understanding the situated meanings behind the  genre does not mean understanding what other s have claimed the genre to be but developing an individual perspective on why this music personally matters or what it means. In thinking through the body of work that defines t he genre, some participants also found themselves in a position where they did not encounter this music as they moved from LPP to veteran members. As one interviewee explained, their journey into the noise music scene revolved e ntirely around women artists: “One of the first noise albums I ever bought was the first Pharmakon album. And Puce Mary was just starting to kick off around that time. I also got really into Lydia Lunch and, because of Genesis P-Orridge, Throbbing Gristle. Noise music was always for the girls in terms of my intro duction to it and how I understood it conceptually. ” Through this particular frame, the participant constructs t he genre as being primarily made for and by people of marginalized gender identities. This conceptualization of the genre positions women and gender nonconforming musicians as the central population within this genre and cisgender men as interlopers, not the other way around. Defining community Shifting towards the practices of noise music scene s, the participants aligned their  actions in these CoPs with previous research that positions the boundary work of defining the communi ty as an important pedagogical practice (Woods, 2019). One participant connected this process to the histo ry of the genre:  “I n these scenes, historically, those people [transphobes, racists, fascists, etc.] exist and there is also the movement of people that put their boots in their faces and get them the fuck out of there. I trust my community and they trust me to do that if [those people show up].” Beyond merely creating a safe space for gender nonc onforming people in the scene, the participant also recognizes this as a crucial form of participa tion with a historical precedent. In turn, kicking people with oppressive ideologies out of the scene exists as part of the joint enterprise of the CoP s ince creating a safe space for gender nonconforming individuals to thrive represents a stated aim of the community. While the previous quote may have conceived of this process through violent imagery, others discussed the process of defining community in less confronta tional terms. As one participant explain ed: “I choose not to support certain artists and labels because I don't want to give my money to people who could be spreading harmful views. It's not something I'm interested in having or consuming. I'm more than willing to consume weir d and fucked up art, but there are some lines that I prefer not to cross. ” Through this process, the musiciandescribes how they purposeful keep their financial resources from supporting community members who espouse viole nt ideologies. While the participant does not necessarily feel the need to avoid interacting with this music, the choice to not support these other artists shows an interest in removing them from the transglobal noise scene. Existence as opposition Lastly, musicians in this study also recognized their existence within these CoPs and the broader culture as a form of resistance that challenges transphobia and heteronormativity. One musiciandescribe d this when they sa id, “People with extreme opinions will always exist. Just by existing and being a queer, nonbinary noise artist, that in itself is opposition. I was saying to [my bandmate] when we were on tour: the way we fight back is by not going anywhere and just getting queerer. ” Not only does this participant see the act of exist ing in a space where transphobic or heteronormative views may dominate the discourse as one of resistan ce, they also recognize the potential in becoming more visibly and actively queer. This expa nds on the kinds of challenges that gender nonconforming musicians pose to the assumed masculinity of noise music scenes. The participants also recognized the variety of inte rsecting identities that contributed to the framing  of existence as a form of opposition. One interviewee exemplified this when she said: “I don't view myself as a trailblazer because there are many other gender nonconforming musicians out there, but I consider myself to be a part of that movement to break up the overwhelming cisnormativity that is present in the noise and exp erimental scene. In practice, creating music as a transgender, Jewish woman who is neurodivergent-I am myself a political statement.” Within this quote, this musician recognizes the intersectional nature of oppression that contributes to her politicized identity  since her  existence as transgender, Jewish, and neurodivergent collectively challenge hegemonic norms. Additionally, this identity does not necessarily only exist as an individual one but connects to a communal identity shared by a sub-CoP comprised of other transgender artists who challenge heteronormativity within this space.

## Discussion

In discussing the CoP of noise music with these gender nonconforming members, a familiar set of exclusionary gendered practices emerges. As Curnow (2013) notes, CoPs “are constituted by individuals living in a materially gendered, racialised, and classed world, thus these  communities tend to reproduce historical inequalit y and inequity within their activity in particular way s” (p. 838). Tokenization and acts of violence thus represent the ways that noise scene s reproduce inequity, creating a barrier to full part icipation for traditional ly marginalized individuals. Outside of these actions, the situated knowledges of the noise scene also contribute to the exclusionary nature of the CoP via the construction of meaning w ithin the genre itself. The (rather contradictory) issue of cultural homogeneity within the sonic and visual aesthetics of noise produce what Hodges (1998) would describe as a homogenization of possible identities and legitimate forms of participation. And the thematic exploration of fascism and other taboo subjects reproduces the exclusionary nature of these spaces (Woods, 2018). This analysis thus aligns with Gerrard’s (2011) claim that  alternate educational contexts can reproduce gendered norms  and practices even as they attempt to challenge extant power rela tions. Despite scholars positioning noise music scenes as spaces where new ways of being can emerge through particip ation (Niknafs, 2018; Woods, 2019), these scenes still intersect with broader  conceptions of gender that reproduce oppression (Woods, 2023). This reckoning with the exclusionary nature of the noise scene thus creates a tension with previous feminist studies of CoPs. As Curnow (2013) has argued, challenging gender norms often involves the creation of alternate or sub-CoPs where new identities and practices can form and new meanings be negotiated. But heteronormative barriers to participation still exist despite the feminization of noise, noise music, and noise music scenes (Rodgers, 2010; Sundell & Berglund, 2020; Tigers, 2 021). Alternate communities that can provide opportunities for gendered forms of liberation thus need further scrutiny, both in practice and within future studies. Yet the participants still recognized powerful forms of liberation within this sociomusic al space, mirroring the kinds of joy  that transgender communities regularly find in the face of marginalization (Shuster & Westbrook, 2024). In relation to the genre itself, the interviewees  rejected both a history of the genre dominated by cisgender men and the importance of canonical al bums that reproduced a hypermasculine ideology. In doing so, the participants build on the inherently emergent nature of noise music, one where what “counts” as noise has less to do with historical continuity and more to do with communal definition and individual perception (Graham, 2023). Because of this multifaceted, polysemic foundation,  th e noise music scene builds on the democratic, educative praxis of experimental music (Kanellopoul os, 2021; Wright, 2019) by  creating spaces where gender nonconforming participants can construct counterhistories through the rejection of reified knowledges or objects within the CoP. This understanding of communal knowledge production thus  aligns with Bessy et al.’s (2023) claim that CoPs can create opportunities to build new worlds a nd imagine new ways of being, but only if they actively embrace difference rather than flattening it through homogenizing processes. Building on this positionin g, the participants recognized the work of defining community membershi p and their ongoing existence within this space as contributing to the liberatory praxes of the noise music scene. In relation to the theme of defining community, the musicians in this study diverge slightly from previous research that positions the formation of alternate CoPs as a means of resistance against heteronormativity (Curnow, 2013; Paechter, 2003; Jones, 2011). While starting queer-oriented scenes as alternate spaces represents a viable opportunity for these musicians  (despite the complications listed above), they also  show that actively removing participants who reproduce heteronormativity and gendered oppression  within extant scenes provides an equally viable means towards establishing queer forms of wh at Atencio and Koca (2011) describe as full, gender ed participation. Additionally, the assertion that existing as an openly queer participant represents a form of opposition connects this liberatory praxis to broader social c ontexts. Because CoPs and the localized notions of gender they contain always interact with  social discourses and institutional forces (Jones, 2021), the ability to exist within the fullness of one’s own conception of queer identity in the noise scene  creates an opportunity to challenge cisheteronormativity  on multiple scales. The highly agentic nature of learning within noise mu sic (Woods, 2022) and the importance of sociopolitical identity development within the scene (Niknafs, 2018) thus positions the educative praxis of this CoP as one of social r esistance. These findings therefore contribute to the ongoing calls of researchers to explore the power d ynamics of CoPs by forwarding how situated learning  itself can exist as a mechanism for liberation.

## Conclusion

As noted by Shrodes & Paré (2022), social conceptions of gender influence all learning  contexts in unique ways and the CoP of the noise scene provides no exception. The gender nonconforming noise musicians in this study build on this contention by recognizing the influen ce of both damaging forms of heteronormativity and the liberatory potential of localized queer identities.  A similar tension to the one described by Shuster &  Westbrook

(2024) therefore exists in the noise scene, one where participants of marginalized genders can experience both

marginalization and liberation simultaneously. To this end, it proves vital for le arning scientists to consider this tension within all CoPs and other learning contexts  alike. Through this focus, scholars can begin to u npack the layered intersections of gender, and the formations of power those intersections entail, across all spaces and praxes of learning.
