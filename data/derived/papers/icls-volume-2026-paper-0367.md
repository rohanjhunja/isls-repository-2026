# Four Learning Ecologies in a Graduate Instructional Design Course

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study used learning experience network analysis to examine nine student reflections in a graduate advanced instructional design course. The analysis identified four distinct learning ecologies, each with unique patterns of struggles, successes, and al ignments with learning theories. Based on these findings, we propose specific, theory-grounded design moves tailored to each ecology. This work demonstrates a method for developing more humanizing and adaptive learning experiences, offering a data-driven alternative to one-size-fits-all course design.

Graduate instructional design courses present complex learning environments where students develop professional identities while engaging in authentic collaborative practice. Complex systems analysis (Jacobson & Kapur, 2012) of student reflections from a graduate-level course revealed diverse learning experiences, with different clusters of unique struggles, aspects that worked, and alignment with specific principles from various theories. By identifying distinct learning ecologies, we can move beyond a one-size-fits-all approach to create more adaptive and engaging learning environments. This paper presents an in-depth analysis of these student experiences to develop iterative course improvements grounded in learning theory. This study draws on six learning theories, selected through open coding as the best-fitting interpretive lenses after patterns emerged from the data. Social constructivism (Vygotsky, 1978) emphasizes how individuals co-construct understanding through dialogue and shared activities in collaborative settings. Situated learning (Lave & Wenger, 1991) argues that learning occurs through legitimate peripheral participation within communities of practice, where novices move toward expertise through authentic tasks and communication. Transformative learning (Mezirow, 2018) focuses on perspective transformation through critical reflection on experiences and assumptions, altering how learners see the world. Cognitive constructivism (Piaget, 1952) centers on how learners actively construct knowledge by interacting with their environment and building on prior understanding. Constructionism (Papert & Harel, 1991) suggests people learn most effectively when constructing personally meaningful artifacts. Finally, the dynamic systems model of role identity (DSMRI; Kaplan & Garner, 2017) views identity as constantly evolving through environmental interactions. Using learning experience network analysis (LENA; Donaldson et al., 2024), we examine how these theoretical principles manifest in actual student experiences, revealing four distinct clusters that each require different learning design adjustments. This study, part of a larger DBR effort (Barab, 2022), was conducted on data from a fully online graduate advanced instructional design course in which students from varied professional backgrounds worked in teams to design technology-enhanced learning solutions for real clients. The primary data were in-depth end-of-course reflection papers submitted by 9 students that invited students to reflect on what worked well, struggles, and what the experience meant to them professionally. While small, this sample provided rich qualitativ e data suitable for an in-depth network analysis aimed at identifying patterns rather than generalizing. To analyze the data, we used learning experience network analysis (LENA; Donaldson et al., 2024), a methodology that connects qualitative coding tonetwork analysis to identify patterns in learner experiences. The reflection papers were imported into MAXQDA Analytics Pro for iterative coding into three dimensions: aspects that worked well, challenges, and alignment with learning theory. This produced 40 codes applied to 197 coded segments. We calculated Pearson correlations at the student level to examine co-occurrence between codes in the same reflection. Edges reflected correlations with p  <.05, which we imported into UCINET for visualization. U sing the Girvan-Newman clustering algorithm (Girvan & Newman, 2002), we identified four learning ecologies (Q = 0.686), each representing a distinct constellation of co-occurring codes rather than a classification of individual students. The analysis resulted in a clustered network map with four learning ecologies, defined here as distinct constellations of co-occurring struggles, successes, and theoretical alignments that characterize qualitatively different ways of experiencing the course.

Unlike the Reflective Navigators, whose reflection was directed inward at identity and assumptions, the Technical Reflectors cluster directed reflection outward at tools and processes. They struggled only with technical or technological issues, and examples were highly beneficial to their learning. This cluster aligned with transformative learning theory (systems thinking), cognitive constructivism (building schemas), and social constructivism (using technology as mediating artifacts for knowledge construction). In the next iteration, students will create short informal videos as worked examples, collectively building scaffolds for clear mental models. A reflective prompt added to assignments will ask students to describe technical challenges they overcame, transforming frustrating roadblocks into valuable problem-solving lessons. This qualitative analysis of an advanced instructional design graduate course revealed four distinct learning ecologies (Q = 0.686), showing that students experienced the course in qualitatively different ways. The findings highlight a critical need for more personalized and adaptive course designs that acknowledge diverse learning ecologies rather than treating all students as a monolithic group. Using learning experience network analysis, we identified theory-grounded interventions tailored to each cluster while also addressing struggles that cut across multiple clusters. These design changes aim to reduce friction and strengthen engagement by connecting existing strengths to related weaknesses, while recognizing that the four ecologies share overlapping theoretical alignments that suggest certain design moves may produce benefits across multiple ecologies simultaneously.  Beyond informing improvements for this specific course, this study demonstrates how LENA can help DBR researchers systematically translate complex qualitative data into actionable design moves.
