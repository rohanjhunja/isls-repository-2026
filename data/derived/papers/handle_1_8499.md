# The Emotional and Corporeal Dimensions of Belonging to STEM Education

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper explores the politics of belonging in STEM education contexts by foregrounding emotions as an analytical lens. We show how political projects of belonging are constructed/resisted affectively in two different STEM postsecondary contexts, within a technical college and a university in Canada. Belonging is examined along two interrelated dimensions: having the ‘right’ kind of body and emoting in the ‘right’ kind of way. Those dimensions reveal subtle and pervasive practices of exclusion and marginalization as science students experience their belonging to STEM fields and to Canada. We discuss how a multiscalar analysis of bodies and emotions in relation to various spaces and various social relations could reveal more layered understandings of how bodies become distributed/valued within STEM fields.

## Theoretical background and purpose

Research on emotions in science education have been garnering increased attention (e.g., Bellocchi et al., 2016). This is in response to a cognitivist bias in scienc e education, that has been attributed to “a long-st anding Newtonian–Cartesian tradition of separation, prizing apart the mind and body, divorcing and polarizing  reason from feeling” (Alsop & Watts, 2003, p. 1044). Vario us methodological, theoretical, and empirical approaches have highlighted how emotions are part and parcel of social practices of teaching and learning in science learning environments (e.g., Tobin & Llena, 2014). For instance, inquiries into classroom’s emotional climate take situated and sociocultural perspectives when studying emotio ns and as such “overcome the limitations of examini ng emotions as individual psychological constructs” (T an, 2013, p. 553) that could naturalize emotions and fixate learning within individuals. Other similar research  traditions have examined how emotions are not just  passive impulses that happen to us but are “fabrications ma nufactured and maintained through corporeal and discursive means” (Butler cited in Chubbuck & Zembylas, 2008, p. 279). Drawing on poststructuralist thought, researchers have argued that analyses of emotions from socio-cu ltural traditions have been limited to micro-proces ses of interaction without addressing how emotions are dis cursively and materially constituted and constituti ng. Focusing on the performative nature of emotions (Ze mbylas, 2015), responds to calls from within the le arning sciences to develop “theories of power, to explain how power circulates in contexts, how it aligns with structural oppression, how it challenges oppression, how it is  subtle, and explicit, pervasive, and yet often inv isible” (Esmonde & Booker, 2017, p. 2).

Notions of belonging elicit emotional attachments t o collectives and imaginaries and are especially relevant when considering institutional practices o f exclusion and marginalization within STEM educati on (Nxumalo & Gitari, 2021). Avraamidou (2019) argued that emotions could be a valuable lens for studying experiences of inequalities and othering and what i t means to ‘become’ a science person in relation to  various systems of oppression and recognition. This paper explores the politics of belonging in STEM education contexts by foregrounding emotions as an analytical lens. We  demonstrate how political projects of belonging ar e constructed/resisted affectively in two different STEM postsecondary contexts. We e xamine emotionality in students’ lived experiences not only as reflective of the “dirty work of boundary maintenance” (Crowley, 1999) but as constitutive of how particular collectivities are made to belon g. We draw from Sara Ahmed’s work who looks at what emotions do and less on what they mean. A performative view of emotions examines how emotions work “through signs and on bodies to materialize the surfaces and boundaries that are lived as worlds” (Ahmed, 2013, p. 191).

Inspired by Sara Ahmed’s various writings including but not limited to the Cultural Politics of Emotions (2013), this paper attempts to release belonging fr om its self-referential associations and positive u ndertones (sense of belonging and feeling ‘at home’) to consider the political landscapes of belonging (see also Yuval-Davis,

2006) as they are formed and reformed through daily affects and emotions experienced by Black, Indigenous and

People of Colour (BIPOC) in postsecondary STEM programmes. ‘Positive’ emotions are not necessarily positive, as they might be connected to narrow conceptions of happiness (Ahmed, 2008) and lives of normativity (Berlant, 2011), inherited from within Western, colonial, cap italist, middle class societies. This helps to situ ate the ICLS2022 Proceedings 1405 © ISLS micropolitics (of lived/bodily experiences of racia lized bodies) in relation to macropolitics (of raci alized and racializing systems) (Mulcahy & Healy, 2021). Polit ical views on belonging allow us to explore how STE M educational contexts extend the “political and affe ctive economies of normativity” with the “productio n of a collective will to imagine oneself as a solitary ag ent who can and must live the good life [read also happy life, Ahmed, 2008] promised by capitalist culture” (Berlant, 2011, p. 167). This has particular significance to contexts of postsecondary STEM, that prepare future workers to enter in relationship with the economy and place s of labour and livelihood.

## Methodology

To explore the corporeal and emotional dimensions of belonging we bring together two data sets; one is from an ethnographic study of a college microbiology labora tory at a Canadian technical college and another da ta set draws from BIPOC students’ responses to an explorat ory survey of their experiences in postsecondary ST EM university programmes in a university setting in We stern Canada. The ethnographic study comes from the  first author’s doctoral research whereby notions of belonging to Canada via STEM education became salient through her various interactions with international students from India. The other data draws from an exploratory survey of BIPOC students’ lived experiences navigating STE M institutional structures, the first author’s post doctoral work alongside the second author. Together, these d ata sets allow us to view how students’ emotions wo rk “to create the very distinction between the inside and the outside” (Ahmed, 2004, p. 28), vis-à-vis STEM a s a disciplinary space and STEM as connected to nation-belonging. Data sources from the ethnographic study included v ideo recordings accompanied by fieldnotes of laboratory practices (e.g., students testing the ef fect of different products on bacterial growth), se mi-structured interviews with the students at the beginning of th e course related to their experiences coming from I ndia and living in Canada and ongoing discussions with the instructor about their emerging observations. For the purposes of this paper, we only draw on those data sources t o focus on data excerpts where themes of belonging were particularly apparent. For our analysis, we combine d thematic coding with collage as a data analysis p ractice (Morgaine, 2018) involving a juxtaposition of fragm ents of data, arranging and rearranging them togeth er to enable an analysis of the micropolitics (Bazzul, 2017) of belonging in relation to different scales (to STEM as a belonging to discipline or STEM as belonging to a nation) and in relation to students’ actual practices in the lab at the college level alongside students’ reflection s on their general STEM experiences at the universi ty level. Hence, this study takes a space-sensitive approach to the study of intersecting social relations to ex amine how “different aspects of belonging such as class (soci o-economic), gender and race (body or corporeal asp ects), ethnicity, and ethical/political value systems (rep resentative and performative aspects) assemble in c ertain locations and situations” (Youkhana, 2015, p. 15-16) and how emotions work to constitute and reconstitute those assemblages.

## Discussion of findings

Our analysis of the data from the two data sets enabled us to examine political work of belonging being exhibited along two interrelated dimensions: Belonging as having the ‘right’ kind of body and belonging as emoting in the ‘right’ kind of way. We present those two dimensions separately to examine how political projects of belonging circulate and are enacted/resisted through bodies and emotions. Belonging as having the ‘right’ kind of body Most of the students in the college microbiology lab were international students from India. In various interviews with students, they discussed their experiences of being channeled into this program by college recrui ters and agents back in India as their only entry ticket to Canada. Intersecting with students’ ethnic and immigration status, another noteworthy observation was that women formed the majority of students in this lab, constitutin g 17 out of 18 students. Nived, the instructor, commented ho w this is not surprising since “most students will become biotechnicians, following protocols”. This comment demonstrates how the program extends women as docile and compliant, doing what they are told, by ‘following’ protocols. In this sense, what it means to have a body in this particular context is configured along racial and g endered lines, reducing bodies to “their working pa rts” (Kimmerer, 2013, p. 49) through tedious and repetitive lab practices and an overemphasis on performing the ‘right’ techniques (as was evident through observations of pedagogies of the lab). STEM college programmes along with formal and informal channels of migration contribute to differential valuations of bodies, perpetuating gendered and racialized allocations of labour (Vora, 2015) f or Western economic gains. This helps to complicate  what it means to belong to STEM beyond acquiring the right bodily dispositions of a biotechnician to consider how learning/belonging in this context are situated wit hin larger gendered, racial, and capitalist formati ons. We ICLS2022 Proceedings 1406 © ISLS juxtapose this analysis with an undergraduate stude nt response on a survey describing his experiences of marginalization and discrimination as a South Asian inside a university lab setting: Often in science labs, sometimes when paired with p eople who are Caucasian, I have experienced lab partners who would disregard my physical being and turn towards other white people with whom they prefer to talk with. I don't think that was an intentional racist instance, I just have found that often when I am paired with Caucasian males in particular, tha t they don't know how to converse with me, want to converse with me or know how to relate to me. Feel somewhat lesser than them. In this case, we witness how practices of exclusion take place through bodily orientations that reinforce the idea that to belong to the lab, one needs to have the ‘right’ kind of body (in this case, a Caucasian male body). Those self-reflective and narrated experiences of margina lization could be equated with the subtle and perva sive practices of inclusion/exclusion, evident in the context of the college microbiology lab.  These analytic examples gesture at how material places (the college lab vs.  the university lab) intersects with social relatio ns to produce the ways that belonging to STEM is encountered/experienced. They both center belonging through corporeality as particular instances of having the right kind of body in STEM places, though each data segment is more nuanced in how different STEM bodies become constituted as ‘less’; this might happen in the moment (in transient bodily orientations of turning towards/ away) or in more pervasive and infrastructural arrangements (with the prevalence of particular bodies in specific places).

Belonging as “emoting in the right kind of way” When students were working in the lab, they often turned to each other or to their instructor to discuss their plans for working and living in Canada. Hence, accompanyi ng their ordinary lab practices, appears to be a sp ectral presence (with its associated fears, anticipation, anxieties, and hope) of wanting to belong with Canada through their labour in STEM. Such ‘positive’ and hopeful o utlooks for the future work to further normalize pr ecarious conditions (Veldstra, 2020) faced by international students as they experience their belonging to Canada. Asked to project themselves five years into the future, m any mentioned having emotional and financial stabil ity in Canada: Anushka: After 5 years, either I am working in my pharmaceutical industry, or I will clear my pharmacy test and become a pharmacist here, I don’t want to go back to India. In between I will get married and have 2 babies. I just need the perfect husband for me. Nisha (jokingly): Don’t worry, we will find a Canadian husband for you. Careful not to downplay international students’ visceral dreams and hopes for a better future, for Anushka, STEM was a pathway for belonging to Canada. However, becomes quickly gendered and racialized through becoming a wife to a “Canadian” man and mother. Juxtaposing th e positive with the negative, emoting “wrongly” works to reproduce exclusionary boundaries, as evident in a student’s response in the survey data: I am constantly seen as an angry person when I am outspoken about my mistreatment. Sometimes it feels as though my value is to entertain white folks in STEM, something to make fun of, something to tokenize. This student self-identifies as Korean and, in this  statement, we could see how anger turns to a techn ology of racism (Zembylas, 2015) that produces bodies in opposition to other ‘composed’ bodies, the white folks in STEM. But also, anger serves to disturb and challenge the “ordinary affects of racialization” (Mulcahy & Healy, 2021). Being an angry person is likened to a “kill-joy” (Ahmed, 2008), someone who disturbs belonging to/of whiteness in STEM education contexts. Reading students’ practices in the microbiology lab alongside students’ responses to the survey, we notice how students in STEM “acqu ire certain subject positions based on regulatory norms of social and political affect” (Zembylas, 2016, p. 20 6), that delimit/resist imaginaries of belonging at  multiple intersecting scales, spaces of STEM education and Canada.

## Conclusion and implications

Attending to the work of emotions enables an appreciation of how constructs of belonging conceal non-innocent relations with possible implications for justice-or iented efforts in STEM education. Our study also sh owed the possible merits in analytically attending to the af fective and corporeal aspects of belonging within s ituated practices and discourses in STEM contexts to examin e how micro-meso-and macro levels of analysis are c oICLS2022 Proceedings 1407 © ISLS constituted (Philip & Gupta, 2020) and to better highlight and along with that, imagine pedagogical possibilities within microlevel interactions, where agentic indiv iduals “improvise with ideologies and practices tha t may otherwise appear static and inevitable” (p. 196). This is an area that we hope to keep expanding on. Our analysis also showed how a multi-scalar analysis of bodies and emotions in relation to various spaces and various social relations might reveal more layered understandings of how bodies become distributed/valued within STEM fields.
