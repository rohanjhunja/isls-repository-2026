# Physics Students’ Metaphorical Gestures About Energy

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Energy is a central, cross-cutting concept in science, but its abstract nature poses challenges for learners. Metaphor has been recognized as a productive resource used by students, teachers, and scientists to understand and communicate about energy. While much research has focused on metaphors about energy expressed in learners’ speech, we know less about the range of ways learners use gesture to evoke metaphors about energy. In particular, the metaphor energy as substance has been found to be useful for conceptualizing various features of energy. Using a microethnographic approach, we demonstrate how students in an introductory algebra-based university physics course use gesture in three different ways to evoke substance-like metaphors that offer valuable affordances for sensemaking about energy: These include (1) container metaphor gestures, (2) stimulus metaphor gestures, and (3) accounting metaphor gestures. Implications for learning and teaching about energy are discussed.

## Introduction

Developing a robust but flexible understanding of e nergy is central to learning science and preparing students to make decisions around pressing 21st-century issues like climate change and the energy c risis (Neumann & Nordine, 2023). However, energy is a challenging to pic for learners due to its abstract and intangible  nature (Brundage et al., 2017; Harrer, 2017; Singh & Rosengrant, 2016). Research in science and physics education has shown that the use of metaphors for energy – for example, treating energy  like a substance – can be productive for students as they develop an understanding of energy, and particularly for specific ideas such as energy transfer and transformation (Close & Scherr, 2015; Lancor, 2 015; Scherr et al., 2012). However, to date, these  studies have primarily focused on metaphors about energy in  speech with less attention given to the role of bo dy-based representations like gestures. One exception is the  work of Dreyfus et al. (2015) who explored how a p rofessor and student used gesture to talk about energy. Gest ure is a key resource for sensemaking in science and mathematics educational contexts (e.g., Alibali & N athan, 2012; Roth & Lawless, 2002; Scopelitis et al., 2010; for a review, see Singer et al., 2017). Representational gestures are visible actions of the hands that illu strate literal or metaphorical objects and processes (Kendon, 2004; McNeill, 1986), and they can be employed by STEM students to imagine together imperceivable or abstr act ideas from astronomy (Euler et al., 2019) to qu antum mechanics (Perez et al., 2015).

Drawing on a microethnographic approach to the stud y of embodied communication (Erickson, 2004; Streeck & Mehus, 2005), we examine how students use representational gesture to evoke different metaphors for energy in the context of an introductory university  physics course. Building on Dreyfus et al. (2015),  we focus specifically on how students use their hands to illustrate various metaphors that depict energy as a substance-like entity (Brookes & Etkina, 2007; Lancor, 2014; Scherr et al., 2012). In this st udy, we characterize and present cases of students’ use of (1) container metaphor  gestures, (2) stimulus metaphor gestures, and (3) accounting metaphor gestures. By characterizing these novel representat ions of metaphor, this study extends our current understanding of how students use gesture to make sense of energy as a resource for learning physics.

## Background

From research in STEM education, we know metaphors play an important role in learning STEM (Abrahamson et al., 2016; Amin et al., 2015; Amin et al., 2012;  Brookes & Etkina, 2015; Dreyfus et al., 2014; Duit, 1991; Haglund, 2017; Haglund et al., 2013; Lancor, 2013; Pulaczewska, 1999; Scherr et al., 2012). In linguis tics, metaphor is defined as the use of familiar, concret e, body-based understandings of the world as the basis for understanding more abstract concepts like justice, love, or concepts from math and science (Cienki, 2016; Lakoff & Johnson, 1980). Students, teachers, and scientists alike use metaphors to ground abstract concepts in embodied, physical experiences (Beger & Jäkel, 2015; Daane et al., 2018; Kersting et al., 2024; La ncor, 2014; 2015). Metaphors support sensemaking in STEM in a number o f ways: They offer possibilities to make specific inferences and predictions by using familiar, concrete source domains (e.g., balance scales, spider webs, factories) and applying them tonew abstract scienti fic or mathematical objects and processes (e.g., eq uations, ecosystems, biological cells). Metaphors are also often shared and can be especially useful as part of negotiating  and establishing shared meanings together (Kersting & Sampieri-Cába, 2024), particularly in cases when learners are still developing their mathematical and scientific language (Abrahamson et al., 2016; Humar, 2021). In physics, researchers have identified a number of different metaphors students and experts use to make sense of energy. These metaphors include energy as a substance that can be accounted for, change from one form to another, flow between objects or systems, and be carried from place to place, lost, or stored (Amin, 2009; Brookes & Etkina, 2007; Duit, 2014; Lancor, 2013; 2 014), energy as a vertical location  (Dreyfus et al., 2014; Harrer, 2017), and energy as a stimulus, fuel, or agent (Harrer, 2017; Scherr et al., 2012), among others. Different metaphors for energy are productive in different co ntexts and situations (Harrer, 2017; Lancor, 2013; Scherr et al., 2012) and each metaphor foregrounds certain pr operties of energy and backgrounds others (Dreyfus et al., 2015; Lancor, 2013). For example, understanding a p hysical change as the movement of energy between energy stores evokes substance-like properties of energy as an entity that can be added to or lost from a container (Brookes & Etkina, 2007; Harrer, 2017; Lancor, 2013). On the other hand, energy migh t be understood as an agent or a substance with stimulus-like properties when making sense of this change in terms of the ro le energy plays to bring about the change  (Harrer, 2017; Scherr, 2012). Learning in physics i ncludes reorganizing knowledge to understand the contexts in which certain resources, like metaphors, are useful (Hammer et al., 2005; Kersting & Sampieri-Cábal, 2024). A number of specific activities and materials have been developed to support students’ productive use of substance metaphors when they learn about energy, including Energy Theater (Daane et al., 2014) and the use of Energy Tracking Diagrams (Scherr et al., 2016). Metaphors in STEM are expressed in modes of thinking and communicating that go beyond speech, such as gesture. Gesture has been shown to be an importa nt resource for sensemaking in STEM by helping stud ents model dynamic, 3D qualities of phenomena (e.g., Eul er et al., 2019; Marin, 2019; Roth & Lawless, 2002;  Stieff et al., 2016), share ideas that cannot be captured well with words (e.g., Alibali & Nathan, 2012; Keifert & Stevens, 2018; Vossoughi et al, 2020), and illustrate sophisticated ideas while learners are still mastering STEM language and terminology (e.g., Abrahamson et al., 2016; Flood et al., 2015; Mathayas et al., 2021; Scherr, 2008). A number of studies have explored students’ use of metaphor in representational gesture (Alibali & Nathan, 2012; Larsson & Stolpe, 202 3; Roth & Lawless, 2002). However, despite numerous studies on metap hor use in physics and metaphoric gesture use in STEM, there is still much  left to discover about how students use metaphor i n gesture when talking about energy.

One study that has bridged this gap is Dreyfus et al. (2015), who analyzed how a physics professor used metaphoric gestures during a lecture about the ener gy dynamics of forming atomic bonds. The physics pr ofessor used gesture and speech to trace an upward  motion on a potential energy graph while talking ab out a release of energy, combining two metaphors – energy as a vertical position  and energy as a substance  – to explain to students that two atoms are in a lower energy state  after they have formed a bond. A student subsequen tly reproduced the vertical tracing gesture while talki ng about energy changes, and also used a grasping g esture, treating energy like a substance an atomic bond cou ld be “holding” (Dreyfus et al., 2015). Dreyfus et al.’s study suggests that gesture is an important mode teachers and students can use to share metaphorical ideas about energy. Our present study builds on Dreyfus et al.’s work b y exploring additional ways that university student s in an introductory physics course use gesture to create s ubstance metaphors when they are discussing energy. Investigating a large corpus of video, we discovere d three additional metaphorical ways students use g esture to talk about energy, drawing on previously identified types of substance metaphors. Theoretical approach to metaphor in gesture To understand how students use gestures that evoke metaphors about energy, we  draw on Streeck (2009) and Cienki’s (2016) perspectives of metaphor in gesture. Building on Lakoff and Johnson’s (1980) conceptua l metaphor theory (CMT), Cienki and Streeck view meta phoric gesture as an interactional resource that pu blicly constructs and d isplays participants’ metaphoric interpretations of  abstract ideas that are not reducible to a conceptual mapping that takes place in the mind. Streeck describes metaphoric gesture as a process o f “ceiving” (2009), where ideas are selectively schematized and rendered visible by actions of the hands in ways that generate an interpretation of an image, event, or experience. To analyze how a gesture evokes a particular metaph or, we draw on Cienki’s guidelines (2016) that involve des cribing form features (e.g., hand shape, orientatio n, movement) and determining their referential functio ns (what they depict) by coordinating them with aff iliated speech. This makes it possible to identify  relevant concrete referents being illustrated throu gh gestures that are used to metaphorically represent a target domain such as energy. Streeck’s and Cienki’s approaches are consistent with the interactionist view of embodiment in learning scien ces, where the body is seen as a “publicly available resource for thinking, learning, and joint attentio n” (Stevens, 2012, p. 338). Through these perspecti ves, we understand gesture to be a publicly observable resource for communicating about and negotiating ideas together.

## Methodology

This study is part of a broader project to investigate students’ use of gesture in a Collaborative Learning through Active Sense-Making in Physics (CLASP) course (Potter et al., 2014), a required introductory physics course for many non-physics STEM majors at a large, culturally diverse university on the US West Coast. We examined and analyzed eight groups of three to four students working collaboratively through mechanics problems to model the energy dynamics of various physical phenomena (22 h ours of video). Students are asked to use the model s they develop to answer questions about puzzling scenario s (e.g., why does an oscillating mass on a spring s top oscillating over time?). The course activities provide numerous opportunities for students to discuss and draw on shared observations and experiences, and in this fa ce-to-face learning setting, students use gesture, drawin g, and other modes as important resources for making sense of and communicating ideas (Scherr, 2008). These features make this student-centered collaborative learning environment a fertile setting for investigating the expression of metaphor through representational gesture.

Our methodology draws on microethnographic approach es to understanding how participants in interactions use particular communicational forms a s part of making meaning together (Erickson, 200 4; Streeck & Mehus, 2005). We began by comprehensively reviewi ng (Erickson, 2004) sequences of interaction containing representational gestures that had been previously identified and coded by our project team. Using Cienki’s (2016) guidelines for identifying metaphoric gestures (des cribed above) we identified 38 example s of interactional sequences containing metaphoric energy gestures whe re students use their hands during an utterance to illustrate a concrete object or process as a non-literal depiction of energy, based on their accompa nying speech. Gestures where the research team could not be sure if the students were illustrating an actual physical object or process (as opposed to energy) were excluded.

From this collection of 38 metaphorical gestures about energy, we characterized how various hand shapes and arm movements made visible different types of c oncrete scenarios related to substance-like metaphors. We found that students used their hands to dep ict concrete imagery visually related to containers, stimuli (one event causing another), and accountable amounts of substance-like quantities. We describe our criteria for characterizing each of these types below and present an exemplar e xample of each ty pe of metaphorical imagery captured by gesture. We selected the examples we present in the  paper because they are clear, representative illus trations of each type of gestured substance metaphor. Clips were annotated and transcribed with ELAN (Lausberg & Sloetjes, 2009), which allows us to track the coordination of part icular imagery in gesture with co-occurring speech. The research team used group analysis, watching the cli ps repeatedly and analyzing them together to elimin ate idiosyncratic interpretations and mistakes with transcription and gesture annotation (Jordan & Henderson, 1995).

## Findings

In this paper, we characterize and present three new types of metaphorical gestures about energy that we found in our video: (1) container metaphor gestures, (2) stimulus metaphor gestures, and (3) accounting metaphor gestures (Table 1). Below, we define each type and give a representative example. Container metaphor gestures: Energy is a substance that can be contained A common metaphor about energy that we observed in gesture is the container metaphor. Brookes & Etkina

(2007) have observed that container metaphors are r ecruited by students and expert physicists alike to  talk about

energy as a substance-like entity that can be “bounded off and set off fr om the rest of the world” (Lakoff & Johnson, 1980, p. 29), and therefore be contained. Brookes and Etkina (2007) argue that container metaphors are productive because they create distinctions between  what is inside (e.g., in a system) and outside (e.g., in the environment), which may be useful when trying to conceptualize how energy is transferred, gained, and lost from different energy stores (e.g., kinetic or potential) throughout a physical process. When students use gesture to evoke a container metaphor for energy, they typical ly show a bounded space with their hands that creat es a metaphorical receptacle that is able to “contain” or “lose” energy. In Figure 1, we present the example of Cora (all student names are pseudonyms) evoking a container metaphor. Cora and her group are working on modelin g the energy dynamics of an oscillating spring mass  to explain why the amplitude of the oscillations decre ases over time. Cora proposes that this decrease in  amplitude is because energy is lost from an open system (the spring+mass+Earth system). She contrast s this with the case of a closed system where energy cannot leave: Creating a gestured container, she argues energy in a closed system would be trapped, so the oscillations would just go  on with the same amplitude forever, which is count er to the group’s observation. Therefore, they should model t he situation with an open system that allows for energy to flow out into the environment.

Cora uses her hands to create a bounded space, positioning her hands with palms orthogonal to and facing each other. Streeck (2009) argues that particular g estures come from manual actions of the hands and p atterns of holding objects, and Cora’s gesture can be interpre ted as a type of “enclosure holding” (Lederman & Kl atzky, 1987). The flattened hands make contact with the im aginary top and bottom surfaces of the container, demonstrating that the “energy system” has fixed, well-defined boundaries (the distance between her hands does not change). Cora’s orthogonally positioned hands suggest the holding of a heavy object, such as a filled container. As a multilingual learner, Cora’s use of the container gesture helps her illustrate her ideas to her group. Although Cora does not use canonical language, we interpret her utterance to mean “energy in the system is cons erved;” it is trapped in the container and cannot escape. The container metaphor gesture illustrates the immutable nature of a closed system with a f ixed and never changing amount of energy that canno t escape the container’s bounds, which would cause the spring to oscillate up and down without any changes in its amplitude over time. Figure 1 Cora’s Container Metaphor Gesture Using container metaphor gestures offers productive affordances when making sense of energy dynamics by highlighting properties of energy that are necessary for conceptualizing abstract ideas like energy conservation (Brookes & Etkina, 2007). To appreciate energy as a conserved quantity, it is useful to localize energy (e.g., in an object or system container, like in Cora’s example), to think of energy as being stored (e.g., in a kinetic energy or potential energy container), and to track energy as it moves from place to place  (e.g., from the system container into the environment container if the system is open, or from one store container to another store container if the system is closed).

Stimulus metaphor gestures: Energy is a substance that initiates change A second metaphor for energy we observed in gesture is the stimulus metaphor. When experts or learners use the stimulus metaphor for energy, they ascribe agency (Lakoff & Johnson, 1980) to energy as a substance that has causal effects on physical objects (Harrer, 2017; Scherr et al., 2 012). Scherr et al. (2012) argue that using the stimulus metaphor to describe energy can be helpful  for understanding the mechanistic relationship bet ween energy system is conservative forces and energy transfers, and it also provides a bridge to socio-political conceptions of energy that foreground energy’s ability to start and sustain processes. When students use stimulus metaphor gestures, they depict energy as a substance-like entity that can be added to a system and that causes a subsequent physical change that is also depicted in gesture.

In Figure 2, we present an example of Ella’s enactm ent of a stimulus metaphor gesture. In this episode, Ella and her group are modeling the energy dynamics  of a bucket being pulled up from a well by a rope.  One of the group members proposes that each time someone pulls on the bucket, it goes higher and faster. Ella disagrees that the bucket’s speed would change and suggests that more energy would need to be added to make it go faster. She uses gesture to illustrate energy as a stimulating agent that causes the bucket to move faster. Holding a marker, Ella sweeps her right hand forward, away from her body, and traces a curved upward trajectory. Ella’s corresponding speech, “put in even more energy” (Figure 2a), indicates that she is illustrating a process of adding energy. Then, she demonstrates the causal effect of adding that energy: Ella propels her hand up in a swift vertical motion as she says, “to make it go faster” (Figure 2b), illustrating the bucket moving quickly upwards along a straight path. The rapid succession  of the putting in more  energy gesture (Figure 2a) and the increased speed of the bucket  gesture (Figure 2b) highlights the causal link betw een adding more energy and visible changes in the physical phenomenon that occur as a result. Ella’s gesture shows energy as a substance-like entity that can be added to a system to cause a physical change (Scherr et al., 2016). Figure 2 Ella’s Stimulus Metaphor Gesture The stimulus metaphor gesture is useful for highlighting  the role energy plays in causal-mechanistic explanations of physical processes. This role is ty pically abstract – changes in the state of a physical system are seen as observable manifestations of changes to the energy within the system – but gesture has concrete, dynamic affordances that allow learners to make the invisible energy changes visible and explicitly connect them with the observable changes in the physical system.

Accounting metaphor gestures: Energy is a substance that can be accounted for A third metaphor for energy we observed in students ’ gesture is the accounting metaphor where students treat energy as a substance that can be accounted for (Lancor, 2014, 2015). Accounting metaphors often ma ke use of spatial metaphors for quantities that Lakoff and Johnson (1980) described as being rooted in physical experiences with substances, like level or height increasing wh en accumulating a substance or stacking physical ob jects (higher is more), or the width increasing when objects accumulate (wider is more). Treating energy as a substance that can be accounted for allows learners and exper ts alike to quantify amounts of energy, identify ch anges in these amounts, and recognize reciprocal relationshi ps between different amounts of energy. This highli ghts the quantitative aspects of the energy conservation principle (Lancor, 2014). Our observations of accounting metaphor gestures also support Dreyfus et al.’s (2015) observation that substance and vertical location metaphors are often combined in gesture when used to describe energy, but we found additional variation in ways students use gesture a b unless you put in even more energy to make it faster Ella to illustrate energy as something that can be accou nted for. When students use accounting metaphor gestures, they use their hands (a) to illustrate energy level s as vertical locations in space, but also (b) to indicate amounts of energy as vertical or horizontal extents or spans of space, and (c) to display reciprocally changing levels of energy in different energy stores.

In Figure 3, we present an example of Alex using ge sture to evoke the metaphor energy as a substance that can be accounted for  by producing a balancing gesture to show compensation.  Alex and her group are working to understand the energy dynamics of a buck et being pulled up from a well (the same problem El la’s group is working on in the previous example). Respo nding to another group member’s request for clarification, Alex explains that the system (bucket+Earth) should be open because there is energy entering the system as work (coming from the person pulling up the bucket) and there is a subsequent increase in potential energy (associated with the height increase of the bucket as it ascend s from the bottom of the well) inside the system. I n contrast, if it were a closed system, the increase in potential ene rgy would require an accompanying compensating decr ease in a different energy store (in this case, kinetic energy), which does not happen here. Alex illustrates this compensation of increasing po tential energy with decreasing kinetic energy to he r groupmate by using her hands to create an accounting metaphor gesture. She says, “because there wasn’t kinetic energy to cancel out the potential,” and moves her two flattened hands alternatingly up and down (Figure 3, a-c), showing that a decrease in kinetic energy would “ca ncel out” or reciprocally balance the increase in  potential energy in a closed system. The heights of her hands illustrate the amounts of energy in each store, treating energy as a substance-like quantity that can be amassed and accounted for. This gesture visualizes the inverse relationship between changes in amounts of kinetic energy and po tential energy in a closed system, foregroun ding energy conservation and, in particular, how energy is transferred between and distributed across different stores. Figure 3 Alex’s Accounting Metaphor Gesture The accounting metaphor gesture highlights the idea that energy can be quantified and allows learners to visually account for energy in its distribution across different objects or systems and across different energy stores (e.g., kinetic and potential). This realization is important for understanding that energy cannot be f orever “lost” or simply “disappear” but rather is conserved throughout its transfers and transformations (Scherr et al., 2016).

## Discussion and concluding remarks

Energy is an abstract and difficult to comprehend c oncept. Our study helps to broaden our understandin g of how physics students draw on substance metaphors in ges ture, and how these gestures can help highlight spe cific properties of energy as part of students’ sensemaki ng about energy dynamics in mechanics problems. Bui lding on the two forms of metaphorical gestures about ene rgy identified by Dreyfus et al. (2015), we discove red three different types of substance metaphor gestures for energy: (1) container metaphor gestures, (2) stimulus metaphor gestures, and (3) accounting metaphor gestures. We show how each type of metaphorical gesture about  energy provides specific affordances for foregrounding par ticular aspects of energy conservation and the role  of energy in bringing about physical change (Brookes & Etkina, 2007; Scherr, 2012). to cancel out the potential a b c In each of the three cases we present, students use d these specific types of metaphoric gestures as supportive resources for making their ideas about e nergy publicly available to their group members. Co ra’s container metaphor gesture, which creates a bounded object like a container, emphasizes the conservation of energy within a closed system. As a multilingual le arner, Cora’s verbal expression (“energy system is conservative”) may be unconventional, but her gestu res clarify and elabo rate her speech. In Ella’s case, the stimulus metaphor gesture  provided evidence for her argument and may have hel ped her group to better understand and address her claim that more energy i s needed to make the bucket move faster. Her gestur e – in addition to speech – provided a multimodal justification, enhancing the  quality of Ella’s argument. Similarly, Alex’s accounting metaphor gesture  effectively illustrates a key idea about an inverse  relationship in her explanation about energy transfer and conservation in a closed system. In the past, the use of substance metaphors to discuss energy has been described as problematic by some (for a review  of both sides of this argument, see Duit, 1987) who  have argued that certain metaphors can pose barriers for students’ learning (Chi and Slotta, 19 93). Metaphors, like models, are limited representations, and there is always potential for students to draw inac curate and problematic comparisons and extensions (de Kirby, 2017). But metaphors can be powerful tools in learn ing if students are supported in refining their und erstanding of which contexts certain metaphors about energy ar e helpful in, and which they are misleading in (Amin et al., 2015; Haglund, 2017; Lancor, 2014). Our study supports arguments that metaphors about energ y (Amin et al., 2015; Daane, et al.; 2014; Harrer, 2017; Lancor, 2014; Scherr et al., 2012) and the gestures students use to evoke these metaphors (Dreyfus et al., 2015) are an impor tant asset that students bring to learning physics (Euler et al., 2019; Mathayas, et al., 2021; Scherr, 2008). We extend the concept of substance metaphors for energy by showing new forms of  metaphorical gestures and providing empirical evidence from classroom interactions: S tudents spontaneously use embodied, metaphorical gestures as resources for reasoning about energy in collaborati ve physics learning settings.

Our findings also have implications for physics ins truction. First, providing students with face-to-face settings to collaboratively model and solve physics problems presents important opportunities for students to use metaphorical gesture as a resource to share ideas and make sense of energy together in ways that are less supported in other settings. Collaborative learning in face-to-face settings involves not only verbal communication but also allows students to use multimodal resources to supp ort their emerging forms of scientific discourse (D anielsson et al., 2023). Encouraging students in collaborativ e, active learning settings to use gesture to share  their ideas as a legitimate tool may be beneficial for supporting collaborative sensemaking conversations and encoura ging students to share ideas even when they feel they ha ve not yet mastered certain language or concepts (R oth & Lawless, 2002). Instructors being aware of and purp osefully leveraging their own metaphorical gestures  about energy might support teaching abstract ideas like energy conservation and quantification. The public availability of students’ metaphorical gestures may allow instructors to glean insight into students’ thinking about energy and provide just-in-time support when necessary (Alibali & Nathan, 2012; Flood et al., 2015; Manogue et al., 2014). We note that our study has several limitations: Fir st, we only investigated metaphorical gesture in a US context. Because some metaphor use is culturally and linguistically specific (Cienki, 2016), we expect  it would exhibit variation across different contexts, and future research should more comprehensively explore metaphorical gestures about energy in non-U.S. and non-Western physics learning settings. Furthermore, whi le we brought to light new types of energy metaphor gestures, we do not claim our curren t characterization of energy metaphor gestures is exhaustive even in this setting, and ou r future work will continue to examine additional v ariations of energy metaphor gestures as part of better understanding how students learn about and make sense of energy.
