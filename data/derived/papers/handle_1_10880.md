# Understanding the Institutional Challenges, Resources, and Strategies for Minoritized Students to Pursue STEM Degrees

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
While enrollment numbers of minoritized students at four-year institutions is increasing steadily, STEM degree retention equity gaps continue to persist. These equity gaps have been and continue to be addressed primarily through instructional reformation, and often fail to consider the role the underlying institutional structures play. In our study, we conducted 50 semi-structured interviews with diverse students across different years and disciplines to identify and compare the most common institutional challenges, resources, and strategies for students across different demographic backgrounds. Our study and seeks to provide insights for institutional leaders to support justice through enacting policies and top-down changes.

## Introduction

The persistence in degree retention equity gaps (Estrada et al., 2016; Fry & Lopez, 2012) underlines the ongoing need for reform in undergraduate science, technology, engineering and mathematics (STEM) education (Borrego & Henderson, 2014; Singer et al., 2012). A few individual-as well structural-level factors have shown to influence a students’ persistence in STEM (Ejiwale, 2013; Hsu & Fang, 2019). However, STEM reform s often focus on improving individual-level factors such as instructional practices empl oyed by individual faculty members (Henderson et al., 2011). Though such reforms may have a positive impact on a single course, they fail to foster broad, scaled-up change in departments and across institutions (Austin, 2011; Corbo et al., 2015). To better acknowledge the structural-level factors influenc ing STEM retention, we utilized a systems value approach, which sheds light on how the interp lay between individual-level (in this case, faculty) and institutional-level contexts (disciplines, departments) contribut e toward potential areas of reform (Ferguson & Austin, 2011; Kezar, 2014). We identify the ways in which localized STEM reform,  such as pedagogical improvements can be leveraged through systemic contexts to scale-up methods of change. Our research questions are: (1) What are the main challenges, resources, strategies students experience in their STEM education? and (2) How, if at all, do these challenges, resources, and strategies across demographic backgrounds?

## Methods

Our study was conducted at a private R1 university. Participants (n=50) were students of all years, across different STEM disciplines and diverse identities, including but not limited to: race, gender and first-generation status. We conducted a 60-minute, semi-structured interview with each participant  about their major challenges, resources, and strategies in the pursuit of their STEM degree. We utilized thematic analysis, via inductive coding, (Charmaz, 2006), to identify common themes. Two coders independently coded a subset of the data to establish inter-rater reliability and produce a final codebook. The final  codebook consisted of 10 institutional and instructional challenges, 14 existing and 14 missing resources, and 8 strategies. To examine the impacts of student identities on these categories, we created an intersectionalit y index to categorize student identities into differe nt tiers of marginalization and subsequently divided students i nto 4 quartiles, from lowest to highest privilege. We determined levels of privilege through leveraging the theory o f intersectional invisibility, which highlights the differences in experiences of oppression of individuals with a single marginalized identity in compari son to individuals with multiple intersecting ones (i.e. Black, first-generation), (Purdie-Vaughns & Eibach, 2008).

## Discussion

An institutional context matters greatly and can enhance or diminish student educational journey in STEM. Table 1 offers a brief insight into how one theme, Challenges, extend past individual-level, course-specific barriers. The code “Course Expectations” underscores how institutional curriculum and pace expectations may not facilitate adequate instructional support for students as they progress through their degree. The other two themes, Resources and Strategies, both demonstrate similar institutional patterns. While indiv idual-level reform efforts through a single faculty me mber, can offer a temporary solution, the proliferation of lo ng-term reform necessitates institutional involvement. Through a multi-faceted approach, involving faculty, institutional leaders, programs, the effort to increase student degree persistence a cross all demographics, especially those from minor itized backgrounds, could be shared, as opposed to a stude nts’ experience being heavily dependent on the limi ted reforms that are currently prominent at four-year institutions, namely pedagogical reform. A primary limitation of this work is its small scope and therefore lack of broad generalizability that we hope to address in future.

## Conclusion

We pose the following approaches to address the challenges identified by the participants in our study: facilitating proactive mentorships for courses and STEM pathways, establishing a central portal for course-specific resources, scaffolded career opportunities and professional development programs, and adding more accessible academic counselors. These suggestions are achievable, concrete stepping-stones towards supporting all students particularly the marginalized ones in their STEM ed ucation. To make such efforts effectively and long lasting, reforms must be symbiotic across the whole institution, not confined to individual courses.
