# Mapping Disinformation: Agent-Based Modelling Instruction for Cognitive and Systemic Dimensions of Media Ecosystems

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
As disinformation ecosystems expand, they present numerous threats to society and challenges to educators and researchers, who employ diverse approaches to prepare learners for the increasingly challenging task of assessing the reliability of information. We developed a curriculum unit based on agent-based computational modeling (ABM) and studied how that unit improved learners’ understanding of individual and systems-level factors of disinformation ecosystems. We used concept maps as approximations of learners’ knowledge structures. We identified changes in those concept maps that are associated with concepts addressed by our ABM unit. More markedly, participants included visual features in the concept maps that correlated with both individual and systems-level aspects of disinformation (such as cognitive biases and echo chambers) and represented concepts that we conjecture go beyond recall of variables present in the models. Evidence points to opportunities for employing ABM for the exploration of the multiple dimensions of disinformation.

## Introduction

Disinformation spread is a global issue that affect s various aspects of society (e.g., Lyngaas et al.,  2023; vander Linden et al., 2020). The inability to critically assess information has serious consequences such as the polarization of the online environment, mistrust and distrust of media, low-quality civic and political online engagement, and susceptibility to fraud and scams (Britt et al., 20 19; Chang et al., 2020). Accordingly, many educatio n programs aim to enhance youth’s media literacy (ML) skills. However, there is limited evidence that such programs enable learners to internalize ML skills and construct the ir own ML knowledge frameworks. To address this gap, we utilized NetLogo (Wilensky, 1999), an agent-based modeling (ABM) environment, to build  a curricular unit to provide youth with the opportunity to test and visualize their assumptions about disinformation spread. We tested those models by interviewing students, who were requested to create concept maps—used in this study to represent learners’ knowledge structures. We observed associations b etween concepts in our unit and visual features of concept maps. Notably, these maps visually depicted  individual and systemic aspects of disinformation that potentially extend beyond model variables. This  paper builds on a research program that employs des ign-based research and social media data to investigate the i nterconnections of cognition, language, and radical ization motivated by online discourse (see, for example, Russo et al., 2021; Russo & Blikstein, 2022; Russo & Blikstein, 2023a).

## Literature review

Many educational activities have emerged recently t hat employ educational technology for learning abou t misand disinformation. Players in games such as Bad News (Basol et al., 2020) and LAMBOOZLED! (Chang et al.,

2020) simulate disinformation spread. Other uses of technology include MOOCs, for example, “English for Media

Literacy” (Fiori & Nichols, n.d.) and a “Media Lite racy” course developed for YouTube (CrashCourse, 20 18). Despite the affordances and potential attractiveness of games and digital media for instruction, their shortcomings include the passive roles of learners in MOOCs and a disconnect between players’ real-life experiences and media literacy games. Another approach includes fully dev eloped curricula, for example, Civic Reasoning Onli ne (Wineburg et al., 202 2) and “Disinformation Detox” (Paris et al., 2022), which focus on the development of “ecological literacy” (Philips & Milner, 2020). Str engths of those curricula include a long history of  testing and iterative development.

These examples demonstrate that many current educational solutions focus either on individuals’ abilities to make sense of isolated bits of information (e.g., via fact-checking) or on comprehension of systems-level factors. We understand that those forms o f understanding are complementary, and draw on Phil lips and Milner’s

(2021) understanding of disinformation ecosystems to draw a parallel between that phenomenon and comp lex

systems “composed of multiple individual elements t hat interact with each other ye t whose aggregate properties or behavior is not predictable from the elements th emselves” (Wilensky & Rand, 2015). We extend that comprehension of complex systems to media ecosystems.

Previous applications of ABM for instruction relate d to disinformation include NetLogo’s Rumor Mill (Wilensky, 1997), the Twitter Behavior Agent-Based Model (Anjum et al., 2023), and Participatory  Propaganda (Johnson, 2021). Those exemplify how ABM enables manipulation of variables associated with disinformation at two or more levels, such as is the case with the individual/cognitive (Britt et al., 2019) and systemic levels, present in disinformation. More concrete examples of those variables include the number of connections present  in a social network (a systems-level variable) or fact-checking behavior (individual-level). Further, ABM facilitates simulations with which learners can visualize the effects of ch anges in the systems at those levels. In the case o f disinformation, these explorations offer an accessi ble means of experimenting without the potential th reat of actually spreading false information. Despite those affordances, little research exists on applying models like the ones above in educational settings. Motivated by that potential, this paper seeks an answer to the question of how computational modeling relates to learners' understanding of disinformation.

## Methods

We conducted task-based interviews using Disinformation Spread (DS), a suite of four NetLogo models that are part of a curricular unit designed to allow for the  visualization and manipulation of variables associ ated with the spread of disinformation. Variables in the models i nclude biases, fact-checking behavior, and political discourse triggers across populations. For space consideratio ns, we did not include a full description of the mo del in this paper. See Russo and Blikstein (2023b) for a complete description of the models and their motivation, as well as design and methodological decisions taken by the research team.

## Participants and procedures

We remotely interviewed 1 high school and 9 college students (18-20 years old; 6 male, 4 female; 5 international). Participants were presented with a concept map of a  “medical ecosystem,” a field unrelated to the task, to avoid biasing learners’ perception s. They were given 7 minutes to draw (using Zoom’s whiteboard feature) concept maps that described their perceptions of disinforma tion ecosystems. Learners then worked with three of  the DS models and were asked to edit their concept maps. We view concept maps as external representations of schemata participants held about disinformation, that is, on e’s cognitive framework for the phenomenon, which i ncludes the concept’s definition and its relationships with other concepts (Neumann & Kopcha, 2018). More specifically, we understand that features of the concept maps are associated with learners’ knowledge structures; analogously, changes in the concept maps indicate changes in knowledge structures.

## Data analysis

We focus our analysis on the post-interaction concept maps. We used interview data only when verification of concept map data was needed; pre-interaction concept maps were consulted for comparison. We first counted the nodes in each of the post-interaction concept maps that made explicit reference to terms used in the models (which we call “model terms”) and how many used different terms (“conceptual terms”). We conjectured that using model terms was associated with a recall-based change in participants’ knowledge representation, w hereas conceptual terms were associated with (arguably primitive) evi dence of transfer (Gentner et al., 2003). Finally, we assigned values to the level at which the new bias-related nodes changed each concept map (Low-Medium-High)—that was meant to measure the impact of that element in the change in participants’ schema of disinformation.

## Findings

Eight out of the 28 nodes added post-interaction referred to biases, one of the many variables used in the NetLogo models. Those newly added nodes were a mix of model  terms (e.g., “biases”) and conceptual terms (“inte rnal biases”). That numerical predominance in the absolu te number of nodes  was reflected in the number of participants who added bias-related nodes to their concept maps —that is, references to bias were present across visual representations of most participants, not just a few: seven out of the ten learners included such nodes. That result is convergent with previous findings (Russo et al., 2023a) and is especially interesting because no particular relevance was assigned to that variable over others  (except it appeared earlier in the unit) in the res earchers’ speech or in DS. Henceforth, we focus on the 7 participants who added bias-related nodes.

## Bias and changes in participants’ schemata

Five out of 7 participants in our final subset incl uded conceptual terms, i.e., terms that were not ex act reproductions of those used in the models (e.g., “e ducation systems” and “status of person”). One part icipant, Julia, connected the “bias” node to “Echo chambers of reinforcing false claims,” which was connected to “social media” (Figure 1a; both were in her pre-interaction concept map). That chain of nodes seems  to represent the understanding that bias was an effect of “echo cham bers” present in social media. Another participant,  Julien, connected “Biases” to the central node “Disinformat ion,” and from the former, she connected three new n odes (“political orientation,” “knowledge,” and “frequen cy to read disinformation”) that seem to mix the ca uses and effects of biases (Figure 1b). Those examples indicate how participants adopte d the concept of bias in their knowledge structures differently, such as cause or effect. We interpret this preliminary evidence as suggesting that working with the models influenced learners’ schema ta for disinformation or at least their description s of the phenomena.

In those concept maps, bias-related nodes did not play the “mediating” role obs erved in concept maps where they connected to both individual and system factors.

## Discussion and conclusion

This paper explores a potential use of computationa l agent-based modeling for the instruction of two dimensions of disinformation—that related to individual, cognitive aspects, and that related to information ecosystems. Most notably, we found that the concept of bias is a potential mediator for learners’ understanding of the interrelations of those two dimensions. That evidence illustrates how computational modeling can foster a more comprehensive understanding of disinformation, one that is in line with Philips and Milner’s (2021) proposition of an ecological literacy perspective as a response to a “polluted” media ecosystem: the consequences of our informatio n intake and output should be considered concerning their “environmental” effects. The multilayered nature of ABM might help in that task as it affords experimentation and  manipulation of the interwoven effects of one dime nsion over another.

From a critical standpoint, we understand that the term bias has r ecently garnered increased public interest, and learners in our sample seem to have i ncorporated the term into their knowledge structure s upon priming. Participants promptly pointed out cases wh ere they thought forms of biases (e.g., cognitive, editorial) were present. The question remains open whether tha t aptness is matched with appropriate judgment or i f that is in itself a form of bias. We anticipate that this r esearch can pave the way for other applications of computational models, specifically the use  of ABM, in the field of social science education, s imilar to its extensive application in natural sciences domains. Our findings point to an auspicious fit between the nature of the content to be learned (the complex, multi-agent interaction giving rise to emergent behaviors in disinformation ecosystems) and the affordances and features of ABM.
