# Refining the Recipe: Ingredients Driving Completion and Satisfaction in Online Hands-On Learning Activities

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This article furthers learning science research by analyzing behavior and sentiment from online hands-on projects to identify what factors drive completion and satisfaction. This article demonstrates the quantitative impact of different design decisions on learner outcomes from a sample of more than 237,000 learners interacting with graded in-browser coding assignments and more than 480,000 learners taking side-by-side instructor-guided projects. For example, including industry scenarios, practice opportunities, complex question types, and supplemental resources emerged as significant drivers of learners’ success and enjoyment in these online experiences. Insights from this analysis can help educators and platforms create more effective hands-on activities for learners globally.

Purpose Hands-on and application-based online activities provide learners the opportunity to gain practical skills relevant to their future careers. These types of activities within online courses align more closely with real-world work scenarios and better prepare students for industry expectations (Thomson et al., 2019). Creative assessments with real-world applications can lead to greater student inno vation and confidence, especially for those with le ss traditional backgrounds or less higher education ex perience (Peoples, 2020). Moving beyond multiple-choice exams into projects, presentations, and group work can increase students’ employability and decrease stress levels, resulting in more equitable outcomes (Whitteck & Fr itz, 2021). Crucially, hands-on activities can increase skills transfered to on-the-job tasks after completing the online course (Burnell, 2019). For this research  analysis, we focused on two of the newest technologies offe red, specifically Graded Lab (GL) assignments and Guided Projects  (GPs). The Labs product feature is a cloud-based environment that enables learners to access and use software without needing to install it on their local machines, which educators can then connect to automatic graders to provide nu meric and written feedback to learners on their sub missions. GPs are short, project-based learning experiences that enable learners to apply new skills in a practical setting while receiving video style instruction simultaneously. Many partners and vendors are already excited to mak e in-browser coding activities and interactive instructor-guided projects but have questions about how to make a great GL or GP within their online courses. At this stage, educators have seen and understood the benefits of including in-browser coding and other hands-on assessments through rigorous quantitative analysis (Hickey et al., 2020; Urban, 2023). The question we’re now hearing for Labs and side-by-side instruction projects is not “Should I use this?” but rather “What design elements can make it most successful for learners?” Thus, that latter question is what we aimed to answer in this study. Theoretical approach and framework This inquiry is grounded in the pedagogical theorie s of Constructivism, Cognitive Load Theory, Self-Determination Theory, and Experiential Learning. Co nstructivism posits that learners actively construc t their understanding and knowledge through experiences and reflection, suggesting that hands-on activities can provide a rich environment for such knowledge construction (Ertmer & Newby, 2013; Huang, 2002). Cognitive Load Theory emphasizes the need to balance the complexit y of the task with the learner ’s cognitive capacity, thereby ensuring that learners are engaged without being ov erwhelmed within complex learning activities such as technical projects (Feldon et al., 2018). Self-Determination Theory underscores the role of learne rs’ perceived competence, autonomy, and relatedness in fostering motivation, suggesting that giving learners more control over their learning and providing opportunities for conn ection can enhance their motivation and satisfactio n (Deci & Ryan, 2000; Jacobi, 2018; Liu et al., 2014; Stolk e t al., 2018). Lastly, Experiential Learning suggest s that direct experiences are one of the most  powerful way to acquire knowledge and skills, especially when it comes to transferring skills from educational experiences to  real-world scenarios  (Anderson et al., 2016; Kong, 2021; Markel & Guo, 2020). In this context, online hands-on activities provide a practical, experiential lea rning environment that can enhance both retention and satisfaction, but only when designed most effectively. Taken together, the Constructivist view of knowledg e building through experiences and reflection is needs to be balanced with the principles of Cogniti ve Load Theory, ensuring that the complexity  of these handson activities in online courses does not overwhelm  the learner’s cognitive capacity. This balance, coupled with the Self-Determination Theory ’s emphasis on fostering learners ’ perceived competence, autonomy, and relatedness, can enhance motivation and satisfaction. Simultaneously, the principles of Experiential Learning are incorporated, leveraging these activities as powerf ul tools for knowledge and skill acquisition, parti cularly in transferring skills from educational experiences to  real-world scenarios. This integrated approach optimizes the learning experience, enhancing both retention and enjoyment.

## Methods

We began our investigation by defining and operationalizing key factors we wanted to examine as potential drivers on the Coursera platform. We manually tagged these criteria, creating new data points to combine with existing data. After cleaning the data, we used a double machine learning model for analysis, controlling for various factors at individual learner and course levels.

Our final sample included over 362,000 unique lab-to-learner interactions across more than 237,000 unique users and 32 labs. For guided projects, our dataset included over 729,000 active enrollments ac ross 75 different projects. We ensured a diverse mix of  projects and difficulty levels for a comprehensive understanding of design impacts.

The learners were globally distributed, with the US A and India comprising about half of the total enrollments. Paying and free consumer learners each  made up about one-third of the total sample for the Labs, with Enterprise learners making up the rest. For the guided projects, Enterprise learners comprised more than 60% of the total learners.

We measured two main outcomes: course completion ra te and learner satisfaction. The analysis aimed to determine the impact of individual features on t hese outcomes but faced challenges due to correlated features and high-dimensional confounding variables. We used the doub le machine learning (DML) method to construct robust controls and account for regularization bias. We controlled for course-level factors, lab-level features, and user-level characteristics.

## Results

Overall, we found a strong alignment between the drivers of course completion and the drivers of satisfaction, an even stronger relationship than found in previous Coursera research (Drivers of Quality, 2020).

## Key findings for in-browser coding assignments

Four main factors emerged from our modeling as posi tive significant factors of both retention and enjo yment. First, we had practice, which looked at whether the re was an Ungraded Lab (UL) before the graded version of a Lab in the course. A lab that is ungraded on the platform is a course item where learners can execute code, build on instructors’ starter code, and try out what happens when they change different variables or functions by running it in the browser  without having a specific task they need to submit for grading. When courses did have this presence of practice (at least one UL) before the GL in question, we saw a 12% increase in the likelihoo d of learners completing the course as well as a 13% inc rease in learners’ provi ding a five-out-of-five star rating for the course. For intermediate difficulty level cours es, we found a 30% increase in course completion wh en a UL, or lab only for practice, is utilized before the GL, as compared with a more modest 5% lift in course completion for beginner courses with a practice lab included.

Secondly, when a clear  industry connection was present with in the GL, learners had a 5% percent increase in their likelihood of course completion a nd an 11% lift in course satisfaction. This impact jumps to a 9% increase when narrowing specifically to job-seeking learners. Third, we examined human support, examining if there was any course staff member answering stud ents’ questions in the discussion forum for the GL. The presence of staff support in the course’s discussion forum led to an impressive 39% lift in course completion and a 25% increase in course five-star ratings. Fourth, we investigated the power of using a solution guide, i.e. a written doc or video walk-through that is locked until after a learner passes the GL itself. Including a solution guide linked to a 45% increase in course completion and a 34% increase in five-star ratings. We also found a few factors that only boosted satis faction and not course completion. The first of these factors was the usage of hints, which looked at whether the  instructor or course team had written  specific recommendations provided to the learner if they became stuck in the GL. The presence of hints within the GL led to a 5% increase in five-star ratings but showed no impact on learners’ likelihood of com pleting the course. The other main factor explaining some of the variance i n satisfaction but not retention was the presence o f external support materials. This factor examined if the inst ructor or course  team had included any external resources to help learners troubleshoot issues while attempting to complete the GL. Including external materials for learner support had only a 1% increase in rating, a small but significant lift.

## Key findings for guided projects

We found that when the instructor used slides and s upporting visuals in the initial task of the projec t, the completion rates increased by 15%, and the five-star ratings saw a significant rise of 150%, which is 2.5 times the initial level of five-star ratings. For intermediate GPs, the inclusion of support materials resulted in a rise of 20% compared to a 11% increase for beginner GPs.

We examined what portion of the quiz questions promoted higher-order thinking and deterred simple copy-paste responses. Complex questions included any quiz prompts that us ed Bloom’s Taxonomy Level Three or higher, meaning questions that are asking at the apply, analyze, evaluate, or create  levels of learning (Krathwohl, 2002). Our research found that when the percentage of complex questions was increased from 0% to 40%, there was a 4% increase in the GP completion rate and a 32% rise in five-star ratings. Third, concluding the project with a cumulative challenge led to a 9% increase in completion rates and a 92% surge in five-star ratings. Fourth, the provision of a summary document at the end of the project, outlining the key takeaways, also significantly influenced co mpletion rates and satisfaction levels. Provi ding such a document resulted in a 13% increase in completion r ates and a 90% increase in five-star ratings. Fifth, we found that a clear reference to an industry scenario or j ob role in the first t ask of the project  led to a 3% increase in completion rates and a substantial 62% rise in five-star ratings. Finally, having at least one practice activity in the GP led to an increase in completion rates by 9% and a significant surge in five-star ratings by 150%, which is 2.5 times the initia l level of five-star ratings. The inclusion of at least one practice activity in intermediate GPs resulted in a 22% increase in completion rates, while the increase for beginner GPs stood at 11%. Plus, t he inclusion of at least one practice assessment in the GP was also found to have a positive impact. Specifically,  it resulted in an 11% increase in completion rates  and a remarkable 183% rise in five-star ratings, which is 2.8 times the initial level. To quantify this effect into meaningful changes, we can see that moving from 0% of all tasks in the project being practice to 15% o f them being practice (which represents the 75th percentil e in our sample) led to a 3% increase in completion  rate and a 17% increase in five-star ratings.

## Discussion

Aligning with industry scenarios and using real-world datasets is what learners want in a Lab assignment as well as a project that is instructor-guided, just as we see in Professional Certificates and c apstone projects across the platform. These real-world scenarios drive motivation, especially for learners seeking a switch or advancement in their career, which continues to be one of the larg est groups learning on the platform. Providing a cl ear industry scenario or job role at the start of a project boos ts learners’ retention and enjoyment, emphasizing the power of Experiential Learning techniques in action (Anderson et al., 2016; Kong, 2021; Markel & Guo, 2020). Practice emerged as an important and powerful theme  across Labs and GPs. Across the board, practice can successfully drive retention in and enjoyment o f hands-on activities, especially as the content gets more difficult, aligning with the Constructivism ideas of building one’s knowledg e through practice and feedback (Ertmer & Newby, 2013; Huang, 2002). Harder GPs see an outsized benefits for providing support materials in the first task and including a summarizing key takeaway document at th e end to increase completion and satisfaction, as suggested by Cognitive Load Theory to help minimize a sense of overwhelm from learners (Feldon et al., 2018). Reinforcing key skills after a GL helps learners to continue successfully with greater understanding, while also boosting a sense of competency crucial to intrinsic motivation (Deci & Ryan, 2000). Within assessments, moving beyond recalling into applying, analyzing, evaluating, and creating questions drives satisfaction, since learners appreciate thought-provoking prompts and real-world style questions. Surprisingly, these more complex question types also boost the completion rate, pote ntially by sparking greater motivation for students to persevere through use of  Experiential Learning (Anderson et al., 2016; Kong, 2021; Markel & Guo, 2020) and a sense of relatedness felt by the questions (Deci & Ryan, 2000). Also in GPs, building toward a final challenge activity boost s persistence and enjoyment, highlighting the utility  of larger cumulative assessments to drive motivation for learners.

Learners require more personalized support with tec hnical assessments, from staff or potentially more tailored automated feedback, with one-size-fits-all hints, generic question feedback, and external resources failing to boost completion rates. Personalization in hands-on tasks requires more investment and innovation. Looking ahead These findings provide clear and actionable insights for educators designing online learning activities. This article provides strong quantitative evidence on the benefi ts of including more practice opportunities, industry connections, staff support, complex questions, and solution guides into online hands-on course activities to strengthen learners’ retention and enjoyment. Our results suggest that providing personalized feedback and support during technical assessments may be an opportunity to enhance learners’ experience further. As we look to the future, we aim to extend this analysis. This expansion could include not only individual course activity types and learner characteristics but also other types of interactions on the platform, such as peerto-peer learning and even more p ersonalized feedback  using the power of generative artificial intelligence (GenAI). We have seen the positive impact that learning scie nce research can have in online course design and look forward to further implementations and explora tions of these findings. Ultimately, our goal is to contribute to a more comprehensive understanding of online learning, providing educators with the knowledge they need to create the most effective and engaging learning experiences.
