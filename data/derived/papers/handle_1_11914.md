# Analysis of Teaching Assistant’s Support Process in Small-Group Discussions

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study conducted interviews using video recordings of the Teaching Assistant (TA) wearing a wearable camera as the stimulus for recall, and analyzed the TA's support process in small group discussions from the interview utterances of the TA. It answered the following the research questions: Does the presence or absence of Web applications affect TA intervention? It was found that using a Web application made it possible to diagnose students’ levels of understanding and provide cognitive support more efficiently.

The role of supporters is important for successful small-group discussions. Teachers ask open-ended questions to promote group progress (Hmelo-Silver & Barrows, 2008). In recent years, Teaching Assistants (TAs) have been expected to play a role in supporting small-group discussions for students (Wadams & Schick-Makaroff, 2022). In addition, the benefits of introducing TAs to small-group discussions in large active learning classrooms have been reported, and students have become more likely to receive specialized advice (Bent et al., 2020). On the other hand, there are problems that the TAs intervened excessively and that there were few interventions to encourage student collaboration (D’Angelo & Rajarathinam, 2024). To provide appropriate support for small-group discussions, TAs must check the progress of each group. It has been pointed out that the use of tools that visualize cognitive activities in group activities by students increases the frequency of teacher intervention and improves the reliability of diagnosis (Van Leeuwen et al., 2015). Thus, we considered that if students used a Web application that could be edited collaboratively, the TAs would be able to grasp their progress and determine the necessity of an intervention. Web applications that can be edited collaboratively are defined as services that allow students to access the same URL and write simultaneously (Google Slides, n.d.). By writing the content of the students’ discussions into a Web application, it is possible for the TAs to support them while monitoring their progress and diagnosing their level of understanding. However, it is not clear what happens to the TA support process when students use web applications. The following research question (RQ) were used to guide this study: “Does the presence or absence of Web applications affect a TA’s intervention? If so, how?”.

## Method

The research subjects were a Japanese university class. The class was a two-credit elective course called “Information Media Education Theory,” which was held from April to July 2023. It was a flipped classroom, and students were instructed to watch preparatory videos, submit assignments, and participate in face-to-face classes. There were 52 first-to fourth-year undergraduate students, one teacher, and two TAs. The class was held after a one-hour meeting between the instructor and TAs. The participant was one of the TAs for this class. The TA was in the second year of a master’s course at a graduate school, and her specialty was educational technology. She had experience taking the class, which was the second time she had acted as a TA for the class. The other TA had no prior experience with that class, and she was a first-year master’s student who had entered the graduate school from another university.

The analysis was conducted in two classes for which the TA provided small-group discussion support. In the class when the Web application was not used (the class without the Web app), the students were not given any specific tools for the discussion and were instructed to give their presentations only verbally. The discussion topic was “Please propose a ‘new school’ that fosters the abilities necessary to survive in an information society.” In the class when Web applications were used (the class with Web apps), students were instructed to summarize the content of the discussion using a Web application that could be edited collaboratively, and to make their presentation while referring to the Web application. The discussion topic was “Please summarize the possibilities and problems of learning using AR (Augmented Reality) technology. Please devise new learning methods and teaching materials using AR technology.” Data were collected in three steps. First, the TA wore a wearable camera (EP8 by Ordro) to record the class. Second, the video recorded by the wearable camera was checked and the scenes in the interview were divided. To determine the need for intervention and extract cases where intervention was required, a scene was defined as a situation in which the subject chose a specific group, approached them, and then moved away to join another group. Third, semi-structured interviews were conducted using video footage recorded by the wearable camera as a stimulus to recall what the TA was thinking at the time, two times within one week after each class. During the interviews, the TA was asked about the perceptions that the participants recognized during the they actually took and why she wanted to intervene. Each interview lasted approximately one hour. The analysis was performed in the following steps. First, the content of the interviews was transcribed into text. The content was then categorized into scenes, coded according to meaning, and organized chronologically. Referring to Stahnke (2016), the categories were defined as follows: [Perception] are utterances related to the perceptions recognized by the TA; [Interpretation] are utterances related to the interpretation and judgement of the perceptions; and [Decision-Making] are utterances in which the TA makes a decision based on [Perception] and [Interpretation]. For chronological order, each category was numbered in the order of appearance in each scene, that is, (1), (2), (3), and (4). In addition, the reasons for the intervention were defined as the factors involved in the decision to intervene, and the content of the interview was classified and given a code name. Finally, it was compared to the two scenes that had the longest intervention times. The categories are shown in [], the codes in <>, and TA’s utterances in “”. In the class without the Web app, the TA was able to understand the content of the discussion as it unfolded through conversations between the students. The TA focused on the students’ <amount of speech> and <facial expressions> in [Perception (1)], and felt that “the students didn't seem to have any initial impetus to expand the discussion” in [Interpretation (1)], and in [Decision-Making (1)], she was “asking what kind of point they were stuck on”. From [Perception (2)] to [Decision-Making (2)], she was asking questions to the students while grasping the content of the discussion. From [Perception (3)] to [Decision-Making (3)], she was passing the floor to the student who she judged could speak. Finally, she heard the <content of the student’s speech> in [Perception (4)], and judged that she felt that the students can continue the discussion on their own in [Interpretation (4)], she took action to <leave the group> in [Decision-Making (4)].

In the class with the Web app, the TA was aware of the content of the discussion before the intervention by checking the Web app. First, the TA recognized <contents of the Web app> in [Perception (1)] and she judged that the students were <confusing the content of the previous lesson> and <intervention was necessary> in [Interpretation (1)], and she took the action of <asking the student a question> in [Decision-Making (1)]. After that, she heard the <contents of the student’s speech> in [Perception (2)], and she felt that the students can continue the discussion on their own in [Interpretation (2)], and in <Decision-Making (2)>, she took action to <leave the group> and said “I thought it would be okay, so I left.” In both classes, the factors involved in the decision to intervene were identified as <Class Information> and <TA’s practical knowledge>. <Class Information> refers to information about the class, such as information about the students’ assignments and class structure. <TA’s practical knowledge> refers to the knowledge that TAs have acquired through their experience and includes how to support small-group discussions. For example, in the class with the Web app, in addition to the <contents of the Web app> in [Perception (1)], the TA used the knowledge of the class content and the information that the report submitted by one of the students in the group was “confused” with the content of the previous lesson, and judged <judging process> in [Interpretation (1)]. Therefore, it is clear that using a Web application made it possible to diagnose students’ levels of understanding and provide cognitive support more efficiently. As shown in previous studies, this result is consistent with the fact that recording the content of learners' discussions visually enables information-based classroom support (Lu, et al., 2010) and provides more targeted support for problematic groups (Van Leeuwen, et al, 2015). And also, it is clear that the TA supported the group based on class information and the TA's practical knowledge. In the Model of Contingent Teaching (MCT) defined by Vande Pol et al. (2014), students’ responses alone were used to diagnose students’ level of understanding. Still, this study added the class Information and TA’s practical knowledge. The main differences from previous research are that this class was structured as a flipped classroom and that the teacher and TAs worked together to support the students. In the case of a flipped classroom, it is possible to use the quality of assignments submitted before class as a diagnostic strategy. In addition, by sharing information about the quality of assignments between teachers and TAs, it may be possible to consider intervention strategies as a team. This study used one TA as a case study and compared intervention scenes. She had taken classes and had experience as a TA, so she was able to provide appropriate support.
