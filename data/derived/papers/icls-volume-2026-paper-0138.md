# Higher-Order Interactions in Learning: Extending Cognitive Load and Self-Regulated Theories through a Network Approach

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
While learning network research focuses on pairwise relationships, we applied higher-order network analysis to three-body interactions among 39 variables (PISA 2015, n=2,189). Our approach, combining network estimation with hierarchical screening and Stability Selection, revealed emergent phenomena: interaction thresholds where two-body effects saturate, competition among multiple regulation processes, and context-dependent motivational reversals. These discoveries extend Cognitive Load and Self-Regulated Learning theories, demonstrating how higher-order analysis can refine learning theories.

Cognitive Load Theory (CLT) has become one of the most influential frameworks for explaining learning efficiency (Sweller et al., 2011). Its central concept, element interactivity, highlights that complexity is shaped not only by the number of elements but also by their interrelations (Sweller, 2010), yet CLT does not address whether qualitative boundaries exist between pairwise and higher-order interactions. A similar gap exists in SelfRegulated Learning (SRL) theory: while models by Zimmerman (2000) and Pintrich (2004) detail regulation processes, they provide little explanation of what occurs when learners manage multiple goals and information sources simultaneously (Winne & Hadwin, 2008).

Recent empirical work increasingly recognizes learning systems as complex systems exhibiting nonlinearity, emergence, and context-dependence (Saqr et al., 2025; Jacobson et al., 2016), prompting growing applications of network analysis in learning science s (Sachisthal et al., 2019). However, these approaches rely predominantly on pairwise relationships, potentially missing higher-order phenomena that network science has demonstrated can substantially alter system behavior (Battiston et al., 2020, 2021; Lam biotte et al., 2019). This study analyzes three-body interactions among 39 learning factors using PISA 2015 science data (2,189 Dutch students). Building on Sachisthal et al. (2019), we identify three phenomena invisible in two-body models: (1) an interaction-order threshold where CLT complexity saturates, (2) competition among regulation processes in SRL, and (3) context-dependent motivational interactions bridging both theories. Dataset Following Sachisthal et al. (2019), our study utilizes data from 2,189 Dutch high school students derived from PISA 2015 (OECD, 2016). The dataset comprises 39 items focused on science interest, classified into six categories: behavior, enjoyment, interest, knowledge, self-efficacy, and values. Responses were binarized, with each item treated as a network node.

Cognitive competition driven by biosphere interest Interest in the Biosphere (Ibi, covering ecological services and sustainability) functions as a systematic inhibitory factor. As Table 2 shows, all significant three-body interactions involving Ibi exhibited strong negative coefficients, with the strongest effect (Efu–Eil–Ibi: −1.298) emerging when Ibi co-occurred with positive attitudes toward broad science learning. Crucially, two-body analysis detected no Ibi-related inhibitory effects. This Emergent Inhibition suggests that domain-specific interest in e nvironmental topics, requiring complex systems-level thinking, competes for cognitive resources with other scientific interests and enjoyment factors —a suppression pattern exclusive to three-body analysis.

Catalytic Interaction of Enjoyment and Value Perception 'Enjoy acquiring broad science knowledge' (Eac) adopts opposite functions depending on context (Table 3). While strongly facilitative when combined with value perception variables (Eac –Cdo–Vwl: 0.369), it turns inhibitory when combined with knowledge-related factors (Eac–Eil–Kkp: −1.130). Particularly noteworthy, no significant two-body interaction existed between Eac and Vdo, yet adding Vwl as a third variable produced the strongest positive three-body interaction. This context-dependent duality reveals that enjoyment can catalyze synergies between intrinsic and extrinsic motivation, or be suppressed by epistemic factors —a network property undetectable by pairwise analysis.

## Theoretical Implications: Reconceptualizing Learning through Higher-Order

Interactions Three-body analysis revealed phenomena suggesting extensions to both CLT and SRL. The hierarchical saturation phenomenon contradicts CLT's assumption that cognitive load increases continuously with element interactivity (Sweller, 2010). The complete vanishing of behavioral effects in three-body space—consistent across nine top interactions—suggests a cognitive "sweet spot" where synergistic effects peak at the two-body level, interpretable in relation to working memory's limited capacity (Cowan, 2010). This  extends beyond non-additive effects documented in multimedia learning (Mayer, 2014), necessitating the concept of interaction-order thresholds in CLT.

The systematic inhibitory role of biosphere interest identifies a gap in SRL theory. Current models largely assume single-goal contexts (Zimmerman, 2000; Pintrich, 2004), but our results show that when systemslevel ecological thinking co-occurs with general affective processing, strong three-body suppression emerges that is absent in pairwise analysis—necessitating "competition among parallel processes" as a core SRL construct. The context-dependent duality of enjoyment (Eac) further demonstrates that mot ivational factors operate as network properties emerging from specific factor configurations rather than fixed valences.

## Implications for educational practice and future directions

These findings translate into three design principles: (1) Optimize, Don't Maximize—add third elements to strong two-factor combinations only when evidence warrants; (2) Manage Cognitive Competition—topics requiring different processing types may interfere when integrated simultaneously; (3) Design for Network Effects — motivational strategies require different pairings depending on learning goals. Future work should explore cross-1175 cultural variations, four-body interactions, and longitudinal designs. The relatively small effect sizes of three-body interactions compared to two-body effects warrant investigation of practical significance thresholds. By revealing phenomena visible only through three-body analysis —hierarchical saturation questioning CLT's continuous complexity assumption, process competition extending SRL's single-goal focus, and networkdependent motivation transcending fixed-valence models—this study demonstrates that pairwise-based theories miss key learning patterns. Understanding how factors combine, compete, and catalyze at different interaction orders represents a promising direction for advancing both learning theory and educational practice.
