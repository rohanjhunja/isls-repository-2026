# Identifying Critical Cognitive and Affective Moments During Problem Solving

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study characterizes how intellectual humility relates to students’ engagement from moment to moment in a reasoning task. Undergraduates (N = 212) completed an IH survey, and a subset (N = 20) completed interviews. We coded cognitive, behavioral, and affective engagement across Task Initiation, Strategy, Answer Reveal, and Reflection. Using contrasting cases, we identified different engagement patterns: lower-IH showed confirmation bias and negative affect, while higher-IH showed strategy shifts and positive reflection. These patterns illustrate how IH aligns with engagement and informs affective scaffolding.

Learning sciences research demonstrates that knowledge is constructed through interaction, and interviews provide a close examination of how learners frame tasks and mobilize cognitive, behavioral, and affective resources on a moment-by-moment basis (Ginsburg, 1997; Russ, 2018). Framing organizes what learners notice, try, and evaluate (Hammer, Elby, Scherr, & Redish, 2005; Scherr & Hammer, 2009). At the same time, emotions are integral to learning dynamics. They can either stabilize productive engagement or derail it at critical moments (brief junctures when framing, strategy, or appraisal shifts) in problem-solving (Pekrun, 2006; Jaber & Hammer, 2016; Radoff & Hammer, 2015). However, most accounts do not show when in the activity these affective shifts occur or how they reorganize action, which limits timely scaffolding, a gap recent work has begun to address (Liu et al., 2024) and that this study extends (Rahmati Masouleh & Lira, 2026). Critical moments can turn work toward or away from the instructional goals (Scherr & Hammer, 2009; Jaber & Hammer, 2016). From a Knowledge-inPieces view, we treat framing as setting the cuing priority of available resources; so small contextual changes reorganize which ideas and actions become salient (diSessa, 1993; diSessa, Sherin, & Levin, 2016). Within this mechanism, epistemic affect functions as a resource that can be reframed in the moment to facilitate exploration or withdrawal (Jaber & Hammer, 2016).

Clinical interviews provide a setting for observing moment-by-moment shifts and for linking the experiencing self with the remembering self (Ginsburg, 1997; Kahneman, 2011; Lee, 2019). In this study, we use interviews to spot critical moments and examine how framing sets cuing priority, how epistemic affect is framed at those moments, and how i ntellectual humility (IH) aligns with learners’ cognitive, behavioral, and affective engagement. Intellectual humility (IH)  refers to the willingness to recognize the  limits of one’s knowledge and remain open to revising one’s beliefs in light of new evidence (Haggard et al., 2018). Rather than treating an interview as a single, coherent episode, our analyses highlight these critical moments as brief junctures when a learner’s framing of the task, strategy, or emotional appraisal changes, redirecting what is noticed, attempted, and evaluated (Scherr & Hammer, 2009; Jaber & Hammer, 2016; Greeno, 1998).

## Methodology & Study Context

The study was conducted at a large R1 U.S. university during Fall 2023– Spring 2024. Two hundred twelve undergraduates (N = 212) completed a self-report IH survey (Haggard et al., 2018), and a volunteer subset (N =

20) participated in laboratory-based interviews that were video-and audio-recorded; all procedures were IRB-approved. The interviews were part of a multi-task study including three problem-solving activities (2–4–6 rule discovery, compound word association task, Rubik’s Cube). In this paper, we analyze the 2–4–6 interview and its reflection and link these to IH scores. Data include transcripts, synchronized video, time stamps, and IH responses, and we used a contrasting-cases comparison. Participants completed a rule-discovery problem modeled afte r Wason’s (1960) 2 –4–6 task, proposing number sets and receiving minimal feedback, followed by a short reflection. We calculated mean IH scores and selected a subset (N = 20), identifying the highest-IH (Helen: 7.33) and lowest-IH (Lily: 4.75) for contrast ing cases analysis as illustrative within the broader sample. Survey data were analyzed for means, skewness, and kurtosis; no outliers were observed  and the data were normally distributed. Transcripts were segmented into Task Initiation, Strategy, Reveal, and Reflection and coded for cognitive, behavioral, and affective engagement using a constant comparative method (Glaser, 1965). Critical 2880 moments were identified through comparison of engagement patterns, and at each moment peak affect was located using synchronized video and transcript alignment; coding was iterative and refined across cases. We begin with a time-aligned overview of the two contrasting cases. At the task initiation episode, Helen (high IH) moves into behavioral engagement, while Lily (low IH) leads with cognitive engagement. In the strategy episode, Helen reorganizes her approach, shifting away from simple confirmation, whereas Lily largely repeats the same confirmatory strategy. At the answer reveal episode, Helen smiles (positive affect), whereas Lily drops her hands and head onto the table (negative affect). In reflection, Helen remembers the episode mostly positively, while Lily’s account is largely negative and self-blaming. Task initiation: Cognitive vs. behavioral engagement Lily oriented to getting the prompt right, repeatedly asking for clarity: Lily: “What you're asking me to do is to find what? … I don't know if I'm, like, writing it the right way.” She tentatively hypothesized: “I feel like the rule is you're like skipping one… I don't know how to say it.” Although Helen initially posed a brief clarifying question, she quickly transitioned into generating number sets, indicating an early shift into behavioral engagement: Helen: “I write down three numbers that conform to the rule?” She then began producing sets of numbers, indicating early behavioral engagement. Lily stayed in cognition, while Helen shifted to behavioral engagement.

Strategy: Confirm vs. falsification Lily’s talk cycled through difficulty articulating the rule and negative self-evaluation: “I don't know how to say it… I don't know how to phrase it… it's making me feel stupid… I'm gonna get frustrated with myself.”  She generated more number sets; all were confirmed, but she kept the same approach. Helen reframed toward falsification:  “I need to get sets of numbers that do not conform to the rule.” “I am overcomplicating it.” Answer reveal: Contrasting epistemic affect Lily: “Oh my gosh, no. That's not what I was thinking at all.” Helen: “Oh, ok, cool.” Post-task reflection Lily reconstructed the episode around confusion and self-evaluation: “It was frustrating… I was frustrated with myself… [I felt] stupid… It's just a weirdly worded question.” Helen: “I wanted to see if it was adding by two… I hoped I could get it right… I felt good.” Cross-case summary Helen and Lily both began with cognitive engagement, but the opening frame differed. Lily focused on clarifying the prompt, while Helen immediately tried sets of numbers. Lily kept confirming and restating and judged herself at the reveal. Helen adjusted her approach, and her reflection stayed positive. IH corresponded to differences in strategy use, affective responses, and willingness to revise initial hypotheses across the two cases. Lily (lower IH) showed more persistent negative self-evaluation and did not reorganize her hypothesis after repeated confirmations. Helen (higher IH) talked about trying contrastive sets of numbers. We propose that early cognitive, as opposed to behavioral, engagement frames how later confirmations and disconfirmations are appraised. Lily remained in a confirm-and-restate loop, whereas Helen shifted to a falsification approach. Effort was comparable; the key difference was how each understood the task.  The reveal and reflection further distinguished the cases. Brief emotional moments organize later narratives.  In school settings, scaffolding should be differentiated. For students like Lily, task clarification and emotional support can help sustain engagement. For students like Helen, extensive intervention is unnecessary. Engagement unfolded in distinct patterns aligned with IH. Helen moved quickly into behavioral engagement and maintained positive affect, while Lily stayed in cognitive engagement longer and showed more negative self-evaluation.
