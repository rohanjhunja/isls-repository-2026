# Using AI-Based Virtual Labs, Log Data, and Reasoning Statements to Assess High School Students’ Understanding of Energy Conservation

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Conservation of energy is a challenging high school science topic, especially when the integration of mathematics is expected, as per the Next Generation Science Standards (NGSS). To address these challenges, Inq-ITS labs were developed to automatically assess and scaffold students’ mathematical competencies on developing models about energy conservation, both with and without air resistance, the latter of which introduces additional complexity. Using real-time assessment data, we examined whether students’ science and math competencies varied across two labs. Further, we performed a qualitative analysis of students’ log data and reasoning statements to determine potential alternative conceptions about their mathematical models as evidence for their claim(s). By analyzing students at the practice level, including qualitative data about their reasoning, we can (1) provide rich data on students’ science and mathematical investigation competencies, (2) identify common alternative conceptions, and (3) identify difficulties students have when linking their mathematical models to their claims about conservation of energy.

## Introduction

The integration of science and mathematics is a key  focus of the Next Generation Science Standards (NG SS, 2013), particularly in Practices 2 (Developing and Using Models), 4 (Analyzing and Interpreting Data),  and 5 (Using Mathematics and Computational Thinking).  Rather than treating these practices as separate sk ills, NGSS emphasizes engaging students in inquiry that reflec t the integrated nature of scientific reasoning.  However, U.S. students continue to struggle in both areas (OECD, 2023), particularly in physics, where concepts such  as conservation of energy require strong math-science connections  (Halilović et al., 2021). These practices are especially relevant in high scho ol physics, where students are expected to generate  and interpret graphs and use mathematical models to explain scientific phenomena such as motion, force, and energy (McDermott et al., 1987; Schuchardt & Schunn, 2015).  In general, students face misconceptions about ener gy, including views of energy as a consumable substance or misunderstanding how i t is transferred (Duit, 1987; Linn & Songer, 1991).  Prior research has shown that students often misunderstand the conditions for energy conservation, struggle with energy transformations, and oversimplify the concept of energy (Halilović et al., 2021; Watt, 1983).  These difficulties are compounded by the mathematical demands of model ing and interpreting graphs, where students frequen tly misapply or fail to understand equations (Sherin, 2 001; McDermott et al., 1987). This is especially pr oblematic in NGSS-aligned phy sics instruction, where students are expected to co nstruct and interpret mathematical representations of physical phenomena (Schuchardt & Schunn, 2015). The current study uses Inquiry Intelligent Tutoring System (Inq-ITS), a browser-based platform with embedded AI that provides real-time assessment and scaffolding to support students  during virtual science investigations (Gobert et al., 2013; Adair et al., 2023). We develope d twonew Inq-ITS labs on conservation of energy, one without air resistance and one with air resista nce, to assess how students engage with mathematical and conceptual modeling of this phenomenon.  Our research questions were: (1) Do stu dents’ performance and competencies change across the two labs, especially when air resistan ce is introduced? (2) What alternative conceptions are revealed through their log data and  reasoning statements, particularly regarding their interpretation of mathematical models and claims about energy conservation?

## Participants

Participants were 24 high school students from a physics class in a diverse, southeastern U.S. school district (50% eligible for free/reduced lunch; 59% students of co lor). These students were selected from a larger da taset and had completed both conservation of energy labs in Inq-ITS during the 2023–2024 school year. Figure 1 Selected Screenshots from Inq-ITS Conservation of Energy Activity (Lab 2) Materials Students individually completed two Inq-ITS labs on energy conservation (NGSS Performance Expectation PS31). Lab 1 investigated whether mechanical energy is c onserved as the mass of a ball changes in an environment without air resistance. Lab 2 introduced air resistance to the same scena rio and variables, increasing conceptual complexity (Figure 1). In each lab, students: formed hypotheses about how mass affects mechanical energy (Stage Stage 1 Stage 2 Stage 3 Stage 4 Stage 5 1); collected data by running  virtual simulations where they could manipulate mas s, drop height, and gravity (Stage 2); graphed initial vs. final mechanical energy (Stage 3); and constructed mathematical models by fitting curves to their data (Stage 4). Finally, Stage 5 introduced a modified Claim-Evidence-Reasoning (CER; McNeill et al., 2006; Brown et al., 2010) task in which stu dents used drop-down menus to make a claim about whether mechanical energy was conserved, selected a model they built as evidence of this claim, and wrote an open-ended reasoning statement to explain how their evidence s upports their claim about energy conservation.  In Lab 1, students were expected to develop a model demonstra ting that the initial and final mechanical energy w ere the same for each data point (i.e., y = x) and thus conclude that mechanical energy was conserved in their experiment. However, in Lab 2, the presence of air resistance means energy is not conserved, and a different model (i.e., a model other than y = x) was expected.

## Measures

Students’ competencies were automatically scored (0  = incorrect, 1 = correct) at a fine-grained, “sub-practice” level across the first four stages (Sao Pedro et al., 2013; Adair et al., 2023). Each stage score was the average of its sub-practice scores. These stages emphasized competencies aligned with NGSS Practices 2 and 5, including identifying variables, conducting controlled experiments, selecting appropriate graph axes, and modeling data. In Stage 5, which was not auto matically scored, we used system log data to evaluate student s’ selected claims and evidence, and conducted thematic qualitative analys is (Clarke & Braun, 2017) of their written reasonin g statements. This analysis was exploratory and aimed to surface potential alternative conceptions for future rubric development.

## Results

The goal of this study was to compare student perfo rmance across two virtual labs about energy conservation (RQ1) and to examine students’ claim and reasoning statements to identify alternative conceptions (RQ2). While there were no statistically significant dif ferences between labs, average scores increased slightly in the first three stages. Scores remained high in the Building Models stage across both labs. These trends suggest students maintained or improved their compe tencies, even as the conceptual complexity increase d with the introduction of air resistance.

RQ2: Student Claims and Reasoning Statements During Stage 5, Analyze Data, students completed a modified Claim-Evidence-Reasoning (CER) task to analyze their results. In Lab 1, 71% of students correctly identified all three components of the claim (i.e., the variable changed in the experiment, the type of energy being investigated, and the conservation outcome). In Lab 2, this dropped to 40%, indicating increased difficulty when air resistance was introduced. Despite having accurate graphs as evidence, several students made incorrect claims or struggled to justify their conclusions. We conducted a thematic analysis of reasoning state ments and identified three categories of alternative conceptions:

1. Misunderstanding the Role of Mass or Forces.  Some students incorrectly attributed changes in ene rgy

conservation to mass or force. For example, one student wrote, “Whe n the mass is greater... it absorbs more energy,” implying mass directly causes conserv ation or dissipation of energy, rather than recognizing the role of air resistance (Watt, 1983; Halilović et al., 2021).

2. Overemphasis on Positive Trends. Several students equated increasing data trends wit h conservation of

energy, regardless of model shape. One student expl ained, “The graph shows the dots climbing higher each time,” suggesting that an upward pattern alone was sufficient evidence of conservation (Cebesoy & Yeniterzi, 2016; Friel et al., 2001).

3. Difficulty Interpreting the Model. Other students referred vaguely to their graphs or misunderstood what

the model demonstrated. For instance, a student stated, “The graph shows it’s conserved since the curved line fits the data almost perfectly,” indicating a misinterpretation of model fi t as proof of conservation (McDermott et al., 1987; Brandiet et al., 2018).

## Discussion & Implications

These findings suggest that while students could of ten generate correct models and data representation s, many struggled with interpreting their meaning or articu lating valid reasoning. Importantly, even students with correct claims sometimes displayed conceptual misunderstand ings in their explanations, revealing a potential g ap in students' reasoning and sense-making about mathematical representations and scien tific phenomena  (Zhao & Schuchardt, 2021).

## Discussion

This study examined high school students’ science and mathematical competencies across two In q-ITS labs on conservation of energy. In RQ1, we found that stude nts maintained high performance in core investigati ve practices across both labs, with slight improvement s in  the first three  stages, even when air resistance was introduced. This suggests the labs and embedded sca ffolds supported students in conducting investigati ons and building models, particularly in math-intensive stages like graphing and building models. RQ2 revealed greater variation in student performan ce during the Analyze Data stage, especially in reasoning about whether energy was conserved. Altho ugh many students constructed appropriate graphs and models, their reasoning statements often revealed p ersistent conceptual misunderstandings, particularly surrounding: (1) the impact of mass/forces on energy conservation (Watt, 1983; Halilović et al., 2021), (2), a focus on positive trends in the data (Cebesoy & Yeniterzi, 2016; Friel et al., 2001), and (3) general interp retations of the mathematical model (McDermott et al., 1987; Brandiet et al., 2018). This suggests that generating representations alone may not be sufficient for students to fully make sense of the underlying phenomena. These findings emphasize the importance of additional supports during the reasoning stage, where students must connect their evidence to scientific explanations.

To address these challenges, we plan to integrate autoscoring and real-time scaffolding into the Analyze Data stage, beginning with the claim and evidence components. For example, if a student selects a model showing energy was not conserved but claims that it was, the system could provide targeted feedback to prompt reflection. This expansion builds on our prior work with automa ted scaffolding during early stages and aligns with  NGSS practices that emphasize constructing explanations and engaging in argument from evidence (Segan et al., 2024; Adair et al., 2023). Additionally, we plan to revise the reasoning task itself. Instead of an open-ended prompt, students could choose from multiple reasoning state ments, s ome scientifically accurate and others reflecting common alternative conceptions, then justify their choice. This approach, grounded in bridging analogies (Clement, 1993), may help students reflect more critically on their thinking and develop more robust explanations. A limitation of this study is the small sample size, as not all students completed both labs. Additionally, a fine-grained operationalization of the sub-practices associated with the final stage (Stage 5)  as well as the accompanying automated scoring algorithms are still  in development. However, findings from this study will inform future iterations of the activity, including  updates to the  structured reasoning tasks, new automated assessment features, and built-in real-time scaffolding for all stages of the activity. Overall, these findings underscore the promise of virtual labs, like the Inq-ITS activities, for supporting integrated math and science learning, while also identifying areas where students need greater conceptual support.

As students use graphs and equations to model real-world phenomena, helping them connect those representations to scientific explanations is key for deepening und erstanding, especially in complex domains like energy conservation (Halilović et al., 2021; Logman et al., 2014). By combining process-based performance data with students’ reasoning statements, this study offers a novel lens for identifying persistent conceptual challenges and designing targeted supports through log file and qu alitative analyses.  Specifically, we highlight the need for scaffolds that address not only how students model data, but how they interpret and explain what those  models mean. This work contributes to ongoing efforts in s cience education, moving towards integrated, NGSS a ligned practices and developing tools that support both disciplinary knowledge and sensemaking.
