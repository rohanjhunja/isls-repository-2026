# The Emotional Dynamics of Collaborative Argumentation: Examining the Role of Framing and Alignment in Discourse Quality

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study explores the emotional dynamics of collaborative argumentation, emphasizing the influence of emotional framing and interactional alignment on the quality of group discourse. Situated in a computer-supported environment, the research analyzes how positive and negative emotional cues shape argument complexity and group talk among middle school students engaged in socio-scientific debates. Findings reveal that emotional framing mediates engagement and focus, while alignment strategies determine the depth and coherence of collaborative reasoning. The integration of emotional and cognitive processes offers valuable insights for educational strategies and the design of collaborative learning tools.

## Introduction and theoretical framework

Argumentation is a structured communication process  where reasoning and evidence outweigh opinions or emotions, encompassing both claim presentation (arg ument1) and debate (argument2) (Gass & Seiter, 2019). Collaborative argumentation integrates reasoning and evidence to enhance critical thinking and knowled ge coconstruction (Gass & Seiter, 2019; Andriessen et al., 2013a). Recent research highlights the interplay  of socio-emotional, cognitive, and temporal dynamics in grou p reasoning. Emotion regulation —such as socially shared emotional management (Järvenoja & Järvelä, 2013; Polo, 2022) and socio-relational prioritization (Andriessen et al., 2013b)—shapes discourse quality and equitable collaboration. Positive socio-emotional interactions enhance reasoning, while unresolved conflicts diminish argu mentation quality (Gao et al., 2023; Jiménez -Aleixandre & Brocos, 2021). Temporal analyses reveal that high-performing groups sustain constructive emotional cl imates, whereas low performers struggle with prolonged negativity (Isohätälä et al., 2018; Wilkie & Ayalon, 2023). Building on this foundation, this study investigate s how emotional framing (valence-based tonality influencing engagement) and interactional alignment  (social-cognitive adjustments shaping group dynamics) affect argument complexity and discourse quality  respectively. Drawing on Polo et al.’s (2017) socio-cognitiveemotional model, it addresses:

1. How does emotional framing influence argument complexity?

2. How does interactional alignment shape group talk?

Emotional framing and argument complexity Emotional framing refers to the tone or mood associ ated with a topic during discourse. Initial framing s, shaped by prior experiences, influence engagement and evolve through interaction, creating an “emotional tonality” (Polo et al., 2016). Although emotions can be analyzed along multiple dimensions—such as valence, arousal, and voice tone—this study operationalizes emotional framing along a binary valence dimension. Grounded in Polo et al. 's socio-cognitive-emotional model, this binary approach captures a ke y functional distinction: positive framing signals approval, fostering deeper engagement and e xploratory, integrative reasoning, while negative f raming cues disa pproval, potentially leading to adversarial and sha llow discussions. This parsimonious framework provides both theoretical clarity and empirical tractability in examining collective reasoning. Interactional alignment and group talk Alignment during collaboration —adjusting social and cognitive positions —determines group talk patterns (Mercer, 1996; Polo et al., 2016). Constructive alignment promotes exploratory talk  (critical yet collaborative), misalignment triggers disputational talk (unproduct ive conflict), and partial alignment yields cumulat ive talk (consensus without critique).

Interactional alignment initiates the type of group talk that emerges; for instance, constructive alignment fosters exploratory talk, while misalignment leads to disputational talk. In turn, the emergent group talk stabilizes the alignment, creating a feed back loop where social and cognitive processes cont inuously shape collective reasoning. Thus, alignment is necessary for group t alk to occur—even cumulative talk requires some degree of alignment—though the talk’s specific nature depends on the alignment’s quality (Mercer & Littleton, 2007; Polo et al., 2016).

This theoretical framework emphasizes that both emotiona l framing and interactional alignment are critical in shaping how collaborative reasoning unf olds. This study's central aim is to unravel how th ese socioemotional dynamics affect argument complexity and group talk.

## Methodology

This exploratory qualitative study investigates collaborative argumentation among middle school students (grades 7–9) in the context of socio-scientific argumentation. Verbal discourse captured through audio recordings of group interactions and thematic analysis was employed to examine how students engage with structured argumentation processes, navigate emotional dynamics, and collaboratively construct arguments.

## Setting and participants

The study was conducted at a research university in  Mumbai, India, with participants aged 11 –15 from middleclass socio-cultural backgrounds. English was the medium of ins truction. Participants were divided into two teams:

• Team 1: Anika (Grade 9), Ananya (Grade 8), and Mitali (Grade 7)

• Team 2: Shivam (Grade 8), Kriti (Grade 8), and Anuj (Grade 7)

Note: The above are pseudonyms and not actual names of the participants. The author of this paper was also the facilitator for this research study. T he teams represented diverse educational and socio-economic contexts. Four participants were children of university professors, while two came from families outside academia.

Collaborative task and goals Participants engaged in a workshop designed to deve lop their argumentation skills through a structured  process of collaborative argument mapping. The intervention  focused on a socio-scientific issue —the efficacy of homeopathy. Tasks included analyzing animated scena rios of a debate between Riya (supporting homeopath y) and Priya (opposing it), constructing argument maps, and achieving consensus on the claims presented. The animated videos served as emotional and cognitive anchors, modeling how to integrate ethos (e.g., Riya’s personal anecdotes) with logos (e.g., Priya’s empirical argu ments), scaffolding participants’ ability to naviga te emotional tension while constructing reasoned claims. Argumen t maps acted as shared 'cognitive-emotional workspaces,' enabling participants to iteratively refine claims by ba lancing personal narratives (emotional framing) wit h external evidence (critical rigor). This approach a llowed educators to use animated videos to model co nstructive emotional framing and argumentation mapping tools t o externalize cognitive-emotional conflict. ## Data collection methods

The broader study incorporated multiple data source s, including audio recordings, screen recordings, a rgument maps, and interview responses, to triangulate findi ngs and ensure methodological rigor. However, this paper focuses specifically on verbal discourse captured through audio recordings of group interactions. Verbal discourse is a theoretically robust data source for analyzing  emotions in argumentation. Polo’s framework priori tizes expressed emotions (emotive cues in language) over felt emotions, aligning with the discursive constru ction of emotions through lexical choices, rhetorical strategies, and argumen tative framing. This linguistic focus avoids speculative inferences about internal emotional states of the participants  and prioritizes how emotions are used rather than felt, aligning with the goal of studying emotions as tools for reasoning and persuasion in collaborative learning.

Coding and analysis The coding framework was deductively derived from P olo et al.’s (2017) socio-cognitive-emotional model to analyze collaborative reasoning in computer-supported environments (CSCL). While Polo et al.’s model did not originally include an explicit codebook, its conceptual categories were operationalized into measurable constructs (Table 2), directly translating theoretical elements into a nalytical codes. Iterative coding cycles and reflec tive refinement of code definitions ensured internal consistency. This study extends prior research by examining how tools like animated videos (emotional  anchors) and argument maps (shared cognitive-emotional workspaces) mediate emotional framing and interactional alignme nt. The approach aligns with the aim to explore dig ital environments’ role in shaping socio-emotional and cognitive dynamics, offering theoretical insights and practical strategies.

Answering Research Questions Research Question 1: How does emotional framing influence the complexity of arguments in collaborative reasoning?

The findings indicate that emotional framing signif icantly shapes argument complexity by directing participants’ focus, engagement, and evaluative dep th. Positive framing—as seen in Anika’s favorable portrayal of Priya’s arguments (e.g., “better”) in Episo de 1—enhances receptivity and alignment. In contrast, di smissive framing, exemplified by Shivam’s humor in Episode 2  (“Riya is a patriarchal... is not patriotic”), di stracts from substantive reasoning and limits critical engagement. Furthermore, emotional framing influences how alternative claims are approached; for instance, Mitali’s hesit ancy (“Riya, Riya! I don’t know”) reflects a desire  to preserve social harmony through cumulative talk, though mome nts of Constructively Critical Footing (e.g., her l ater prompt) suggest that framing can evolve to foster e xploratory reasoning. Overall, emotional framing me diates argument complexity by impacting the depth of engag ement, the salience of competing claims, and the gr oup’s evaluative processes.

Research Question 2: How does interactional alignment shape group talk in collaborative reasoning? The findings reveal that interactional alignment cr itically shapes the quality of group talk and depth  of argumentation. Cumulative talk—exemplified by Ananya’s affirmations in Episode 1 —promotes consensus and social cohesion but often limits critical evaluation and diverse perspectives. Disputational talk, as seen in Episode 2 with Shivam’s sarcastic framing (“Riya is a patri archal… is not patriotic”), disrupts coherence by p rioritizing adversarial tone over substantive reasoning. While humor can foster disciplinary norms in certain contexts (e.g., Berge’s 2017 study on physics problem-solving), here it fragmented alignment by diverging  from task goals. Facilitators play a key role in steering discussion s toward exploratory talk through structured prompt s (e.g., “Do you agree or disagree and why?”), bridging alignmen t and critical reasoning. Ultimately, exploratory t alk— supported by constructive alignment —provides the richest foundation for collaborative r easoning, balancing cohesion with critical depth.

## Discussion

This study provides nuanced insights into the emoti onal and interactional dynamics of collaborative argumentation, enriching Polo et al.’s (2016, 2017) socio-cognitive-emotional framework. By exploring emotional framing and interactional alignment, it u nderscores the intertwined nature of emotional, soc ial, and cognitive processes in shaping argumentation quality.

Emotional framing and argument complexity Findings confirm Jiménez -Aleixandre and Brocos’ (2021) observation that emot ional tension influences reasoning and decision-making. In Episode 1, Anika’s positive framing of P riya’s arguments fostered group receptivity, illustrating Polo et al.’s concept o f emotional tonality in guiding focus and engagemen t. Although cumulative talk emerged as a limitation by prioriti zing harmony over critical evaluation, negative emo tions can also have positive effects. For example, Knight and  Eisenkraft (2015)  demonstrate that externally triggered negative emotions can foster honest feedback and strengthen social integration, enhancing group problem-solving. Conversely, in Episode 2, Shivam’s humor—perceived as dismissive—disrupted focus, aligning with Gao et al.’s

(2023) findings that unresolved negative interactions hinder argument quality. Notably, Mitali’s hesitancy evolved

into constructive prompts that shifted the dialogue  from cumulative to exploratory talk, supporting Is ohätälä et al.’s (2018) view of socio-emotional and cognitive interconnectivity. Interactional alignment and group talk The study’s group talk typologies align with Mercer ’s (1996) framework. Cumulative talk (e.g., Ananya’s affirmations) fostered cohesion but limited critica l evaluation, while disputational talk in Episode 2  disrupted coherence. Exploratory talk, as evidenced by Mitali ’s queries, demonstrated the benefits of construc tive alignment, echoing Gao et al.’s (2023) insights on high-performing groups. Facilitators played a pivotal ro le in redirecting participants toward evidence-based reasoning, underscoring the importance of structured interventions in sustaining exploratory talk. Interactional align ment thus emerges as a dynamic process where indivi dual contributions and facilitation reinforce or hinder discourse quality.

## Limitations and future research

Verbal discourse is a robust source for analyzing e motions in argumentation, but  its binary (positive/negative) valency framework risks oversimplifying nuanced emotional states (e.g., frustration vs. anger) and subtle valences (e.g., ambivalence, hope). Future research could en hance this approach by integrating multidimensional emotion frameworks and multimodal methods (e.g., vocal tone a nalysis, facial expression coding) to better disent angle how specific emotions and their intensity shape argumentative complexity.

## Implications

The findings of this study offer theoretical and pr actical implications for the broader field of argum entation, particularly in the context of collaborative reasoning and socio-emotional dynamics.

## Theoretical implications

Integration of Emotional Framing: This study extend s existing frameworks by showing how emotional fram ing mediates argument complexity through its impact on focus, engagement, and claim receptivity. Future theories of argumentation should incorporate emotional dynamics as a core element. Dynamic Interactional Alignment: The findings highl ight that alignment is an evolving process; constructive alignment fosters exploratory talk, while misalignment can impede critical evaluation.

## Practical implications

Designing Collaborative Learning Environments: Educ ators can leverage argument mapping tools and anima ted videos, as used in the homeopathy debate interventi on, to scaffold emotional regulation and constructi ve alignment, fostering evidence-based reasoning.

Facilitation in Collaborative Argumentation: Effect ive facilitators use structured prompts, reflective questions, and conflict resolution strategies to gu ide groups from cumulative or disputational talk to ward more dialogic and exploratory exchanges.

Balancing Emotional and Cognitive Processes: Interventions should integrate emotional regulation with critical reasoning to support both engagement and e valuative depth, applicable across diverse educatio nal and socio-scientific contexts.

By bridging emotional and cognitive dimensions, this research offers actionable insights into enhancing collaborative argumentation and highlights avenues for future inquiry into the nuanced roles of emotional framing and interactional alignment.
