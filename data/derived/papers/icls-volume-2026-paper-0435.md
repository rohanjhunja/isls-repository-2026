# Embodied Geometric Reasoning in Augmented Reality: A Video Coding Framework for Cognitive Functions

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study examine d how embodied processes in augmented reality (AR) support geometric reasoning by distinguishing among pragmatic, epistemic, and complementary actions. High school students (N=14) reasoned geometry conjectures using GeoGebra-MR. We developed a video coding framework to classify the cognitive functionality of embodied processes. Findings show ed that AR manipulations provided cognitive offloading, while gestures coordinated mental operations. Deductive reasoning elicited all action types, whereas inductive reasoning relied mainly on pragmatic and epistemic actions.

There is growing scholarly interest in how augmented reality (AR), technology that overlays virtual information onto the real world, can enhance geometry learning (Garzón & Acevedo, 2019). AR affords novel representations (e.g., opaque or inscribed shapes) and manipulations (e.g., reorienting, rescaling, unfolding) that create new ways for learners to engage with geometric content, raising questions about embodied learning in such environments (Walkington et al., 202 5). From a grounded and embodied learning (GEL; Nathan, 2021) perspective, these interactions can transform how people generate and share geometric ideas (Dimmel & Bock, 2019). However, little is known about how students interact in AR-based geometry tasks (Walkington et al., 2025) and how these interactions function cognitively. To address this gap, this study introduces a video coding framework that classifies students’ embodied geometric reasoning in AR based on the cognitive functionality of their actions.

## Theoretical framework

GEL refers to the process of connecting unfamiliar and abstract ideas to learners’ perceptual and motor experiences (Barsalou, 2008; Nathan, 2021). GEL is especially evident when students reason about abstract geometric concepts with arbitrary symbolic mappings.  For example, when learning that the circumference of a circle equals 2πr, students may trace the circle’s edge with a finger to connect the symbolic formula to the perceptual experience of “going around.” Such embodied actions provide a perceptual  bridge between a mathematical object’s meaning and its abstract representations (Nathan, 2021). In this paper, we examine how students physically interacted with geometric objects while reasoning about the veracity of geometry conjectures. Gestures are spontaneous hand and arm movements that accompany speech and play a key role in GEL (McNeill, 1992). Pointing gestures ground cognition in the physical environment, whereas representational gestures depict mental simulations (Alibali & Nathan, 2012). These include dynamic depictive gestures, which simulate transformations, and non-dynamic depictive gestures, which represent static properties (Garcia & Infante, 2012).  Prior work  (Nathan et al., 2021; Pier et al., 2019) shows dynamic gestures support proof and generalization, while non-dynamic gestures support insight  for mathematical conjectures. Although previous studies have established important links between gesture types and geometric reasoning, the functionality  that these gestures serve cognitively within their reasoning processes remain s underexplored. To explore this functionality, we introduce a new analytical layer to examine the specific cognitive roles that gestures play in geometric reasoning.

Kirsh and Maglio (1994) highlighted cognitive roles of action beyond direct task completion, introducing epistemic (Kirsh & Maglio, 1994; Kirsh, 1996) and complementary actions (Kirsh, 1995a, 1995b). According to their framework, actions can serve three cognitive functions:  (1) Pragmatic actions directly advance task goals (e.g., placing a zoid to clear a row in Tetris; Kirsh & Maglio, 1994). (2) Epistemic actions modify the environment to reveal information that is difficult to compute mentally, thereby reducing cognitive load  (e.g., moving a zoid to test spatial fit; Kirsh & Maglio, 1994). (3) Complementary actions coordinate attention and support ongoing mental processes without generating new information (e.g., pointing while counting; Kirsh, 1995a). Building on these action types and a related study that applies this approach to analyze geometric reasoning  (Kim & Nathan, 2026), we developed and applied a video coding framework to examine how students’ interactions with AR function cognitively during geometric reasoning.

2734 Fourteen high school students (7 dyads) participated in a summer course. Dyads collaboratively reasoned about two geometric conjectures using GeoGebra-MR. The cylinder task (deductive) asked whether a cylinder with radius r and height h  can be unrolled into a rectangle of length h and width 2πr. The pyramid task (inductive) asked whether the number of vertices always equals the number of faces. Interaction was limited to predefined functions (unfold cylinder; change pyramid sides). Sessions were recorded fr om first-and third-person perspectives and transcribed. Video data were analyzed using a two-stage coding scheme. First, interaction types were identified, including AR manipulation, pointing to observable or imagined objects, and depictive gestures (non-dynamic for static properties; dynamic for transformations). Second, each action was classified by cognitive function: pragmatic, epistemic, and complementary  actions. Qualitative analysis further examined how these codes varied across tasks.

AR manipulations as pragmatic, epistemic, and complementary actions The same AR manipulations served different cognitive functions depending on context. In the cylinder (deductive) task, all dyads used the unfold function, but its role varied. As a pragmatic  action, it directly demonstrated that the unrolled cylinder forms a rectangle. As an epistemic  action, repeated unfolding helped reduce cognitive demands and verify relationships (e.g., linking height and circumference). It also functioned as a complementary action, coordinating and externalizing students’ ongoing reasoning without generating new information.  In contrast, in the pyramid (inductive) task, changing the number of sides served pragmatic and epistemic functions but not complementary ones. It enabled students to visualize and verify relationships between vertices and faces across cases. The absence of  complementary actions may reflect task differences: the cylinder (deductive) task involved abstract relations, prompting complementary  actions to ground ideas in perceptual experience, whereas the pyramid (inductive) task relied on concrete exemplars, leading to greater use of pragmatic and epistemic actions. Overall, pragmatic and epistemic actions mainly generated or verified information by offloading mental manipulations onto AR objects, whereas complementary actions primarily supported communication by externalizing ongoing reasoning without changing the information state. Gestures as complementary actions All gestures—whether dynamic, non-dynamic depictive, or pointing—were coded as complementary  actions, as they coordinated ongoing mental processes rather than generating new information.  Given their communicative role (Hostetter et al., 2008; McNeill, 2005), classifying gestures as complementary actions reinforces our interpretation of complementary AR manipulations as similarly communicative in dyadic collaboration. Dynamic depictive gestures appeared only in the cylinder (deductive) task, simulating transformations of AR or imagined objects, suggesting a need to ground abstract operational concepts in perceptual experience. In contrast, their absence in the pyramid task  is likely reflects  its inductive focus, which promoted reliance on concrete representations rather than simulation. Non-dynamic depictive gestures further reflected this contrast. In the cylinder conjecture, gestures depicted the outcomes of mental operations (e.g., the unfolded cylinder) or realworld analogs, showing how the deductive nature of the task prompted perceptual grounding. In the pyramid conjecture, these gestures mostly depicted static features (e.g., the shape of a vertex) without mental manipulation, consistent with the task’s inductive focus on concrete exemplars. Pointing gestures also differed, with references to both actual and imagined objects in the cylinder task, but only visible features in the pyramid task, reflecting how the inductive nature of the task encouraged reliance on concrete examples. This study introduces a video coding framework to identify how students’ embodied processes in AR-based geometry tasks serve distinct cognitive functions. By distinguishing pragmatic, epistemic, and complementary actions, the framework shows how AR manipulations support cognitive offloading, while gestures function as complementary actions that coordinate ongoing reasoning.  Cross-task comparisons revealed differences by reasoning type: deductive  reasoning (cylinder) elicited all action types and gestures toward both visible and imagined objects, whereas inductive reasoning (pyramid) primarily involved pragmatic and epistemic actions with gestures directed toward visible objects.  Although this pilot study involved a small sample and two tasks, it demonstrates how this framework can systematically capture the cognitive functions of embodied processes in AR. Future work looks to implement this framework across a range of contexts to examine the cognitive roles of embodied processes and how they vary across manipulatives to deepen understanding of grounded and embodied cognition in the learning sciences.
