# Cultivate Future-Oriented Identities among High School Students through Authentic Experience

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study investigates how high school students explore future-oriented identities that align with emerging possibilities in their academic, professional, and personal lives. To support this identity exploration, the school implemented a community-based, authentic research program in which students collaborated with professionals from diverse fields to engage in real-world research and practices. Data were collected through semi-structured interviews with seven students who reflected on their experiences. Using thematic analysis, we examined how students’ identities were shaped through their authentic engagements with both the business and academic worlds. The findings suggest that the program helped students discover personal goals by reflecting on their personal characteristics, exploring the social worlds, and constructing a sense of who they aspire to become. This process of self-construction activated their agency and cultivated a dynamic mindset for their future self.

Education is often framed as a pathway to certainty, with the assumption that after graduation, individuals will know their paths ahead. With students spending most of their  life in school, how can students find their future directions and know what career paths may be suitable? Thus, it is important for schools to  create authentic experiences by which students explore who they want to be.  Researchers need to  understand how such environments shape students’ self-concepts and help them find their paths in life (Marsico et al., 2019). This study aims to understand how high school students explore their identities related to their future  trajectories. Their personal exploration is  supported by an authentic learning program in which students work with business and academic partners to engage in real-world research and professional practices. Our research questions include: (a) How do students reflect on who they aspire to become as they participate in the authentic learning program? And

(b) what features of the learning program support students’ engagement, enabling them to enact their agency and

explore possible future trajectories?  By examining students’ reflections across authentic experiences, this study contributes to understanding how structured engagement with real-world partners supports students’ future-oriented identity work. The findings offer insights for designing learning environments that help students explore possible selves in relation to college, careers, and life beyond school. The literature highlights the need to better understand and support the development of young students’ identities:  to help them reflect on their knowledge, goals, and values so that they can find their future paths in the fast-paced world (Kaplan & Flum, 2012;  Flum & Kaplan, 2006; Wright et al., 2026).  The way students define themselves in relation to their environment—and how the environment defines them —forms students’ identities  (Marsico et al, 2019; Zajda, 2025). This sense of affiliation  with the context shows  a dialogical perspective of identity (Marsico  et al., 2019). Dialogue in educational spaces is  crucial as  it shapes students' identities. Therefore, education needs to offer a dialogic experience in a collaborative community (Langer-Osuna, 2015). Students in educational spaces internalize  and externalize the dialogue that they receive from others. Externalization is, therefore, how students make meaning out of their environment, which influences their behavior in educational spaces. Internalization, on the other hand, is the meaning-making on the notion of self  that is nourished by the environment. Internalization shapes the self-talk of students. Their self-stories (narratives) serve as semiotic  mediations in which students tell themselves about their surroundings as they adjust their psychic and social life accordingly (Holland et al. 1998; Marsico, et al., 2019). The internal dialogue  forms the imagination of students through “Figured Worlds" (Holland et al., 1998). The figured world is about “people’s abilities to form and be formed collectively in realized ‘as if’ realms. Figured worlds  are the spaces in which learners historically and collectively interpret meaning in different contexts in terms of “actions, cultural productions, performances, disputes, for the understanding that 801 people come to make of themselves, and for the capabilities that people develop to direct their behavior in these worlds” (p. 60). Students  in the figured world  follow activities and norms that they value and  have historically resonated with them. They follow the rules of an imaginary world that they relate  to and are followed by others, but these figured worlds are not limited to imagination but rather they “happen, as social process and in historical time” (Holland, 1998, p. 55). Yet the perspective has been undermined with other studies showing that the identity and the position that students take towards their studies is dependent on the school's reputation, social class, and not just merely the historical background of the students. It means that mere dialogue and interaction are not the means of self-development, but rather spaces and activities give opportunity to students to build their notion of self and their outlook (Barron, 2006; Caza et al., 2018; Grant, 2020).  Barron (2006) emphasizes the importance of ideational resources, which spark and sustain student interest in learning. Thus, finding activities and events that enhance the participation and engagement  aligned with their interest becomes necessary. Once an interest is sparked, students start to search for opportunities to develop their interests if they have the time, resources, and the freedom to learn. Therefore, the space and opportunities provided by the school can be the foundation to construct students' paths towards their future world through authentic experiences with real-world partners. Students play an active role in the construction of the self and finding the notion of self. Their autonomy should be developed so that they engage in activities that make them resilient in the face of challenges and internally motivate d for further exploration. Marsico and colleagues  (2019) propose that “the set of adults’ discourses about the student provides a wide repertoire of self, which the student internalizes from a very early age to elaborate on their own meaning” (p. 54). This self-repertoire represents the ongoing discourse between self and context. The internal dialogue determines how students position themselves in the face of challenges and how they relate to the content, enabling them to take responsibility for their learning and develop competence (Calabrese Barton et al., 2013; Carlone & Mercier, 2026; Deci & Ryan, 2000; Kang et al., 2018; Marsico et al., 2019). Through this internalized voice, students shape their internal dialogue by calling upon their agency; authoring oneself consists of a continuous process in whic h the author of self exercises agency  through figured world (Barton & Tan, 2010; Holland et al. 1998). However, student agency is not limited to dialogic interactions alone; it is also developed through actions and relationships that position them as certain kinds of people over time and across contexts (Caza et al., 2018). Through this ongoing dialogue with context —whether involving people, spaces, or temporal experiences —students come to see themselves in particular ways and rework their sense of self thr ough identity work (Caza et al., 2018). Importantly, the development of a future-oriented self is not linear but directional towards the “ as if realms”, reflecting how individuals envision who they are becoming over time. Students go through an exploratory  process to build their notion of self, as identity play that looks  at the provisional self (Holand et al., 1998). Provisional self puts students on an exploration disposition, where the students can push the edges of their comfort zone without fear of losing the notion of self while discovering the aspect of self that they have not been aware of (Holland et al., 1998). Consequently, when students are provided with space and opportunities to express themselves and explore their provisional self, their agency is activated, fostering the formation and evolution of who they aspire to become (Carlone & Mercier, 2026; Grant, 2020; Holland et al., 1998).

The study aims to understand how high school students explore future-oriented identities that connect with unfolding possibilities in their studies, careers, and lives. To support such identity exploration, a communitybased, authentic research experience program was implemented, as elaborated in the method section.

## School and context

The research took place at Tech Valley  High School, a four-year regional public high school  located in Albany, NY, USA. For the past four years, our research team has been collaborating with three teachers to design authentic research opportunities for 12th-grade students (17-to 18-year-olds), supported by a cluster of business partners who worked  with the students to investigate authentic challenges  (Ding et al, 2025; Underwood et al., 2025). Aligned with their authentic research experience, students were further engaged in a field-based experience project (I-Term) in which students explore d specific career fields with community partners and college professionals. The design of the I-Term project integrated the concept of Ikigai, a Japanese concept that refers to how  a person defines the meaning (reason) for their being as related to  oneself and the larger society. As students participated in the I-Term experience, they searched for their Ikigai by reflecting on questions such as what you are good at, what you love, what the world needs, and what you can be paid for. 802 Data sources and analysis For the larger research project, we collected multiple data sources to trace students’ research experiences supported by their teachers and community partners. Students with consent  approved by IRB  participated in a semi-structured post-interview. The interviews solicited students’ reflections on their research  experiences and their school experiences in general. After reviewing student interviews conducted during the recent two school years (2022-2023 and 2024-2025), seven students were selected as their interviews included explicit reflection on their identities and futures. A thematic analysis method was used as it “allows a nuanced understanding of what people say and do within their particular social contexts” (Jowsey et al., 2021, p. 475). First, we chose three anchoring questions in the interviews: a) “Would you be interested in doing research as part of your future study, work, or life? " b) "I want to ask a question about your I-Term project. What did you do for the I-Term? What did you learn or discover? How may the I-Term experience influence your  planning of future development?” c) “Looking back at your experiences at school in the past few years, what are the high points of your learning gains?”.  For the analysis,  researchers adopted an inductive approach by assigning open codes in the first round of coding. This open coding was conducted to develop an initial understanding of the data.  In the second round of coding, the lead  researcher reviewed and re fined the coding.  In the last round,  the same researcher extracted themes through MAXQDA. After the extraction of themes and situating themes in the paper, the themes were also re-read by another researcher. Moreover, pseudonyms were  used for the names  of participants to secure the privacy of the participants. For the validity of the findings,  extracted themes were reviewed by another researcher in connection with the raw data. Limitation and future direction However, this study is in the preliminary stages, which limits a deeper understanding of the program’s effects on students’ identity exploration. Future research should include a more detailed examination of the program’s design features, particularly how teachers support the development of students’ identities and what types of resources schools need to help students work on their knowledge, values, and goals. In addition, longitudinal research is needed to follow students beyond high school in order to examine their satisfaction with their educational and career choices across different contexts, as well as their performance within those pathways. Further quantitative investigations would also be valuable for examining the replicability of the program’s design in different educational settings, which could provide deeper and longer-term insights into its impact on students’ identity development. Moreover, existing literature highlights the influence of students’ historical and social backgrounds —such as race, gender, and social class —on identity positioning; these factors were not the focus of the current study and should be examined in future work. Finally, additional research is needed to investigate the role of teachers’ identities in designing and enacting the program, as well as business partners’ experiences working with students.
