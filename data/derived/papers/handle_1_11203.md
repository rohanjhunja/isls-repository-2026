# Exploring the Nexus of Gaming Frequency, CS Interest and Self-Efficacy: Preliminary Results from a Randomized Controlled Trial of Scalable Game Design’s Impact in Elementary Schools

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study examines the between Scalable Game Design (SGD) experience and 5th graders’ CS interest and self-efficacy, particularly assessing variances related to students’ gaming frequency. Utilizing an RCT design, we assigned 14 schools to implement SGD (Treatment), while 13 schools continued with their standard curriculum (Control). The Treatment group implemented week-long SGD activities twice, once in the fall and once in the spring of one academic year. Our analyses include data from 425 students. CS interest, CS self-efficacy, and learning engagement surveys were used as primary data sources. Findings revealed no significant differences in CS interest and self-efficacy between Treatment and Control groups. Within the Treatment group, students with higher gaming frequencies exhibited significantly greater CS self-efficacy and interest post-intervention thandid students with lower gaming frequency. Moreover, high-frequency gamers were relatively more engaged in SGD activities than their low-frequency counterparts. These results underscore the potential for tailoring CS curricula to resonate with specific groups of students based on interests.

## Introduction

Over the past few decades, there has been a notable surge in computer science (CS) job opportunities, particularly in the United States  (US Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2022). Specifically, the US Burea u of Labor Statistics estimated a 15% growth in CS jobs from 2021 to 2031, a rate much faster than the average growth across all jobs (US Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2022). This forecas t aligns with previous insights shared by the Burea u in 2015. These findings prompted the US to launch the “CS fo r All” initiative in 2016. Its primary goal is to i mmerse K12 students in CS activities from an early age, ens uring they are both interested in the subject and p repared for the workforce. Since its incept ion, CS for All has spawned many efforts to develop  high-quality CS curricula, incorporate CS into other subjects, and promote CS as a stand-alone subject in many K-12 schools (code.org et al., 2022). However, while numerous curricula are in use, most have not been studied using a randomized controlled trial (RCT), particularly to gauge their relative impact on students’ cognitive and affective domains. One such curriculum is Scalable Game Design (SGD). SGD involves a series of activities related to game and simulation design and development to foster and  maintain student interest in computer programming. Grounded in a “project-first” approach, SGD allows students to create thei r own arcade-style games after only a few lessons, bypassing traditional theoretical groundwork. As Repenning et al. (2015) highlighted, this approach “allows students to immediately engage in computer programming design experiences and to learn concept s as the need arises” (p. 8). The hands-on nature of this approach is believed to bolster s tudents’ sense of ownership, which could subsequently boost their CS self-efficacy and learning motivation (Repenning et al.,  2015). Numerous studies have explored how playing games affects stu dents’ STEM-related self-efficacy and interest (e.g., Drummond & Sauer, 2020; Ritzhaupt et al., 2011), and some have focused on the potential benefits of game creation and customization (e.g., Basawapatna et al., 2018) – activities deeply intertwined with computer programming. However, existing research on the relationships among video games, students’ STEM self-efficacy, and academic performances has produced conflicting results. Some studies found positive correlations (Ritzhaupt et al., 2011), while others found the relationship to be negative or insignificant (Drummond & Sauer, 2020). These inconsistencies could stem from the lack of true ex perimental designs in past studies. This gap prompt ed us to conduct a RCT, examining the relationships between SGD experiences, CS interest and self-efficacy of students at the upper elementary school level– a pivotal age when many begin to shape their career and academic identity. We recognized students’ diverse prior experiences with computing could influence their engagement with SGD. Thus, we hypothesized that students’ video gaming frequencies might be associated with their attitudes towards computing. We believe that our RCT could offer insights into how CS activities can be tailored to specific student interests (i.e., in the case of this study,  categories of students’ gaming frequency). To expl ore this relationship, we posed the following research questions:

1. Does students’ gaming frequency associate with the relationships between SGD activities and elementary

school students’ self-efficacy and interest in computer science?

2. How does students’ gaming frequency relate to their learning engagement during SGD activities in

elementary schools?

Theoretical frameworks: Flow theory and self-efficacy The present study is grounded in the intersection o f Csikszentmihalyi’s flow theory (1990) and Bandura ’s selfefficacy theory (1977). Flow theory fundamentally i nforms the development of SGD activities (Basawapat na et al., 2013). This theory delineates a phenomenon whe re individuals enter a state of “flow,” deeply enga ging in enjoyable activities perceived as challenging and w orthwhile. In a flow state, individuals often exhibit intrinsi c motivation, demonstrate focused concentration, and lose awareness of time (Csikszentmihalyi, 1990). Individuals tend to recreate these flow experiences, drawn to t heir intrinsically rewarding nature. Sharek and Wie be (2014) correlate flow state with interest, self-efficacy, and engagement-proposing that when individuals are engrossed in activities they find interesting, the chances of entering a flow state increase. This engagement persists when the activity’s difficulty aligns with their actual and perceived abilities, o r self-efficacy. Conversely, disengagement occurs when there is a mismatch, causing frustration and diminishing the likelihood of task completion. Achieving success in certain tasks or activities, k nown as mastery experience, constitutes a significa nt source of self-efficacy. Bandura (1977) posits self-efficacy as an individual’s beliefs in their capability to perform specific behaviors that yield desired outcomes, inc luding, but not limited to, academic performance or  attitudes. Consequently, CS self-efficacy refers to students’ confidence in their ability to excel in CS-related tasks, courses, or activities. In addition to  mastery experience, Bandura identifies three additi onal sources of self-efficacy: vicarious experiences, social or peer persuasion, a nd emotional and psychological states.  Mastery experiences encompass instances where students successfully com plete tasks. Vicarious experiences involve individu als witnessing others similar to them achieving success  through sustained effort. Peers and teachers signi ficantly shape students’ sel f-efficacy, and their emotional and psychological sta tes, which can include enthusiasm and anxiety. Numerous studies have provided evidence fo r the connections between these two theories, parti cularly within the realms of programming and game-based learning (Kong & Wang, 2021; Rachmatullah et al., 2021).

## Study context and participants

The current study is part of a broader two-cohort project, examining the effectiveness of SGD activities on rural 5th grade students’ computational thinking  (CT), CS self-efficacy, and interest in CS, using a RCT design. Here we focus solely on the first cohort of schools and students. Data collection is ongoing at the time of this paper ’s writing, hence the resul ts reported are preliminary. Additionally, the curr ent study emphasizes CS self-efficacy and interest, rather than integrating findings on CT with these constructs. Initially, 27 schools, comprising 64 teachers and 1 308 students, participated in the study. The school s were randomized and assigned to either a comparison group, where students continued learning via the standar d curriculum (business-as-usual), or a treatment group, where students engaged in a series of SGD activities spread over two semesters, with approximately one week of activities per semester. The schools were located in four US states: Alabama, Arkansas, Missouri, and Utah. In total, 14 schools (27 teachers and 553 students) were randomly assigned to the treatment group, and 13 schools (37 teachers and 755 students) were assigned to the Control group. However, we encountered an overall attrition of 67. 51% and a differential attrition of 2.10%, owing to  schools and teachers dropping out, students who did not hav e parental/guardian consent to participate, and mis sing outcome variables (i.e., they did not take the posttest). This attrition reduced the analyzable student data to a total of 425 students, comprising 252 students in the Control group and 173 students in the treatment group.  We acknowledge this as a limitation of the study. Demographic information of these students is presented in Table 1.

## Curricular context

Before implementing the SGD activities in each semester, the participating teachers completed six weeks of online asynchronous professional development (PD). The PD provided training on both conceptual and practical elements of SGD, such as computational thinking pra ctices and patterns, game-design theory, key ideas in CS, and step-by-step instructions for constructing games with Agent Cubes Online (ACO) —a 2D and 3D visual programming environment that allows novice students to build games and simulations. In the fall semester of 2022, teachers learned about content and pedagogical practices for designing two 1980s arcade-style games: Frogger and Pac-Man. Frogger involves guiding a frog through heavy traffic and over a river, skillfully avoiding obstacles along the way. Pac-Man challenges players to navigate the character through a maze, consuming dots and avoiding four Ghost Monsters. After completing PD courses focused on these game creation activities, the teachers implemented what they had learned in their classrooms. Participating  teachers were expected to complete the Frogger and Pac-Man projects, in sequence, as part of their instructional plan. In the spring semester of 2023, teachers extended t heir knowledge of the platform  through building an advanced version of Pac-Man and scientific simulations. In revisiting their Pac-Man games, teachers learned how to simulate elements of artificial intelligence, us ing collaborative diffusion and hill climbing, with  the ghost agents. They then led their students through more complicated game building challenges. ## Discussion and implications

The current study aimed to examine relationships am ong a two weeks of SGD activities spread over two consecutive semesters and fifth-grade students’ CS self-efficacy and interest. We also sought to identify whether there was any variability in these relationships based on the students’ self-reported video gaming frequency. Our findings for research questions 1 and 2 support previous studies that hypothesize a  connection between interest, flow state, and self-efficacy (Rachmatullah et al., 2021; Sharek & Wiebe, 2014). In our study, students with high gaming frequency w ere found to have significantly higher CS self-efficacy and interest after participating in SGD ac tivities, compared to those with low gaming frequen cy. Moreover, the highly frequent gamers were relatively more engaged during activities. This suggests that students with an interest in game design and creation, as in dicated by their high gaming frequency, find SGD ac tivities even more intriguing than low frequency gamers. Their immersion in the activities and determination to complete them aligns with flow theory, suggesting that they find the familiar activities worthwhile even when t hey might be challenging. Their prior interest in gaming interest may support their flow state and persist through the sequence of activities (Csikszentmihalyi, 1990). By striving to complete the activities and creat e functional games, these students likely accumulated what Bandura referred to as mastery experience—a source of self-efficacy (Bandura, 1977). As a result, they developed higher CS self-efficacy, believing they could program a game. In terms of CS interest, although we found signific ant differences between students with low and high gaming frequencies in the Treatment group, these differences were already apparent even without engagement in SGD activities, as shown in the Control group, even the CS interest of high programing students in Control group was higher than those in the Treatment group. Students in the Control group exhibited a high level of CS interest typical for elementary school students (Vandenberg et al., 2021). The lower CS interest in the Treatment group may stem from the students’ realization of the challenges in CS activities given their recent experience with a new interface and learning to code, affecting their perceptions of CS. This phenomenon is consistent with ExpectancyValue Theory  (Eccles & Wigfield, 2002), where a misalignment between expec tations and tasks may lead to decreased interest. The SGD activities in the secon d semester might account for this misalignment, esp ecially as students engaged in progressively more challenging coding techniques, resulting in lower engagement compared to the first semester (fall 2022). Research on stud ent interests in STEM education demonstrates simila r findings: as students progress further in the discipline and encounter more challenging topics, more students indicate lower interest in the discipline (Adams et al., 2016).  Further studies are needed to more fully understand the relationship between topic interest, engagement, and CS interest.

Our findings have broader implications for CS educa tion. Although the SGD activities did not have a significant overall impact on fifth graders’ CS sel f-efficacy and interest, the varying effects based on  gaming frequency might caution CS teachers to choose their  curriculum carefully, considering students’ intere st. In addition, Webb et al. (2012) identified a relationship between pedagogy a nd student motivation, suggesting that teacher use of guided discovery pedagogies sustain students’ dispositions towards CS. Teachers and cur riculum developers might consider additional support for students who have less familiarity with gaming or cod ing, such as through introductory or unplugged activities to increase their pre-unit interest.

## Conclusion, limitation, and future direction

Utilizing a randomized controlled trial design, we investigated the relationships among  two week-long SGD activities and 5th graders’ CS self-efficacy and interest and examined whether the relationships varied according to students’ gaming frequencies. Our findings revealed no significant differences in CS self-efficacy and interest between the Treatment and Control groups following the intervention. However, the relationships did differ based on students’ gaming frequencies, with students with  high gaming frequency displaying significantly hig her CS self-efficacy and interest after the intervention compared to those with low gaming frequency. Like many research endeavors, the current study’s f indings should be interpreted with caution, as ther e were limitations in the sample size. Although we us ed an RCT design, a high attrition rate resulted in only thir ty percent of the student data from the full sample being analyzable. Consequently, the data used for analysis might not fully represent the entire sample. In addition, the results were derived from the first cohort group, hence, they are still preliminary. We intend to conduct a second cohort of the study a nd will seek strategies to secure a more comprehensive and representative sample size.
