# Temporality: Meditations on Time and Learning in the ‘Anthropocene’

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper explores the intersection of temporality and learning in the context of transformative education, particularly in response to the Anthropocene and climate change. Recognizing the paradoxes inherent in modern education—where the urgency of transformation conflicts with the time required for deep learning—we engage in two philosophical meditations to critically examine how time constructs shape and constrain educational practices. Drawing from continental philosophy, we interrogate dominant temporal paradigms in modern schooling, particularly the accelerationist pressures of neoliberalism, which prioritize efficiency and assessment over reflective learning. We argue that enriched conceptions of temporality—rooted in expansive, relational, and historically situated understandings—offer pathways for world-making practices that support just and sustainable futures. Through engagement with cultural tools, decolonial perspectives, and alternative knowledge systems, we propose an expanded temporality for education that fosters deep, transgressive learning. Our meditations invite further contemplation on how time can be refigured in the learning sciences to support transformative change.

## Introduction

The need for radical forms of learning-centred transformation is increasingly recognised i n transformations to sustainability, the climate sciences, and calls for  associated education system and process transforma tions. Yet the desired approaches to learning remain under-developed (cf. Lotz-Sisitka et al., 2015; Lotz-Sisitka, 2024). The call for social transformation is accompanied b y sociological and philosophical critique s that contemporary society moves at an accelerating pace (e.g. Rosa, 2013; Han, 2015; 2017), making the deep learning and reflection necessary for meaningful transformation increasingly difficult. A rguably, the reasonable call for transformation, the struggle to find time to achieve this transformation, and the pressure on education to prioritize assessment—positioning schools as goal-oriented institutions that turn students from knowledge-seeking subjects into knowledge-driven projects—present a paradoxical situation that educators and educational researchers must address. There is also a paradox in an apparent dis juncture between the pace of climate  change impacts and the urgency to respond, and the time needed to bring ab out the cultural (and thus learning-centred) shifts required to respond to this urgency (e.g. Gardiner, 2011; Shriv astava et al., 2020).  One way to navigate these challenges is to create space for contemplating the concept of time and its significance within educational sciences.

In this paper, we use the term meditation to refer to what can be described as a continental style of philosophizing (in African society this same practice is found in reflective, rhythmic praxis of various kinds). This philosophical approach assumes that certain intelle ctual problems can never be fully resolved, yet sti ll require critical engagement (Baumann, 1993). Accordingly, t hrough our meditations, we aim to: (a) identify fea tures, presuppositions, and comple xities in time constructs; (b) explore implicit con nections between the paradox of time and other relevant questions; (c) critically a ssess existing strategies that fail to address the challenges posed by different time constructs; and (d) identify resp onsible ways to engage with diverse conceptions of time (Donahue & Ochoa Espejo, 2016). Importantly, this style—or method—of philosophizing departs from analytical philosophy by allowing for a more contemplative and  explorative approach to intellectual proble ms, while maintaining clear boundaries for each meditation as  defined by the core concepts in Meditations One and Two below.

It is in this context that this paper delves into temporality as an emerging area of research in the learning sciences, necessary for world-making in times of climate change. This paper contributes to theory-building work related to this focus in educational research, offered here as meditation, and invitation. We offer a brief review of an emerging interest in more complex notions of tem porality in the educational and learning sciences t han were applied in most modern / colonial education systems  and their associated constructs of learning.   Then, through two meditations which bring our work into conversat ion, we reflect on how temporality concepts can inf orm world-making practices oriented towards embodied, justice seeking ‘earthly learning’ processes that connect time with learning and living well as interconnected species being(s) in the Anthropocene era. The meditations presented here form part of an ongoing collaborative international research programme that explores meanings and praxis of transformative, transgressive learning in times of climate change  (LotzSisitka et al., 2015; Lotz-Sisitka, 2024).   Our research is transformatively and transgressivel y interested, and reflexively situated in times where the fall out of coloniality and fossil capital collide in an increasingly regressive political landscape described as “the deadening ideology of late-stage capitalism and its corollaries of patriarchy, rationalism, white supremacy and anthropocentrism” (Akomolafe & Ladha 2017). To this we add the isolat ed individualism and accelerationism of neoliberalism (Han, 2017; Rosa, 2013). World-making through learning is clearly needed, but of which kind and how constituted?

Temporality and learning All forms of learning are processes that unfold ove r time. Learning and time are inherently intertwine d in a continuous process of change—sometimes transformative, sometimes aligned with so cial and individual reform, and at other times reinforcing stabilit y and conservatism. A key feature of modern educati on systems is their structuring and functioning as a form of 'time mana gement' for teaching and learning institutions, edu cators, and students alike. The context of global change challe nges ushers in the n eed to re-think relationships between learning and time in and for collective learning pr ocesses, as these require time to engage over diffe rent stakeholder epistemologies as outlined below in Meditation One.  Such collective learning processes can’t easily be inscribed with the structural efficiencies of ti me management as practiced in most formal education environments.  And, failure to give critical attention to temporality in learning and education for change is already resulting in new forms of instrumentalism seen in the advancement of rational choice theory logics of behavioral economics in education with the assumption being th at some should think and act for others as there is  ‘no time’ to allow them to think or learn for themselves, either individually or collectively (Archer & Tritter, 2000).  Time, or various constructs of time, are thus embedded in  both learning and education. Framing time, tempora l paradoxes, and time constructs as focal points for critical and constructive inquiry is therefore a si gnificant and necessary endeavor for the learning sciences in contemporary times. There is an increased interest in more complex noti ons of temporality in guiding and framing transformative education, with for example Facer (2 024) proposing enrichment of ‘temporality imaginati on’ in education and learning, and Alhadeff-Jones (2017) arguing for a rethinking of adult lear ning rhythms from a temporal complexity perspective. From a critical vantage point, Bennet and Burke (2017) argue that education is dominated by hegemonic concepts of time that can st ructurally and practically limit or impoverish noti ons of student capability and learning success in modern e ducation systems through concepts of time that indi vidualise and decontextualise learning. Although this paper d oes not directly address childhood, Tesar et al. (2 016) exemplify the growing interest in more complex time  constructs. They describe childhoods as “temporal encounters that are vibrant, changing, shifting and, in some discourses, even disappearing”, noting that childhood is a ‘temporal encounter’ full of unknowns. Yet chi ldhoods are “constructed and compartmentalised” and  overly structured by modernist histories that suggest caus ality, linear time, evolution, and progress from pa st to future, oftentimes in a structurally defined ‘tyranny of time’ (Wien & Kirby-Smith, 1998). Gibbons (2016) describes the tyranny of time as “new relationships that involve a matrix of new media, new pedagogy and new subject knowledge, informed by digital-age discourses that underscore the notion of rapid change and the claim that teachers need to ‘get with the times’.” Importantly, Lewis (2014) argues that learning invo lves differentiations internal to the learning proc ess that should not be conflated or reduced to modern e ducation’s reliance on limited temporality forms, h inting at enriching temporality perspectives in education. We consider this further in our meditations below. Meditation 1:  Education in the ‘Anthropocene’: how might we figure temporalities in transformative learning processes?

The Anthropocene era and concept is an outflow of m odern industrial and colonial cultures. It is a tem porality construct that denotes regression over time of human-nature relations within a frame of deep earth time (Parikka, 2014). Environmental scientists  suggest that rates of change between fast, non-linear environmental changes characteristic of the Anthropocene era, and accompa nying social changes are ‘out of sync’ (Shrivastava  et al., 2020). It is said that cultural and value changes h ave to occur within a generation, rather than over generations, via advancing ‘new ways of learning’ (ibid). A temporality paradox lies in an assessment that this is problematic because it ‘takes time to engage over different stakeholder epistemologies’ (ibid), in other words, we don’t appear to have the time to learn to address the Anthropoce ne crises together, in the ‘new ways’ suggested. However, a growing body of transformative, transgressive learning cases in a southern African research programme where people are struggling to advance sustainability, livelihoods and well being (Lotz-Sisitka, 2024), shows that it is possible to co-produce possibility knowledge(s) and transformations in shared activity (i.e. worldmaking practices) through expansive learning.   This has potential to address the paradox outlined above. Engeström(2007, pg. 275) explains that expansively learning new ways of working [and living] require ‘destabilising categorical or stabilized knowledge in order to turn it into possibility knowledge’, a learning process that involves putting ‘inert stabilizing knowledge into movement’. Such a process, he argues, requires development of new instrumentalities or cultural to ols (cf. Engeström, 2024), includ ing new temporality constructs to underlabour such processes.

In this research programme we have been exploring u se of temporally imbued cultural tools to produce such possibility knowledge as catalyst for transforming activity. For example, co-constructing local and possible histories drawing on Koselleck’s (2018) w ork on ‘sediments of time’  (e.g. deep time records; anti-colonial / Black Anthropocene geo-time records (cf. Yusoff, 2018)) has helped to brea k out of contemporary linear / narrowed colonial histories as sedimented.  Cultural tools using the notion of multiplicities of time (Adam, 1994) in the everyday lives of people as found in the ent angled, convergent or divergent, overlapping or dia lectical unfoldings of geo-historical time, racialized and /or gendered time, attentive time, practice time, skills development time, reflexive time etc, offer a rich source of possibility knowledge(s) for activity transformations. For example colonial / apartheid coal fired energy production leading to loadshedding is affecting coo king, working, learning etc. time, with inherent contradi ctions surfacing interest in alternative energy world-making practices. A third temporality resource for co-constructing possibility knowledge(s) exists in the richness of vernacular languages and indigenous temporality exp eriences. For example, we found that the isiXhosa concept of ‘ilima’ [meaning ploughing or working co llectively together] is a form of embodied collecti ve time for co-learning and ubuntu-praxis across epistemologies and ‘daily schedules’.  This offers temporally fr amed possibility knowledge(s) amongst the amaXhosa in just food transitions. Through use of these cultural tools in expansive le arning interactions oriented towards transformation and transgression of ‘stuck points’ in the Anthropo cene, and resolution of the paradox above, we are n oting that temporalities of world-making in education are at the same time foundational (needing more time), immanent and urgent, complex and social-ecological, and deeply embedded in decolonial processes of reclaiming and sustaining relations to land, air and waters.

In short, our learning research is showing that the re is a need for more refined analysis of, and juxtaposings of temporality perspectives in figurin g transformative learning in the Anthropocene, a pe rspective we enrich with mediation 2 below.

Meditation 2:  Education over-directed by ‘Vita Activa’:  could there please be more time for ‘Vita Contemplativa’?

Relevant to the learning paradox outlined above, So uth Korean philosopher Byung-Chul Han's Scent of Time

(2017) argues that the “digital order” has created a temporal construct called “point-time,” that is tied to an

“ahistorical space.” According to Han (2017), the time of the internet is a fragmented, discontinuous "Now-time” (p. 40). In this model, time, including in and for education and learning, becomes a commodity, someth ing that can be in short supply or abundant. As touched upon  above, time as a commodity extends to formal educa tion systems, where only time spent in structured, teleo logical learning is seen as valuable, reinforcing c olonial and industrialist education models.

This emphasis on “point-time” undermines opportunities for deep, emergent learning that is essential for social transformation, particularly in the context of climate change. Point-time, lacking temporal continuity, forces a constant rush from one moment to the next, leaving no space for reflection or self-gathering—qualities that are central to ‘vita contemplativa’. Instead, we are tr apped in vita activa, where learning is no longer g uided by narrative time. In this framework, time becomes dis jointed and devoid of substantive direction, resulting in what Han (2024) calls a “crisis of narration”. Students, along with society at large, are caught in a world driven by toxic positivity, where the illusion that “anything is po ssible” leads to a lack of real possibility, and le arning is lost in the gaps between disconnected moments.

This societal shift toward achievement and producti vity, driven by excessive positivity, often leads t o stress, depression, and self-reproach, as individuals struggle to meet unattaina ble standards. As Han (2015) explains, the “achievement-subject” fights itself, and depression is a symptom  of a society that has internalized an unrelenting demand for success. In ‘anthropocene ’ point-time, deep, transgressive learning becomes impossible because true learning requires relationships—connections that are only possible in narrative time and space conducive to contemplation. Han (2017) argues  that truth is rooted in relationships based on sim ilarity, commitment, and closeness. In this sense, point-time is not only a threat to meaningful learning bu t also to the very nature of human connection. The absence of temporal depth deprives students of the time needed to challenge toxic values and norms, preventing the transformati on needed for a dignified life in the face of clima te crises. Furthermore, the lack of narrative coherence in poi nt-time caused by loss of mythological and historical time leads to a “crisis of identity”, of detachment from world and self. This crisis of identity calls for a return to deep transgressive learning that can reconnect individuals to a more grounded, narrative form of earthly rhythm tim e; time for relationality required of bridging ‘epi stemic divides’ noted in the learning paradox above, and f or which we have sought out temporality resources f or enriching such learning, shared above. Time, and en riched temporalities in education are needed for le arning in relationship with others, and living within the tem poral and ethical framewo rks necessary for collective transformation. Time for narrating lives in alignme nt with earthly time and space; with each other, and through this for world-making in the Anthropocene.

## Conclusion

Our meditations above shared two engagements with diversely constituted, yet co-enriching temporality concepts and processes shaping our thinking and praxis of transformative, transgressive learning in the Anthropocene. The intense, lingering form of vita contemplativa; and the more complexly constituted p rocesses of mobilising possibility knowledges for just transitioning with temporality constructs. The meditations offer insight into some relations of temporality and learning that can co-exist in world making. Our intent was simply to ill uminate intersecting and enriched perspectives on temporali ty in and for transformative earthly learning proce sses and to invite further contemplation(s) and meditation on (re-)figuring time in the learning sciences in tim es of climate change.

What implications might this have for education and  learning as practice? One possible answer is that both students and teachers require more time to exp lore the extent to which —and how—education and learning can serve as leverage for social transformation  in the face of a climate-vulnerable future. And with multi-actors engaging more in learning processes necessary to co llaborate across epistemologies to transform their activities and worlds,  adequate attention to the time necessary for this is needed.  However, if we accept the argument that time is widely perceived as 'scarce' and that we are constantly 'running out' of it, then merely wishing for 'pockets of time' to enable the kind of curiosity-driven and critical reflection necessary for this understanding seems futile, and we may fail to create the conditions necessary for collective, transformative learning. As philosophers and educators, we can offer an alte rnative response. One possible strategy is to infus e teacher education programmes with empirically well-informed and enriched spaces dedicated to the kind of critical reflection this paper advocates. And to engage teacher students in the question of dealing with time in their teaching. Such a didactic model must be adapted to the specific contexts of education, yet it could also serve as a model for reintroducing a philosophy of education t hat critically examines the foundations of learning  through troubling and expanding on concepts that are often taken for granted —such as time, place, space, the learning subject, and the self.  Another possible strategy is to empirically explore temporalities embodied in different forms of learning (e.g. individual and collective learnin g), and especially the temporalities embodied in tr ansformative learning given the need for world-making, making them more visible to the learning sc iences and thus to educational scholars.  As indicated, our meditations are invitational, thus we foresee further alternative responses arising as our enquiries proceed.
