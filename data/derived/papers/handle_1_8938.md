# Becoming Visible in a New Learning Environment

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper explores an episode of a Black student seeking attention from a classroom adult at a majority white, middle-class coding camp. I explore how the theory of perceptual affordances and attunement intersect with racialized experiences at school to understand how attention moved in a classroom and was ultimately distributed in a way that the Black student’s needs were missed.

## Introduction

The racialized practices of policing and disciplin ing Black children while at school is well-established in the field of education (Monroe, 2005; Lewis et al., 2010; Skiba et al. 2011, etc.). In particular, research has consistently shown that Black boys are more likely to be suspended, referred to the office, and expelled than their White peers (Welsh & Little, 2018). This creates a culture where doing the exact right thing at all times becomes pertinent for Black students’ survival of school. Understandably, one survival tactic for Black students is to be less visible. This is necessitated particularly in contexts such as charter networks using behavior management strategies based in the theory of broken-windows policing (Lemov, 2010, p. 195), which encourage teachers to monitor even the smallest behaviors, and settle for no less than 100% compliance. But what happens when students who were socialized  to behave in this way once they enter new environments? Research has raised concern about such behavior management practices due to their mis alignment with cultures found in the colleges and workplaces students will encounter after they leave school (Golann, 2015). To highlight this lack of alignment more concretely at the micro level, I explore a case study of a Black boy from a low income, high discipline urban middle school attempting to gain attention in a coding summer camp with a majority White, middle-class student population. Even though the summer camp had a student/teacher ratio of 2:1, the boy remained mostly invisible for 7 minutes as he tried to seek help on his coding project. In my analysis I seek to show not only that a student was missed in a highly supervised environment, but I also try to make sense of why, and how he attempts to understand the adults’ system of attention, that does not at first seem to operate in familiar ways.

## Conceptual framework: How attention works in classrooms

Attention and its distribution is a joint accomplishment between group members, environmental affordances, and attunement. However, there are certain patterns in identity that have been demonstrated to be predictive of who will gain the most attention, which is often highly inequitable. For example, students who receive the most attention in a classroom are most likely to be high achieving white males (Martin, Milne, & Scantlebury, 2006). There can often also be a socioeconomic divide, with wealthier students being more likely to actively seek help than their economically disadvantaged peers (Calarco, 2011). The expectation of “seeking help if you need it” is also commonly upheld by teachers, in alignment with the culture of middle-class students (Calarco, 2011). The picture of a white, middle-class, high-achieving male student as the most likely collector of attentional resources is central to this analysis of a focal student: a Black boy named Prince.

## Methods

I used interaction analysis (Jordan & Henderson, 1995) as well as Gibson’s theory of affordances (1979) to explore an episode where Prince, is attempting to gain an a dult’s attention for help on his coding challenge. Prince was stuck on his code for 20 minutes, and the focal 7-minute clip encapsulates the moments where he sought attention from an adult. This is an anomaly during this activity, as all other students during this time can be seen receiving substantial support during the same focal minutes.

Data The focus episode occurred on the first day of a we ek-long camp called Code Your Art, an introductory coding class in which students learn how to create various art projects using NetLogo (Tisue & Wilensky, 1999). During the clip, students were engaged in an independent “ coding scavenger hunt” where they were challenged t o use code to create different images, such as stripes or a border. The focus student for this analysis is Prince, a ri sing 9th grader. Prince attended East Middle School, a majority Black and Latinx urban school with a suspension rate of 15%, compared to the district average  of 9%. At camp, he was the oldest student in the class of 10, one of two Black students, and the only Black boy. However, as commonly seen, he was writing code using plain English, and continuously received error messages. The clip begins when Prince started attempting to ask for help. The adults relevant to the analysis are “Researcher,” Ms. Turner, the lead  teacher of the class, and Victor, the classroom TA and coding expert. While the activity lasted about 30 minutes,  Prince struggled for the first 20 minutes to accomplish any of the challenges. The focal clip details the final 7 minutes of his struggle with the code as he is trying to troubleshoot this new system of adult attention. For this piece, I analyzed both screencapture and webcam data from Prince’s laptop. The screencapture software depicts both what Prince was currently see ing and doing on his laptop while the webcam and la ptop microphone recorded Prince and his immediate surroundings. Analytic Framework Gibson’s (1979) theory of perceptual affordances states that what can happen in any particular moment is made up of possibilities that are co-constructed by the affordances of the environment, effectivities of the people in the environment, and the dynamic intentions of those in the environment based off of the current situation (Gresalfi, Barnes & Cross, 2011). Affordances, are the actions that particular objects or stimuli invite in an environment (Greeno, 1994). For example, in a classroom a raised hand often invites a teacher to call on that student. The ability for student bids for attention to become affordances in a classroom setting can strongly depend on the attunement of the adults in the room. Attunement is shaped by a person’s culture, identity, and previous experiences, and therefore poses a situated set of limitations in what can become possible for a given individual. For example, in a culture that values students who are quietly following directions, it will be common for the teacher to be more attuned to the activity of the active student, sometimes at the expense of missing quieter bids from others.

## Findings

Figure 1 shows an overview of Prince’s four attempts at interacting with adults in the space. A common strategy he employed throughout the episode was using eye contact and other quiet bids for attention. However, as will be shown below, the organization of adult attunement during this activity left his requests for help unanswered for 7 minutes.

Figure 1. An overview of the interactional bid that outlines the attempt and the reasons adults did not respond.

Episodes 1 & 2 In the beginning, Prince offers interactionally quiet bids for attention as he is trying to understand how to interact with the adults. To begin, “Researcher” was working with the student sitting directly to the right of Prince. After she finished, she looked quickly at Prince, turned back to her original position, and then exited the room. From the first image in Figure 1, we can see that Prince  was anticipating Researcher’s appearance on his le ft side. Because we can see that Researcher was not looking when the hand is offered, we know that Prince’s raised hand did not become an affordance for help, and he was missed. The second moment of Prince’s bidding to lead teacher Ms. Turner seems to, in part, be a response Researcher’s failure to recognize Prince’s silent hand. As Ms. Turner was working with the student directly to ICLS2022 Proceedings 1342 © ISLS the left, Prince let out a long, audible groan, and looked toward Ms. Turner. However, it is unclear from the record if Ms. Turner heard the groaning or not, and Prince was missed a second time. Episode 3 Later in the activity, Prince was approached by Victor, the class TA. In the approximately 9 second exchange, Victor asked how it’s going, to which Prince replied, “It’s good.” Prince also nodded when Victor asked if he was “getting this figured out.” However, by this time, Prince had still not been able to complete a challenge of the scavenger hunt, and his screen remained blank. The challenge here, then, was in Victor’s position in the camp was that of “coding expert,” as he spent the class period solving various errors and debugging code. Although Prince’s screen remained blank due to not yet solving any of the coding problems, it is entirely possible that since Prince didn’t have buggy code, Victor was not attuned in a way to help him get started. It is unclear from the record why Prince says that things are good after a string of error messages. It could be that in this moment Prince was developing an idea that he wanted to try before asking for help. Seconds before this interaction, Prince let out a verbal “mmmm!” as if he had an idea. Nonetheless, “How’s it going” did not become an affordance for Prince to ask for help, and Victor moved on. Episode 4 The moment on the record where it is most obvious that Prince was needing of attention was when he verbalized it directly to Ms. Turner. Approximately one minute after Prince expressed to Victor that things were “good”, he employed his new idea, which again received an error message. With signs of exasperation, Prince offered a strong bid with three potential affordances: eye contact, a raised hand, and verbal confirmations that he needed help. However, Ms. Turner’s attention was not on Prince. She was instead attuned to his more vocal and active neighbor, and at the same moment Prince asked for help, Ms. Turner was already asking what the neighboring student was working on. After his bid, there was never a moment where Ms. Turner cycled back to Prince, or acknowledged that she would get to him soon, so these bids for attention, though interactionally strong, did not ultimately become affordances for Ms. Turner to help.

Much could be said about this interaction when it comes to the movement of Ms. Turner as well as her attunement. It could, for example, speak to Ms. Turner’s attunement toward certain children and behaviors. Prince’s neighbor was, as Ms. Turner described, a “squirrely” kid, while Prince was extremely compliant. Prince, for example, had thus far not done anything on his laptop that he was not explicitly told to do. On the other hand, his neighbor had consistent redirects to maintain his attention to the task at hand. This shows up as well in adult attentional patterns. In the same 7 minutes, Prince’s squirrely neighbor is attended to by adults four separate times. It is possible that Ms. Turner’s attunement was seemingly toward the kid with more “squirrely” tendencies, with less of an urgent need to attend to the activities of student who was extremely compliant. Finally crossing the threshold The moment where Prince was finally able to be noticed and receive help from Ms. Turner was still fairly quiet, in line with Prince’s other gestures and potential bids. In a moment where she was standing behind him, Prince simply turns to make eye contact and says, “I don’t know how to separate it,” which alludes to the first challenge of having a split screen that is half one color, half another. This sets off an extended interaction between Prince and Ms. Turner that puts him on the path to understanding how to use NetLogo syntax to accomplish his goal. Ultimately, Prince did not have to change his strategy to finally receive attention. However, by the time Prince had received meaningful help, every other student had had significantly more interaction and help from adults in the room. What seems to have made the difference for Prince in his actions becoming affordances in the end was his ability to correctly understand and anticipate Ms. Turner’s movements in a way that he could employ eye contact and gain attention.

## Discussion

When Prince did seek attention, he generally relied on quiet bids such as eye contact to signal his need for help. Due to his identity as a Black boy in a new environment, and the consequences for expressing defiance for Black boys has historically been severe, expressing silen t bids for attention can be seen as reasonable on h is part. However, these quiet bids seemingly took adults longer to notice in this environment. It is clear that temperaments such as his neighbor’s “squirreliness” was worthy o f a great deal of attention, but historically, a sq uirrelly disposition is not one that is available to Black b oys. Prince’s quiet demeanor paired with his ultra-compliance and status as the oldest student seemingly created a “perfect storm” of adults perceiving Prince as a kid that that didn’t have to focus on as much. This was not the s tory of Prince throughout the whole camp: eventuall y, he ICLS2022 Proceedings 1343 © ISLS would go on to become incorporated into the classroom and even receive special recognition for some of his work. However, this case has implications for educators serving marginalized students, as it presents a concrete example of the unequal weight that many may carry intonew environments. Creating educational spaces in urban contexts that necessitate quiet compliance may create order in the short term, but disadvantage marginalized students in the long term as they need tonegotiate how to participate in new spaces. And in learning environments that bring together students from different contexts, the tacit assumption that students are asking for help if they need it can lead to less visibility for marginalized students, who may be more used to environments where being si lent is imperative for survival.

## Conclusion

This paper explores a 7-minute episode of Prince, the focus student, attempting understand the workings of adult attention in a new, less formal coding environment. In the beginning, teachers were seemingly attuned elsewhere (e.g. to the more “squirrely” kid), but there was seemingly, at first, a learning curve for Prince to understand how to seek help in such an environment. Learning how t eachers moved was consequential for Prince, who rel ied heavily on quiet but reasonable methods to get noticed that were unsuccessful in the new learning environment. Further analysis around how other students gained attention is needed.
