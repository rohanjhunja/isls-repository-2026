# Experimental Investigation on Reciprocal Teaching Using Misconception-Based Teachable Agents in Collaborative Learning

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study examines methods to promote mutual explanatory activities in collaborative learning using a teachable virtual peer learner. Its purpose is to determine whether the quality (error level) of knowledge generated by a teachable virtual peer learner can encourage learners to reevaluate their understanding. This study uses the ACT-R cognitive architecture to examine a teachable agent that learns from learners' concept maps and generates feedback maps. Further, a comparative experiment is conducted on a non-agent, an erroneous knowledge agent, and a non-erroneous agent. Results reveal that the use of a teachable agent enhances the learning activities of learners. Specifically, when learners interact with the erroneous knowledge agent, they actively revise their concept maps and, thereby, improve learning outcomes. This revision process was prompted by observing incorrect maps and encouraging learners to review and correct their knowledge.

## Introduction

The e xamination of effective instructional methods in collaborative le arning is a core theme in collaborative learning support research. Studies highlight the importance of the active engagement of learners in explanatory activities with others while constructing knowledge. For instance, research indicates the importance of constructive knowledge building with others, which is exemplified by the ICAP(Interactive, Constructive, Active, and Passive) framework (Chi, 2009) and enables learners to gain new perspectives through collaborative interactions (Shirouzu et al., 2002). Research emphasizing learners’ active knowledge construction often focuses on learning through dialogue and interaction s within a community (Scardamalia, 2006). However, learner-centered collaborative learning in volves some challenges such as the risk of shared misconceptions (Chi, 200 9) and need for teacher intervention to ensure deep un derstanding (Webb, 2009). Teacher intervention has limitations, as well, particularly in online learning environments, which give rise to communication synchronicity issues. Even in face-to-face settings, large class sizes can cause a shortage of human resources to provide adequate support. Technology, including artificial intelligence (AI), holds promise as a solution to such challenges and supports collaborative learning. Therefore, this study investigates the types of tech nologies and designs that are most beneficial in facilitating learner-centered collaborative learning. Reciprocal teaching and teachable agents in collaborative learning The examination of  effective methods for learners to construct knowled ge independently and achieve in-depth understanding of text highlights the utility of reciprocal teaching, which was proposed by Palinscar and Brown

(1984) as a method to improve reading comprehension. In reciprocal teaching, teachers and learners take turns to

play the teaching role and adopt four strategies —summarization, question generation, clarif ication, and prediction—to enhance text comprehension. This process enables learners t o actively construct their knowledge and develop metacognitive skills. Furthermore, the concept of “learning by teaching,” according to which learners teach others, is particularly significant  in educational research. Teaching enables learners to organize and systematize their knowledge to achieve in-depth understanding, whereby learners improve their explanatory skills, reconstruct concepts and, thereby, facilitate the solidification of knowledge. Recent technological advancements initiate new possibilities for learning by teaching. In part icular, learning by teaching using agents, which enables learners to take on a teaching role with a simulated learner (agent) on a computer, has gained  significant attention. Among such agents, teachable agents are particularly important because they  are AI systems that acquire learners’ knowledge and perform reasoning and problem-solving based on this knowledge. These agents provide learners with appro priate feedback on their teaching activities and support the improvement of their understanding and explanatory skills. A distinctive  feature of teachable agents is that they construct their own knowledge structures based on learners’ explanations, which can then be visualized. This enables learners to directly observe the impact of their te aching activities and help them develop effective teaching strategies.

Depending on the knowledge acquisition approach, t here are several methods for teachable agent construction: (1) In one method, the agent inductively examines learner s’ knowledge using machine learning techniques (Matsuda et al., 2015). (2) A knowledge-based approach involves learners teaching predefined content to the agent (Pareto, 2014). Finally, (3) a structure is prepared in advance with a knowledge base related t o the knowledge to be taught to the agent (e.g., graph representations for causal inference) (Biswas  et al., 2016). In a study by Biswas and colleagues (2016), which used method (3), learners provided knowledge to the agent through concept maps, and the agent used the maps to solve learning tasks in the Betty's Brain system. When a teachable agent is taught erroneous knowledge, it uses this incorrect knowledge to complete tasks,  prompting learners to clarify why the error occurred. However, this process does not address collaborative learning, which is t he focus of our study; further, it lacks cognitive fidelity, such as learning new know ledge like humans in method (1) or possessing prior knowledge, as in method (2).

Research on the use of teachable agents in collaborative learning contexts remains limited. For example, authors examined scenarios in which dyads were engaged in explanatory activities using concept maps, comparing conditions with a pedagogical agent and a virtual l earner equipped with learning-by-teaching functions (Hayashi et al., 2025). The agent used in this study featured a hybrid teachable agent design that incorporated both bottomup knowledge acquisition from learners and prior co ncept map knowledge. The system’s agent learn t from the learners’ concept maps  and generated and provided a new concept map as feedback to the learners, who then modified their own maps. The experiment revealed that the la tter condition induced equivalent or greater metacognitive reflection than the former. However, the study provided insufficient insight into how the presented examples’ quality influenced the facilitation of reflection. Role of incorrect knowledge in learning-by-teaching using teachable agents Studies have examined the usefulness of agent-generated knowledge, which is different from what was taught to the agents, in learning environments facilitating learning-by-teaching. When agents are considered collaborative partners, the generation of knowledge by agents indicates one’s collaboration with a partner possessing a different perspective.

Researchers have examined t he usefulness of solving problem s with collaborators having differing perspectives in collaborative learning settings. Once knowledge differences among collaborators become apparent, processes emerge to facilitate learners’ engagement in new explanatory activities to understand these differences (Miyake & Kirschner, 2014) or reconsider their conceptualizations. This causes learners to reexamine their knowledge through metacognitive reflection, which deepens their understanding. Therefore, encountering or discovering different re presentations during learning can benefit learning activities. Studies and evidence from analogy resea rch in cognitive science and studies of worked-out examples indicate that providing learners with examp le cases from various perspectives enhances their u nderstanding of problems. For example, a comparison of examples can  help induce an abstract schema including a general principle (e.g., Gick & Holyoak, 1983). Such schema s can be applied to transfer problems in future pro blemsolving. Using erroneous examples is another method  to encourage  examinations from different perspectives(Stark et al., 2011; Heitzmann et al., 2018). Errors can serve as productive elements in learning  (cf. impasse-driven learning; Van Lehn, 1999). For instance, Siegler and Chen (2008) found that self-explaining both correct and incorrect solutions is more beneficial than self-explaining correct solutions alone. By focusing their attention on common errors with incorrect examples, students can engage deeply with correct concepts (VanLehn, 1999), creating new mental representations that lab el incorrect concepts wrong (Vanden Broek & Kendeo u, 2008). Further, according to Durkin and Rittle-Johnson (2012), regardless of prior knowledge level s, studying both correct and incorrect examples is more effective than studying only two correct examples. In particular, when correct and incorrect examples are presented simult aneously, analyzing them and reflecting on why a certain reasoning is incorrect helps students understand th e essential characteristics of correct concepts. Ho wever, the spontaneous comparison of incorrect and correct examples requires strong metacognitive skills. Research on Intelligent Tutoring Systems clarifies that creating impasses or confusion (D'Mello & Graesser, 2012) through interactions among agents positively affects learning. Accordingly, instructional agents containing errors potentially facilitate learner understanding. When an instructi onal agent intentionally includes misunderstandings, learners may actively explain co ncepts to the agent  and, thereby, produce self-explanation effects. However, in learning environments employ ing learning-by-teaching with teachable agents, only limited examination of the types of representations generat ed by collaborative partners with incorrect (differ ing) perspectives has been done. Moreover, earlier research on erroneous examples has primarily been conducted within the mathematics domain, where, because of the nature of mathematical tasks, the quality of erroneous examples has not been adequately investigated in explanation activities of concept learning. This study’s research question expresses our suspicion that the quality of incorrect examples of teachable agents in the collaborative learning domain require s further investigation. By explicitly comparing the incorrect reasoning of a virtual learner to the correct reasoning, learners can maximize the benefit of using incorrect examples for learning.

Research goal and hypotheses This study identifies ways to support explanatory activities in autonomous collaborative learning among learners, using AI agents and cognitive modeling techniques as scaffolding support. It focuses on scenarios in which learner pairs collaboratively create concept maps and explain foundational concepts within a specialized field. To scaffold the learners’ collaborative activities, we used a reciprocal learning agent, a type of teachable agent that learns the content of the concept maps collaboratively created by learner pairs in real time. Based on this content, the agent generated new concept maps tailored to the learners’ input and provided continuous feedback. We used the ACT-R cognitive architecture  to imbue the knowledge generated by the teachable a gent with cognitive fidelity. Learners observed the agent’s feedback and were expected to deepen their understanding of the knowledge by reevaluating their explanations and refining their concept maps. Appropriate feedback from the teachable agent  enabled learners to reevaluate their concept maps. However, the types of teachable agent feedback that most effectively encourage learners to reevaluate their explanations and refin e their concept maps remain unclear.

This study’s purpose  is to examine whether the quality (error level) of knowledge generated by a teachable agent can encourage learners to engage in  reevaluation. This aspect was investigated by a controlled laboratory experiment using concept maps. The study considered the following hypotheses: H1: The learners who interact with virtual peer learners (teachable agents) perform better than those not using such support.

H2: Interaction with a virtual peer learner having erroneous knowledge facilitates more reflective activities and, thereby, enhanced learning performance compared to the interaction with a virtual learner with nonerroneous knowledge.

## Materials and methods

To examine the hypotheses presented in the Introduction, we conducted an experiment using CoCot(Collaborative Concept-map Tutor) Ver2. The experiment included three conditions for comparison: (1) an erroneous agent condition, in which a teachable agent generate d erroneous knowledge to encourage  learners to perform  further refinement; (2) a non-erroneous agent condition, in which the agent’s knowledge contain ed no errors; and (3) a control condition that did not have agent feedback.

## Participants

In our experiment, 100 undergraduate students  participated. The participants (hereafter, "learners") took part in the experiment in exchange for class credit. This s tudy was approved by the Ethics Committee of Ritsumeikan University (Kinugasa-Hito-2020-7). The learners were randomly assigned to one of the three conditions mentioned in the earlier section.

Experiment design Learners participated in an explanatory task that was designed to interpret and explain a person’s mental  model using the attribution theory framework, as indicated by Hayashi (2020). The authors analyzed a fictional scenario involving a student named Peter, who was anxious about starting a new school year. Learners were provided with a text on attribution theory outlin ing the three dimensions of causal attribution: (1) int ernal–external, (2) stable– unstable, and (3) controllable–uncontrollable. Whereas internal attributions link causes to factors within a person, external ones link them to factors outside the person. Stability refers to whether these causes remain consistent over time, and controllability indicates whether th ey can be managed by the individual or others. Lear ners read an adapted episode by Weinberger and Fischer (2006) describing Peter’s anxiety regarding his math teacher, stemming from past experiences, and were instructed  to apply these attributional dimensions to explain  Peter’s mental state  of anxiety. To structure their explanations, learners utilized text-based chats and concept maps, applying these theoretical concepts to clarify how Peter’s internal and external attributions, stability of causes, and level of control influenced his anxiety. This experimental setup encouraged learners to use their theoretical knowledge to analyze complex psychological phenomena. For example, a correct causal relationship of “Peter is feeling anxiety because he thinks that everyone in his family is not good at math and he has negative influenced from them” can be expressed as a concept map: anxiety(node)–external(link)–family(node).

## Experimental procedure

The experiment was conducted in a room with 60 networked personal computers (PCs). Learners were randomly assigned to experimental conditions and seated free ly at the PC terminals. They received instructions, practiced using a concept map, and read an episode designed t o teach attribution theory; they focused on explain ing behaviors based on causal factors, such as internal –external, stable –unstable, and controllable –uncontrollable attributions. Learners were instructed to create an  attribution theory–based explanation using a concept map and chat system. All nodes were instances of textual knowledge (anxiety, Peter, Teacher, Family, etc.), and links were theoretical knowledge(internal external, etc.).

A pretest assessed the  learners’ initial understanding of attribution theory  and required them to write freely on the topic. Subsequently, in the “single phase,” the learners independently created concept maps linking the events they had learned about, the maps being saved to a central server. In the “collaborative ph ase,” the learners worked in pairs and used a chat system to discuss and jointly construct a sh ared concept map based on their individual maps. This phase lasted 20 minutes and enabled the dyads to collaboratively apply the theory and refine their understanding through discussion.  Finally, a posttest identical to the pretest measured any improvement in their understanding of attribution theory. This design enabled researchers to assess individual and collaborative learning gains  and reveal the impact of concept mapping and collaborat ion on learners' comprehension of psychological concepts.

System This study examined a scenario in which learners created a collaborativ e concept map with a partner while engaging in explanatory activities through text-based chat. The system we develop, enables a teachable agent to participate as a semi-member, acting as a “virtual student” in the group.  This agent learns the content of the concept map created by learners and generates a new  concept map as a learning outcome, providing feedb ack based on the learn ers’ teaching. This agent assumes the role of a “naive student,”  generating either erroneous knowledge (erroneous agent condition) or accurate knowledge (non-erroneous agent condition) like an exemplary student. Equipped with a cognitive model of human l earners using a cognitive architecture, this agent is capable of knowledge reasoning.

Additionally, the system includes a facilitative agent that supports group progression and metacognitive activities, encouraging effective communication and smooth collaboration based on insights from earlier studies. This system comprises six main modules: (1) a server to manage map data and logging;  (2) a client module for user interaction; (3) a module for drawing concept maps; (3) a prompt-generating agent; (4) an agent linked to an LLM (GPT-3.5); (5) an agent interfaced with a cognitive architecture (ACT-R); and (6) a module that incorporates the cognitive architecture(ACT-R) to define the knowledge in the dyads' concept maps (nodes and links) and infer new knowledge using a rule-based model, thereby generating a concept map that differs from the learner's map. The system was d eveloped in C#, linked to ACT-R, written in LISP, and employed socket communication. The facilitation strategy in this system is based o n ESPA(Explanation Support by Pedagogical Agent) developed by Hayashi (2020). CoCot provides metacognitive facilitation prompts, includ ing those (1) clarifying learners’ goals, (2) reflecting on their reasoning,  and (3) planning effective communication strategie s (Hayashi, 2024). To mimic human-like knowledge generation, the system integrated ACT-R, a cognitive architecture recognized for its ability to simulate human knowle dge acquisition. In this study, an ACT-R-based model generated concept maps using learner-created maps as input. The ACT-R model did not replicate learners’ maps similar to other teachable agents (Biswas et al., 2016; Matsuda et al., 2015); instead, it used typical learner knowledge and prior knowledge to select and retrieve nodes and links from its own predefined knowledge base. This knowledge base was designed according to a student’s naive concept map knowledge aggregated from an earlier study (Hayashi, 2020). Moreover, the model adapts to learner input  and adjusts knowledge retrieval activation levels according to usage frequency. On viewing the concept maps created by the virtual peer agent, learners encounter ideas that are similar but distinct from their own, encouraging them to reflect on and reevaluate their work. Dependent variable Learning performance The pre-and posttests administered before and after the tas k were used as the dependent variables for assessing learning performance because they reflected learners' understanding of the concept. The depende nt variable was calculated as the difference between the pre-and posttest scores. Two coders evaluated the pre-and posttest responses on a 1–5 scale according to earlier coding criteria (Hayashi, 2020). Further, Krippendorff’s α coefficient was 0.857, and any disagreements were resolved through discussion. Concept map activities: Node and links To measure learners’ constructive activities during  task work, we analyzed the nodes and links in the ir concept maps throughout the task. Specifically, we examined the nodes and links to determine the extent of the generation and updating activities performed for each. This an alysis was based on server-side log data. The specific metrics were (1) node generation, (2) node update, (3) link generation, and (4) link update.

## Discussion

In this study, we highlighted how encountering diff erent representations during learning could benefit learning activities. We examined two hypotheses by creating a situation i n which learners compare d their concept maps with the maps generated by an agent with misconceptions (erroneous knowledge). First, we tested H1 by comparing two experimental conditions with teachable agents to a control condition by focusing on learning performance and the amount of concept map creation. Results revealed that both the conditions using the agent facilitated understanding activities more than the control condition, supporting H1. This finding demonstrates the utility of using t eachable agents in collaborative learning context s. As mentioned earlier, previous research on teachable a gents primarily focused on (1) individual learners and (2) restricted learning domains, such as in math ematics. In contrast, this study examined learner interaction  and clarified how the virtual peer learn ed from the interaction and provide d feedback as a quasi-member. Hence, it offers a new approach for the collaborative use of teachable agents in college students’ concept learning. Second, we examined H2 by comparing erroneous and n on-erroneous agent conditions. Although analyses revealed no direct difference in learning performance or concept map creation between the two, an assessment of learners’ performance levels showed that, in the erroneous agent condition, learners who acti vely updated nodes achieved enhanced learning performance. This relationship was not observed in the non-erroneous agent condition. Therefore, in the erroneous agent condition, the active revision of concept maps promote d learning performance, supporting H2. This outcome i ndicated that on viewing the “incorrect” concept maps generated by the erroneous agent, learners asked  themselves “Why did this happen?”; this prompted them to review and revise their own concept maps.

In contrast, the non-erroneous agent, which did not contain any errors, did not expose learners to such discrepancies, due to which they did not engage in any concept map revisions that would have enhanced their understanding. As discussed earlier, the knowledge generated by the agent functioned a s an erroneous example, providing learners with reevaluation opportunities (VanLehn, 1999). This finding offers new insights into the use of teachable agents in collaborative learning and suggests that integrating a virtual peer learner as a quasi-member and using a teachable agent with misconceptions can support learners' metacognition and constructive interactions within collaborative learning.

## Conclusions

This study investigated a method to promote mutual explanatory activities among learners in autonomous collaborative learning by using a virtual peer lear ner with teachable agent capabilities. It examined whether the quality (erroneous level) of knowledge generated by  the virtual learner, acting as a teachable agent, could encourage learners to reevaluate their self-explanations. The experiment compared an erroneous agent containing misconceptions with nonerroneous agents containing correct knowledge  and clarified whether the use of a teachable agent enhance d learning activities and whether learners were encouraged to scrutinize their own knowledge more c losely when the agent generated incorrect knowledge and, ultimately, resulted in improved learning performance. The study’s results demonstrated that using a teachable agent e ffectively enhanced learners’ understanding of learning activities. Furthermore, in the erroneous knowledge agent condition, active revision of concept maps significantly improved learning performance. This outcome suggest s that the incorrect concept maps generated by the erroneous knowledge agent caused learners to find the reason  for this outcome  and engage in activities to reevaluate and revise their own concept maps.  Moreover, newer AI-based methods such as interaction partners (agents) creating the concept maps would be depicted by an  AI-based avatar (Fink et al., 2024). Finally, further investigations such as sample size, cultural characteristics of the participants, the domain of learning, and the pedagogical approaches used (e.g., individual vs. collaborative learning) would strengthen the study’s validity. This study provides new insights into the effective  use of teachable agents in collaborative learning, specifically those regarding how they facilitate reflection and constr uctive interaction  among learners. These findings have valuable implications for the design of AI-enhanced Computer-Supported Collaborative Learning and other learning support systems.
