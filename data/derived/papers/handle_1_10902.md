# Students’ Systems Thinking with the Water Cycle Simulation

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study presents two middle school students’ development of systems thinking as they explored the water cycle using a virtual interactive ecosystem simulation.

## Background

The water cycle is usually students’ first introduction to matter cycles, laying a foundation for other complex systems. Despite being a common topic in science te xtbooks from fourth grade onwards, research reveals  many naïve conceptions among students that persist through higher education, such as failing to consider infiltration or runoff (Cardak, 2009; Shepardson et al., 2009). These difficulties arise because students fail to see the water cycle as a system. According to O’Connor & McDermott (1997) systems thinking involves understanding: (a) the parts of a system, (b) the connections among these parts,  and (c) the system as a whole. Ben-Zvi Assaraf and Orion

(2010) expanded this framework for the water cycle by breaking down (b) and (c) into more specific abilities.

The advent of technology has allowed students to actively interact with diagrams, change variables, and construct relationships, can support students’ systems thinking (e.g., Draper, 1993; Provost et al., 2023). However, existing water cycle virtual animations and games often  include static diagrams that allow minimal student interaction. To address this need, we designed a virtual interactive ecosystem simulation to fill in that gap aiming to challenge students’ naïve conceptions and develop their systems thinking. Specifically, we examined: (a) How may students’ systems thinking about the water cycle progress as they interact with a virtual interactive ecosystem simulation? (b) What components of our design were useful for students’ system thinking development? The design study We designed the Water Cycle simulation to illustrat e how five climactic factors affect the water cycle (https://acmes.online/noauth/Water_Cycle_MSU.html) (Figure 1). The user can change the relative humidity and air, mountain, land, and lake temperatures by dragg ing the sliders between values of 0-100% and 0-100 degrees Fahrenheit, respectively, and study the resulting changes in the quantity of water throughout the processes of the water cycle (precipitation, runoff, evaporation, condensation, a nd infiltration). To support their coordination of the different components of the water cycle we asked questions prompting students to identify the variables of the system, such as What is changing in the simulation, and their relationships, such as How does [variable]affect precipitation? and How are evaporation and precipitation related? We also designed an activity in Scratch where students were asked to program a path for a water m olecule through the water cycle  (Figure 1). The goal was to examine whether students’ perception of the water cycle was as an interconnected and unified system. Figure 1 The Water Cycle Simulation (left) and the Water Cycle Scratch Program (right) We examined video-recorded data from one pair of students, Danny and Stephan, that was observed and interviewed by a researcher as they participated in  a whole class design experiment (Cobb et al., 2003) that was conducted on Google Meet due to COVID-19. We analyzed the data using our adaptation of evidence of systems thinking that is presented in the third column of Table 1. When asked what was changing in the simulation during their initial exploration, students identified the independent and dependent variables in the system. As students continued to explore and were asked questions about the relationships between the variables and processes, they identified relationships between these variables. For example, when asked about the relationship between the lake temperature and evaporation, Danny described: “If you increase the lake temperature, the amount of evaporation and the speed of the cloud formation increases.” Students discussed the water cycle system as a whole in the Scratch activity. For example, Stephandescribed the route that he progra mmed his water molecule to take, which demonstrated understanding of both the differ ent pathways and the repeating cyclic nature of the system. In sum, a s they manipulated the variables and studied the change in the simulation, students  showed evidence of all the elements of systems thinking in Table 1 (Ben-Zvi Assaraf & Orion, 2010). As Stephan argued, it was valuable to be “able to move the variables. I learned a lot of good stuff from that.”  Additionally, targeted questioning helped students explore these different levels of s ystems thinking. We plan to further examine other students’ reasoning and how a teacher can use such guided questioning in a whole class setting.
