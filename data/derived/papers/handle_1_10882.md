# The Emotional Toll of Proleptic Imagining on the Job Market: When Possible Futures are Crushed

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Prolepsis is a cornerstone concept in the Learning Sciences that typically describes an expansive discursive move in which adults talk about young people’s futures, as framed by the past, to mediate activity in the present. This poster looks at prolepsis on the academic job market, expanding conceptions to include (1) perspectives of those whose futures are being constructed, (2) harmful outcomes when imagined futures are crushed, and (3) ethical concerns for interviewing job candidates.

Theoretical groundings: Prolepsis and imagining futures Prolepsis is a cornerstone concept in the Learning Sciences that describes a discursive move in which speakers talk about the future, as framed by the past, to me diate activity in the present. It is “the cultural mechanism that brings the end into the beginning” (Cole, 1996, p. 183). Scholars have observed this as cultural practice amongst parents and grandparents when talking with their (g rand)children about their futures and as a pedagogi cal move amongst educators when talking about and with their students as a means of working towards futures they propose as desirable for their students and communities. Al though there has been some critical work on prolept ic pedagogies (Sengupta-Irving et al., 2023), for the most part prolepsis and imagining futures for learners has been framed as a positive and generative pedagogical mov e, emphasizing the politically transformative poten tial of learning to turn imagined futures into reality (deL una, 2018). The trend in this work is that (1) it t akes an observational persp ective, looking at utterances made to (or with) youth and (2) positions proleptic talk as  a positive move. While I do not disagree with the wor k that has positioned prolepsis as an expansive for m of pedagogy, the purpose of this paper is twofold: (1) to consider the perspective(s) of the person(s) whose future(s) is being constructed proleptically in order to show  (2) the effort involved in imagining such futures and the potentially negative effects prolepsis can have whe n desired and imagined futures are c rushed. These ideas feel palpable to me and I use my experiences on the acad emic job market as a case study for thinking about the emotional side of prolepsis and the negative effects such imagining can bring. Proleptic talk and the job market To illustrate the potential downside of prolepsis, let’s relook at the opening vignette. In this story, I shared my perspective on what it feels like to be an early ca reer scholar on the job market attempting to get my  first tenure track job. In particular, I describe what it feels like to engage in self proleptic talk for a search committee audience to see “fit” and experience proleptic talk from potential-future colleagues during interviews and on-campus visits. Writing job application materials is a complex and draining process that entails an abrupt switch from writing humble conjectures as a doctoral student to  writing confident, affirmative descriptions of you rself as a professor and showing an unknown search  committee how you belong and would contribute to th eir insular community. This work is not easy. It has entailed learning a new genre of writing that was intimately tied to imagining myself in a place and position I had never been before, a position that I potentially co uld hold for the rest of my career and a place where I could develop  my research, where I would live, and where my daug hter might grow up. These have not been easy things to imagine or to bring into the present, and the more time I ’ve spent finding information to fill in the details of these  imaginings, the more I ’ve began to long for the reality of these imagined futures. And I  have not only writ ten and spoken proleptically to myself during the application process, but others frequently used proleptic talk when talking to me as a candidate or “finalist” during interviews. Frequent uses of “when you’re here,” are used to help sell a candidate on an institution at this point in the process. E ngaging in such proleptic writing, talking, and ima gining has not only been necessary to successfully sell myself as a good candidate for position s but also to genuinely consider a move to a new plac e since most offers come with short acceptance timelines. The de cision to take a tenure track job is not an easy on e. It usually entails moving to a new, unfamiliar place, in my ca se with a family, and co mmitting to at least five years until tenure with the expectation to stay post tenure (because isn’t that why you get tenure in the first place). It can feel like a marriage proposal, and while I am fully awar e that not all marriages last forever and neither d o all jobs, similar to divorce, leaving a position and institut ion can be messy and challenging, so the re is a weight to the commitment in taking a job (if I can even get an offer). The heaviness of this decision mixed with the short amount of time to make such a decision puts pressure on imagining a future well before an offer is on the table. And since more often than not offers have not materialized (for me and most of my peers in the past decade), these exciting futures crumble time and time again and with them our hope, dreams, and resilience. Ethical Wonderings: Recognizing the dignity of candidates As learning scientists, we have an ethical responsi bility to consider the dignity of all learners (Esp inoza et al., 2020), not just the youth that make up the majority  of our research participants, but our colleagues, peers, and collaborators as well. The ideas and concepts we develop cannot just be used to better understand youth learning but must extend to the learning environments we populate and influence as well. In particular, I want to challenge the Learning Sciences to begin to imagine and enact  more dignity affirming paths to assistant professo rdom. We know that the design of liberatory dreaming spaces framed by prolepsis are how we cult ivate this change, but in the field of the Learning Sciences, like in other spaces, it is those in power—committee members, tenured faculty, chairs, deans—that can most easily affect change.
