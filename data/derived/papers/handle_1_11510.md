# Conceptualizations of Creativity as Complex Systems in Learning

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Complex systems theory was used to explore creativity conceptualizations in learning. Network/cluster analyses of 200 participants’ definitions identified co-existing attractor states—distinct clusters of interconnected ideas. Assuming learning is inherently creative, findings revealed creativity conceptualizations as emergent systems, advocating for inclusive environments that recognize varied pathways. By transcending reductionist views of creativity as isolated traits, this systems-based perspective supports diverse creative potentials, informing designs for adaptive, culturally responsive learning communities aligned with their dynamic complexity.

## Introduction and theoretical framework

In the learning sciences, the way concepts are unde rstood—whether learning, education, or creativity —shapes educational practices, policies, and outcomes. Creativity is an inherent aspect of the nature and process of learning (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2022). While a robust body of research has explored vari ous aspects of creativity, less attention has been paid to how different conceptual izations of creativity emerge from complex systems of interdependent ideas, beliefs, and practices. This study maps the current dominant conceptualizations of creativity. This study is grounded in complex systems theory (T hurner et al., 2018; Bar-Yam, 2003), and the sub-theory of complex conceptual systems which argues that concep tualizations are distributed, socially-situated complex systems comprising numerous interconnec ted elements (ideas) that self-organize and give rise to emergent practices (Donaldson, 2025).

## Methods

In this study of conceptualizing creativity, participants were asked to define creativity in their own words without consulting dictionaries or search engines. The stud y involved 200 individuals, each providing a 3-minute audio recording of themselves responding to the prompt “W hat is creativity?” These individuals were hired on Amazon’s Mechanical Turk (MTurk) platform where the y were compensated. The number of participan ts was based solely on our budget, although we would have preferred a larger sample. We transcribed the audio  files, resulting in a corpus of 51,216 words. We then analyzed these responses by coding them in MAXQDA Analytics Pro according to three categorie s: non-metaphorical characterizations (such as originality  or innovation), metaphors, and individual values, resulting in 112 codes and 2,147 coded segments. Only one researcher  coded the data due to budgetary restraints, and therefore  it was not possible to conduct inter-rater reliability testing. However, both authors met regularly to discuss and negotiate the evolving codebook. The three categori es were determined based on prior studies using complex con ceptual systems theory (Donaldson, 2025). Non-metaphorical characterizations focus on observable, meas urable, and cognitive aspects of creativity, wherea s metaphors rely on figurative language to capture ab stract qualities, often evoking vivid imagery to co nvey the nature of creative processes. Individual values reflect personal beliefs, motivations, and p rinciples that shape creative expression, influencing the purpose, metho ds, and priorities in creative activities. We condu cted Pearson’s correlation analysis for all possible com binations of pairs of codes and exported the result s to Excel, generating a network map in UCINET and NETDRAW desc ribing the relationships between codes that were correlated at a confidence level of P < 0.01 for further analysis. We performed a Girvan-Newman analysis (Girvan & Newman, 2002) to detect distinct clusters, helpin g us identify and describe unique conceptualization s of creativity represented by related ideas. Finally, w e adjusted the node si zes to enhance visibility and refined the map layout to ensure that all nodes were clear and easy to read.

## Findings

We developed a network map to highlight key relatio nships, achieving a confidence level of p < 0.01. T he map utilized codes representing non-metaphorical characterizations of creativity, metap hors, and individual values.

Using Girvan-Newman cluster analysis, we identified six distinct clusters at Q = 0.553 (Figure 1).  Each cluster reflects a conceptualization in which ideas and opi nions closely align with one another.  One of these clusters, represented by black-upward triangles, was labeled “Expansive Thinking.”  This group perceives creativity as a process that transcends conventional limits, emphas izing imagination, curiosity, and perspective-taking. Creativity, in this context, is viewed not as an innate trait but as a skill requiring abstract thinking and overcoming challenges. It is not confined to select individuals; instead, it demands a conscious effort to "dig deeper" and think outside the box. The cluster represented by red circles, labeled “Social and Practical Utility,” highlights the social and functional dimensions of creativity. It emphasi zes communication, interpersonal interaction, and s erving others. In this view, creativity is regarded as practical, context-dependent, and often overlapping with intelligence and critical thinking. Additionally, this clust er acknowledges the enriching qualities that a crea tive environment can foster, enhancing collaboration and problem-solving. Figure 1 Complex Conceptual Systems Network Map of Conceptualizations of Creativity The "Exceptional and Unbounded" cluster, depicted by pink hourglasses, presents creativity as extraordinary and limitless. This perspective is characterized by quick thinking, a wide range of ideas, and the notion of being “one in a million.” It emphasizes  idea fluency and the belief that creativity has no limits, embodying the ability to go “above and beyond” conventional thinking and break free from standard constraints. The "Process and Method" cluster, represented by green diamonds, centers on creativity as both a process and a method involving  strategic thinking, flexibility, and originality. Here, creat ivity is seen not only as a tool but also as a qual ity that's challenging to quantify. This perspective acknowled ges that creativity often requires bend ing societal rules and constraints, moving at one’s own pace, and embracing individuality. The final cluster, represented by blue squares and named "Diverse and Fluid," offers a multifacete d view of creativity. In this cluster, creativity i s shaped by personal knowledge, a willingness to take risks, and th e courage to challenge societal norms. It’s seen as  a trait accessible to everyone, manifesting in various form s, including playfulness and artistic expression. T his cluster also emphasizes that creativity can be collaborative and unrestricted by constraints or stereotypes, such as gender stereotypes.

## Implications and conclusion

The findings from this study implications for both the design of learning environments and the broader  field of the learning sciences. Learning designers could con sider multiple, co-existing conceptualizations of creativity, reflecting complex systems prin ciples of emergence and non-linearity (Thurner et al., 2018; Bar-Yam, 2003). Understanding creativity as a complex, self-organizing system encourages us to move beyond redu ctionist paradigms that may inadvertently limit learners' ca pacity for creativity by focusing on isolated traits or narrowly defined “creative” activities (Sawyer, 1999; Glăveanu & Beghetto, 20 21). By applying complex systems theory to conceptualizations of creativity, we provide a t heoretical framework that can potentially shed ligh t on how different understandings of creativity emerge and p ersist within learning communities. The findings su ggest practical guidance for designing learning environment s that support multiple approaches to creativity, m oving beyond one-size-fits-all approaches to development of creative potential. This work tentatively opens new directions for research on creativity in l earning contexts, particularly around how different  conceptualizations might interact, evolve, and impact learning. Contri buting to a growing body of creativity literature c hallenging simplified notions of creativity (Sawyer & Henriksen, 2024), suggesting the need for more nuanced and inclusive approaches to understanding, facilitating, and eval uating creative expression in learning contexts.  Future work could explore how different conceptualizations manifest in specific learning contexts and how learning scientists might leverage them to create more inclusive, gener ative, and effective learning environments. Future research could seek to understand how conceptualizations mig ht transform through intentional design interventio ns, as well as ways in which they may influence learning outcomes across different domains and populations. This study was the first to use complex systems theory to inve stigate conceptualizations of creativity, and there fore there is a need for many more studies before we could make c laims regarding conceptualizations of creativity mo re broadly.
