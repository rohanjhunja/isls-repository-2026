# Examining Knowledge-Sharing Across Digital Modalities: Preliminary Insights from Goodreads and TikTok Book Reviews

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Digital content platforms have become key spaces for informal, self-directed learning. Understanding how knowledge takes form and evolve within these environments is crucial to understanding the nature of learning that unfolds. This study compares 34 book reviews from Goodreads, a text-based platform, and TikTok, a short-form video platform, to explore how distinct modalities influence knowledge-sharing. Preliminary findings suggest that Goodreads emphasizes evaluative reasoning, while TikTok highlights personal learning and growth.

## Introduction

Beyond merely tools for consuming information, digital content platforms have emerged as environments for the co-construction of knowledge. Learning within these spaces is often self-directed and incidental, unfolding through content exposure and user interaction. As content on these platforms increasingly shapes public discourse and individual understanding, it becomes critical to examine how ideas take form, evolve, and are received by users.

This research poster presents a preliminary study a imed at contributing to this area of investigation by examining how knowledge and interpretations are exp ressed within online book communities. Goodreads, launched in 2007, is a well-established platform for book discussions, offering a text-based, user-generated space where reviews can range from brief comments to detailed critiques. In  recent years, TikTok has also emerged as a significant hub for book discussions. Although a general-purpose social media platform, its algorithm-driven reach and short-form video format have attracted a g rowing community of readers engaging with book-related content with more than 40 million videos uploaded u sing the hashtag #BookTok (TikTok, 2024). The rise of TikTok as a space for book discussions, with its di stinct design and functionality compared to G oodreads, motivated this research. Specifically, this study e xplores the following questions: How do popular book reviews on Goodreads and TikTok compare? What, if anything, is omitted or amplified in TikTok reviews due to its shortform video format? In this study, knowledge-sharing is conceptualized as a socially situated pr ocess that contributes to collective knowledge construction—a process in which individuals create new understanding through interaction with others in shared environments. This process is emergent, mean ing that collective insights arise through the accumulation, juxtaposition, and synthesis of indiv idual contributions (Cress & Kimmerle, 2008). Platforms like Goodreads and TikTok enable users to contribute knowledge and perspectives that can be encountered, endorsed, challenged, or extended by others. Yet there is still much to learn about  how their distinct interaction structures shape the kinds of knowledge that are externalized, valued, and amplified. For the learning sciences community, a central question is, how might these differences influence the potential for epistemic engagement and collective sense-making over time ? This preliminary study is an initial step toward addressing such questions, u sing a comparative lens to examine how Goodreads and TikTok—two platforms with markedly different affordances— shape what and how readers share.

## Methodology

Using a qualitative content analysis, t his study compares user-generated reviews of four bestselling nonfiction books—Sapiens by Yuval Noah Harari, Atomic Habits by James Clear, The Psychology of Money  by Morgan Housel, and The Anxious Generation  by Jonathan Haidt —on Goodreads and TikTok. Nonfiction books were selected based on the assumption that they are typically read with a stronger intention of learning. Sampling A total of 34 publicly available reviews were analy zed: 5 Goodreads and 4 TikTok reviews of Sapiens, 5 Goodreads and 4 TikTok reviews of Atomic Habits, 4 Goodreads and 4 TikTok reviews of The Psychology of Money, 4 Goodreads and 4 TikTok reviews of The Anxious Generation. For the Goodreads reviews, the top English reviews were selected based on the platform ’s “Popular” sorting  filter. Half of the reviews were accompanied by a rating of 4 or 5 stars, the other half a mix of 1-, 2-, and 3-star reviews. On TikTok, a purposive sampling method was applied, selecting top results in English from searches formatted as “[title] #boo ktok” that provided sufficient content for analysis. The selected videos ranged in duration from 40 seconds to 4 minutes and 8 seconds and represented a mix of sentiments.  As a baseline of engagement, all Goodreads and TikTok reviews included at least 10 comments. This focus on popula r and engaged reviews was intentional, as such cont ent is more likely to influence the evolving content culture.

## Preliminary findings and research development

Based on the code presence, sampled reviews from Goodreads more prominently expressed the code “evaluating arguments or writing” compared to TikTok. Notably, the three TikTok reviews that exhibited the code were for The Anxious Generation. Conversely, sampled reviews from TikTok more freq uently expressed the code “highlighting learning or growth,” with at least one review for each book exemplifying this aspect. On Goodreads, the occurrence of this code was limited to a single title—Atomic Habits, a self-help book. These findings lead to the hypothesis that text-based environments may afford more elaborate and argument-centered contributions because cognitive effort is concentrated on the con tent of the ideas themselves. Sharing through videos, on the other hand, involves attention to multiple layers of expression, such as self-presentation, tone, and audiovisual storytelling. Additionally, the brevity inherent to short-form videos encourages affective, relational, and identity-driven expressions to quickly engage viewers. Further research will explore the generalizability of these trends and test th is hypothesis by incorporating a broader and more diverse datas et of book reviews across multiple genres and user demogr aphics. Subsequent mixed-methods analysis will also examine patterns of interaction  such as replies, comments, and remixes that may indicate whether and how externalized knowledge is taken up and built upon. This work aims to identify the digital affordances and cultures that support or constrain meaningful knowledge construction, ultimately informing the design of  better digitally mediated learning environments.
