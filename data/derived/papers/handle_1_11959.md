# Human-AI-human Collaboration: Learning about Climate Change in Virtual Reality

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
The aim of this study is to explore human-AI-human interactions in virtual reality and how AI characters can be designed for enhancing collaborative learning. The data analysis explores human-AI-human interactions and identifies how human-human and AI-human interactions fluctuate during a collaborative learning task about climate change. This study contributes to the research area of computer-supported collaborative learning and gives practical information about human-AI-human interaction possibilities in higher education contexts.

To succeed in today's society, learners must combine expertise and ideas, solve problems and create new knowledge in diverse collaborative settings. In the age of artificial intelligence (AI), this involves working not only with peers but also with multiple agents. The fast development of generative AI technologies has opened new opportunities for the use of virtual reality (VR) environments in educational contexts, including the use of AI-powered avatars or characters. These can serve as interactive learning companions, fostering knowledge construction and collaborative learning in simulated scenarios. Collaborative learning has proven to be an engaging pedagogical approach (Hesse et al., 2021; Näykki et al. 2021; Scoular & Care, 2020), and the use of AI chatbots in educational settings has been associated with better learning outcomes (Wu & Yu, 2024). The expansion of learning spaces highlights the need for research into human-AI-human interaction and the ways in which AI can support learning and interaction processes (Cress & Kimmerle, 2023; Tan et al., 2022). Although the use of VR environments for learning has gained interest in research and education, the focus of prior research has been more on the usability of VR applications and their effect on learning than on the actual learning process (Petersen et al., 2022; Radianti et al., 2020). Furthermore, less is known about the educational effectiveness of VR environments (McKerlich & Anderson, 2019), and the use of AI-agents for collaborative learning in VR environments.

Against this background, this pilot study focused on a collaborative learning task in which climate change served as the pedagogical context, and AI characters provided a digital storytelling element rather than acting solely as traditional pedagogical agents. Based on cognitive and sociocultural frameworks, such as motivation and situated-cognition theories, incorporating narrative elements in educational technologies can enhance students’ motivation and interest in the topic, and support engagement with multiple identities (Mawasi et al., 2020). In general, digital storytelling refers to the educational practice of telling stories involving the use of digital tools (Isaacs et al., 2024) and its added value lies in their potential for interactivity, creativity, critical thinking and immersion (Isaacs et al., 2024; Kuznetcova et al., 2023). The aim of this study is to explore human–AI–human interactions in a non-immersive VR environment, guided by the following research question: How do the students and the AI characters interact with each other? Further analysis will focus on how AI characters can be effectively designed to support collaborative learning.

## Research design, the AI-characters and the VR environment

The pilot study, conducted in October 2024 as part of an Introduction to Digital Technologies for Learning course for international master’s students in education sciences, explored human–AI–human interaction in a collaborative learning task. Thirteen students (five dyads and one triad) used the WondaVR environment on laptops to learn about climate change by interacting with AI characters through a chat interface. Data sources included video and screen recordings, AI character design prompts, and Wonda VR log data. A preliminary qualitative content analysis was conducted on the log data. The activity itself builds on an earlier virtual drama concept originally developed for the AltspaceVR platform, which has since been discontinued (see Nousiainen et al., 2023; Näykki et al., 2024).

In Wonda VR, the text generation model used for AI characters was gpt-4o-mini-2024-07-18, provided by OpenAI. The AI characters were assigned roles via written prompts, and their appearances were designed in Ready Player Me before being uploaded into Wonda VR. Each character had a different level of detail in their Michael, a climate activist, was created using online material such as interviews, but without personal details or a complete storyline. Diana, a climate scientist, was prompted solely based on her role and expertise, without narrative elements.

## Preliminary findings

The content analysis of the log data examined how human–AI–human interaction unfolded and how the balance between human–human and AI–human interactions shifted during the collaborative climate change task. In the first phase of analysis, the number of messages sent to each AI character was counted. Results showed that students interacted most frequently with Michael and least with Jane. Notably, dyad 3 was the most active overall: they engaged with all AI characters multiple times, unlike the other groups, who typically contacted each character only once (Figure 1).

Figure 1 Interaction Patterns of the Groups with AI Characters and Number of Sent Messages The content of the sent messages was analyzed in two phases. First, each message was coded by sender and type— for example, “S-question” for a question from a student and “AI-question” for a question posed by an AI character. Second, messages were categorized by theme, such as “backstory” (e.g., asking about the AI character’s personal history) and “content/climate” (when the topic focused on climate change). The analysis revealed differences in how students interacted with each AI character and how the characters responded: Jane was mostly asked about her personal experiences, with little focus on climate change content. Michael received questions about climate change, his backstory, and educational perspectives. Diana was primarily asked about climate change, with only a few messages referring to her backstory. Students’ approaches for conversations varied. Some personified the AI characters by using greetings and expressions of gratitude, while others raised ethical concerns about using AI to learn about environmental issues. Some even tested the AI by adopting roles (e.g., pretending to be a 12-year-old), revealing differences in the characters’ adaptability based on how detailed their design prompts were. Notably, only Jane improvised an explanation for her virtual surroundings, drawing directly from her backstory, when asked about her environment.

## Limitations and future work

The small sample size limits the generalizability of this study. Potential biases and limitations related to the human-AI interactions in VR will be addressed when further analyzing and reporting the findings. This pilot study provides early insights into human–AI–human interaction in a VR learning environment. As a practical implication, the study highlights the potential of such interactions in higher education settings. It contributes to the emerging field of AI-supported collaborative learning in VR by offering early evidence on how students engage with AI characters differently depending on character design and context. Future work will explore interaction processes further using log data and video recordings of student activity, with the aim of improving character and task design to better support collaborative learning. While this study was conducted in a nonimmersive VR setting, the goal is to redesign the learning task for immersive VR as well. As Davis et al. (2021) suggest, although an AI character’s persona may have limited influence in one-dimensional multimedia interactions, it may play a more significant role in immersive VR environments. Such environments more closely resemble human-to-human communication, potentially influencing learning outcomes and therefore requiring further exploration.
