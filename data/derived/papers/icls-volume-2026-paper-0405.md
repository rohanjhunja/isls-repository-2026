# A Common Language for Infrastucturing Maker Education: Mapping Stakeholder Challenges and Opportunities using a Five-Dimensional Framework

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
The promise of maker education is frequently constrained by entrenched educational infrastructures. Efforts to create sustainable change, a process known as infrastructuring, require a deep and shared understanding of systemic challenges and opportunities. However, discourse among stakeholders can be diffuse, hindering collective action. To address this, we propose a five-dimensional analytic framework —epistemological, pedagogical, social, material-technological, and organizational —as a tool for mapping the landscape of maker education. We convened a conference of diverse stakeholders and analyzed their roundtable discussions about the future of maker education. Preliminary findings reveal that stakeholders’ articulations of core problems and potential solutions align with the five dimensions of our framework, even without its explicit introduction. This suggests the framework's potential as a tool to help communities surface, structure, and formalize their grounded knowledge, fostering a common language vital for intentional infrastructuring work.

The integration of constructionist innovations into formal schooling has historically been a profound challenge, often thwarted by the inertia of established educational infrastructures (Papert, 1997). Maker education, a contemporary manifestation of this tradition, holds immense pedagogical potential by centering learning in acts of creation, collaboration, and tangible exploration (Blikstein, 2018). Yet, its implementation in schools often remains limited or superficial, curtailed by significant infrastru ctural barriers (Valente & Blikstein, 2019). The concept of infrastructuring  offers a powerful lens for examining how educational systems can be intentionally redesigned to support and sustain such innovations over the long term (Penuel, 2019). A vital step in any successful infrastructuring effort is the mapping of the existing landscape (Chen, 2024). This involves identifying the nuanced challenges and latent opportunities as perceived by the full range of actors within the ecosystem. Without this shared map, efforts can be misguided, and innovations can fail to take root (Authors, 2023). The central contention of this research is that such a mapping process can benefit from a structured approach to make the often-invisible aspects of infrastruc ture visible and actionable. We therefore explore how a multi-dimensional framework can bring coherence to stakeholder discourse, enabling a clearer understanding of the complex interplay of factors that shape the potential for maker education.

## Theoretical framework: A five-dimensional lens for infrastructuring

The learning sciences has seen significant progress in understanding how to intentionally build and sustain maker education. Scholars have illuminated this infrastructuring work from multiple angles. Groups have investigated how multi-stakeholder partnerships can foster change across entire regions (Santo et al., 2015), while others have demonstrated the critical role of teachers as local infrastructuring agents who adapt projects and alter their contexts (Hyden & Roque, 2024). On an even larger scale, initiatives like the Innokas network provide a model for creating systemic support across hundreds of schools (Korhonen, 2023). These examples, among many others, highlight a growing body of rich, context-specific knowledge about this work. Yet, a central challenge remains, one that addresses this year’s Annual Meeting’s theme of Partnering with Purpose. Intentional partnerships require a shared understanding and language, but the discourse surrounding infrastructural change is often fragmented, hindering stakeholders’ ability to translate disparate ideas into collective, actionable steps. To bring structure to these conversations, we build upon and extend analytical frameworks in this area (e.g., Riikonen et al., 2020) to propose a novel fram ework for maker education infrastructuring that includes five dimensions: epistemological, pedagogical, organizational, materialtechnological, and social. This approach allows us to analyze the infrastructural elements relevant to maker education and to what extent stakeholders are attending to them. Our goal is not to impose a top-down schema, but to use it as a tool to listen more carefully to the needs, assets, and ideas emerging from diverse local contexts, 2644 and to help bring people together into a shared problem space. We ask: What challenges and potential opportunities across the five infrastructural dimensions do diverse maker stakeholders identify? To investigate this question, we convened a one-day conference that brought together a diverse group of 37 stakeholders in maker education, including teachers, school leaders, informal educators, researchers, and policymakers. Participants were organized into multiple roundtable discussion groups, each with a heterogeneous mix of roles and backgrounds. These groups were tasked with discussing the primary challenges facing maker education and potential opportunities and solutions. Data were collected through audio recordings of all group conversations, supplemented by photographs of artifacts and notes produced by the participants. Our analysis employed a qualitative approach. After transcribing the discussions, we systematically coded the discourse, mapping statements about challenges and opportunities to our five-dimensional framework, while remaining open to themes that fell outside this initial structure.

## Preliminary findings

Our analysis reveals that the stakeholders' discourse naturally and consistently canvassed all five infrastructural dimensions. The framework proved highly effective in organizing their grassroots concerns and proposed solutions into a coherent map. Our preliminary analysis suggests a n alignment between the practitioners' grounded knowledge and the infrastructural dimensions. T heir conversations about real-world difficulties mapped onto the epistemological, pedagogical, social, material-technological, and organizational domains. This finding suggests the framework is not merely an abstract academic imposition but a structure that may reflect the lived realities of educators. This finding has potential implications for infrastructuring work. We do not suggest that this framework should be used in a top-down manner. Rather, we propose it as a tool for a process that begins by listening to and honoring community expertise. Subsequently, the framework can help stakeholders progressively formalize  their own ideas —to build a common language and see systemic connections between, for instance, a pedagogical challenge and an organizational policy.

As this poster represents an early stage of research, these ideas are tentative. By helping to structure stakeholder discourse, this mapping process shows promise as an important step toward building partnerships for sustainable change. Future research will extend this analysis to more stakeholder groups and investigate how these dimensions manifest in local contexts with active infrastructuring initiatives. Crucially, we will remain critical of the framework itself—recognizing it as one of many possible le nses—to ensure our analysis is not boxed in by a single perspective, even one found in scholarship. The goal is an evolving tool for the mapping stages of the infrastructuring process that helps bring diverse stakeholders together for this challenging work. 2645 Authors (2023).
