# Peer Feedback’s Role in Undergraduates’ Interdisciplinary Collaborative Learning Outcomes and Regulated Learning Patterns

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Interdisciplinary collaborative learning (ICL) is critical in 21st-century education, yet supporting disciplinary boundary crossing remains challenging. Peer feedback has the potential to help students cross boundaries by identifying the gaps between single-disciplinary and interdisciplinary approaches. However, the impact of peer feedback on ICL outcomes and regulated learning patterns is underexplored, despite the critical role of regulatory processes in students’ engagement in collaborative learning. This study employed a counterbalanced design with 52 undergraduates randomly assigned to two clusters experiencing conditions with and without peer feedback in reverse sequence. Group concept maps and verbal discussions from the 50-minute learning task were collected and analyzed. Results showed that groups performed significantly better with peer feedback in involving diverse perspectives, integrating knowledge, and reflecting on solution impacts and limitations. Additionally, students learning with peer feedback demonstrated an evaluation-engaged regulatory pattern, whereas they showed a planning-engaged regulatory pattern when peer feedback was not given. These findings suggest peer feedback’s potential for supporting ICL.

These Interdisciplinary collaborative learning is a learning process in which individuals bring different sets of skills and knowledge to the teams to achieve a common purpose (Miles & Rainbird, 2015; Su et al., 2026). Introducing ICL into academic and professional curricula helps prepare students to make connections between seemingly fragmented knowledge and to apply that knowledge to real-world problems (Holley, 2017). ICL  provides opportunities for students to learn.  However, students usually face challenges in utilizing diversity  and contradictions of perspectives to cross disciplinary boundaries (Repko, 2008). How to effectively  utilize diverse knowledge and perspectives to cross-disciplinary boundaries is a problem to address. Transformation has been identified as a mechanism that constitutes the learning potential of boundarycrossing (Akkerman & Bakker, 2011). Transformative learning theory suggests that activating disorienting dilemmas, a sense of uncertainty, discomfort, and frustration, can facilitate disciplinary boundary crossings  to achieve fresh and integrated understanding (Mezirow, 2000).  Research found that disorienting dilemmas can be activated when students encounter interdisciplinary approaches to address complex real-world probl ems that transcend single-discipline solutions that they are familiar with (Feng et al., 2023). Peer feedback, a communication process through which learners enter dialogues related to performance and standards (Liu & Carless, 2006), among interdisciplinary group members has the potential to  activate disorienting dilemmas (Roberts, 2013). Providing and receiving peer feedback helps students identify and reflect discrepancies between peers’ and their own current and desired performance levels (Hattie & Timperley, 2007). Existing research on peer feedback in ICL mainly focuses on students’ perceived role of peer feedback in the learning process (Wilmot & McKenna, 2018). More empirical research is needed to unpack how peer feedback impacts students’ ICL outcomes and processes.

Increasing research has investigated regulated learning  mechanisms used in collaborative learning to determine how both groups and individuals initiate, maintain, facilitate, and successfully navigate collaborative learning (Zheng et al., 2019). Two key concepts that describe these collaborative learning processes are self-regulated learning (SRL) and socially shared regulated learning (SSRL) (Hadwin et al., 2011).  SRL involves individuals deliberately modifying their cognitive processes, behaviors, motivational states, and emotional responses toward their learning objectives (Zimmerman, 2000). Meanwhile, groups engaged in SSRL use various social processes to regulate their collaborative activities (Volet et al., 2009). However, r esearch has traditionally examined SRL and SSRL separately instead of investigating them jointly (Järvelä et al., 2023), and little is known about how SRL and SSRL co-occur in learning tasks. Furthermore, existing research has primarily concentrated 70 on regulatory processes within single-discipline groups, offering minimal understanding of how interdisciplinary groups regulated their learning (O'Donnell et al., 2024). This study aims to explore whether and how peer feedback influences the regulated learning patterns and learning outcomes of undergraduate students engaged in ICL.

## Literature review

Interdisciplinary collaborative learning Shifting from monodisciplinary to interdisciplinary in higher education is motivated by future professional demands and the need to prepare students to deal with complex societal challenges that cannot be addressed by a single discipline (Schijf et al., 2023). Different from interdisciplinary learning, which can be achieved by individuals through integrating knowledge from two or more disciplines, ICL focuses not only on interdisciplinary learning but on collaborative learning in which interdisciplinary work can be associated with collaboration (Verster & vanden Berg, 2022). In ICL, team members from different disciplines contribute and integrate their knowledge to study a shared topic collaboratively (Claus & Wiese, 2019).  Being exposed to interdisciplinary problems, students not only need to learn about other disciplines but alsoneed to be able to integrate different views and positions.

Existing research on ICL  focuses on the enhancement of students’ 21 st-century skills, academic performance, and knowledge acquisition. Some studies sought to enhance learners’ 21st-century skills through ICL. For instance, Tan and Vicente (2019) designed innovative experiential learning by bringing in students from marketing management, computer science, and production design to train their collaboration and teamwork, creativity, critical thinking, and proble m-solving skills. In addition, some studies aim to facilitate students’ academic performance and knowledge acquisition by designing and implementing ICL. For example, Paredes-Velasco et al. (2023) designed a quasi-experiment to study the impact of interdisciplinary practices and collaboration between Computer Science students and Education students within realistic contexts in university education. The experimental group followed the interdisciplinary realistic project methodology, while the control group followed a more traditional methodology based on collaborative learning. Results supported the effectiveness of the ICL approach in enhancing knowledge acquisition when compared to a more traditional collaborative learning approach. Despite the increasing number of studies on ICL (Heikkinen & Räisänen, 2018), there is still a lack of empirical research on whether pedagogical support will affect students’ knowledge integration outcomes and the trajectories of ICL. This gap is particularly concerning given the growing importance of ICL in addressing complex real-world challenges. We need more evidence-based insights into how researchers and educators can effectively foster students’ ability to work across disciplinary boundaries and integrate diverse perspectives.

Peer feedback for interdisciplinary collaborative learning Achieving effective communication and knowledge integration across disciplines is  the main challenge of ICL identified in existing literature (Brassler & Dettmers, 2017).  P edagogical support is needed to address the challenges. Working with diverse and unfamiliar areas of expertise, cultures, habits, roles, and knowledge (Edmondson & Harvey, 2018) can be considered a process of crossing boundaries in ICL. Akkerman and Bakker

(2011) identified four mechanisms that constitute boundary-crossing, i.e., identification, coordination, reflection,

and transformation. Different from the other three mechanisms, which maintain existing boundaries and practices, transformation leads to fundamental changes that can alter or dissolve existing boundaries (Akkerman & Bakker, 2011).

Transformative learning theory suggests that new, integrated conceptual understanding is initiated by disorienting dilemmas (Mezirow, 2000). It happens when students encounter situations in which previous methods of coping or meaning-making become ineffective (Damianakis et al., 2020). During ICL, disorienting dilemmas emerge when students encounter unfamiliar interdisciplinary approaches to address complex, ill-structured realworld problems that transcend single-discipline solutions that they are familiar with (Feng et al., 2023). Based on this, a possible  scenario in which peer feedback could activate disorienting dilemmas  is when peer assessors evaluate the work of their peers using interdisciplinary criteria, limitations, and weaknesses derived from the gaps between discipline-specific and interdisciplinary approaches. The gap-based peer feedback process serves as a critical mechanism for students to discover gaps between their current performance and expected standards (Boud & Molloy, 2013). When providing feedback, students engage in problem detection, diagnosis, and s trategy development (Hayes et al., 1987), which helps them identify discrepancies between current and desired performance levels while offering suggestions for improvement (Hattie & Timperley, 2007; Huisman et al., 2018). 71 Drawing from existing research, empirical studies specifically examining the role of peer feedback in ICL contexts remain notably limited, despite its potential significance. Self-and socially shared regulated learning in interdisciplinary collaborative learning To achieve quality collaborative learning outcomes, groups need to be willing to engage in the co-construction of knowledge and regulate their strategies (Järvelä & Hadwin, 2013). However, previous studies have main ly examined regulated learning in mono-disciplinary groups with limited insights into the ICL  contexts (O’Donnell et al., 2024). Homogeneous  groups working on interdisciplinary projects (e.g., DiDonato, 2013) differ significantly from interdisciplinary groups. Students taking part in interdisciplinary group work are introduced tonew ways of thinking, communicating, and working in a way they would not usually do (Miles & Rainbird, 2015). Furthermore, until relatively recently, research on the regulation of learning has predominantly focused on self-regulated learning (SRL). SRL encompasses metacognitive, cognitive, and behavioral processes to achieve learning goals (Zimmerman, 2015), providing a lens into how individuals regulate their learning process. However, SRL does not adequately address regulation processes within collaborative learning where individuals’ learning can be regulated by others (O’Donnell et al., 2024).

When individual students work together, both self-and socially shared regulated learning contribute to productive collaboration (Hadwin et al., 2018). SRL happens when students actively manage their own learning using cognitive, metacognitive, and motivational strategies (Zimmerman, 201 3). These strategies are used to set personal learning goals, monitor progress, implement effective learning approaches, and reflect on outcomes to improve future learning. Zimmerman (201 3) characterized self-regulated learners as proactive individuals who effectively monitor and skillfully adjust their behaviors to achieve positive learning outcomes. SSRL goes beyond the individual to group settings, focusing on how group members collaboratively regulate their learning processes. In SSRL, group members work together to establish shared goals, monitor collaborative understanding, and change strategies to achieve learning outcomes effectively (Michalsky & Cohen, 2021). These collaborative regulatory processes enable teams to coordinate their interactions, overcome challenges, and maintain productive collaborative engagement. Research has traditionally examined individual regulatory processes (SRL) and group regulatory processes (SSRL) separately instead of investigating them jointly. For example, Lobczowski et al.

(2020) investigated SRL patterns in collaborative learning contexts and how individual regulation results in

learning outcomes.

The joint impact of SRL and SSRL on group performance, specifically within collaborative learning contexts, is still underexplored (Zheng et al., 2019). Understanding the co-occurrence of SRL and SSRL behaviors in ICL is crucial because the dynamic interaction between individual and shared regulation significantly influences learners ’ ability to collaborate effectively. R ecent research has started to address these challenges through innovative methodologies and analytical methods. Ordered Network Analysis (ONA) emerges as a particularly promising analysis method for investigating the temporal dynamics of SRL and SSRL in collaborative argumentation. ONA enables researchers to map and analyze sequential patterns and relationships within learning interactions, providing insight into how individual and collective regulation processes unfold over time  (Fan et al., 2023). In the context of collaborative learning, ONA visualizes and quantifies how learners interact, share resources, resolve conflicts, and regulate their learning processes both individually and collectively (Nguyen et al., 2022). This approach is especially  valuable for capturing the dynamic interplay between self-regulation and socially shared regulation in collaborative environments. Taken together, this study aims to investigate whether and how peer feedback  impacts undergraduate students’ regulated learning patterns and learning outcomes in ICL. Specifically, the research questions are: (1) What are the differences in the quality of the final products  between groups learning with and without peer feedback in interdisciplinary collaborative learning ? (2) How do regulated learning patterns  differ between undergraduate students learning with and without peer feedback?

## Participants and context

A counterbalanced design was implemented to investigate the effects of peer feedback on undergraduate students’ quality of interdisciplinary work and regulated learning patterns in ICL. In this study, the participatory groups were randomly assigned to Cluster 1 with 5 groups and Cluster 2 with 4 groups.  Groups in cluster 1 experienced the experiment condition (i.e., with peer feedback) before the control condition (i.e., without peer feedback). A total of 52 undergraduate students (29 males and 23 females) from different disciplinary backgrounds, such as computer science, engineering, business, and psychology, participated in this research. All participants participated in the research based on their own willingness. Most of them (N=42) were second-year students, and 72 some were in their fourth year (N=7), third year (N=2), and first year  (N=1). They enrolled in a compulsory ICL course at a university in Singapore. Students were randomly assigned to nine interdisciplinary groups of 5 or 6. In this course, students were required to analyze current real-world challenges regarding sustainable development goals (SDGs), such as “how can we coexist with macaques in Singapore to create a sustainable city?”. To ensure the comparability of the difficulty, the topics were all about SDGs and were selected by the course instructor, with four years of experience teaching this course.

This study took place in a multimedia-enabled tutorial classroom, where group members gathered around round tables equipped with electronic display screens. Students utilized their personal mobile devices while engaging in face-to-face discussions and simultaneously creating concept maps to visualize their group solutions through Miro (https://miro.com/). Miro is a collaborative learning platform, providing teams with a dynamic space to develop visual representations and foster collaborative ideation and problem-solving. The platform’s real-time collaboration features, including synchronous editing, enable seamless interaction among team members.

## Research design

Figure 1 shows the learning task design throughout the study sessions. First, individuals spent 15 minutes creating their own concept maps based on their disciplinary background knowledge. After that, students under the control condition took turns to share with other group members their ideas on the individual concept maps. By contrast, students under the experimental  condition were required to give feedback to another group member from a different discipline. The feedback providers and receivers were randomly assigned by the researchers. Based on a peer assessment model developed by Sluijsmans and Van Merrie  ̈nboer (2000), students need to (1) defin e assessment criteria—think about what is required; (2) judge the quality of a peer’s product; and (3) provide feedback for future learning —giv e constructive feedback about the work produced by a peer.  The assessment criteria were provided to guide assessors to identify gaps between their peers’ current discipline-bound approaches and the desired interdisciplinary performance that requires integrating multiple perspectives  (see Table 1). Adapted from Gan and Hattie’s (2014) guiding questions for peer feedback generation, students needed to answer the three questions to give peer feedback in 15 minutes: (1) What are the expectations of an interdisciplinary approach to address the problem, and what are the differences between a disciplinary approach and an interdisciplinary approach to solve the problem? (2) What didn't he/she do well, according to the interdisciplinary criteria and differences identified? Give explanations to support your feedback, and (3) How can he/she improve the current piece of work, based on the interdisciplinary criteria? Give explanations to support your feedback. Each group’s generated concept maps  (18 concept maps generated by nine groups in the two sessions)  were gathered as the final products of their ICL. In addition, group audio discussion data during each session was collected. To answer the first research question, we adopted a rubric for assessing the quality of group reports adapted from Mansilla et al. ’s (2009) criteria and Tripp and Shortlidge’s (2020) rubric  (Su et al., 2024; 2025) (https://drive.google.com/file/d/1ggzl_BWq5O5c2N6eJgSXZZF8lHic77sC/view?usp=sharing). The four dimensions of the rubric were purposefulness, disciplinary grounding, advancement through integration, and critical awareness, and there were two sub-dimensions under each dimension. Under purposefulness, students are assessed on whether they frame problems with an integrative approach and their level of stakeholder consideration. The disciplinary grounding dimension examines the range of disciplinary perspectives students incorporate and the ir ability to explain how different disciplines contribute to solutions. Advancement through integration dimension includes  the number of disciplines integrated and the depth of that integration, evaluating whether students merely list contributions or actively leverage and build upon different disciplinary methods to address problems effectively. Finally, c ritical awareness assesses whether students identify local and broader societal effects and can articulate potential barriers with resolutions. Two authors scored 4 concept maps (22% of the 18 concept maps) individually. The inter-rater reliability (Cronbach’s Alpha) of the four dimensions was.928,.882,.951, and.985, respectively. The two raters discussed the differences in scoring. The first author evaluated the remaining 14 maps. After assessing the quality of reports, a Shapiro-Wilk test was conducted to explore the normality of the data. Considering the data was normally distributed (p=.192 >.05), a paired-sample t-test was used to compare the differences in the quality of the ICL product between the two conditions.

To answer the second research question, the verbal discussion of each group was first segmented into utterances as the unit of analysis. Each utterance was coded using a coding scheme adapted from Zheng et al.

(2019) and vander Graaf et al. (2022) (see Table 2). The coding framework consists of  Orientation, Planning,

Monitoring, and Evaluation elements at both individual and group levels. A total of 764 utterances were coded. Two coders coded 145 utterances (about 19% of the dataset) independently, and th e inter-coder reliability was good (Cohen’s Kappa =.855). The remaining 619 utterances were coded by one of the two coders. Then, an ONA (www.epistemicnetwork.org, Tan et al., 2022) was conducted to model and visualize the temporal co-occurrence of the SRL and SSRL patterns demonstrated by students under the two conditions. The stanza size was four, which means that the co-occurrence of collaborative learning codes in every 4 utterances. Previous research found that the stanza size of four is an appropriate value for synchronous discourse-natured data (Ruis et al., 2019). When students engaged in peer feedback, they became aware of gaps in their own understanding and examined their peers’ approaches (Chen et al., 2023; Tan  et al., 2023). This awareness prompted students to develop more detailed explanations and justifications to address identified shortcomings and deepen their understanding (Chen et al., 2024). Consequently, groups with peer feedback demonstrated evidence-based reasoning and deep knowledge integration after knowing that their previous solutions based on a single discipline were insufficient to address a complex problem. Furthermore, peer feedback enabled groups to critically reflect on the social impact and limitations of their collaborative work. This reflective capacity likely stems from the nature of peer feedback as a collaborative activity between individuals, 76 which functions as reflective engagement influencing both feedback givers and receivers (Chen et al., 2024). Such reciprocal reflection promoted deeper critical thinking about the strengths and limitations of proposed interdisciplinary solutions.  Nevertheless, no significant difference was found in purposefulness, i.e., indicating and justifying the purpose of using interdisciplinary approaches to address the real-world problems between two conditions. Possibly, this finding reflect ed the complex nature of purposefulness, which challenges students to present their object of study in ways that are both viable in scope and clearly multidimensional  (Mansilla et al., 2009). Students may need more instructions or prompts, beyond what a single cycle of peer feedback can provide. Additionally, the network analysis revealed distinct regulatory patterns between experimental and control conditions. Groups receiving peer feedback demonstrated more frequent self-loops in SSE and SSM than groups learning without peer feedback and  exhibited transitions from monitoring to evaluation (SSM → SSE). The emphasis on self-transitions within SSE suggests that students are repeatedly engaged in evaluation processes, which is consistent with research by Panadero et al. (2019), who found that feedback interventions  enhance students’ evaluative judgment capabilities. Furthermore, the  sequential pattern (SSM → SSE) demonstrated by the experimental condition aligns with Zimmerman ’s (2000) cyclical model of regulated learning. The model positions that reflection occurs after efforts to learn and is intended to optimize a person’s reactions to his or her outcomes. In contrast, control groups exhibited more frequent self-loops in planning behaviors and demonstrated transitions from evaluation and planning back to monitoring (SSE → SSM), reflecting what Zimmerman (2013) describes as a reactive regulatory approach. These reactive groups  rely on post-performance self-reflections to learn. But this post hoc focus is hypothesized to diminish their learners’ effectiveness.

## Limitations and implications

This study has several limitations. First, while the study examines the impact of peer feedback on ICL  outcomes and regulation patterns, it does not investigate how the quality and nature of peer feedback influence these outcomes and patterns. Future studies can unpack whether and how variations in peer feedback quality and nature shape ICL. Second, the data sources are limited to verbal discussion and learning outcomes. Future research may include additional data sources and analysis methods to achieve a more comprehensive understanding of the learning process, such as  adding emotional and behavioral perspectives through physiological sensors, video can include larger participant samples and employ longitudinal designs to investigate the sustained effects of peer feedback on ICL over time.

This study implies that designing and implementing peer feedback among interdisciplinary group members can facilitate involving more disciplinary knowledge with strong knowledge integration and reflecting on the impact and limitations of interdisciplinary group work. Additionally, learning with peer feedback promotes proactive evaluation-oriented regulated learning. Educators and researchers can support feedback between students from different disciplines to enhance interdisciplinary task performance and regulated learning in ICL contexts.
