# Sharing About Risks on Social Media: Learning Environments to Address

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Youth navigate social media risks daily, yet their strategies to address them are often underrepresented. This study explores how youth identify and address these risks within learning environments. Three workshops were conducted with 32 8th-grade participants in Germany. Findings indicate that sharing facilitated risk identification and addressing. Creating learning environments that encourage sharing experiences can leverage youth cultural practices for online safety and could empower youth to enhance their social media experiences.

## Introduction

Social media platforms are ubiquitous in youth culture, offering opportunities for connection and self-expression (Osgerby, 2021). Youth develop skills in how to use social media in effective ways, for example, by engaging in complex practices similar to a lgorithms (i.e., sharing content, customizing feeds, or curating personal profiles; Keune & Hurtado, 2023). However, the algorithmic pe rsonalization of content and the potential for inte raction with a broad and sometimes anonymous audience expose young people to various risks. Research on social media risks focuses on its impact on mental health and we ll-being (Odgers & Jensen, 2020; Valkenburg, 2022) as well as risks related to privacy, false information, or anxiety (Eckert, S., & Metzger-Riftkin, 2010; Hinds et al., 2020; Lannette et al., 2018). Youth’s often years-long experience with social media can build strong awareness and strategies to navigate social media, however, youth  may be shamed by adults regarding their social med ia use, creating barriers to communicating about risks they encounter and the strategies to navigate them (Lanette et al., 2018; Odgers & Hayes, 2024). This underscores the n eed for designing learning environments that empowe r young people to share experiences and develop best practices for safe and responsible online engagemen t. This study explores design recommendations for social me dia-based learning environments, specifically focusing on how 13-14-year-olds can collaborate to share experiences and approaches to mitigating online risks. Drawing on a constructionist framework for learning  (Papert, 1993; Holbert et al., 2020), which emphasizes learning through the creation of persona lly meaningful and shareable artifacts, we investig ated how the creation of tangible maps representing social  media practices could bridge young people's persona l interests with abstract knowledge and skills related to onlin e safety. We conducted a series of workshops with 3 2 eighthgrade students tasked with identifying specific soc ial media risks in their exper ience and creating tangible maps that visually illustrated their approaches to the identified risks. This approach can empower youth to develop safer, more responsible, and more resilient strategies for engaging with social media.

## Methods

This study is part of a larger project investigatin g algorithms and youth algorithmic practices on soc ial media (Keune & Hurtado, 2023; Keune & Hurtado, 2024). The study was organized in two phases. In this paper, we focus on the second phase. The first phase conducte d social media walkthrough interviews that identifi ed risks youth experienced on social media, informing the design of the second phase of the study. In the second phase, a series of three workshops (each 55 minutes long, totaling 2.75 hours) were conducted throughout one month with 32 eighth-grade students at a school in southern Germany. Stu dents were divided into seven small groups and recorded as such. During the first session, participants learned how to document their social media content sharing practices through the creation of tangible maps of their social media content creation process. The second session focused on youth identifying  risks they face on social media and creating tangib le maps of how they would approach the identified risks. During the third ses sion, youth collected their results from the first two sessions to design social media features that accounted for or addressed the risks they previously discussed. The present study focuses on the second session of the workshops. Thr ee main data sources were collected: (1) video recordings of the session of seven participant groups (6.4 hours of video data), (2) video recorded closeups during conversational interventions in each participant gr oup during the sessions (68 minutes of video data),  and (3) pictures of the creation process of tangible maps (65 pictures). To answer the research question, we conducted an iterative and thematic analysis (Morgan & Nica, 2020) of the worksho p session recordings and transcriptions to identify the specific social media risks youth disc ussed and visualized and the strategies they shared  to mitigate the risks. We also conducted artifact analysis (Trăușan-Matu & Slotta, 2021) of the tangible maps to identify how youth visualize, trace, and map social media risks, possible approaches to the risks, possible consequences to their approaches, and how social media platforms and features are connected.

## Findings

This study aimed to design learning environments fo r young people to share their experiences with soci al media risks and to share their approaches to address them. Youth visualized their social media practices thr ough workshops, where engaging in discussions about their social media practices seemed to happen for the first time. The collaborative production of tangible maps to id entify and address risks supported small-group discussions around youth interests. Surprisingly, some groups d iscussed what counted as social media and agreed on  which applications counted, such as WhatsApp or Snapchat. This suggested the participants did not often discuss social media applications and what social media is. Moreov er, the open discussions about the group members' s ocial media experiences legitimized their social media histories and experiences towards safely navigating online. Here, we present excerpts from two groups and their tangi ble maps during the second session to showcase how participants shared experiences about risks and mapped possible approaches to those risks. One prevalent risk discussed in various groups was strangers contacting them online. This included scam texts (SMS) or WhatsApp messages such as “Hi mom, I lost my phone. Please delete the old number and add this as the new one” or messages from strangers on Instagram or Snapchat. Here, we showcase one group’s approach. This group comprised six participants (five boys and one girl; self-identified). They started creating their tangible map by adding post-it notes to three categories they placed: (1) what is creepy on social media, (2) how it happened, and (3) did you warn anyone?

The group created a tangible map which describe d their experiences of something they categorize as creepy on social media. Specifically, they mention that “strangers contact them,” “random people calling,” and “everyone seeing what you are doing.” The group also developed reasons for these risks, visualizing that they can happen by “ mistyping a number ” or “ sharing your details with someone else.” They also visualized strategies, like “tell a trusted adult” and “tell my parents about it, ” and described scenarios of what happened when the y told others or not, visualizing that “ not warning others can get them in danger,”  that telling others “ could help solve the issue,” and that it could “ stop the issue [from] happening again. ” This example showcases how youth can identify specific risks and strategies to addre ss them by sharing them with their peers within sch ool-based learning environments. They connected the tangible map to the social media platforms they identified, visualizing that these risks are present in all the social media platforms they included. Overall, the workshop provided a learning environme nt for participants to share various risks they experienced on social media and share approaches to  these risks. The tangible maps enabled visualizati ons of risks, how they happened, and possible ways to addr ess them. Moreover, participants visualized challenges in asking for help from adults while recognizing the n egative implications of not doing so. This points t o the need to support environments where young people can share their experiences to receive support.

## Discussion

This research could help leverage social media and similar youth spaces as conducive to a wide range of learning opportunities to highlight youth’s experience with social media. Youth can encounter risks online, and  creating learning environments for them to share experiences and best practices within educ ational settings can help address some of the stigma about social media while  supporting youth in staying safe online. The findi ngs presented show how designing a learning environment  for youth to discuss their experiences also fosters discussions about best practices and learning about  how others’ experiences can help them. Designing l earning environments that leverage youth interests as perso nally meaningful can position youth as capable and provide opportunities for them to honor their experiences to sha re with their peers. Exploring how the tangible map ping of social media experiences can help young people i dentify risks online and develop practices for the identified risks. Moreover, breaching the gap young people feel, like one of the groups mentioned, between adults and them when encountering risks is necessary to support young people with their interests while staying safe online.
