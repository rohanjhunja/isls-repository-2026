# Deconstructing the Logic of Schooling: Children’s Practices in In-School Educational Support Spaces in Japan

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study explores how children who find it difficult to attend their regular classes— but are not classified as long-term absentees—spend time in spaces that allow for moments of pause. Focusing on an in-school educational support center in an urban public elementary school, the research combines text mining of 198 pages of fieldnotes with case study analysis. The results reveal that children frequently spend time in a carpet space, creating home-like conditions that allow for moments of pause within the flow of formal learning. These practices temporarily suspend the dominant “logic of education,” giving rise to moments of “non-doing” that restore emotional balance and readiness to learn.

Children's behavior in school changes depending on how the school environment is organized. It has been pointed out that the educational environment also influences children's learning attitudes, and that a comfortable classroom environment can lead to changes in their motivation and academic achievement (Holley & Steiner, 2005; Park & Choi, 2014). The design of such a classroom environment is also linked to children's attendance and overall wellbeing. It is emphasized that not all children interpret space in the same way, and that in environments that ensure flexibility and choice, it is important to create conditions that provide appropriate stimulation without distracting from children’s focus (Barrett & Zhang, 2009). While it is essential to consider the impact of the design of learning and school environments on children, relatively little attention has been paid to where children actually spend time beyond the regular classroom and what they tend to do in these spaces.  This study therefore focuses on an in-school educational support center—an institutional space designed to provide temporary respite —and examines patterns in children’s ways of spending time through text mining of fieldnotes. Contexts and issues Currently, Japan is facing a rapid increase in the number of children experiencing school non-attendance (children who do not attend school regularly). This phenomenon highlights the urgent need to reconsider the traditional model of modern education, which is characterized by mass education and homogeneous classroom environments. According to the Ministry of Education, Culture, Sports, Science and Technology in Japan, the number of children experiencing school non-attendance in elementary and junior high schools in 2024 increased from the previous year, reaching a total of 3 53,970. Here, " school non-attendance" refers to children who have been absent from school for 30 or more days a year, excluding cases of illness or economic hardship. Many of these children report feeling "unmotivated toward school life," "experiencing anxiety," or being "sensitive about friendships." This indicates the need to create a school environment where children can feel secure and comfortable about attending school. This situation also raises questions from the perspective of children who continue to attend school, particularly regarding how they spend time within schools and engage with its everyday practices. However, in Japan's current schools, classrooms often maintain an asymmetrical power relationship where teachers are positioned as the authority who teach, and children are seen as passive recipients of learning. Although the national curriculum emphasizes "active and interactive learning," aiming for exploration driven by the children rather than a teacher-centered, knowledge-transmission approach, in practice classroom environments are typically structured in ways that reinforce hierarchical relations. As a result, the classroom experience is often confined to a homogeneous space with no variation or flow (Inagaki & Sato, 1996). In such environments, as Nihei (2018) indicates, the logic of education often emphasizes "making students capable of performing tasks"— a logic that may hinder the cultivation of "logic of inaction"  (Ibid.), where children are allowed to simply "be" without constant pressure to perform.  As a result, children’s bodies are continuously controlled to focus on learning and acquiring new knowledge.

Yet, children's lived experiences are not solely constructed around the act of "learning." For children, the acts of being educated, learning, and playing are inextricably intertwined. Olsson (2023) argues that educators aligning their timing with the rhythms of children and adapting to these rhythms is one of the conditions for fostering children's potential freedom. To avoid suppressing the subtle initiatives of children, it is essential to wait, be patient, and hesitate (Ibid.). Therefore, when thinking about the future of school education, we should not focus on forcing children to engage in learning. Instead, we need to create conditions that encourage children to want to learn naturally. This involves providing "enabling constraints" (Manning, 2016) that allow children to feel 1572 secure and have the freedom to explore and spend time in ways that foster their curiosity.

## Research question and framework

This study focuses on how children tend to spend time across different spaces within schools, particularly those that allow for moments of pause and non-doing. It aims to explore how children—particularly those who find it difficult to attend regular classes due to developmental characteristics or other reasons —spend time across different spaces within schools.  While building on Nakano (2025), which examined the fluidity of object arrangements and children’s autonomous spatial configurations, this study extends that perspective by focusing on patterns in how children tend to spend time across different spaces and the practices that emerge within them. Through this exploration, this study will discuss how schools can be structured to provide a sense of security for a wider range of children, including those who struggle to attend school.

## Participants

The fieldwork for this study was conducted between June 202X and March 202X+1. The research took the form of participant observation, carried out approximately once a week at an in-school educational support center (hereafter referred to as “Class X”) established in a public elementary school located in an urban area  in Japan. During this fieldwork, the author engaged in conversations and learning support activities with the children attending Class X, as well as with support staff, teachers, and the school  nurse. Class X, like ordinary classrooms in Japan, is equipped with a blackboard, desks, and chairs (see 1 in Figure 1). In addition, there is a space arranged with face-to-face desks and chairs that facilitate peer communication (see 2 in Figure 1), a the carpet space where children can relax or lie down for rest (see 3 in Figure 1). The carpet space, in particular, is stocked with books, comic books, craft materials, and card games, providing a space where children can temporarily step outside the logic of schooling and spend time in ways that allow for moments of pause. The furniture in Class X, such as desks and chairs, is movable  (Nakano, 2025). It should be noted that Class X is not a special support classroom for children with emotional, developmental, or intellectual disabilities; separate facilities exist for such specialized support. Rather, Class X is open to any child who feels the need for respite.

Prior to conducting the fieldwork, the author obtained permission from the school principal, who served as the administrator, and consent from the lead support staff member responsible for the site. Following the completion of each fieldwork session, detai led fieldnotes were compiled as soon as possible, following the procedures outlined by Emerson et al. (1995), focusing primarily on patterns in how the attending children spent their time in Class X. In total, 32 sessions of fieldwork were conducted, and 198 pages of fieldnotes (equivalent to 189,083 Japanese characters) were collected and used as the data for analysis. Analytical procedures To address the research question of this study, a two-stage analytical process was employed. First stage: Text mining was conducted on the 198 pages of fieldnotes using Tiny Text Miner β version (TTM), developed by Naohiro Matsumura and Asako Miura. The purpose of this analysis was to identify patterns in how children spent time in and around the in-school educational support center by examining the equipment, objects, and spatial elements across these environments. Therefore, verbs, adjectives, and adverbs were excluded, and only nouns were analyzed. Pronouns and specific personal references such as “I,” “you,” “this,” and “teacher” were also omitted, as they were not directly related to the children’s modes of engagement with the classroom environment. Instead, nouns referring to objects, spaces, and facilities used by children were extracted. The top fourteen extracted words were then presented to identify tendencies in how children spent their time in and around the center.

Second stage: Based on the findings from the text mining, the analysis focused on the most frequently observed modes of spending time in Class X. Following Yin (2017), a case study approach was adopted. In selecting cases for the case study, the following criteria were established based on the research question, with the premise of relativizing the "logic of education" (Nihei, 2018) that is strongly present within the school. The selection criteria were as follows: 1) Situations where children are engaging in some form of activity within and around Class X, and 2) Situations where children are engaged in non-learning activities, including moments of pause or non-doing. At the same time, they moved back and forth between other spaces within the school —such as the gymnasium, library corner, balcony, and principal’s office —indicating that their daily school life involved a circulation among multiple environments. It was also observed that the children often spent time near the entrance or hallway, where they could observe both Class X and their homeroom classes. In doing so, they not only produced a “home-like” atmosphere 1574 but also enacted forms of bodily relaxation that are typically excluded from schooling, which is governed by the logic of education—that is, the logic of “enabling what cannot yet be done.” These behaviors, including the case presented in Table 2,  represented the insertion of an entirely new mode of activity—doing nothing—within the intervals of formal learning. Here, children were neither engaged in learning nor acting toward any explicit goal; rather, they were creating a space in which their mere presence was sustained. In this moment, the dominant logic of education, which demands constant progress and productivity, was suspended, and what Nihei (2018) calls a logic of inaction  was temporarily guarantee d within the school. Such a space of non-doing enabled children to regain the psychological leeway necessary for self-regulation. The findings of this study indicate that non-doing should not be understood merely as rest or disengagement from school life. Rather, it constitutes a process through which children cultivate the energy and motivation necessary to re-engage with learning in a secure and comfortable manner. In school settings where learning is strongly structured by curriculum-based expectations, the use of text mining of fieldnotes to identify the spaces where children tend to spend time provides important insights into the characteristics of environments in which children feel at ease. Such understanding is essential not only for designing sustainable and inclusive school environments that accommodate children who find it difficult to attend regular classes, but also for reconsidering the rhythms through which children engage with learning. While this study presented a case based on patterns identified through text mining of fieldnotes, it has not fully captured how children concretely move across different spaces within the school building in their everyday practices. Future research should therefore incorporate analyses of children’s movement trajectories within schools.
