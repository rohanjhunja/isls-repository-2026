# The Effects of Technology-enhanced Embodied Learning Intervention for Mathematics in K-12 Classrooms

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This research explores the effects of a mobile device-based embodied learning intervention that leverages computer vision techniques, on fourth graders' understanding, attitudes, and engagement in geometry learning. We found significant improvements in students’ understanding between pre-and post-tests, whereas their attitudes remained unchanged. A multiple regression on log data showed that time spent on making-angle activities predicts students’ cognitive learning gain. These findings highlight the potential of applying mobile device-based embodied learning in classroom settings.

## Introduction

The theory of embodied cognition suggests that huma n thinking processes are not just confined to the b rain, but extend through our bodies and bodily interactions (Wilson & Golonka, 2013). Recent technological developments, such as motion-detecting technologies and mobile devices, have provided new opportunities to design technologyenhanced embodied learning interventions, particularly in mathematics education (Alberto et  al., 2022). While previous studies have shown the positive effects of such interventions on students’ learning, the specific mechanisms of embodied learning engagement during t he process and their effects on learning gains rema in largely unexplored (Nathan & Walkington, 2017). Fur thermore, a significant portion of research has bee n conducted in laboratory settings (Georgiou & Ioannou, 2019), which leaves a significant gap in our understanding of how such technology-enhanced embodied learning interventions can be integrated into K-12 classroom contexts. To address this gap, the current study aims to inve stigate the effects of the  mobile device-based embodied learning intervention designed for geometry education in 4th-grade classrooms. We sought to answer two research questions: ● RQ1. Does the mobile device-based embodied learning intervention make changes in 4th-grade students’ geometry understanding test scores and attitude towards geometry? ● RQ2. How do 4th graders engage in the mobile device-based embodied learning intervention?

## Methods

A mobile device-based embodied learning intervention This study used a mobile device-based application, developed by the authors  using Unity3D and Google Mediapipe—a computer vision-based gesture-tracking algorithm that directly captures  hand movements and gestures using mobile device cameras without relyin g on additional external devices. This application aimed to facilitate the learning of  geometric concepts (i.e., vertices, angles, diagona ls, and sides) on two-dimensional shapes (e.g., pentagons), by solving concept-related problems through their hand gestures.  The application, employing machine-learning-enhanced computer vision techniques, recognizes stu dents’ body movements and hand gestures in real-time without the need for external devices. Three hand gestures were implemented in the application: lining (i.e., tracing the sides or diagonals on shapes with an index finger), picking (i.e., emulating the action of picking vertices with a thumb and index f inger), and making-angles (i.e., connecting their wrists or middle fingers to form shapes like 'A' or 'V'). The se hand gestures were designed to enhance students’ understanding of geometric concepts and promote embodied ways of mathematical meaning-making regarding sides, diagonals, vertices, and angles (Na, 2022).

## Data collection and analysis

This study adopted a quasi-experimental design, employing a one-group pretest-posttest design. The study involved 38 fourth-grade students from an elementary school in Seoul, South Korea, engaging in a 40-minute session within their classrooms. Utilizing the mobi le application, students individually explored and solved problems related to geometric concepts on two-dimensional shapes (see Figure 1). Before and after  the intervention, pre and post-tests were administered to explore its impact on co gnitive and affective learning outcomes. A geometry understanding test instrument consisting o f 16 items developed by researchers and an attitude toward geometry questionnaire developed by  Utley (2007) were used. In addition to pre-and post-test, comprehensive data logging was integrated to trace students’ body and hand gestures. One-sample t-tests, log data analysis, and a multiple regression were conducted to address RQ1 and RQ2. ## Results

RQ1. Impacts of a mobile device-based embodied learning intervention After excluding two students due to in complete instruments or dropouts during the intervention, the analysis involved 36 students. There was a significant difference in geometric understanding test scores (t = 2.62, p =.013) between the pre-test (M = 12.11, SD = 2. 56) and post-test (M = 13. 28, SD = 1.43). However, there was no significant difference in students' attitudes towar ds geometry (t = 1.54, p =.13) between the pre-test (M = 3.29, SD =.36) and post-test (M = 3.37, SD =.47).

RQ2. Students’ engagement in mobile device-based embodied learning intervention On average, students engaged in the mobile device-based embodied learning intervention for 1,955 seconds (SD = 546.2). Predominantly, students dedicated time to making-angles activities (M = 952.6, SD = 623.0), followed by picking vertices activities (M = 564.4, SD = 464.3), lining sides (M = 381.3, SD = 349.4), and lining diagonals (M = 147.8, SD = 162.4). A multiple regression was conducted to predict students’ cognitive learning gain (i.e., a score difference between post and pre-test) from the time spent engaging in four differen t learning activities through hand gestures. Among time spent on four learning activities as predictors (F(4, 31) = 3.149, p =.028, R2 = 0.29), only time spent on the making-angle activity was a significant predictor of stude nts’ cognitive learning gains (t = 2.373, p =.024).

## Discussions

The significant improvement in students' geometry u nderstanding underscores the effectiveness of the mobile device-based embodied learning intervention in classroom settings. This finding aligns with exi sting literature, suggesting that body movements and hand gestures can positively impact students’ cognitive processes in learning (e.g., Nathan & Walkington, 2017). Interestingly, our analysis revealed that among the four embodied geometric activities, only time spent on making-angle activities significantly predicts students’ cognitive learning gain. This implies that not all gestures are equally effective in enha ncing learning in specific learning contexts. Fu ture research should further explore how to design technology-enhanced embodied learning that can support a diverse range of students with different learning needs and how to integrate it into classroom settings.
