# Beyond “Across Time and Settings”: Situating Teacher Learning in the Multiple Rhythms of Teacher Learning Ecologies

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In this conceptual paper, we consider how the multiple rhythms of teacher learning ecologies shape the ways teachers experience change, portraying a framework for understanding teacher professional learning with its multiscale complexity. In earlier work, we used the idea of adaptive cycles (Gunderson & Holling, 2002) to conceptualize the temporal dimension of teacher learning. We take that work further by extending our prior single-adaptive-cycle lens into a multiscale view (sometimes called Panarchy). We illustrate this idea with three studies: experienced teachers in video-based professional development, pre-service teachers’ learning between university settings, and teachers in provisional schools for displaced students. We show how teacher learning in all cases was influenced by interwoven rhythms. We argue that teacher educators and researchers can better develop their responsiveness to teacher learning when they pay attention to these multilayered temporalities, beyond more typical linear models or additive notions of learning across time and contexts. In our quest, we would like to discover ways to integrate and extend existing theory to achieve a requisite level of simplicity, just complex enough to capture and explain the behaviors we see… (Gunderson & Holling, 2002, p. 19) Objective This conceptual paper extends our ongoing efforts to better understand how teacher learning is situated in the multiple rhythms and settings of teachers’ learning ecologies, how to describe with just enough complexity processes of teachers’ learning, and h ow teacher educators can support different forms and rhythms of learning in responsive ways. Indeed, scholars remind us of the importance of enriching our temporal imagination and our educational theories to better understand rhythmic influences from an educational philosophy perspective (Alhadeff-Jones, 2016; Facer, 2023; Mikulan & Sinclair, 2023), from an interaction analysis perspective (Erickson, 2004), from indigenous perspectives (Fish & Syed, 2018), and environmental ecology (Gunderson & Holling, 2002), just to name a few. In a recent article in the Journal of the Learning Sciences (Ehrenfeld & Stengel, 2025) we suggested possible guidelines for capturing what we titled “the temporal dimension of teacher learning.” That work built on the Adaptive Cycles Framework (Gunderson & Holling, 2002) from the discipline of environmental ecology, to suggest four cyclic phases of change: growth, conservation, release (problematization), and reorganization. We then synthesized Gunderson and Holling’s framework with sociocultural, ecological, and complexity theories of (teacher) learning, each theoretical lens contributing a crucial aspect for those of us striving to see teaching and teacher learning as highly contextual practices. Sociocultural research on teacher l earning informs our approach by privileging contexts and conversations, and positioning teachers as agentic learners and sensemakers (e.g., Horn & Garner, 2022; Lefstein & Snell, 2013; Vedder-Weiss et al., 2018); Ecological perspectives guide us to see teacher learning as emerging from the interconnectedness of experiences and contexts within their learning ecologies (e.g., Barron, 2006; Bronfenbrenner, 1979; Ehrenfeld, 2022; Hecht & Crowley, 2020; Louie & Zhan, 2022); And complexity scholars push us to see teaching and teacher learning as complex adaptive systems, rather than linear, complicated, or simple ones (e.g., Cochran-Smith et al., 2014; Marco et al., 2025; Opfer & Pedder, 2011). Within such complex systems, outcomes are unpredictable yet are often patterned (Cochran-Smith et al., 2014; Opfer & Pedder, 2011). The Adaptive Cycles Framework helped us conceptualize such patterns of teacher learning through its four cyclic phases of release, reorganization, growth, and conservation (see Figure 1). Then, we illustrate the new multiscale lens with examples from three published empirical studies from three very different contexts for teacher learning we conducted in the last few years: (1) a study of video-based professional development for experienced in-service teachers (Ehrenfeld & Stengel, 2025); (2) a study of pre-service teachers’ learning across the School of Education and their Department of Mathematics (Ehrenfeld & Brady, 2025); and (3) a study of teachers in provisional schools for displaced students (Marco et al., 2025). In the first two examples, we originally conducted and published the single-layer adaptive cycle analysis, and here we aim to illustrate what moving to the multiscale Panarchy affords. In the third example, we did not originally use the adaptive cycles for analysis, but we do it now as a secondary analysis to further illustrate its usefulness in the study of crisis education and teacher autonomy. Finally, we conclude with a discussion of theoretical and practical implications for researchers’ and teacher educators’ responsiveness to teacher learning ecologies. Our goal in this paper is to explore and illustrate how the multiscale adaptive cycles framework (Panarchy) can be adapted and used to explain professional learning within the multiple rhythms of teachers’ learning ecologies. Conceptual framework: multiscale adaptive cycles (“Panarchy”) As an analogy for our argument regarding temporalities, consider our professional lives as educational researchers, and the way they are existentially influenced by the different rhythms of our work: our academic teaching ecologies might have certain temporalities of semesters, relationship building, assignments, teaching old or new courses, and perhaps sometimes even reshaping the whole program of studies; our research publications have other temporalities that include cycles of grant writing, data collection and analysis, manuscript writing, and so on; and our research student mentoring has its own temporalities of recruitment, shared work, community building, and graduation ceremonies. The question for professional learning then becomes, What difference d oes it make, or what difference can it make, when one enters a professional learning cycle, to have their different learning ecologies at different phases of change? Our starting point for answering such questions echoes a call by the psychosociologist and philosopher of education Alhadeff-Jones (2016) in his book Time and the Rhythms of Emancipatory Education, Rethinking the temporal complexity of self and society: If the idea of time suggests some form of singularity, temporalities are always plural. The multiple changes they refer to (e.g., individual, organizational, institutional, and environmental) all contribute to the definition of who we are, how we behave and what we learn….[time] relates to heterogeneous forms of change, occurring at the different levels of one’s existence (e.g., physical, biological, psychological, social, esthetic, and spiritual), plaited together, interwoven in a way that is hardly apprehended by the mind, not easily analyzed or disentangled. Time appears thus as a truly complex topic of investigation. But what does it mean to make such complexity intelligible? How does it impact the way we conceive educational theories and practices? And more fundamentally, how does it relate to transformation and emancipation? (Alhadeff-Jones, 2016, p. 3) We strive to guide our own work and other researchers’ and teacher educators’ in asking similar questions in the case of teacher professional learning. Many of us in the Learning Sciences are doing tremendous work in developing our responsiveness, or response-ability (Stengel, 2023), to individual teachers. The conceptual framework we present and illustrate here aims to further develop our understanding of teacher professional development experiences as strongly influenced by the intersection of the temporalities of their different learning ecologies. We contrasted it in the title with the more typical “learning across time and context” to highlight that we do not refer here to accumulation of learning as one travels between experiences, but to the interactive (and possibly multiplicative) effects of the multiple temporalities that are all part of any experience. We ask, as Alhadeff-Jones does, “what does it mean to make such complexity intelligible?”. We start with explaining a single adaptive cycle (see Fig ure 1) as a lens for responsiveness to patterns in teacher learning (Ehrenfeld & Stengel, 2025). We then move to introduce the multilayered adaptive cycles (“panarchy”) framework, and illustrate it with three studies. Notably, none of the studies was designed with the adaptive cycles in mind, but the first two were initially analyzed through the lens of a single adaptive cycle. We would argue that since the three institutional contexts in these studies represent different phases of adaptive cycles, they allow us to see some of the differences that having broader learning ecologies at different phases of change make for the teachers who enter a professional learning cycle of their own. The comparison opens questions about the resources teachers and teacher educators 82 could work with, and the constraints they work against, in each one of these phases (see Fath et al., 2015 for a similar mindset in the case of socioeconomic organizations). The representation of only three phases on the institutional level through the thr ee examples also opens theoretical questions about the fourth phase not represented. A single adaptive cycle The adaptive cycle was originally suggested as a response to a more traditional environmental ecology scholarship (Gunderson & Holling, 2002) in which a forest, for example, was considered as having two main phases of change: first growth and then conservation, where it would reach its climax. The new perspective suggested that conservation itself is just a temporal phase, often disrupted by forest fires, droughts, or intense pulses of grazing. That release phase of the previously well-connected system opens up space for new species in a reorganization phase, eventually leading again to a new growth phase of an ecological system that is similar to the previous one in some ways, but different in others. A similar argument can be made about teacher learning. It is often seen as a linear process that is rooted in a single professional development experie nce, with pre-and post-measures explaining the effect of that PD program on teachers’ knowledge, beliefs, and practices (e.g., Desimone, 2009). Such linear models are useful and have an important role in educational research. However, they often avoid or significantly simplify the interconnectedness and complexity in processes of learning and change. In Ehrenfeld & Stengel (2025) we suggested the adaptive cycle as one way to shift away from linear models to more complex and ecological ones. As Table 1 shows, we “translated” each of the phases to the case of teacher learning and illustrated the lens with empirical data from a video-based professional development for mathematics high-school teachers. Release represents the internal or external problematization or disruption of teaching norms. It can emerge from an external teacher-educator’s provocative feedback, from an internal sense of responsibility that resists institutional routines, or from much broader external events such as war and displacement as in one of the cases we illustrate below. Then, a reorganization phase would mean the planning and recruitment of new ideas and educational arrangements to stabili ze the system, albeit potentially with new goals in mind. Growth is the relatively linear and cumulative processes of experimenting with these new ideas, slowly incorporating some into routines of instruction. And finally, conservation is a phase where these new professional arrangements are consolidated into a relatively stable system of teaching. Multiple adaptive cycles (“Panarchy”) While a single adaptive cycle provides language and lens to notice complex learning processes, it is still missing the notion that interacting ecologies as well are not static and have their own rhythms. Partnering with teachers when their schools – an adaptive cycle in which their practice and PD are nested – are in a conservation phase (e.g., stagnant in a specific institutional policy) provides different opportunities and constraints than working with teachers in a school that is in a reorganization phase, recruiting ideas and making plans for new educational arrangements for students and teachers. And in the other direction, smaller and faster moving cycles could also affect broader and slower institutional temporalities. Panarchy was conceptualized to capture such interactions between fast and slow processes, between local and distant ones, between change and persistence, and between the predictable and unpredictable. As Gunderson et al. (2022) explain: The word panarchy is a scientific portmanteau, coined by combining the word pan with the root archy. Pan was the Greek god of nature, part animal and part human, who scattered discord, chaos, and ensuing panic. Archy is derived from the Latin and Greek word for “rules” and is cognate with words such as monarchy (“one ruler”) and hierarchy (“sacred rules”). Hence, 83 panarchy describes the rules of nature or nature’s rules. As Pan is a composite entity, part human and part animal, so are managed resource systems. That is, they are not just ecosystems, nor are they solely human systems, but rather they are coupled systems of people and nature. Hence coupled, multiscale social-ecological systems (panarchies) reflect these characteristics of Pan (unpredictable nature) and the emergent rules by which these systems operate that generate abrupt, episodic, and nonlinear changes (Gunderson et al., 2022, p. 3). Figure 2 visualizes possible relationships between levels of adaptive cycles. Gunderson and Holling suggested two such relationships they named “remember” and “revolt”. The first refers to the “memory” that large and slow levels have on the reorganization of the smaller and faster ones. We can imagine, for example, that when a school leadership team is making new plans for the semester, they might ask for advice from a retired teacher about how things used to be back in their days (or get that advice without asking), which can be considered one way of relying on institutional memory as a resource. The second (revolt) refers to the influence of small and fast levels in shifting away larger and slower levels from their conservation phase. We can imagine, for example, a small group of teachers who attended a conference or workshop and return ready to put what they learned into practice immediately or another group of teachers and parents, resisting a specific school policy they conceive as harmful for students, by that “pushing” the organization from conservation to a state of disruption and release. One of the main advantages of putting the three following empirical examples next to each other is to further articulate possible relationships between levels of adaptive cycles in our case of teacher learning. Figure 2 Illustrative Relationships between Levels of Adaptive Cycles (Adapted from Gunderson and Holling, 2002) Comparing three studies through the lens of multiple adaptive cycles Research contexts of the three illustrative studies We briefly introduce the research contexts of the three recent studies, focusing mainly on the details relevant to our main inquiry goals. The first study (Ehrenfeld & Stengel, 2025) explored two teams of mathematics teachers who participated in a video-based PD program. The two teams worked towards supporting more collaborative student engagement in small groups in mathematics classes. The left cycle in Figure 3 illustrates the learning processes of one of these teams (the Rees Team) during one year of participating in the PD program. The starting point of the two teams was different, and as you can see in Figure 3, we argued the Rees Team started their work with the research team in a reorganization phase. The reason was that the team came to the PD with a strong sense of a problem related to their school tracking policies. In other words, the release phase, and its strong problematization motive, happened before the PD, and the research team mainly supported the teachers through reorganization and growth in light of that strong sense of a problem. The second study (Ehrenfeld & Brady, 2025) followed three mathematics pre-service teachers (PSTs) who participated in a Course Assistants (CA) program in the Department of Mathematics and a related Pedagogy Seminar in the School of Education. In brief, we identified a main theme of interest and change that each PST worked towards, as they moved back and forth between working with calculus students in the Department of Mathematics, and imagining their future secondary mathematics teaching in the School of Education. The middle cycle in Figure 3 illustrates the learning processes of one of these PSTs, Daphne, during one year of participating in the CA program and the Pedagogy Seminar. Daphne’s theme of interest and change was her resistance to 84 collaborative mathematics learning. Her resistance was a well articulated one, strongly linked to her epistemological notions of what is worthwhile knowing in math (e.g., “I would like to have like some sort of big-picture explanation, like a one-word or one-sentence explanation of the field, and then like have everything connect to that”) and how one comes to know it (e.g., “if I were a student, that’s the kind of class I would want to sit in.”) In that sense, the starting point of Daphne’s adaptive cycle navigation was a conservation phase. The problematization to that stance was her experience as CA which included her role as groupwork facilitator. While by no means did Daphne become a groupwork fan (and neither did we hope that she would become one), she did talk in some discussions in the Pedagogy Seminar about the usefulness of student collaborative learning in certain conditions and for certain pedagogical goals. We consider these episodes as planning and recruitment of new arrangements and practices for her future secondary mathematics teaching, that contrast her former conservation, and thus argued it is a reorganization phase. The third study (Marco et al., 2025) investigated the work of teachers in provisional schools for displaced students in Israel, built overnight after the October 7 attack by Hamas on Israel, to serve villages which were moved by the country to its inner areas for their security. Note that unlike the first two studies, in this work we did not originally use the adaptive cycles as an analysis lens. The study took place in November-December 2023, when we interviewed 14 such teachers from nine schools about the ir work and experiences. The teachers were local to the school cities and so did not suffer direct violence and trauma as many of their students did. The right adaptive cycle in Figure 3 illustrates a collective change process we synthesized from the teachers’ stories. Two main concepts were salient across the teachers’ stories and are strongly connected to this paper: disorder and autonomy. The starting point of the teachers’ work was very similar, and was characterized by high levels of disorder and chaos. For that reason, as you can see in Figure 3, in our current interpretation we argue they started their work in a state of release and disruption. The stories that most of the teachers told us is one of stabilizing the system, in light of the lack of clear guidance and a priori plans for such cases. Their experience was one of strong sense of autonomy. Sometimes that autonomy was experienced positively and sometimes as a burden, but it was on them to make the new plans on content, pedagogy, and student wel l-being, which we interpret in this paper as reorganization. Figure 3 The Three Studies through the Lens of a Single Adaptive Cycle The three studies through the lens of two adaptive cycles We now move to look at the three studies from the new lens suggested here of multiple adaptive cycles. We start by adding adaptive cycles to represent the institutional mesosystem. Then, we add a third adaptive cycle for each study at the broader macrosystems level, trying to articulate several connections between the levels. First, recall that the Rees Team already came to the PD with a strong sense of a problem. The teachers were frustrated by the leadership team’s focus on recruitment, and were concerned about the “unofficial tracking” in their school, which created what they called a “sharp divide” between different groups of students. For example, in one of the video-based sessions, one of the teachers said: “One thing I do fear, so like what’s bothering me in this school, we do. Okay unofficially, unofficially we track kids…so I’ve had kids tell me, ‘oh, we’re in the dumb class.’ They know, they already have that label.” Connecting the prior cycle to an institutional one, given that teachers shared plenty of stories about unsuccessful attempts to change policies they were concerned about, we interpret the institutional level at a conservation phase (see Figure 4 on the left side). The meaning of that institutional phase as a resource and constraint for the teachers’ learning was dual: on the one hand, it provided teachers with the motivation to reorganize and learn new practices and navigate a new adaptive cycle. On the other hand, teachers had to work against (rather than with) their leadership team on their new strategies and teaching arrangements. Second, recall that in the second study, the PSTs were CAs in a calculus course in the Department of Mathematics, as well as students in a Pedagogy Seminar in the School of Education. This unique situation was 85 not random, but rather, it was part of an overall reorganization within the Department of Mathematics (see Figure 4). Within this pedagogic reform there was a main focus on Calculus as an entry-level course and as a gatekeeper for many undergraducate programs across the university. The department reform included the design and funding of the CA program, where first year students meet in small groups in a “Math Lab” on a weekly basis. Math Labs were guided by the CAs (undergraduate students who took the cour se in previous years), and the CAs weekly training was led by the first author and a partner from the Department of Mathematics. All of this backstage institutional context might not matter for a typical study on mathematics PSTs. Our claim here is that for the three CAs that were both learning to become teachers at the School of Education, and serving as CAs in the Department of Mathematics, the department reorganizations provided unique learning opportunities to build on their everyday problems of practic e as CAs and to guide them into developing their identities as soon-to-be secondary mathematics teachers. Such opportunities might or might not be realized, and the responsiveness of the Pedagogy Seminar’s facilitators to these issues in a timely manner mattered. Finally, while the first study illustrated institutional conservation, and the second study illustrated institutional reorganization, recall that teachers in the provisional schools for displaced students worked in chaos and disorder that stemmed from their school being in a release phase of disruption. Within this context high levels of teacher autonomy were evoked by the circumstances, and our data reflected both the benefits and concerns associated with teacher autonomy. For example, Jonathan, one of the teachers, described the sense of chaos and autonomy as follows: “Everything is so chaotic. Like, we get organized from day to day [...] yesterday was the first time we got a weekly schedule and not just for tomorrow. There's a lot of chaos, so I'm very independent. I have to decide what I teach and which processes I conduct in my class.” And yet side by side with freedom and autonomy, he described the support he receives: “I mostly feel like the message from the Management is that they are here to help me, to provide me with all the assistance they can, whether it's pedagogical, systemic, or technical assistance, meaning the message is ‘push forward, and we will help you.’ And not, ‘there are expectations from you, meet them.’” On the one hand, by getting support and overcoming the challenge of excessive autonomy, some teachers like Jonathan navigated the unique schooling conditions well and reduced some of the disorder it implies. In other cases, on the other hand, we found that the extensive autonomy and heavy responsibility on teachers’ shoulders, without the proper support, was experienced as a paralyzing hardship. For example, as Ethan shared: “It’s day after day and every day we have new challenges, and how we recenter the students [mentally]. There was no discussion, not with the principal, not with a pedagogical coordinator, there is no such a thing. Not a chair, advisor, therapist, nothing. Nothing. Now that I think about it, it's truly missing.” Providing autonomy and flexible support to deal with that autonomy constitute an essential element of the ability of a system to adapt. Figure 4 Illustrative Relationships between Two Levels of Adaptive Cycles (adapted from Gunderson and Holling, 2002) The three studies through the lens of three adaptive cycles We end this section by adding another, third, adaptive cycle at the broader macrosystems of each study, trying to articulate connections between these levels as well. First, in the case of the Rees Team, the institutional adaptive cycle (in the conservatio n phase) provided teachers with the motivation and activation energy to reorganize and for growth, while on the other hand, teachers had to work against their school leadership team. On the macrosystem level (see Figure 5), there were broader social proces ses that interconnected with the leadership’s focus on student recruitment. As teachers in the Rees team explained to us, the “unofficial tracking” mentioned above was in fact favoring affluent families, newcomers to the school’s gentrified neighborhood, t hrough 86 classrooms that are de facto tracked by things like ESL status and electives. In that sense, the advanced growth in gentrification in the school neighborhood directly led to the leadership team’s persistent focus on recruitment, on the expense of education and equity. In turn, this directly influenced the problem of practice teachers referenced within the context of the PD program, and influenced as well the possible solutions to experiment with in their growth phase. Second, in the case of the Department of Mathematics, it is not a coincidence that within the reorganization phase, the department settled on steps that included forming the CA program and the Math Lab as a new learning environment, which is more collaborative and conceptual in nature than typical math lectures and recitations with teaching assistants. These decisions were part of a larger growth in practitioners and scholars work in undergraduate science and mathematics education in the United States, that underscored the promises of implementing teaching approaches that provide students opportunities to engage in active learning. For example, in the Learning Assistants (LA) model, undergraduate students facilitate small group discussion during lectures. In other models, often called Supplemental Instruction (SI), Peer-Led Team Learning (PLTL), or Course Assistants (CA), senior students facilitate undergraduate collaborative learning outside of lectures. It is the temporal growth (see Figure 5) of such programs that provided the context for the reorganization of the department in that specific way, in turn, providing the PSTs with unique learning opportunities as CAs, to be realized in their Pedagogy Seminar. Finally, in the case of the provisional schools, adding a third, macrolevel adaptive cycle is relatively straightforward. The chaos and disorder described by the teachers in their schools, was a reflection of the more general chaos in the Ministry of Education and the government institutions as a whole. There were no clear directions going down to the provisional schools how to manage their routines, just as the local leadership teams left these decisions to teachers in their classrooms. This again raises q uestions about teacher autonomy, its importance and necessity in crisis education, and what is required to support teachers in managing such extreme autonomy. Notwithstanding the advantages of the high autonomy level, it entails many challenges, and requir es flexible support with it regarding educational visions and practices. (Mikulan & Sinclair, 2023, p. 10) By now, we hope we convinced the readers that it is worthwhile to think about time, or about the qualitative aspect of time as humanly and ecologically experienced. We refer to temporality in the sense of time that Erickson (2004) described as kairos, the Greek word for opportunity, or “a brief strip of the right time” (Erickson, 2004, p. 7). It is not simply duration (as often time is discussed in literature of teacher learning) or the sequential chronos from point A to point B, but rather a lens for supporting teachers with responsibility and attention (Stengel, 2023). We found complexity thinking here useful, as Alhadeff-Jones explains: 87 A complexivist understanding of time requires interpreting phenomena through the circular logic linking order, disorder, interactions and organization….in education, the assumption that the universe was created in time and space, and the neglect of the fact that living forms of organization produce their own temporalities, ground research efforts designed to manipulate time as an isolated, independent and quantifiable variable (Slattery, 1995, p. 612). It seems however preferable to assume that the rhythms of education emerge through their self-organization as a product of the order that constitutes them (e.g., repetition, periodicity and regularity) and the disorder that influences their evolution (e.g., accident, event and historicity). (Alhadeff-Jones, 2016, p. 27) Indeed, the adaptive cycles framework used in this paper to conceptualize the temporal dimension of teachers’ professional learning is rooted in complexity thinking and encompassing the activation energy of tensions between order and disorder, as well as between fast and slow processes, between local and broad contexts, between change and conservation, and between the unpredictability of complex adaptive systems and the search for pattern of change within it. With the Panarchy framework and complexity thinking in hand, our goal was to explore and illustrate how the multiscale framework can be adapted and used to explain professional learning within the multiple rhythms of teachers’ learning ecologies. We examined the shift from a single adaptive cycle lens t o a multiscale one in three empirical studies: (1) in-service teachers in a video-based professional development program (Ehrenfeld & Stengel, 2025), (2) pre-service teachers navigating between mathematics departments and schools of education (Ehrenfeld & Brady, 2025), and (3) teachers in provisional schools for displaced students during crisis (Marco et al., 2025). In the first study, adding the institutional level brought forth the notion that school conservation might provide teachers with initial motivation and activation energy, but it needs to be taken as a power for teachers to navigate and potentially work against. Note that since our study focused on teacher learning we developed a good sense of the influence of the institutional context on teachers. However, we did not study school changes and thus cannot say a lot about how small and fast levels influence larger and slower levels in this case. In the second study, adding an institutional level, highlighted the resources that broader reorganization may provide. Note that a recurring critique of teacher education programs points to the structural fragmentation of their different components. In light of that critique, institutional resources for change might stay untapped, as teacher educators in one university department oversee students’ experiences in the other. In this case, the PSTs participation in the CA program, afforded the Pedagogy Seminar with relevant problems of practice on which to bolster the learning. Put simply, PST educators can and should build on the multiple rhythms of the university’s available infrastructures, linking students' ongoing temporal experiences to their upcoming future teaching. In the third study, adding the institutional phasing of release called attention to the important role of teacher autonomy in crisis education. Here, we did have some access to the ways the teachers’ fast and local actions balanced the system in the provisional schools (insofar as the teachers’ perceived it). The fourth example, that is missing here, would be of an institutional context in the phase of growth. One could then ask about the constraints and opportunities for teacher learning in this case. Adding a third adaptive cycle in each study helped explain how and why the learning processes ended up to be that way, with much of the explanations provided by the macrosystem not directly at the scope of influence of the PD providers or the PSTs’ educators. That last point is crucial if one wishes to make good use of complexity thinking. Teacher learning is often seen as a linear process, rooted in a single professional development experience. Such models often simplify the interconnectedness of uncontrolled or uncertain factors in processes of learning and change. Some of these unpredicted factors end up being the most salient for the teachers within professional development activities, waiting to be discovered as relationships are being built and attention to teachers’ experiences is being developed. One major aspect of these experiences is rooted in the intersection of multilayered temporality across domains of action. Indeed, to once again echo Alhadeff-Jones (2016), “heterogeneous forms of change, occurring at the different levels of one’s existence” are “interwoven in a way that is hardly apprehended by the mind, not easily analyzed or disentangled”, however, as we showed here, the multilayered adaptive cycle framework (Panarchy) is one way to understand teachers’ experiences with complexity and therefore it may be useful to both teachers and teacher educators. We acknowledge, of course, there are other frameworks that account for broad and deep contexts in complex ways. Beyond “Across Time and Settings”: Situating Teacher Learning in the Multiple Rhythms of Teacher Learning Ecologies Nadav Ehrenfeld, Weizmann Institute of Science, nadav.ehrenfeld@weizmann.ac.il Barbara Stengel, Vanderbilt University, barbara.stengel@vanderbilt.edu Abstract: In this conceptual paper, we consider how the multiple rhythms of teacher learning ecologies shape the ways teachers experience change, portraying a framework for understanding teacher professional learning with its multiscale complexity. In earlier work, we used the idea of adaptive cycles (Gunderson & Holling, 2002) to conceptualize the temporal dimension of teacher learning. We take that work further by extending our prior single-adaptivecycle lens into a multiscale view (sometimes called Panarchy). We illustrate this idea with three studies: experienced teachers in video-based professional development, pre-service teachers’ learning between university settings, and teachers in provisional schools for displaced students. We show how teacher learning in all cases was influenced by interwoven rhythms. We argue that teacher educators and researchers can better develop their responsiveness to teacher learning when they pay attention to these multilayered temporalities, beyond more typical linear models or additive notions of learning across time and contexts. In our quest, we would like to discover ways to integrate and extend existing theory to achieve a requisite level of simplicity, just complex enough to capture and explain the behaviors we see… (Gunderson & Holling, 2002, p. 19) Objective This conceptual paper extends our ongoing efforts to better understand how teacher learning is situated in the multiple rhythms and settings of teachers’ learning ecologies, how to describe with just enough complexity processes of teachers’ learning, and h ow teacher educators can support different forms and rhythms of learning in responsive ways. Indeed, scholars remind us of the importance of enriching our temporal imagination and our educational theories to better understand rhythmic influences from an educational philosophy perspective (Alhadeff-Jones, 2016; Facer, 2023; Mikulan & Sinclair, 2023), from an interaction analysis perspective (Erickson, 2004), from indigenous perspectives (Fish & Syed, 2018), and environmental ecology (Gunderson & Holling, 2002), just to name a few. In a recent article in the Journal of the Learning Sciences (Ehrenfeld & Stengel,

2025) we suggested possible guidelines for capturing what we titled “the temporal dimension of teacher learning.”

That work built on the Adaptive Cycles Framework (Gunderson & Holling, 2002) from the discipline of environmental ecology, to suggest four cyclic phases of change: growth, conservation, release (problematization), and reorganization. We then synthesized Gunderson and Holling’s framework with sociocultural, ecological, and complexity theories of (teacher) learning, each theoretical lens contributing a crucial aspect for those of us striving to see teaching and teacher learning as highly contextual practices. Sociocultural research on teacher l earning informs our approach by privileging contexts and conversations, and positioning teachers as agentic learners and sensemakers (e.g., Horn & Garner, 2022; Lefstein & Snell, 2013; Vedder-Weiss et al., 2018); Ecological perspectives guide us to see teacher learning as emerging from the interconnectedness of experiences and contexts within their learning ecologies (e.g., Barron, 2006; Bronfenbrenner, 1979; Ehrenfeld, 2022; Hecht & Crowley, 2020; Louie & Zhan, 2022); And complexity scholars push us to see  teaching and teacher learning as complex adaptive systems, rather than linear, complicated, or simple ones (e.g., Cochran-Smith et al., 2014; Marco et al., 2025; Opfer & Pedder, 2011). Within such complex systems, outcomes are unpredictable yet are often patterned (Cochran-Smith et al., 2014; Opfer & Pedder, 2011). The Adaptive Cycles Framework helped us conceptualize such patterns of teacher learning through its four cyclic phases of release, reorganization, growth, and conservation (see Figure 1).

Figure 1 Phases of Adaptive Cycles (Adapted from Gunderson and Holling, 2002) 81 In this theoretical paper we present a conceptual and empirical extension of our prior work in the following way: First, we explain how we moved from thinking about one level of adaptive cycle that represents the temporality of teacher learning, to conside ring multiple (nested) scales of adaptive cycles with their multiple rhythms (a structure which Gunderson & Holling, 2002, named “Panarchy”). Then, we illustrate the new multiscale lens with examples from three published empirical studies from three very different contexts for teacher learning we conducted in the last few years: (1) a study of video-based professional development for experienced in-service teachers (Ehrenfeld & Stengel, 2025); (2) a study of pre-service teachers’ learning across the School of Education and their Department of Mathematics (Ehrenfeld & Brady, 2025); and (3) a study of teachers in provisional schools for displaced students (Marco et al., 2025). In the first two examples, we originally conducted and published the single-layer adaptive cycle analysis, and here we aim to illustrate what moving to the multiscale Panarchy affords. In the third example, we did not originally use the adaptive cycles for analysis, but we do it now as a secondary analysis to further illustrate its usefulness in the study of crisis education and teacher autonomy. Finally, we conclude with a discussion of theoretical and practical implications for researchers’ and teacher educators’ responsiveness to teacher learning ecologies. Our goal in this paper is to explore and illustrate how the multiscale adaptive cycles framework (Panarchy) can be adapted and used to explain professional learning within the multiple rhythms of teachers’ learning ecologies.

## Conceptual framework: multiscale adaptive cycles (“Panarchy”)

As an analogy for our argument regarding temporalities, consider our professional lives as educational researchers, and the way they are existentially influenced by the different rhythms of our work: our academic teaching ecologies might have certain temporalities of semesters, relationship building, assignments, teaching old or new courses, and perhaps sometimes even reshaping the whole program of studies; our research publications have other temporalities that include cycles of grant writing, data collection and analysis, manuscript writing, and so on; and our research student mentoring has its own temporalities of recruitment, shared work, community building, and graduation ceremonies. The question for professional learning then becomes, What difference d oes it make, or what difference can it make, when one enters a professional learning cycle, to have their different learning ecologies at different phases of change? Our starting point for answering such questions echoes a call by the psychosociologist and philosopher of education Alhadeff-Jones (2016) in his book Time and the Rhythms of Emancipatory Education, Rethinking the temporal complexity of self and society: If the idea of time suggests some form of singularity, temporalities are always plural. The multiple changes they refer to (e.g., individual, organizational, institutional, and environmental) all contribute to the definition of who we are, how we behave and what we learn….[time] relates to heterogeneous forms of change, occurring at the different levels of one’s existence (e.g., physical, biological, psychological, social, esthetic, and spiritual), plaited together, interwoven in a way that is hardly apprehended by the mind, not easily analyzed or disentangled. Time appears thus as a truly complex topic of investigation. But what does it mean to make such complexity intelligible? How does it impact the way we conceive educational theories and practices? And more fundamentally, how does it relate to transformation and emancipation? (Alhadeff-Jones, 2016, p. 3) We strive to guide our own work and other researchers’ and teacher educators’ in asking similar questions in the case of teacher professional learning. Many of us in the Learning Sciences are doing tremendous work in developing our responsiveness, or response-ability (Stengel, 2023), to individual teachers. The conceptual framework we present and illustrate here aims to further develop our understanding of teacher professional development experiences as strongly influenced by the intersection of the temporalities of their different learning ecologies. We contrasted it in the title with the more typical “learning across time and context” to highlight that we do not refer here to accumulation of learning as one travels between experiences, but to the interactive (and possibly multiplicative) effects of the multiple temporalities that are all part of any experience. We ask, as Alhadeff-Jones does, “what does it mean to make such complexity intelligible?”. We start with explaining a single adaptive cycle (see Fig ure 1) as a lens for responsiveness to patterns in teacher learning (Ehrenfeld & Stengel, 2025). We then move to introduce the multilayered adaptive cycles (“panarchy”) framework, and illustrate it with three studies. Notably, none of the studies was designed with the adaptive cycles in mind, but the first two were initially analyzed through the lens of a single adaptive cycle. We would argue that since the three institutional contexts in these studies represent different phases of adaptive cycles, they allow us to see some of the differences that having broader learning ecologies at different phases of change make for the teachers who enter a professional learning cycle of their own. The comparison opens questions about the resources teachers and teacher educators 82 could work with, and the constraints they work against, in each one of these phases (see Fath et al., 2015 for a similar mindset in the case of socioeconomic organizations). The representation of only three phases on the institutional level through the thr ee examples also opens theoretical questions about the fourth phase not represented.

A single adaptive cycle The adaptive cycle was originally suggested as a response to a more traditional environmental ecology scholarship (Gunderson & Holling, 2002) in which a forest, for example, was considered as having two main phases of change: first growth and then conservation, where it would reach its climax. The new perspective suggested that conservation itself is just a temporal phase, often disrupted by forest fires, droughts, or intense pulses of grazing. That release phase of the previously well-connected system opens up space for new species in a reorganization phase, eventually leading again to a new growth phase of an ecological system that is similar to the previous one in some ways, but different in others. A similar argument can be made about teacher learning. It is often seen as a linear process that is rooted in a single professional development experie nce, with pre-and post-measures explaining the effect of that PD program on teachers’ knowledge, beliefs, and practices (e.g., Desimone, 2009). Such linear models are useful and have an important role in educational research. However, they often avoid or significantly simplify the interconnectedness and complexity in processes of learning and change. In Ehrenfeld & Stengel (2025) we suggested the adaptive cycle as one way to shift away from linear models to more complex and ecological ones. As Table 1 shows, we “translated” each of the phases to the case of teacher learning and illustrated the lens with empirical data from a video-based professional development for mathematics high-school teachers. Release represents the internal or external problematization or disruption of teaching norms. It can emerge from an external teacher-educator’s provocative feedback, from an internal sense of responsibility that resists institutional routines, or from much broader external events such as war and displacement as in one of the cases we illustrate below. Then, a reorganization phase would mean the planning and recruitment of new ideas and educational arrangements to stabili ze the system, albeit potentially with new goals in mind. Growth is the relatively linear and cumulative processes of experimenting with these new ideas, slowly incorporating some into routines of instruction. And finally, conservation is a phase where these new professional arrangements are consolidated into a relatively stable system of teaching. Multiple adaptive cycles (“Panarchy”) While a single adaptive cycle provides language and lens to notice complex learning processes, it is still missing the notion that interacting ecologies as well are not static and have their own rhythms. Partnering with teachers when their schools – an adaptive cycle in which their practice and PD are nested –   are in a conservation phase (e.g., stagnant in a specific institutional policy) provides different opportunities and constraints than working with teachers in a school that is in a reorganization phase, recruiting ideas and making plans for new educational arrangements for students and teachers. And in the other direction, smaller and faster moving cycles could also affect broader and slower institutional temporalities. Panarchy was conceptualized to capture such interactions between fast and slow processes, between local and distant ones, between change and persistence, and between the predictable and unpredictable. As Gunderson et al. (2022) explain: The word panarchy is a scientific portmanteau, coined by combining the word pan with the root archy. Pan was the Greek god of nature, part animal and part human, who scattered discord, chaos, and ensuing panic. Archy is derived from the Latin and Greek word for “rules” and is cognate with words such as monarchy (“one ruler”) and hierarchy (“sacred rules”). Hence, 83 panarchy describes the rules of nature or nature’s rules. As Pan is a composite entity, part human and part animal, so are managed resource systems. That is, they are not just ecosystems, nor are they solely human systems, but rather they are coupled systems of people and nature. Hence coupled, multiscale social-ecological systems (panarchies) reflect these characteristics of Pan (unpredictable nature) and the emergent rules by which these systems operate that generate abrupt, episodic, and nonlinear changes (Gunderson et al., 2022, p. 3). Figure 2 visualizes possible relationships between levels of adaptive cycles. Gunderson and Holling suggested two such relationships they named “remember” and “revolt”. The first refers to the “memory” that large and slow levels have on the reorganization of the smaller and faster ones. We can imagine, for example, that when a school leadership team is making new plans for the semester, they might ask for advice from a retired teacher about how things used to be back in their days (or get that advice without asking), which can be considered one way of relying on institutional memory as a resource. The second (revolt) refers to the influence of small and fast levels in shifting away larger and slower levels from their conservation phase. We can imagine, for example, a small group of teachers who attended a conference or workshop and return ready to put what they learned into practice immediately or another group of teachers and parents, resisting a specific school policy they conceive as harmful for students, by that “pushing” the organization from conservation to a state of disruption and release. One of the main advantages of putting the three following empirical examples next to each other is to further articulate possible relationships between levels of adaptive cycles in our case of teacher learning. The first study (Ehrenfeld & Stengel, 2025) explored two teams of mathematics teachers who participated in a video-based PD program. The two teams worked towards supporting more collaborative student engagement in small groups in mathematics classes. The left cycle in Figure 3 illustrates the learning processes of one of these teams (the Rees Team) during one year of participating in the PD program. The starting point of the two teams was different, and as you can see in Figure 3, we argued the Rees Team started their work with the research team in a reorganization phase. The reason was that the team came to the PD with a strong sense of a problem related to their school tracking policies. In other words, the release phase, and its strong problematization motive, happened before the PD, and the research team mainly supported the teachers through reorganization and growth in light of that strong sense of a problem. The second study (Ehrenfeld & Brady, 2025) followed three mathematics pre-service teachers (PSTs) who participated in a Course Assistants (CA) program in the Department of Mathematics and a related Pedagogy Seminar in the School of Education. In brief, we identified a main theme of interest and change that each PST worked towards, as they moved back and forth between working with calculus students in the Department of Mathematics, and imagining their future secondary mathematics teaching in the School of Education. The middle cycle in Figure 3 illustrates the learning processes of one of these PSTs, Daphne, during one year of participating in the CA program and the Pedagogy Seminar. Daphne’s theme of interest and change was her resistance to 84 collaborative mathematics learning. Her resistance was a well articulated one, strongly linked to her epistemological notions of what is worthwhile knowing in math (e.g., “I would like to have like some sort of bigpicture explanation, like a one-word or one-sentence explanation of the field, and then like have everything connect to that”) and how one comes to know it (e.g., “if I were a student, that’s the kind of class I would want to sit in.”) In that sense, the starting point of Daphne’s adaptive cycle navigation was a conservation phase. The problematization to that stance was her experience as CA which included her role as groupwork facilitator. While by no means did Daphne become a groupwork fan (and neither did we hope that she would become one), she did talk in some discussions in the Pedagogy Seminar about the usefulness of student collaborative learning in certain conditions and for certain pedagogical goals. We consider these episodes as planning and recruitment of new arrangements and practices for her future secondary mathematics teaching, that contrast her former conservation, and thus argued it is a reorganization phase.

The third study (Marco et al., 2025) investigated the work of teachers in provisional schools for displaced students in Israel, built overnight after the October 7 attack by Hamas on Israel, to serve villages which were moved by the country to its inner areas for their security. Note that unlike the first two studies, in this work we did not originally use the adaptive cycles as an analysis lens. The study took place in November-December 2023, when we interviewed 14 such teachers from nine schools about the ir work and experiences. The teachers were local to the school cities and so did not suffer direct violence and trauma as many of their students did. The right adaptive cycle in Figure 3 illustrates a collective change process we synthesized from the teachers’ stories. Two main concepts were salient across the teachers’ stories and are strongly connected to this paper: disorder and autonomy. The starting point of the teachers’ work was very similar, and was characterized by high levels of disorder and chaos. For that reason, as you can see in Figure 3, in our current interpretation we argue they started their work in a state of release and disruption. The stories that most of the teachers told us is one of stabilizing the system, in light of the lack of clear guidance and a priori plans for such cases. Their experience was one of strong sense of autonomy. Sometimes that autonomy was experienced positively and sometimes as a burden, but it was on them to make the new plans on content, pedagogy, and student wel l-being, which we interpret in this paper as reorganization.

First, recall that the Rees Team already came to the PD with a strong sense of a problem. The meaning of that institutional phase as a resource and constraint for the teachers’ learning was dual: on the one hand, it provided teachers with the motivation to reorganize and learn new practices and navigate a new adaptive cycle. On the other hand, teachers had to work against (rather than with) their leadership team on their new strategies and teaching arrangements.

Second, recall that in the second study, the PSTs were CAs in a calculus course in the Department of Mathematics, as well as students in a Pedagogy Seminar in the School of Education. This unique situation was 85 not random, but rather, it was part of an overall reorganization within the Department of Mathematics (see Figure 4). Within this pedagogic reform there was a main focus on Calculus as an entry-level course and as a gatekeeper for many undergraducate programs across the university. The department reform included the design and funding of the CA program, where first year students meet in small groups in a “Math Lab” on a weekly basis. Math Labs were guided by the CAs (undergraduate students who took the cour se in previous years), and the CAs weekly training was led by the first author and a partner from the Department of Mathematics. All of this backstage institutional context might not matter for a typical study on mathematics PSTs. Our claim here is that for the three CAs that were both learning to become teachers at the School of Education, and serving as CAs in the Department of Mathematics, the department reorganizations provided unique learning opportunities to build on their everyday problems of practic e as CAs and to guide them into developing their identities as soon-to-be secondary mathematics teachers. Such opportunities might or might not be realized, and the responsiveness of the Pedagogy Seminar’s facilitators to these issues in a timely manner mattered. Finally, while the first study illustrated institutional conservation, and the second study illustrated institutional reorganization, recall that teachers in the provisional schools for displaced students worked in chaos and disorder that stemmed from their school being in a release phase of disruption. Within this context high levels of teacher autonomy were evoked by the circumstances, and our data reflected both the benefits and concerns associated with teacher autonomy. For example, Jonathan, one of the  teachers, described the sense of chaos and autonomy as follows: “Everything is so chaotic. Like, we get organized from day to day [...] yesterday was the first time we got a weekly schedule and not just for tomorrow. There's a lot of chaos, so I'm very independent. I have to decide what I teach and which processes I conduct in my class.” And yet side by side with freedom and autonomy, he described the support he receives: “I mostly feel like the message from the Management is that they are here to help me, to provide me with all the assistance they can, whether it's pedagogical, systemic, or technical assistance, meaning the message is ‘push forward, and we will help you.’ And not, ‘there are expectations from you, meet them.’” On the one hand, by getting support and overcoming the challenge of excessive autonomy, some teachers like Jonathan navigated the unique schooling conditions well and reduced some of the disorder it implies. Now that I think about it, it's truly missing.” Providing autonomy and flexible support to deal with that autonomy constitute an essential element of the ability of a system to adapt. First, in the case of the Rees Team, the institutional adaptive cycle (in the conservatio n phase) provided teachers with the motivation and activation energy to reorganize and for growth, while on the other hand, teachers had to work against their school leadership team. On the macrosystem level (see Figure 5), there were broader social proces ses that interconnected with the leadership’s focus on student recruitment. As teachers in the Rees team explained to us, the “unofficial tracking” mentioned above was in fact favoring affluent families, newcomers to the school’s gentrified neighborhood, t hrough 86 classrooms that are de facto tracked by things like ESL status and electives. In that sense, the advanced growth in gentrification in the school neighborhood directly led to the leadership team’s persistent focus on recruitment, on the expense of education and equity. In turn, this directly influenced the problem of practice teachers referenced within the context of the PD program, and influenced as well the possible solutions to experiment with in their growth phase.

Second, in the case of the Department of Mathematics, it is not a coincidence that within the reorganization phase, the department settled on steps that included forming the CA program and the Math Lab as a new learning environment, which is more collaborative and conceptual in nature than typical math lectures and recitations with teaching assistants. These decisions were part of a larger growth in practitioners and scholars work in undergraduate science and mathematics education in the United States, that  underscored the promises of implementing teaching approaches that provide students opportunities to engage in active learning. For example, in the Learning Assistants (LA) model, undergraduate students facilitate small group discussion during lectures. In other models, often called Supplemental Instruction (SI), Peer-Led Team Learning (PLTL), or Course Assistants (CA), senior students facilitate undergraduate collaborative learning outside of lectures. It is the temporal growth (see Figure 5) of such programs that provided the context for the reorganization of the department in that specific way, in turn, providing the PSTs with unique learning opportunities as CAs, to be realized in their Pedagogy Seminar.

Finally, in the case of the provisional schools, adding a third, macrolevel adaptive cycle is relatively straightforward. The chaos and disorder described by the teachers in their schools, was a reflection of the more general chaos in the Ministry of Education and the government institutions as a whole. There were no clear directions going down to the provisional schools how to manage their routines, just as the local leadership teams left these decisions to teachers in their classrooms. This again raises q uestions about teacher autonomy, its importance and necessity in crisis education, and what is required to support teachers in managing such extreme autonomy. Notwithstanding the advantages of the high autonomy level, it entails many challenges, and requir es flexible support with it regarding educational visions and practices. (Mikulan & Sinclair, 2023, p. 10) By now, we hope we convinced the readers that it is worthwhile to think about time, or about the qualitative aspect of time as humanly and ecologically experienced. We refer to temporality in the sense of time that Erickson (2004) described as kairos, the Greek word for opportunity, or “a brief strip of the right time” (Erickson, 2004, p. 7). It is not simply duration (as often time is discussed in literature of teacher learning) or the sequential chronos from point A to point B, but rather a lens for supporting teachers with responsibility and attention (Stengel, 2023). We found complexity thinking here useful, as Alhadeff-Jones explains: 87 A complexivist understanding of time requires interpreting phenomena through the circular logic linking order, disorder, interactions and organization….in education, the assumption that the universe was created in time and space, and the neglect of the fact that living forms of organization produce their own temporalities, ground research efforts designed to manipulate time as an isolated, independent and quantifiable variable (Slattery, 1995, p. 612). It seems however preferable to assume that the rhythms of education emerge through their self-organization as a product of the order that constitutes them (e.g., repetition, periodicity and regularity) and the disorder that influences their evolution (e.g., accident, event and historicity). (Alhadeff-Jones, 2016, p. 27) Indeed, the adaptive cycles framework used in this paper to conceptualize the temporal dimension of teachers’ professional learning is rooted in complexity thinking and encompassing the activation energy of tensions between order and disorder, as well as between fast and slow processes, between local and broad contexts, between change and conservation, and between the unpredictability of complex adaptive systems and the search for pattern of change within it. With the Panarchy framework and complexity thinking in hand, our goal was to explore and illustrate how the multiscale framework can be adapted and used to explain professional learning within the multiple rhythms of teachers’ learning ecologies. We examined the shift from a single adaptive cycle lens t o a multiscale one in three empirical studies: (1) in-service teachers in a video-based professional development program (Ehrenfeld & Stengel, 2025), (2) pre-service teachers navigating between mathematics departments and schools of education (Ehrenfeld & Brady, 2025), and (3) teachers in provisional schools for displaced students during crisis (Marco et al., 2025).

In the first study, adding the institutional level brought forth the notion that school conservation might provide teachers with initial motivation and activation energy, but it needs to be taken as a power for teachers to navigate and potentially work against. Note that since our study focused on teacher learning we developed a good sense of the influence of the institutional context on teachers. However, we did not study school changes and thus cannot say a lot about how small and fast levels influence larger and slower levels in this case. In the second study, adding an institutional level, highlighted the resources that broader reorganization may provide. Note that a recurring critique of teacher education programs points to the structural fragmentation of their different components. In light of that critique, institutional resources for change might stay untapped, as teacher educators in one university department oversee students’ experiences in the other. In this case, the PSTs participation in the CA program, afforded the Pedagogy Seminar with relevant problems of practice on which to bolster the learning. Put simply, PST educators can and should build on the multiple rhythms of the university’s available infrastructures, linking students' ongoing temporal experiences to their upcoming future teaching. In the third study, adding the institutional phasing of release called attention to the important role of teacher autonomy in crisis education. Here, we did have some access to the ways the teachers’ fast and local actions balanced the system in the provisional schools (insofar as the teachers’ perceived it). The fourth example, that is missing here, would be of an institutional context in the phase of growth. One could then ask about the constraints and opportunities for teacher learning in this case. Adding a third adaptive cycle in each study helped explain how and why the learning processes ended up to be that way, with much of the explanations provided by the macrosystem not directly at the scope of influence of the PD providers or the PSTs’ educators. That last point is crucial if one wishes to make good use of complexity thinking. Teacher learning is often seen as a linear process, rooted in a single professional development experience. Such models often simplify the interconnectedness of uncontrolled or uncertain factors in processes of learning and change. Some of these unpredicted factors end up being the most salient for the teachers within professional development activities, waiting to be discovered as relationships are being built and attention to teachers’ experiences is being developed. One major aspect of these experiences is rooted in the intersection of multilayered temporality across domains of action. Indeed, to once again echo Alhadeff-Jones (2016), “heterogeneous forms of change, occurring at the different levels of one’s existence” are “interwoven in a way that is hardly apprehended by the mind, not easily analyzed or disentangled”, however, as we showed here, the multilayered adaptive cycle framework (Panarchy) is one way to understand teachers’ experiences with complexity and therefore it may be useful to both teachers and teacher educators. We acknowledge, of course, there are other frameworks that account for broad and deep contexts in complex ways. Whatever framework one employs, our ongoing call is for researchers to center temporality, the qualitative aspect of time as humanly and ecologically experienced, in multilayered ways, as a main consideration in everyday work with teachers in research and practice. 88 Alhadeff-Jones, M.
