# Exploring the Features and Implications of a Project-Based Professional Development Program

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper presents a pilot program for educators that focuses on designing for equity, justice, and innovation. The program leverages Project-based Learning (PBL) as an approach for effective teacher professional development. This study analyzes the presence of the core features of effective educator professional development—collaboration, inquiry and active learning, and supportive instructional practices—within the pilot program as well as what program attributes support participants’ experience of these features. The findings have implications for designing and developing teacher professional development programs.

## Introduction

Teacher professional development (PD) is essential to improving school and student success. Traditiona l in-service methods like one-time workshops have given way to constructivist and  situative approaches to teacher PD, which treat teacher learning as interactive and social (Borko et al., 2010; Desimone, 2011; Desimone & Garet, 2015). K-12 and post-secondary education have adopted Project-based Learning (PBL) as an innovative and effective method that improves teacher competencies (Guo & Yang, 2012). PBL is a systematic teaching method that actively engages students in learning with an in-depth, authentic inquiry process and carefully craf ted products and activities to achieve student acquisition of knowledge and skills (Ravitz et al., 2004). PBL provides a relevant learning context for learners to draw on a nd use cultural knowledge and ensures learners acqu ire knowledge, skills, and competencies (Al-Busaidi & Al-Seyabi, 2021). This study examines if and how PBL may provide collaboration, inquiry, active learning, and supportive instructional practices for effective PD.

## Background

There exists much variation in the purpose and stru cture of professional development (PD) offered to teacherlearners (Desimone & Garet, 2015), as well as divergent ideas on what criteria should be used to determine “effectiveness,” thus making it challenging to learn which factors can lead to the success of PD efforts (Desimone & Garet, 2015; Guskey, 2003; Sancar et al., 2021). Despite the variation, there is growing consensus r egarding the core features of PD for improving teaching practice (Borko et al., 2010; Desimone, 2011). These features include (1) collaboration, (2) inquiry and active learning, and (3) supportive instructional practices. (1) Collaboration should be structured a nd purposeful, link theory and practice, and cultiv ate an interactive learning community (Desimone, 2011; Gus key, 2003; Sancar et al., 2021). Professional devel opment opportunities can address individual needs while al so organized around collaborative problem-solving and learning (Borko et al., 2010; Sancar et al., 2021).  Community building enables effective collaboration  (Borko et al., 2010). (2) Inquiry and active learning: Teachers should be active participants and direct th eir inquiry, reflection, and experimentation and participate in developing learning opportunities (Borko et al., 2010). Teacherlearners should have opportunities to observe and receive feedback, analyze student work, or make presentations (Desimone, 2011). (3) Supportive instructional prac tices include coaching and mentoring (Sancar et al., 2021). Preferred instructional practices should also be modeled for PD participants (Borko et al., 2010). These features shape how PD is structured, but their impact on effectiveness is complex (Guskey, 2003). Student complexity, teacher characteristics, expert ise, classroom environment, and school/community co ntext also affect professional development (Roseler & Den tzau, 2013; Sancar et al., 2021). This complexity illustrates the need for more research on designing, implementing, and studying effective professional development (Borko et al., 2010; Desimone, 2011; Sancar  et al., 2021). PBL is an appealing method for effective PD because it emphasizes inquiry, active learning, and collaboration and allows teacher-learners to take charge of their learning, accounting for the complexity of their field. Effective PBL requires a challenging problem or question, sustained inquiry, authenticity, student voice and choice, re flection, critique and revision, and public product  (Larmer & Mergendoller, 2015). PBL encourages problem-solving and critical thinking which improves comprehension and retention (Lambros, 2002), motivates and engages students by letting them explore personal topics (Savery & Duffy, 1995), and promotes community and communication by having learners work together (Hmelo-Silver, 2004).

## Research methods

This study explores  the following: “What program attributes support participants’ ex perience of collaboration, inquiry and active learning, and supportive instructional practices in a project-based PD opportunity?” The pilot professional development program The pilot PD program for practicing middle and high school educators, offered online in spring 2023 by a midwestern university, focused on three competencies: Teaching for Justice, Thinking Like a Designer, and Grounding in the Science of Learning and Development. The 14-week program included a weekly 3-hour block of contact time. During the first six weeks, participants learned program c ompetencies through a foundation institute which consisted of instructor-led activities, self-guided online learning modu les, and case studies. Synchronous meetings also focused on relationship building and shared reflections on course materials and were structured to foster trust and support for the subsequent 8-week project phase. During the project phase, participants created their driving questions and an actionable, learner-focused project. Scaffolding and structure followed the seven Essential Project Design Element s (Larmer & Mergendoller, 2015). Synchronous meetin gs allowed participants to receive feedback and iterat e on emergent ideas from coaches and peers, creating a community of inquiry and reflection. A final showca se featured project design, adjustments, results, a nd reflections to their peers and community members.

## Participants

Seven educators participated in the program. Analysis was limited to three participants who provided full consent to the use of their materials. To protect anonymity, we provide an aggregate description. Despite the small sample size, they reflect the cohort's professional and personal diversity. Two participants identify as female, one as male; one identifies as white, one as Black, and one as multiracial. Two are classroom teachers, and one is a curriculum instructional coach; their experience ranged from  two to more than ten years of secondary school teac hing experience. Their subject areas are English, math, and social studies. All  taught in large cities in the Northeast, Midwest, and South-Central United States. Their highest educational achievement was a Bache lor's, a Master's, and a doctorate. Their schooling experience varied. One had "teachers whonever looked like us"; one de scribed a school experience filled with a "high level of privilege," while another "grew up going to a Title I school." One of the participants had previous PD in PBL and used PBL in their teaching. One was well-versed in culturally responsive teaching. None had design training.

Data sources Data include artifacts from participants’ project work, transcripts of virtual synchronous sessions (weeks 1, 6, and 14), and open-ended responses to a post-experience survey. The artifacts consisted of parti cipants’ reflections, plans, and documentation of implementation throughout the project cycle.  All data were coded to identify recurring and emergent themes.

## Data analysis

Thematic analysis was used as a qualitative researc h method to identify themes that emerged from the d ata. We looked for statements where participants described experiences related to the PBL learning structure, and how they carried these experiences into their own practice. The process followed a guide ad apted from Braun and Clarke (2006): 1) Familiarizing with the data, 2) Generating initial codes, 3) Searching for themes, 4) Reviewing themes, 5) Defining and naming themes, and 6) Producing the report.

## Results

We identified three themes  in participants’ experience and practice: Collaboration & Community Building, Directing Inquiry, and Model-the-Model. Cohen’s kappa was calculated as a measure of  interrater reliability for two raters and was sufficiently high for each theme  (Collaboration & Community-Building: 0.9344; Directing Inquiry: 0.9060; Model-the-Model: 0.7107). Below we describe how each theme was instantiated in the data, how it affected teacher-learners’ experience, and related program attributes that facilitated these experiences. Collaboration and Community Building  involves participant  and instructor interactions that support learning. Participants' direct mentions of collaborating with others revealed this theme. Participant 3 said, "I find the class to be a great way to challenge my own thi nking and biases and then collaborate with other le arners to see actionable steps within my own work." Participa nt 1 added, "I was able to just have that  spirit of community to help me like, revise and think through these thi ngs and being able to hopefully implement some awes ome professional learning." Participants  also mentioned community-building or belonging. At the end of the pilot program, Participant 2 praised the community formation, stating, "I just appreciated that deeper conversation and how you know all of us, you know, genuinely care ab out disrupting those inequities […] I guess that's kind of what I talk about when I say coconspirators." Three main program attributes were repeatedly featured in participants’ experienc e of and reflection on this theme. The first program attribute was the foundation institute that occurred in the  first six weeks of the program, during which participants spent intensive time analyzing authentic problems of practice toget her and introducing themselves and their educational journe y to each other. This allowed participants to build  a community foundation they could build on throughout the rest of the program. The second program attribute was dedicated time and space during the following 8-week project cycle for working with peers, sharing ideas and prototypes, giving and receiving feedback, and chal lenging each other’s thinking. Weaving across the f irst two program attributes was instructor support, the third main program attribute. Instructors facilitated collaboration and community building between participants within each weekly meeting throughout the program. Directing Inquiry includes participants' design questions, projects, and activities within their professional settings. Participants chose design questions and activities based on personal interests and perceived learner needs. Participant 1 explored the design question of, “How can I support [district] teachers in providing psychologically safe classrooms for students?” by designing and facilitating a PD session for other teachers focused on the impact of implicit biases in the classroom. Participant 2’ s design question was, “How can I use students’ aspira tional capital to help them properly navigate the course a nd their future?” For their project, they facilitat ed students through PBL-inspired collaborative learning activities and plan ned to ask students t o collaboratively design assessment tasks for the class. Participant 3 explo red the design question of, “How do I center collaborative problem solving [...] so that all my learners exper ience a sense of belonging, a deep conceptual under standing within our  content, and recognize the assets they bring with t hem?” by introducing “challenge problems” for students to solve collaboratively and allowing students to define a set of “community agreements” for evaluating their collaborative work.

The data showed this theme in several ways. Partici pants explained their rationale for the design questions and their relevance to students. Particip ant 1 wanted to "spark awareness, a desire to unlea rn the unintentional, ingrained, and harmful ways bias sho ws up in so many discretionary spaces to truly crea te psychologically safe spaces for students to learn a nd grow." This influenced their project goal to "bu ild professional learning that would call awareness to having biases." The theme also appeared whe n participants discussed working with their students in ways that shifted their perspectives and goals. Participant 2  said design iterations taught them "that allowing students to u se each other's work as models and expressing the i mportance of presenting one's own work created more productiv ity, motivation, and industry to the ends of the pr oject." Participant 3 also found that surveying their stude nts during course work helped them "check [their] o wn assumptions," which influenced change in their project. Two main program attributes emerged from participants' experience and reflectio n. The first program attribute was the introduction and repeated use of a design t hinking framework to structure and scaffold participants’ project work and reflections. Participants frequently used the design vocabulary from the framework to describe their process and progress. The second program attribute came from using templates that helped participants document and share their project work. These templates were used throughout the project cycle. The Model-the-Model theme encompassed instances when participants made connections between practices they experienced within the pilot and cha nges in their teaching practice and within themselv es. Participants applied what they had learned from the pilot to their teaching. Participant 3 implemented a specific practice from the pilot in their classroom: "And we  do bright spots at the end of class, which is insp ired by [instructor] and this actual class every Saturday." This theme was also explicitly captured in participants' projects. Participant 1 adapted a learning experience from th e pilot and facilitated it with other educators in their school. Participant 2 implemented PBL features from the pil ot with their students. Participant 3 taught their students collaborative practices from the pilot. A final "Mod el-the-Model" example emerged as participants experienced shifts in perspective of their role as educators, in this case, from an expert to a collaborator. Participant 1 explained this shift: "I love using  the iteration and the revisions and getting the fee dback and just this collaborative community-based spirit of all the things that we did. I didn' t have to be the sage on the stage and know it all and do it all." Two main program attributes were identified for this theme. The first was the l ibrary of resources and learning modules on teaching and learning practices that participants could work through and reference to further their understanding and practice. The second attribute, as referred to by Participant 3’s quote, was the explicit modeling of teaching and learning practices by the instructors, who approached their role in the program from the position of both expert and learner.

## Discussion

This study investigated if and how  a PBL approach could provide the core features of e ffective educator PD – collaboration, inquiry and active learning, and sup portive instructional practices. The data analysis revealed the presence of these three themes in participants’ exp erience and program attributes that supported each theme. Participants reported a strong sense of community a nd an increased sense of ownership and autonomy in their learning process. They also reflected on the import ance of collaboration and the practices they experienced and learned during the program. All of this aligns well  with the key features of PD (Borko et al., 2010; Desimone, 2011). Different program attributes were found to support each of these themes. For exa mple, dedicated ideation and feedback time with peers supported collaboration, using a design thinking framework supported inquiry, and explicit modeling by instructors supported instruct ional practice. This speaks to the importance of PD  program design and how program attributes can affect teacher-learners’ perceptions and experience and, consequently, the efficacy of the PD. As such perceptions are affecte d by the complexity of individual students, individual t eacher characteristics, teacher expertise, classroom environment, and school/community context (Guskey, 2003; Roseler & Dentzau, 2013; Sancar et al., 2021), the approach shown here was found to both provide and support the core features of effective educator PD and also to accou nt for this complexity by allowing individual teach er-learners to have a degree of agency over their PD experience based on their own needs and interests.
