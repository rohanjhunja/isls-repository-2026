# Lenses for Characterizing Equitable College STEM Teaching

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study examines what helps university STEM instructors learn and adopt inclusive teaching practices, such as Adaptive Equity-Oriented Pedagogy (AEP). AEP has been shown to improve student achievement by over a full-letter grade. This study draws on data from a randomized controlled trial of 129 student-instructors who were either in a treatment course that modeled AEP or a control course that taught about AEP. This paper reports qualitative analyses of instructors in the top and bottom 25% of the treatment condition that modeled AEP. High-growth instructors more frequently aligned their academic and equity goals for student learning (e.g., promoting students’ sense of belonging as they developed academic skills). Similarly, high-growth instructors more often defined equity as adapting to students’ needs than low-growth instructors. A few low-growth instructors defined equity as giving everyone the same thing, but this definition was not consistently used when describing their pedagogical practices.

## Introduction: problem statement and research questions

Research shows that the quality of college teaching directly impacts student achievement and retention in STEM, especially for underrepresented students (URM; West on et al., 2019). To improve STEM teaching, we stud ied adaptive equity-oriented pedagogy (AEP). Previous randomized controlled trials found that compared to an active learning control course, STEM instructors applying AEP improved mean student achievement by over a let ter grade for all student demographic subgroups (Phuong & Nguyen 2019; Phuong et al., 2022). This study extends previous work by examining how p edagogy courses teaching AEP principles can support STEM student-instructors’ implementation of inclusive pedagogies. For simplicity, we use the term “instructor” for graduate and undergraduate student s who teach and are enrolled in the pedagogy course s. The term “facilitators” refers to the pedagogy course instructors. 129 instructors participated in a treatment or control AEP pedagogy course where they completed a teaching  portfolio that included written reflections, evidence of student learning, and feedback on their teaching. T he treatment course was adapted weekly based on instructors’ written reflections, whereas the control was not. U sing qualitative methods, our research question foc uses on treatment instructors because the treatment course was more effective at increasing AEP competencies over time (Phuong et al. 2022). We focus on the research question: How do low-and high-growth treatment instructors on AEP competency differ with respect to their learning goal and equity definitions and perspectives lenses?

## Background literature

Research has shown that instructors’ beliefs, goals, and practices shape student outcomes (Smith, 2020). The learning sciences literature underscores the import ance of engaging students’ repertoires of practices  (e..g, bridging their cultural knowledge and lived experie nces with disciplinary content) as a means to suppo rt both inclusion and productive disciplinary engagement (Engle & Conant, 2002; Gutiérrez & Rogoff, 2003; Nasir, 2002; Nguyen et al., 2021-2024). Teacher noticing, as highlighted by van Es et al. (2017), complements this approach by advocating for the integration of content-specific and non-content specific noticing, where teachers recognize and address equity barriers to learning. We seek to leverage these sociocultural perspectives to examine how AEP is adopted.

## Background context: AEP pedagogy course design

The AEP pedagogy courses teach instructors about th e key AEP principles. AEP is a framework for adapti ng teaching to address equity barriers to learning bas ed on student data (e.g., formative assessment, obs ervations, surveys) (Phuong et al., 2017). AEP pr actice is characterized by instructors’ use of 6 co mpetency elements (Phuong et al., 2017; Phuong et al., 2022):

(1) Clarify learning outcomes, equitable course policies, and prerequisites;

(2) Align formative assessments and activities with outcomes;

(3) Identify students’ competencies, interests, & needs;

(4) Understand equity barriers and contextual challenges to meeting outcomes;

(5) Adapt teaching practices based on these needs and barriers; and

(6) Iterate: Reflect upon pedagogy to support continuous learning, adaptation, and growth.

Unique element of treatment:  A feature of the treatment was that facilitators ex plicitly modeled and applied all six AEP elements as a tool to support instructor learning. Facilitators used weekly instructor reflection data to continuously adapt the pedagogy course disc ussions and ac tivities. In alignment with AEP, facilitators tailored the treatment in response to instructors’ reflections on their cultural perspectives, biases, and backgrounds, which include their lived experiences, discourse, r acial experiences, and STEM department culture (e.g., what is valued methodologically).

Conceptual framing We are interested in the reasons that drive instruc tors to learn and implement pedagogical changes. We  are motivated by the idea of professional vision (Goodw in, 1994), which involves cultivating ways of notic ing and making sense of phenomena that align wi th advancing expertise. We examine lenses for pedagogical change, which include the following: beliefs and goals around equity, and the kinds of student learning goals they prioritize when making pedagogical choices. These lenses, or m echanisms, allow us to address content-specific and non-content specific teacher noticing skills (e.g., a f ocus on students’ sense of belonging). We draw on t he teacher noticing literature to examine how instructors adju st their teaching based not only on what they obser ve in the classroom, but also on additional information, such as students’ equity goals. ## Goal Lens” and “Equity Definition and Perspectives Lens.”

Learning goal lens. The Learning goal lens is defined as the goals, concepts, or ideas that one foregrounds in their mind. These goals, concepts, or ideas may include a focus on academic content and/or equity issues. This lens speaks to the purpose and beliefs that instructors have for their teaching. For example, instructors may focus on learning outcomes that emphasize academic concep ts. Instructors may also have goals focused on addr essing equity, such as addressing stereotype threat and im proving student sense of belonging in the classroom. In the teacher noticing literature, van Es et al. (2017) w ould argue that content-specific (e.g., academic concepts) and non-content specific noticing focused on equity (e.g., stereotype threat) is important for promo ting a more inclusive classroom. Building on this work, we exam ine whether high-growth instructors trained in AEP competency have more equity-and academic content-driven goals than low-growth instructors.  Fink (2013), Davis (2009), and Weinstein (2009) highlight the im portance of clarifying and supporting students in m eeting rigorous academic learning goals.  Nguyen et al., (2022) discusses the importance of p ursuing equity goals. To build on the literature, we leverage the Learning Goal lens to examine whether instructors who demonstrate greater AEP competency align their equity and academic goal s where equity both play an important role in advan cing inclusive learning environments.

Equity perspectives and definitions lens.  The equity perspectives and definitions lens posits  that instructors’ definitions of equity can motivate the m to adopt or not adopt AEP. One definition of equi ty may include providing all students with the same learni ng opportunities. Another definition may focus on a dapting learning opportunities based on students’ learning needs. Building on teacher noticing, we hope to exa mine how pedagogy courses can utilize reflection and experie ntial learning to address instru ctors’ perspectives on equity and inclusion. We are interested in how instructors ’ perspectives and definitions of equity motivate t heir use of equitable pedagogies. Here are questions that one m ay consider when reflecting on this lens: How do I define equity? Is equity responding to all students’ needs or  providing everyone with the same thing? What is th e connection between equity and student success? Is equity only about academic achievement? Many scholars have argued for expanding the definitions of equity and distinguishing it from equality (Espinoza, 2007; Jordan, 2010; Phuong et al., 2017). We contribute to this scholarly conversation by leveraging the Learning Goal lens to examine whether high-growth instructors have more expansive definitions of equ itable teaching than low-growth instructors (e.g., equity does not mean equality).

## Methodology

STEM instructors at a R-1 public university participated in semester-long AEP pedagogy courses for first-time instructors where they completed a teaching portfol io. The pedagogy courses primarily served instructo rs who taught computer science, data science, math, and statistics courses. Instructors partici pated in either a treatment or control pedagogy course. The treatment and control courses were co-led by the same facilitators who employed exemplary active learning activities and provided an AEP curriculum. In this experimental study, instructors were in treatment or control pedagogy courses. The treatment has approximately 28% women and 9% URM. All instructors completed AEPC assessments at the baseline, midterm, and final timepoints (Scale 0-22). The AEPC assessment contained reflection quest ions that asked instructors to provide evidence of applying AEP competencies via t eaching practice stateme nts, written reflections on instructional and assessment methods, evidence of s tudent learning, visualizations of s tudent progress scores, multimedia elements, and feedback from peers and students regarding their teaching. A detailed rubric (See Table 1 for a summary) was applied independently by two r esearchers to score anonymized assessments. Based o n partial credit Rasch modeling (Masters, 1982), the AEPC measure de monstrates high reliability (0.94), strong validity aligned with Wilson’s (2005) strands of va lidity framework, no evidence of gender or URM bias, and significant correlation with researcher reviews of teaching and pedagogical materials (Phuong et al., 2022). ## Discussion and conclusion

Equity definitions and perspectives lens.  High-growth treatment instructors consistently reference d their definitions and perspectives for equity in early qu estions and the later questions to see if these def initions acted as a consistent lens for reflecting on and making p edagogical decisions. As  such, these high-growth instructors consistently discussed their rationale and approach  for their pedagogical adaptations, which were grou nded in their different definitions and perspectives of equ ity, which appeared to be more entrenched when they reflected and made pedagogical decisions. Therefore, we argue  that the Equity Definitions and Perspectives Lens appear to be more salient in motivating the perspectives o f the high-growth instructors as they engage with students, adopt equitable pedagogies, and advance success for  all learners. Moreover, high-growth instructors defined equity as adapting to students’ needs more often th an low-growth instructors. A few low-growth instructors defined equity as giving everyone the same thing, b ut they did not invoke that definition consistently  when describing pedagogical practices.

Learning goal lens. Our findings support the conjecture for the Learnin g Goal Lens in that high-growth instructors tend to report “Equity goal” and “Acade mic goal” subcodes more often than low-growth instructors, and often together in the same response. Like the E quity Definitions and Perspective Lens, high-growth instructors’ “Academic goal” and “Equity goal” subcodes for student learning are more salient and frequent when they describe their pedagogical decisions in respon se to reflection questions. By considering both an “Academic goal” and “Equity goal,” the instructors are notici ng and addressing that psychosocial factors and equ ity barriers impact students’ learning of academic content. Results from examining instructors’ “Academic goal” and “Equity goal” subcodes demonstrate that equity is central to the goals that the high-growth instructors report when discussing their beliefs and practices. Furthermore, we discovered that instructors in the high-growth group tend to follow certain patterns in their reflection responses. For example, the instructors:

1. Expressed an equity goal or belief, noticed the equity barriers impacting their students, and adapted their

teaching to address the barrier; or

2. Noticed equity barriers impacting their students, expressed an equity goal or belief, and adapted their

teaching accordingly.

Through these approaches, high-growth instructors addressed the exclusionary exper iences that many STEM students faced. Nevertheless, the sample size of 20 instructors is limited and th is research is not causal or fully generalizable. Future research can explore ad ditional reflection responses and more instructors in other college contexts.

Connections to literature. Van Es and Hand (2017) emphasize the importance of not icing both contentspecific (e.g., academic concepts) and equity-focused (e.g., stereotype threat) factors for foste ring inclusive classrooms. Extending this work, we developed an approach to examine whether high-growth instructors prioritize equity-driven and academic content-driven goals more than low-growth instructors. Building on the teacher noticing literature from learning sciences in this way, we defined and contributed  two lenses for developing instructors and helping them notice and address students’ equity barriers. We specifically focus on equity goals that emphasiz e engaging students' repertoires of practices and value their cultural knowledge, experiences, and wa ys of thinking as assets in the learning process (G utiérrez & Rogoff, 2003; Nasir, 2002). This approach supports disciplinary engagement by creating connections bet ween students’ lived realities and academic content, fos tering deeper participation and understanding (Barr on, 2006; Engle & Conant, 2002). Inspired by Goodwin’s (1994)  concept of professional vision, we argue that refi ning “noticing” requires the integration of learning and equity goals, fostering an empathetic understanding of students’ lived experiences. High-growth instructors seamlessly align academic and equity goals, embedding strategies such as promoting belonging, mitigating stereotype threa t, and addressing resource gaps as integral to adva ncing conceptual mastery. This integration of equity as a  guiding framework engages the calls in the learnin g sciences to consider the social and historical dimensions shaping pedagogy (Gutiérrez & Rogoff, 2003). Scholarly significance.  Building on the work of Fink (2013), Davis (2009), and Weinstein (2009), this study extends the literature by demonstrating how a ligning academic and equity goals fosters inclusive  teaching practices and enhances attention to student equity barriers. Our findings identify this alignment as a potential mechanism for promoting more inclusive pedagogies. We show that high-growth instructors consistently operationalize equity by aligning their definitions  with documented practices, whereas low-growth instructors exhibit inconsistencies. This work highlights that goal alignment can promote equitable pedagogy. Practical significance.  Our findings about high-growth instructors demonstrates the importance of creating instructor professional development strate gies that expand instructors’ definitions of equity  through experiential activities related to AEP. In addition, we suggest incorporating professional development  activities where instructors can obtain more support with alig ning equity and academic goals because we have seen  how they can work in tandem to support pedagogical adaptations that advance more equitable student learning.
