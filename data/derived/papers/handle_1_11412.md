# Instructional Principles for Inclusive AI in K-12 Education

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
As AI becomes prevalent in education, it offers transformative opportunities but also risks widening inequalities, particularly for underserved students. This study explores instructional strategies and design considerations for fostering inclusive participation in AI and technology through a scoping review. After applying screening criteria, 17 articles were analyzed, revealing five main themes and 14 pedagogical principles. These findings provide valuable insights for educators and researchers in developing inclusive and equitable AI curricula.

## Introduction and research aim

As AI becomes prominent in education, concerns and challenges have emerged. Among the challenges identified by Pedró et al. (2019), ensuring inclusion and equi ty is paramount. While privileged students gain sub stantial benefits from new technologies, potentially widening achievement gaps, underprivileged students tend to benefit far less (Luckin & Holmes, 2016). While AI offers transformative opportunities, it risks exacerbating inequalities, with underserved populations particularly vulnerable to exclusion from its benefits (Pedró et al., 2019). To address these disparities, inclusive instructional strategies are crucial for fostering equitable participation in AI-integrated classrooms. Despite growing interest in the educati onal applications of AI, limited research has focus ed on pedagogical approaches that support underrepresented groups, leaving a gap in guidance (Chen et al., 2020). Thus, this study aims to address this gap by explor ing instructional strategies and design principles that prioritize equity and diversity. It seeks to provid e practical guidelines for creating AI-integrated classrooms that support underrepresented students. The research question guiding this study is: What are instructional strategies and design considerations to broaden student participation in inclusive AI and technology?

## Theoretical background

The growing emphasis on diversity, equity, and incl usion (DEI) in education reflects a significant shi ft toward making learning environments more accessible and eq uitable. However, persistent achievement gaps continue to disadvantage African American and Latino students c ompared to their White peers (Ladson-Billings, 2006). Identity plays a crucial role in DEI, as it directl y influences motivation and engagement in learning.  Research underscores the importance of aligning students' cu ltural and historical i dentities with their educational experiences to foster a sense of belonging and impr ove learning outcomes (Altugan, 2015). Similarly, teacher identity shapes instructional practices and classro om dynamics, further impacting student engagement (Trent, 2010). The integration of AI in education presents opportunities such as providing personalized learni ng (Lee,

2024) but risks exacerbating existing inequities. Underserved groups including students of color, female students,

and those from low-income or rural areas often face barriers to AI and computer science education, such as limited representation, lack of role models, and reduced ac cess to resources (Solyst et al., 2023). To address these disparities, culturally relevant computing has emerged as frameworks to bridge cultural practices and educational technologies. These approaches emphasize the inclusion of students' identities and cultural backgrounds to create engaging and equitable AI learning experiences, promoting retention and empowerment among underrepresented groups (Kafai et al., 2014).

## Methods

Identification This study adhered to the PRISMA (Preferred Reporti ng Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) framework. The protocol encompassed data collection from reputable online databases, establishing inclusion and exclusion criteria, and following key steps such as identification, screening, and data abstraction. Employing this framework ensured a precise and reliable search for  evidence-based practices of inclusive AI education in K-12 classrooms. The  literature search utilized four databases: EBSCO, W eb of Science, ACM, and IEEE Xplore. Search strings were tailored to each platform’s log ic and included terms such as inclusive AI in K-12, culturally relevant AI in K-12, and culturally relevant technology in K-12. Researchers independently applied these terms, comparing results to minimize bias. Additionally, a snowballing approach identified further resources. Screening Eligibility criteria required articles to (1) be published after 2013, (2) be in English, (3) be peer-reviewed journal articles or conference proceedings, and (4) provide full-text access. Further exclusion criteria narrowed the scope to empirical research conducted in K-12. Using backward snowballing, researchers identified additional relevant papers, resulting in 17 eligible studies (see Figure 1). Data abstraction and analysis This study employed a scoping literature review (Mu nn et al., 2018) to examine the principles of inclu sive AI in education. Using the PRISMA approach, the 17 selected articles were analyzed to extract themes and sub-themes (Braun & Clarke, 2012). Iterative rounds of review ensured consistency in  coding and thematic categorization. Themes were grouped into broader categories, validated through rigorous cross-checking among three researchers to ensure reliability and accuracy.

## Results

To examine the research question, themes and sub-themes were synthesized from our data analysis. Fig ure 2 illustrates the frameworks of pedagogical and design principles for inclusive AI in education. Our findings identified five main themes: (1) the critical role of identity, (2) considerations for technology use, (3) design approaches, (4) content development, and (5) fostering a sense of belonging, and fourteen principles. Figure 2 Pedagogical Framework for Inclusive AI in Education Identity ● Principle 1. Cultural Synchronization: Among our findings, this was the most frequent sub-theme. This concept emphasizes that lessons and curricula should be culturally relevant, reflecting students' identities. For instance, Kafai et al. (2014) demonstrated the use of culturally responsive open design with electronic textiles to enhance indigenous knowledge. Incorpora ting students' cultural  backgrounds and elements into lessons promotes engagement in their learning.

● Principle 2. Positionality: This highlights the importance of instructors recog nizing and respecting the intersectionality of cultural and ethnic identities. By acknowledging their own positionality, instruc tors can foster affirmation and advocate for student diversity (e.g., Codding et al., 2019). ● Principle 3. Relevance: This stresses that lessons and curricula should connect to students' everyday lives and experiences, making the AI-related task process more meaningful and engaging (e.g., Bertrand et al., 2024; Fowler & Khosmood, 2018).

Technology ● Principle 4. Accessibility: When incorporating advanced AI tools, ensuring acce ssibility is crucial, especially for low-income schools. Expensive technologies can be disruptive in such contexts. Teachers should prioritize cost-effective AI devices to support underserved or low socioeconomic status (SES) students. For example, Manikutty et al. (2019) demo nstrated the use of low-cost robotics in low-SES schools, addressing budget constraints and fostering diverse participation. ● Principle 5. Up-to-date AI: AI technologies in education must be current. Emerging technologies enable students to actively engage in learning and foster interaction. For instance, Druga et al. (2019) highlighted the benefits of integrating cutting-edge tools to enhance student learning. ● Principle 6. Digital Literacy: Instructors must consider the diverse digital skill  levels of their students when integrating technology. Addressing these diffe rences ensures equitable learning opportunities. Bertrand et al. (2024) emphasized peer-assisted learning, where students with h igher digital literacy support those with lower skills.

Design ● Principle 7.  Participatory Design:  Involving various stakeholders such as teachers, students, AI developers, and researchers is essential for foster ing ownership and ensuring all voices are heard. Participatory design promotes inclusivity in the development of curricula and learning experiences (e.g., Dangol et al., 2024; Louie et al., 2022).

● Principle 8. Student-Centered Design: This principle emphasizes student agency by allowing learners to choose different AI tools and opportunities, thereby promoting responsibility and ownership of their learning. A student-centered approach enhances engagement (e.g., Franklin et al., 2020; Vakil, 2014). ● Principle 9.  Personalized Design:  Curricula should support personalized learning by a ccommodating diverse needs and prior knowledge. Instructors can achieve this by offering varying levels of technologyrelated activities tailored to students’ digital skills (e.g., Ladeji-Osias et al., 2018; Simley et al., 2020). Content Development ● Principle 10. Open-ended Tasks:  AI learning tasks should be designed as open-ended activities to encourage creativity and exploration (e.g.,  Franklin et al., 2020;  Kafai et al., 2014;  Ladeji-Osias et al., 2018). Franklin et al. (2020) highlighted that such tasks empower students to become digital creators. ● Principle 11.  Collaboration: Collaborative work in lessons and activities helps students develop teamwork skills, embrace diverse perspectives, and support peers with varying abilities (e.g., Bertrand et al., 2024; Dangol et al., 2024).

● Principle 12. Multimodality: Providing various forms of expression and represent ation such as images, symbols, and tools ensures inclusivity and engages multiple learning styles. Multimodal approaches cater to diverse learners and enrich the educational experience (e.g., Franklin et al., 2020; Solyst et al., 2022). Sense of belonging ● Principle 13. Rapport: Building strong rapport between teachers and students is fundamental to fostering a supportive learning environment. Positive teacher-student relationships enhance students' engagement and inclusive learning experience in AI (e.g., Dangol et al., 2024; Solyst et al., 2022). ● Principle 14. Community: Engaging with the local community plays a critical role in education (e.g., Lee et al., 2015; Vakil, 2014). Community involvement promotes student participatio n, increases engagement, and nurtures a sense of belonging. Coll aborative efforts with local organizations or stakeholders can provide additional technological r esources for meaningful learning. For example, Codding et al.’s study (2019) involved a local library to implement a coding club for students.

## Discussion and implication

Our study investigated instructional principles to broaden participation in AI education. The findings  emphasize the critical role of integrating culture and identity into the curriculum. Instructors also play a pivotal role in shaping student outcomes in AI. Our analysis underscores ho w instructor positionality impacts learning, with f emale students particularly benefiting from same-gender role models. Building strong student-instructor relationships can encourage female students to pursue AI, promoti ng br oader participation in the field (Lee et al., 2023). Additionally, community engagement is crucial, espe cially for underserved schools lacking AI courses. Partnerships with local organizations can provide e ssential resources for low-income students. Our findings also highlight effective design approaches, including participatory and student-centered designs, aligning with Lee and Park's (2024) study, which emphasizes that involvin g students and practitioners fosters ownership and improves academic performance. Furthermore, open-ended tasks, diverse representations, and collabora tive activities like pair programming enhance creativity and critical thinking. However, of the 17 studies reviewed, few studies addressed approaches related to 'technology,' whereas other principles were mentioned frequently across numerous studies. Limited empirical research addresses AI ethics, accessibility, and strategies for varying digital skill levels. To bridge these gaps, we recommend providing digital literacy training to promote equitable access. Additionally, orientations on AI tool exploration are essential before starting AI projects, fostering a more inclu sive AI-driven educational environment. Our findings and suggestions will have significance for educators and researchers when they develop an inclusive and eq uitable curriculum in AI-related classes.
