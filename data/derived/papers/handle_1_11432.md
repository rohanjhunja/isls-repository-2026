# The Effects of Emotion Regulation and Students’ Perceived Challenges on Emotion Synchrony in Collaborative Learning

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In this study, we explored the relationships among students’ perceived challenges in collaborative learning, emotion regulation strategies (i.e., reappraisal and suppression), and emotion synchrony (i.e., valence synchrony and arousal synchrony) in natural classroom settings. We performed the partial least squares structural equation modeling for data analysis. The results revealed a positive and direct effect of cognitive reappraisal on arousal synchrony, and a negative and direct effect of cognitive reappraisal on valence synchrony. Students’ perceived challenges had a negative effect on their arousal synchrony. Students’ perceived challenges also played a mediating role in the relationship between cognitive reappraisal and arousal synchrony. As a total effect, cognitive reappraisal still positively and significantly predicted arousal synchrony. This study provided insights into students’ emotional responses in collaborative learning and informed the design of collaborative learning environments. This study has also methodological implications regarding the analysis of emotion synchrony.

## Introduction

Collaboration can be challenging, considering the involvement of individuals of diffe rent backgrounds and the complexity of peer interactions. Participation or one-way interaction does not always result in effective collaboration. Group members face difficulties due to divergent goals, priorities, and expectations. In particular, group members experience negative and aroused emotio ns while facing conflicts during collaboration, which influence their effort  in collaborative learning. In collaborative learnin g contexts, individuals need effective strategies to regulate their emotions so that their groups can fu nction toward collective efforts effectively and successfully (Huang & Lajoie, 2023). Cognitive reappraisal and expressive suppression (Gross, 2015) are known as the two most beneficial emotion regulation strategies in self-regulated learning. In contrast, the effects of the two emotion regulation strategies on students’ emot ions in collaborative learning environments are relatively underexplored.

Emotions could be contagious in a collaborative group. When one group member experiences a particular emotion, it can lead the other members to experience the sam e emotion. Consequently, one popular approach is to examine the synchrony of group members’ emotion valence, i.e., positive versus negative emotions. The other way to study the compliance of group members’ emotions is by measuring physiological synchrony. For instance, researchers who took the first steps to examine the  physiological synchrony between triad members found that group members were usually at different emotion arousal levels, and when they were at the same leve l, it was mostly the low arousal or deactivated level. The valence and arousal aspects of emotions fluctuate w ith the regulatory episodes in collaborative learning. Still, most research on synchrony has focused on intimate relationships, such as those between couples or par ent-child relationships. Although emotion synchrony in collaborative learning is gaining increasing attention from investigators, this area of research is still in its nascent stage. To our knowledge, no study has examined the influences of students’ emotion re gulation strategies (i.e., cognitive reappraisal and expressive suppression) o n the valence and arousal synchrony of emotions in collaborative learning, especially when considering  their perceived collaborative challenges. This study has theoretical implications in that it will help develop a model to explain how emotions co-occur among group members. This study  also informs the research and analysis of emotion synchrony in collaborative learning. Practically, the findings will provide insights into the effective management of collaborative learning communities.

## Theoretical background

Emotion arousal and emotion valence Emotion refers to spontaneous psychological or phys ical reactions to external stimuli and internal men tal representations. Emotion is a multidimensional construct that cons ists of subjective, behavioral, cognitive, and internal or peripheral physiological components. Ac cording to the Control-Value theory (CVT, Pekrun & Perry, 2013), valence and arousal are two dimensions that provide a comprehensive picture of the nature and function of emotions. Valence refers to the subject's feeling of positive and negative emotions (e.g., enjoyment as a positive and anxiety as negative), whereas arousal refers to the perceived intensity of an emotional state (e.g., anger as activating and boredom as deactivating).

Emotions are subjective and vary from person to person. Thus, even people in the same situation might have distinct types and intensities of emotions in response to the stimuli (Frijda, 201 7). However, emotions can be influenced among individuals through social inte ractions within a collective. Research demonstrated  that emotions are contagious in collaborative learning environments by aligning group members' emotions at a similar valence and arousal level during the learning process (Järvenoja  et al., 2018). Furthermore, emotional responses are associated with cognitive functions. The different valence of emotions (i.e., positive and n egative) affect cognitive processing in distinct ways, including memory encoding, attention scope, cognitive flexibility, creative problem-solving, cognitive control, knowledge retrieval, and perceptual processing. In terms of arousal of emotions, it also affects cognitive processing. Research indicated that emoti on regulation is intertwined with the mental manifestation of regulated learning and other emoti onal processes during collaborative learning (Hadwin et al., 2018). Although the research on emotion valence and arousal has a solid base, emotion valence and arousal alone may not be sufficient to explain the complex interplays  between emotion and group functioning in collaborative learning. An important mediator  is the group members’ perceptions toward collaborative tasks, which might influence a group’s collective emotional responses to learning. Emotion synchrony in collaborative learning Simultaneous arousal and valence experienced by group members could be studied in terms of emotion synchrony. As is evident in most  collaboration studies, synchrony is widely investigated using physiological (e.g., electrodermal activities, Dindar et al., 2019; electromyographic, Mønster et al., 2016) and behavioral data (e.g., facial expressions, Chikersal et al., 2017). The literature presented two primary arguments regarding synchrony and its role in teamwork. On the one hand, research ers discovered a negative correlation between physiological synchrony and  cognitive and emotional factors. For example, arous al synchrony is associated with perceived challenges (Malmberg et al., 2019), and increased p sychological synchrony is associated with group ten sion and negative affect during teamwork (Monster et al., 20 16). On the other hand, some suggested that physiological synchrony was not always attached tonegative situations but indicated rich interactions among group members. For instance, researchers revealed positive relationships between physiological synchrony and group satisfaction, and concordant metacognition about content knowledg e (Dindar et al., 2019). Meanwhile, facial expressi on synchrony is found to be positively associated with collective intelligence (Chikersal et al., 2017). In contrast to psychological synchrony, team member s can observe and influence individuals' collaborative experiences through facial synchrony.  Therefore, the perception of emotion is alsonecessary for collaborative learning. According to the constructi ve view, i ndividual emotion is constructed  based on prior experiences and external stimuli (Barrett & Westlin, 2021), and group members may experience distinct emotions during collaborative learning. In this study, we us e the term “emotion synchrony” to represent the sim ilarity or consistency of group members' experienced emotions, particularly in arousal and valence synchrony. Emotion regulation and emotion synchrony Emotion regulation (ER) is the process of modifying emotional responses to achieve a goal (Gross, 2015). Emotions can be regulated in different stages (Gros s, 2015). During the activity, students can re-evaluate the situation to influence their emotional response (i.e., cognitive change, Gross, 2015). Reappraisal is the most common strategy of cognitive change, which implies reframing the situation to align it with one’s goal s (Gross, 2015). When it comes to emotions, students can modulate their cognitive responses to get a desired emotional experience. If the emotion is perceived as threatening the go al, students might attempt to block the emotional reaction, which is known as suppression. The strategies of reappraisal and suppression have antagonist effects on learning (Gross, 2015). Reappraisal is effective in downregulating negative emotions and is associated with better retention (Gross, 2015). Suppression tends to block  positive emotions but does not reduce negative one s (Gross, 2015). Further, reappraisal is positively linked to short-term and long-term health outcomes, positive affect, and cognitive outcomes, whereas suppression results in harmful consequences for overall well-being. In social situations, suppression is correlated with higher physiological arousal and less connection to peers (Butler et al., 2003). Effective ER needs integrative efforts to regulate one’s and the group’s emotions simultaneously. The link between emotional synchrony and ER is deemed a significant interaction to understand collaborative learning; however, research is still in its infancy. In this study, we aim to add new empirical evidence to understand the relationships between ER and emotion synchrony in higher education contexts. Emotion regulation and perceived challenges in collaborative learning Challenges occur in various forms, depending on contexts, task complexity, and task importance (Bradley et al., 2015). For example, challenges could be triggered by membe rs’ discrepancies in the priority of the task and working styles (Järvenoja et al., 2013). According to Järvenoja et al. (2013), group members could face collaboration challenges when there are misunderstandings about group members’ thinking or cognitive conflicts. Further, Järvenoja (2018) and her colleagues identified different challenges and their roles in triggering different group-level regulation strategies in collaborative learning.  Individual emotion regulation skills and group-level emotion regulation are pivotal to supporting a positive collaborative learning climate. If addressed appropriately, challenges could be beneficial for learning (Bradley et al., 2015). The impacts of conflicts depend on their nature and how we tackle them, as that “as we cannot avoid it, we should, I think, use it to work for us” (Graham, 2003, p. 67). A previous framework on organizational teamwork addressed two types of team conflicts (task conflict and relationship conflict), where task conflict could be beneficial and relationship conflict is detrimental  (Jehn, 1997). It is more complicated to describe the effects of t ask conflicts on a team’s function compared with the influence of relationshi p conflicts. A meta-analysis has found positive relationship s between task conflicts and team performance. Specially, the relationship of task conflicts with team performance and relationship conflicts are moderated by task complexity, trust, and team members’ emotion regula tion strategies (Bradley et al.,  2015). I ndividuals skilled in emotion regulation took advan tage of task conflict to perform effectively and limited the negative impact of relationship conflict (Jiang et al., 2013). The current study While the relationship between students’ perceived challenges and performance has been well studied, there is limited research on how students’ emotion regulation strategies affect their perceived challenge and, ultimately their emotion synchrony with peers  in collaborative learning. In this study, we addressed this research gap  by exploring the relationships among  emotion regulation strategies, students’ perceived challenges, and emotion synchrony in a natural classroom setting. Specifically, we addressed the following three research questions:  (1) Do emotion regulation strategies (i.e., cognitive reappraisal and expressive suppression) affect students’ perceived challenges in collaborative learning? (2) How do emotion regulation strategies affect students’ emotion synchrony, i.e., valence synchrony and arousal synchrony ? and (3) Does students’ perceived challenge play a mediating role in the relationship between emotion regulation and emotion synchrony?

## Participants and learning environment

A total of 84 undergraduates at a large North American University voluntarily participated in this study. The sample consisted of 40 female and 44 male students. The average age of the participants  was 20.66 (SD = 1.27). Most of  the students were in their third year of study (45. 2%), with 10.7%, 9.5%, 31.0%, and 3.6% in their preparatory, first, second, and fourth years of study, respectively. Moreover, the participants had diverse academic backgrounds (Arts and Humanities: N = 38, Management: N =16, Education: N =14, Medicine and Health Sciences: N = 16). However, they were all taking an elective course “ Creating and Using Media for Learning ”. We collected data from the participants as they accomplished classroom activities in groups. It is worth mentioning that this study had been approved by the Institutional Research Ethics Board prior to its implementation. Procedures Students were randomly assigned into 16 groups by t he instructor at the beginning of the semester. In each class, students in the same group were required to work together on that week’s topic (i.e., the use of different media in learning) and submit a final report. The data collection was conducted on the eighth week of the semester since students got familiar with their peers and the collaborative  tasks. Specifically, the surveys were sent out to participants via LimeSurvey after the in-class group activity. We will introduce the surveys in the following section. Students voluntarily completed the online questionnaires about their perceived emotions (i.e., arousal and valence), emotion regulation strategies, and collaborative challenges. Measurements This study employed self-reported questionnaires to measure students' emotio n valence and arousal, emotion regulation strategies (i.e., cognitive reappraisal and expressive suppression) and students’ perceived collaboration challenges (i.e., perceived cognitive discrepancies). Specifically, w e use d Bradley and Lang ’s (1994) Self-Assessment Manikin (SAM) scale to measure students’ perceived emotion valance and arousal. Notably, students were asked to select the most proper images in SAM regarding their level s of experienced valence and arousal. The Self-Assessment Manikin (SAM) is an inexpensive, easy me thod for assessing participants’ affective responses across contexts (Bradley & Lang, 1994) and has been used f or the self-assessment of an individual’s arousal and valence. We calculated group members’ emotion synchrony base d on individuals’ self-reported emotion arousal and valence. In the calculation, we applied mathematics algorithms to every two individuals in the same group for both dimensions (i.e., valence and arousa l). The Emotion Regulation Questionnaire (ERQ) was used to assess students’ use of emotion regulation strategies (Gross & John, 2003). The 7-point Likert scale contains ten items, and it assessed two emotion regulation strategies—reappraisal and suppression. Reappraisal refers to regulating emotions by reappraising perceived control and value in a task. Suppression is an appr oach by which students inhibit ongoing emotion-expressive emotions. The internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha) reported by previous research within university samples was.73 for the suppression subscale, and.79 for the reappraisal subscale, indicating both subscales were consistent and reliable.

The Social Challenge Scale is adapted from a part of  the Adaptive Instrument for Emotion Regulation (AIRE, Järvenoja et al., 2013). The original social challenge scale includes twelve scenarios ba sed on common social conflicts. In this study, we examined the function of perceive d challenge with a focus on the cognitive aspect of collaboration. Therefore, we chose the category of perceived collaboration challenges that match our study best. Specifically, the two collaboration challenge questions are: “Our ideas about what we should do were not the same” and “We differed in our understanding of the concepts/task”. The students were requested to indicate their experiences on a Likert scale ranging from 0 to 4 (0 = It did not happen; 4 = It was a big challenge). The internal consistency of the collaboration challenge scale was within an acceptable range from.63 to.79 at the.01 significance level (Järvenoja et al., 2013).

## Data analysis

We first performed preliminary data analyses. Afterward, we conducted the partial least squares structural equation modeling (PLS-SEM) method to analyze our data using SmartPLS 3 (Ringle et al., 2015). PLS-SEM is considered as an variance-based analysis approach since it uses the total variance to estimate parameters  (Hair et al., 2017). A notable feature of PLS-SEM is that it candeal with data of small sample sizes, allowing researchers to estimate complex models without testing distributional assumptions of the data (Hair et al., 201 7). The PLS represents a family of alternating least squares algorithms, whi ch extends the principal component and canonical co rrelation analysis (Henseler & Sarstedt, 2013). It consists of two sets of equations, i.e., a measurement model and a structural model. A measurement model  describes the relations between a latent variable and its obs erved indicators. A structural model defines the relations between unobserved or latent variables. We evaluated the indicators of the measurement model (i.e., reflective indicator loadings, internal consistency reliability, convergent validity, and discriminant validity) and the structural model (e.g., collinearity). We performed a mediation analysis to examine the role of perceived challenge in the relationship between emotion regulation and emotion synchrony.

## Results

Preliminary analysis We examined the skewness and kurtosis values of all variables for normality using SPSS version 23.0 (IBM Corp., 2015). We examined the descriptive statistics of the varia bles for the emotion regulation (i.e., cognitive reappraisal, emotion suppression strategies) survey, collaboration challenge scales and emotion synchrony values; and the results suggested that the skewness and kur tosis statistics for all variables were acceptable.  We also examined the differences across groups on each of t he variables and calculated the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) to examine whether nested analyse s were necessary. Given no differences between grou ps on the variables of interest (ps >.05) and low ICC value (ICC < 0.5), the individuals were combined into one for following processes.

Assessment of the measurement model We conducted the PLS modeling using SmartPLS3. We first assessed the quality of the constructs based on the evaluation of the measurement model. The assessment  of the quality criteria starts with evaluation of the factor loadings which is followed by establishing the construct reliability and construct validity. Reliability and validity Factor loadings represent the degree to which each item in the corr elation matrix is associated with a specific principal component. As suggested by Hair et al (2017), an acceptable value for factor loading is above 0.7, which indicates that the test term can represent most of the latent variables. After applying the PLS method, the results show that item SUP3 and SUP4 for emotion suppression, and RAP3, RAP4, RAP6  for cognitive reappraisal received lower loading than the required factor loading value of 0.7 and were therefore removed. After removing these unsuitable items, the factor loadings of the remaining items range from.70 to.97, that are sat isfied to construct an acceptable outer model.

We tested the reliability and validity of all const ructs using Cronbach's alpha, rho_A, composite reliability and Average Variance extracted (A VE). As an alternative of Chronbach’s alpha where the it ems are unweighted, rho_A is an approximately exact measure of construct reliability and represents a good com promise if one assume that the factor model is correct (Drolet & Morrison, 2001). The Cronbach's alpha and rho_A of all constructs were higher than 0.7, meeting the criterion of “satisfactory to good” reliability suggested by Drolet and Morrison (2001). Further, average variance extracted (A VE) for all items on each construct was used for evaluating the construct’s convergent validity. The composite reliability of all constructs was beyond 0.8 and the A VEs were also above the minimum threshold of 0.50, showing the research has proven reliability and validity. Discriminant validity and collinearity In this study, the discriminant validity was assessed by the Heterotrait-monotrait (HTMT) ratio of the correlations. The HTMT is defined as the mean value of the item correlations across constructs relative to the geometric mean of the average correlations for the items measuring  the same construct; and in most contexts, an value exceeds 0.90 would not suggest the present of the discriminant v alidity (Henseler et al., 2015). All constructs in the estimated model satisfied the criterion of discrimi nant validity suggested by Henseler et al (2015). In sum, the measurement model was satisfactory, and the next step was to evaluate the structural model. We then assessed the structural model by operating 5,000 bootstrapping p rocedure model operations on the data. We applied the biascorrected bootstrapping method because it employs a symptotic and resampling tactics by treating the sample as if it were the population.

Before interpretating the structural relationships, we examined collinear ity to make sure it did not bias the regression results. Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) statistics were used to evaluate the collinear ity among the constructs. All the VIF values for the structural model in the study and reveal an ideal result of less than 3. Goodness of model fit The research deliberately adopts standardized root mean square residual (SRMR) for model fitting a s PLS path modeling cannot provide a widely accepted global model fitting (Hair et al., 2017). ## Discussion

Findings from this study indicated that only the em otion regulation strategy of cognitive reappraisal has a direct and significant effect on emotion synchrony. The em otion regulation strategy of expressive suppression  has no direct effect on neither valence synchrony nor arousal synchrony. The beneficial influence of cognitive reappraisal over expressive suppression has been discussed by t he majority of studies on emotion regulation. For i nstance, John and Gross (2004) reviewed experimental finding s and synthesized that reappraisal has a healthier profile o f short-term affective, cognitive, and social consequences than suppression. Cognitive reappraisal and emotion synchrony in collaborative learning We further examined how cognitive reappraisal strat egy affected the synchrony of emotion in the dimens ions of valence and arousal. The results of the PLS-SEM indicated that cognitive reappraisal positively  and directly predicted arousal synchrony. Individu als with a higher level of cognitive reappraisal sk ills experienced more similar arousal levels within group members. This f inding corroborated previous studies on the positiv e association between physiological synchrony and met acognitive and cognitive reg ulation (Dindar et al., 2019; Malmberg et al., 2019). For instance, Dindar and colleagues (2019) found that students who had concordant metacognitive evaluations about their task-related knowledge showed more physiological synchro ny. Our study added evidence to the literature that cognitive reappraisal, as one type of emotion regulation strategies, positively contributed to arousal synchrony. One explanation is that cognitive reappraisal also falls in the sphere of cognitive regulation that requires an individual to cognitive ly reframe the situation to align  with one’s goal (Gross, 2015). In contrast, we found that cognitive reappraisal ne gatively and directly predicted valence synchrony. In other words, individuals with more cognitive reappraisal skills experienced less similarities with their pee rs’ emotion valence. According to the Control-Value theory (Pekrun & Perry, 2013), cognitive reappraisal learners adapt their way of thinking while facing different situations. Moreover, reappraisal is associated to better reten tion in front of over-positive emotions (Gross, 2015). Therefore, individuals with cognitive reappraisal  strategies are more logistic thinkers among peers and could focus more on the situation rather than immersed in the positi ve group vibe. It is therefore understandable that individua ls with higher cogniti ve reappraisal skills reported calmer and more neutral emotion valence while other group members are ‘high’ and more positive. Perceived challenges and emotion synchrony in collaborative learning The result indicated that students’ perceived chall enges (i.e., perceived cognitive discrepancies) neg atively predicted arousal similarities among peers. Cogniti ve discrepancies could lead to different arousal le vels among peers since individuals had different understanding s about the task. Other studies have shown that tea ms experiencing conflicts such as cognitive discrepanc ies or alternative solutions of the task, tend to m ake better decisions as a result of a better cognitive understanding of the issue at hand and divergent thinking (Behfar et al., 2011). Furthermore, perceived conflicts could result in a higher decision acceptance since team members perceive they were heard when discussing the issue, which may boost engagement and job commitment. Additionally, perceived challenge played a mediating role in the relationship between cognitive reappraisal and emot ion synchrony. Individuals with higher cognitive reappr aisal skills recognized more cognitive discrepancie s among peers, which negatively predicted their arousal synchrony. This mediated pattern was in line with previous findings that students with high level of emotion regulation skills could benefit from conflicts in learning  (Jiang et al.. 2013). Individuals with higher cognitive reappraisal skills may recognize cognitive discrepancies; they ‘stay calm’ to overcome the obstacles by focusing more on efficient problem-solving.

## Conclusion

This study provided insights into students’ emotions by bridging emotion synchrony with students’ percei ved challenges and emotion regulation strategies in nat ural classroom settings. We initiated a method to calculate students’ emotional synchrony in two dimensions (ar ousal and valence) that can be further investigated  together with other physiological and behavioral data. Findings informed the design of information-rich environments (e.g., social annotation, Huang et al., 2024)  by addressing aspects such a s human-computer interactions, instructional strategies, and interventions to optimize collaboration.
