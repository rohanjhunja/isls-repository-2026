# Using Embodiment to Reason About Geometry in an Integrated Computational Thinking and Mathematics Activity

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This work uses grounded and embodied cognition (GEC) perspectives to explore how young learners reason through computational thinking (CT) concepts and geometric angles in integrated mathematics and CT-enhanced robotics activities. Findings from video data reveal that different modalities (e.g., Sphero robot, Sphero.edu app, tape, a triangle path) enabled learners to reason through geometry. We highlight the importance of examining the complex contexts in which young learners learn and apply CT and mathematical knowledge. We show that embodied gestures can advance our understanding of young learners’ reasoning about geometry, particularly in robotics activities requiring spatial reasoning abilities for navigation. Analyses support claims that geometry and spatial thinking skills in integrated mathematics, CT and, robotics environments for young learners can be expressed through embodied gestures. These findings have implications for instructional practices and the design of educational technologies and activities to support students' embodiment of concepts in ways that can support learning.

## Introduction

Understanding and applying computational thinking (CT) skills is essential in today's technology-driven world. As Wing (2006) emphasized, CT skills are as fundame ntal as reading, writing, and arithmetic in shaping  every child’s analytical ability. Research ers have made significant efforts to integrate CT i nto classrooms, either by advocating for broader participation or by developi ng curricula that provide opportunities for student s to learn and build foundational CT skills (National Research  Council, 2010; Shute et al., 2017; Wing, 2006; Weintrop et al., 2022). CT integration across grades, contexts, and classrooms differs significantly based on activity and tools. Early-grade learners often have little prior experience in programming or advanced mathematical concepts due to limited exposure to tools and activities that combi ne these disciplines (Fofang et al., 2020). Identif ying young learners' understanding of mathematics concepts thr ough verbal explanations, written responses, or cre ated artifacts during robotics learning activities can be challenging. By observing learners’ interactions and embodied ways learners navigate space, move, and gesture, we can understand how their reasoning is expressed. Reasoning through mathematical ideas and notation systems is a core focus in STEM education (e.g., Schoenfeld, 1992). Scholars have increasingly explored the role of gro unded and embodied cognition (Barsalou, 2008; Glenberg & Robertson, 2000). For example, scholars have argued that we might understand mathematical i deas through metaphors involving movement or space, maki ng abstract ideas more meaningful through the body' s interaction with the world (Lakoff & Nunez, 2001). Research on embodied mathematics has explored how young children build foundational math skills by linking mathematical concepts with physical actions and sen sory experiences (e.g., Butterworth, 1999).

In more complex, multimodal activities that integrate CT and mathematics, such as activities that involve robots, bringing an embodied perspective can help d eepen our understanding of how learners reason with  and about CT. Investigating whether and how embodied ex periences facilitated by CT tools support students' mathematical reasoning and learning is essential.  This integration goes beyond coding skills to show how programming robots supports embodied understanding of geometry. It uniquely com bines CT, spatial reasoning, and physical interaction, helping young learners grasp abstract math concepts like angles through real-time, handson experience. This shift in focus to investigating mathematical r easoning in CT-enhanced robotics contexts is especially relevant to advance our understanding of  how young learners learn complex topics such as ge ometry, a branch of mathematics concerned with generalizabl e properties of shape and space. Traditional mathem atics curricula focus on lowering complexity in the presentation of the angles, primarily focusing on concrete concepts in geometry, such as identifying and naming shapes,  recognizing their attributes (l ike sides and vertices), and understanding fundamental spatial relationships (Pi er et al., 2019). As such, instruction often relies  on manipulatives and visual aids rather than engaging students in the more advanced logical reasoning of values of an angle associated with abstract mathematics (Nathan & Walk ington, 2017). Programming geometric shapes in robotics tasks requires students to use spatial rea soning skills to abstract and articulate concepts l ike angles, challenging learners to reason about space and shapes integrated into programming activities. Young learners can sometimes rely on embodied gestures to express spat ial reasoning skills during robotics programming ta sks. Investigating the role of embodied gestures in lear ning and programming geometric angles in robotics t asks for young learners could uncover how embodied experienc es and spatial reasoning could enhance students' understanding of abstract mathematical concepts such as angles and CT skills. The primary goal of this paper is to examine fourth-grade students' geometric and spatial thinking skills through an embodied lens, drawing on empirical data  from activities in which learners program robots i n integrated mathematics and CT tasks. More concretely, this work seeks to answer the following research question. How do young learners without programming experienc e express their reasoning about geometric angles wh ile programming a robot to navigate a triangle path?  We focus on the role and nature of students' embodied gestures as they program the Sphero robot to navigate geometric shapes and angles in CT tasks. We use frameworks from grounded and embodied cognition (GEC) to investigat e how young learners reason abo ut geometric shapes as they program the Sphero robot to navigate a triangle. This research is significant for extending the application of GEC frameworks to dynamic environments such as inte grated mathematics and CT robotics contexts. This advances our knowledge of how bodily experiences th rough gestures and spatial interactions can facilit ate complex reasoning processes, such as understanding and applying geometric concepts in dynamic environments. Insights from this work can inform the design of effective, evidence-based approaches to designing integrated CT and mathematics activities for early-grade learners, consequently shaping design strateg ies for both students' learning environments and teacher understanding of CT knowledge acquisition. Previous work Computational thinking in elementary grade classrooms Papert's work made a groundbreaking contribution by demonstr ating that computers can be powerful tools to support learning, especially for younger students (Papert, 1980). His work on Constructionism, alongsi de his collaborators, set the stage for what we now refer to as Computational Thinking (Wing, 2006). In response to the increasing recognition of CT as essential, a growin g number of initiatives are aimed at integrating CT  into K-12 classrooms (Weintrop et al., 2016, 2022). CT encomp asses the concepts, practices, and skills necessary  to solve problems using computational tools, including devel oping algorithms, breaking down complex problems, a nd debugging solutions (Grover & Pea, 2013; Shute et a l., 2017). Research on CT has expanded significantl y, with efforts to integrate CT into various disciplines (e.g., Bar r & Stephenson, 2011; Wilensky et al., 2014) as wel l as research focused on supporting younger learners in engaging with CT concepts (Weintrop et al., 2022). The past decade has witnessed a significant increas e in initiatives to leverage computational tools as contexts for teaching foundational CT concepts (Shu te et al., 2017; Weintrop et al., 2016), with some using mathematics as a context for embedding CT (e. g., weintrop et al, 2022). A key premise for integr ating CT into mathematics is the mutually supportive relationship  between CT and mathematics disciplinary content: C T can enhance understanding within mathematics while math ematics lends itself to relevant  contexts for applying CT concepts and practices (e.g., Abelson & diSessa, 1986; Papert, 1972; Bih et al., 2020).  This study builds on prior research that uses robotics to contextualize mathem atical concepts and engage young learners in applyi ng computational thinking practices. Scholars have argued that introducing robots into c lassrooms can support learners to develop CT skills, especially in the early  grades. (e.g., E. Kazakoff & Bers, 2012; Mioduser, Levy, and Talis, 2009). As part of integrating constructionist and programming activities into early-grade classrooms, a good number of studies have cautioned about the importance of scaffolding these activities using developmentally appropriate tools and interventions in order to mee t the cognitive expectations of learners at differe nt stages of their development (e,g. Marina U Bers, 2005, 2010; Brennan and Resnick, 2012). Recent work in STEM education emphasizes that CT is not only a logical or symbolic process but also deeply tied to learners’ physical interactions with tools and space. Embodied cognition frameworks  suggest that thinking emerges through action, perc eption, and sensorimotor experience s, aligning well with CT activities that require physi cal programming of robots or navigating spatial problems. By integrating these p erspectives, we can better understand how learners use gestures, movement, and physical enactment to reason through and express CT concepts. This study extends prior work by using a Grounded and Embodied Cognition lens to examine how young learners use gestures and bodily movement to reason about geometric angles during robot programming. Unlike earlier studies that focus on coding outcomes or verbal reasoning, this work highlights dynamic depictive gestures as tools for thinking, showing how students embody both robot and path, to understand spatial relationships. This offers new insight into how embodiment supports abstract mathematical reasoning in CT-integrated tasks. Reasoning about mathematics through embodiment A growing body of research on grounded and embodied cognition (GEC) suggests that thinking and reasoning are closely linked to body-based processes, such as gestures and physical or imagined actions. For example, gestures (Alibali & Nathan, 2012; Goldin-Meadow, 2020),  real and simulated actions (Hostetter & Alibali, 20 19), have been found to play a role in how we understand and process information. In mathematics, embodied cognition has offered potential insights into how learners can gr asp highly abstract concepts and symbols. For examp le, theoretical frameworks, such as those by Abrahamson  & Lindgren (2014) and Lakoff & Núñez (2000), sugge st that even the most abstract forms of mathematical t hinking may be grounded in bod ily experiences. While grounded and embodied cognition (GEC) theories may vary, they share some core ideas. One key idea is t hat thinking and CT are not solely based on abstract, s ymbolic operations; instead, reasoning and computat ion can engage sensory and motor systems. Additionally, programming in robotics supports embodied learning by turning abstract math concepts into physical experiences. As students program movement, they use gestures or walk paths to reason about angles and distances, grounding mathematical ideas in action and sensory feedback. Research has shown that, when planning or problem-solving without physical cues, the mind often uses mental simulations of past sensory and motor experi ences, which play a crucial role in shaping reasoni ng and problem-solving (Barsalou, 2008). Some researchers have noticed that learners often naturally and spontaneously use gestures and body movements to help them think through and communicate mathematical ideas. For example, learners have been reported using their hands to show the size of an angle, tracing the shape of a graph in the air, or moving their bodies to illustrate a transformati on like rotation or symmetry(e.g., Alibali & Nathan, 2012). These physical actions can serve as tools for explo ring mathematical concepts, making abstract ideas m ore concrete, and expressing reasoning directly and intuitively. Furthermore, most embodied mathematics research has  focused on young learners' early mathematical development, examining how body-based actions and gestures can help them understand  basic concepts (e.g., Butterworth, 1999).  Our focus on geometry for early-grade learners in robotics tasks is important becau se geometry requires students to articulate logically sound, generalized truths about shapes and spatial relationships to program robots. Exploring young learners’ geometric reasoning in dy namic robotics contexts could shed light on how embodiment and gestures can be explored to e nhance our understanding of learning through practi cal experiences in integrated mathematics and CT contexts.

Dynamic gestures and spatial reasoning Research suggests that compared to learners with lo wer spatial reasoning skills, learners with stronge r spatial reasoning skills tend to use more dynamic gestures to help them convey complex ideas about how objects  can move or change (Harel & Sowder, 2005;  Pier et al., 2019).  Real-time interactions afforded by dynamic robot behaviors can contribute to transforming abstract mathematics activities into concrete, felt experiences, deepening understanding through action. Some studies have shown that learners who used dynamic gestures were likelier to have correct insights and successfully produce corr ect proofs of mathematical concepts than those who did not (Göksun, Goldin-Meadow, Newcombe, & Shipley, 2013). Dynamic gesture s are movements of the hands, arms, or body that simulate or represent motion, transformation, or changes in objects or spatial configurations. Dynamic gestures can engage cognitive processes in ways tha t are especially useful for generalizing mathematic al reasoning. For example,  studies have reported that when learners perform a gesture to simulate a movement, like rotating an imagined triangle, their brain generates a "motor program" that predicts how the triangle might change (e.g., Chu & Kita, 2011). Th is kind of prediction may help learners make infere nces about future states or transformations of the shape or reason about the shape (Nathan & Walkington, 2017). This action-based thinking process can allow learners to predict potential out comes, consequently supporting their reasoning abou t spatial properties essential for mathematical proofs (Hoste tter & Alibali, 2019). Thus, an embodied approach t o inference-making in spatial reasoning contexts can help learners form the generalizations needed for mathematical understanding, which young learners might find challenging to create without embodied cognitive support. The data for this study was analyzed using a GEC fr amework, the Gesture as Simulated Actions (GSA) framework by Hostetter and Alibali (2019), to understand how lea rners' embodied actions and gestures contributed to the quality of their geometric reaso ning as they programmed a Sphero robot to navigate an equilateral triangle. The Gesture as Simulated Actions (GSA) framework explains that gestures arise from mental simulations of actions or perceptual states, and th eir likelihood increases when these simulations are  highly activated (Hostetter & Alibali, 2019). By examining  connections between gestures and interactions with  objects, this research seeks to identify meaningful links between embodied processes like gestures and geometric thinking and how these links facilitated learners' understanding of geometric shapes and angles in a robotics programming task.

## Methods

The Robot.Math project The Robot.Math  project is developed through a research practitione r partnership (RPP). The Robot.Math curriculum was designed through collaborations betw een researchers working on the project, a technolog y specialist, an instructional coach, and 4th-grade mathematics teachers (Authors, 2021). Robot.Math was designed to understand the feasibility of integrating CT in mathematics classrooms and how this cross-disciplinary integration can support student’s unde rstanding of CT while deepening their understanding  of mathematical concepts. Each lesso n includes materials for both students and teachers, with teacher-facing materials clearly defining the lesson objectives and outlining how the lesson aligns with the 4th-grade Common Core Mathematics Standards, followed by the district. ## Setting and participants

The study took place over two years in a 4th-grade mathematics classroom in an urban public scho ol district in the Mid-Atlantic region of the United States. The school has a total enrollment of 255 students with the following racial breakdown: 45.1% Hispanic/Latino, 52.2% Black/African American, 2% White non-Hispanic, 0.8% Asian or Asian/Pacific Islande r. This study involved focal students identified and selected for observation by the classroom teacher based on their ability to participate and express t hemselves in group activities. For this study, we share video data and field notes of one student’ s interactions from a group of three students (in one focal group) as they programmed the Sphero robots to navi gate a triangle to ground their understanding of CT  and mathematical concepts.

## Data collection

The primary data sources for this study were videos collected during the Sphero.Math lessons and student artifacts, including images of codes written in notebooks and images of their programs. We collected close-up video footage using phones, iPads, head-mounted cameras worn by students, and stationary cam eras positioned throughout the activities. This setup allowed us to capture data o n student-to-student interactions and perspectives, focusing on students' interactions with the Sphero robot as they completed 14 lessons of integrated CT and mathematics tasks. For this particular study, the focus on one student allows for in-depth analysis of gestures and embodied reasoning, offering rich insights into how CT and geometry connect in action. While not generalizable, this approach surfaces detailed patterns that can inform future research and broader studies.

## Data analysis

We used coarse-grained interaction analysis (IA) for data analysis. We provide analysis in the form of data captured in ways that provide opportunities for rep eated examination, allowing for alternative interpr etations supported by evidence through transcripts from reco rdings and observations (Sacks, 1995; Atkinson & Heritage, 1984). Our data analysis was conducted as follows. Initial ly, team members reviewed all recorded videos to identify and segment video clips with instances whe re students used embodied gestures to navigate task s. This process was accompanied by group discussions to ref ine our observations (Jordan & Henderson, 1995). We anchored our insights in concrete evidence of embodied practices observed, recorded on field notes, and captured on video. One researcher documented descriptive and analytical memos based on transcripts from video segments where students used embodiment to complete tasks. We maintained flexibility in our analysis in order to uncover and explore forms of embodied gestures and actions that  learners used during activities, leading to fresh perspectives on articulating and interpreting the d ata in ways to provide opportunities, “space and pl ace for exploration and discovery” (Charmaz, 2006, p. 81 –82). One researcher compiled all the memos and vide o links into a shared spreadsheet, including specific evidence of observations. This structure enabled the team to analyze within and across memos.

Finally, the research team analyzed these memos using the GSA framework (Hostetter & Alibali, 2019) to highlight instances where students used embodied  gestures while programming the Sphero robot to navigate a triangle path. Video segments were selected through a review of all recorded sessions, focusing on moments when students were actively programming or discussing ro bot movement. Segments were flagged when students u sed their hands, arms, or body to simulate motion, dire ction, or spatial transformatio ns. We narrowed our focus to analyze only gestures that reflected dynamic depictive gestures, defined by GEC frameworks as hand or body movements that illustrate changes or transformation s in shapes and spatial relationships  (Nathan et al., 2021, p.7). For example, three kinds of gestures were tak en into consideration in our analysis:  gestures that visually demonstrated properties that can be generalized, such as enlarging or shrinking (dilating) and slanting or distorting (skewing) shapes,  providing us with insights as to how learners are r easoning about geometric shapes, gestures that visually depicted movement, such as turning, r otating, or walking the robot's path  and, m ovements that corresponded with specific mathematical ideas (e.g., angles, direction changes).

## Findings

In our selected observations, we report instances when one student from our focal group used embodied gestures aligned with dynamic depictive gestures to reason a bout an equilateral triangle's geometric shapes and  angles. Throughout the activity, the learner used dynamic depictive gestures in three instances: (a) swerving his palm and body to depict the turns (angle) the robot would ma ke; (b) walking on the triangle path, making adjust ments on the virtual protractor on the iPad, meas uring the triangle’s angles using a physical protra ctor, and (c) walking on the triangle with his right hand stretched out in front, without the virtual or physical protractor. He repeatedly used his palm to demonstrate how he anti cipated the robot would move, emphasizing the angle s with the movement of his hand.

The student’s decision to embody both the robot and the path into his cognitive schema demonstrates the student’s reliance on embodiment and gesturing as t ools to support him refin e his reasoning and ideas about mathematical concepts. Further, in reasoning about how the robot can move or change direction, he conn ects his spatial awareness and kinesthetic sense with the ro bot’s path. By walking along the triangle’s path, t he student does not explicitly apply mathematical formulas or calculations to determine the angles. Measuring and simulating angles with tools and gestures The third example is from clips from two consecutiv e lessons on programming triangles. We observed a t hird instance where the student used a physical protractor to measure the angles of the triangle, as seen in Figure 4 (a) and (b). The learners used a phy sical protractor to measure the 60-degree inner angles of the equilateral triangle and then entered values of the angles into their programs for the Sphero to navigate. The 60-degree angles entered did not make the robot roll as desired. After sever al tries, the robot kept rolling off the triangle’s  path. Sphero robots are designed to use a geocentric reference system rather than an egocentric system. Geocentric systems use a global reference frame based on the external environment, like the GPS or compass. Sphero robots are designed to move forward (0 degrees), move right (90 degrees), etc, using an internally designed compass system. For example, if the first angle the robot has to turn i s 60, which is the case for an equilateral triangle, the next angle needs to be 180, i.e. (60 + 120). The learner grappled with understanding the geometric angles by referencing the robot’s start position as scaffolds. After making measurements with a physical and virtual protractor, the student walked on the triangle’s path with his right hand s tretched, this time without the virtual or physical  protractors (see Figure 5 (a), (b), (c)).

The student use d his body and gestures to map the path of the equila teral triangle into his cognitive schema. The student assumed the robot operated using an egocentric system, where navigation and perception are based on the robot's local frame of reference. With out formal knowledge of such systems, the student r elied on his own physical movements to align and ground his understanding of the robot’s coordinate system. To program the Sphero robot to navigate an equilateral triangle, the vertice (angle) of 60 degrees for each vertice (angle) must be translated into the robot's “direction” (angle) on the roll block. For instance, the robot must mak e three 120degree left turns, or 240-degree right turns at the vertices to follow an equilateral triangle path. These adjustments in understanding angles in dynamic activities with moving objects signifi cantly shift from the static 60-degree angles typically taught in pen-and-paper contexts, presenting a challenge for young learners. The learner’s spatial reasoning skills displayed in this activity were visible through his use of depictive dynamic gestures, through his use of hand gestures, and walking on the triangle’s path to simulate the robot movement. The learner’s consistent use of depictive dynamic gestures in this activity suggests that he engaged in action-based thinking as part of his cognitive processes, using embodied gestures to sup port his reasoning about the triangle’s spatial pro perties in ways that suggest his geometric (mathematical) reas oning of the path and the angles.  This example illustrates a shift from external measurement to internalized, em bodied reasoning,  providing evidence of  how physical interaction and repeated gesture use  can help internalize geometric concepts, supporting lea rning through embodied simulation.

## Discussions

Our findings indicate that the focal student’s thinking, reasoning, and learning were deeply intertwined with their embodied gestures and manipulations of tools in the  environment. We found that the student used embodi ed gestures to reason about the geomet ric shape of a triangle. In all of our selected obs ervations, the learner consistently used dynamic depictive gestures in thr ee instances: swerving his palm and body to depict the turns(angle) the robot would make, walking on the triangle path, making adjustments on the virtual protractor on the iPad, measuring the triangle’s angles using a p hysical protractor, and walking on the triangle wit h his right hand stretched out in front, without the virtual or  physical protractor. Examining these gestures and embodied actions supports us in making inferences about what  is happening in terms of geometric reasoning (Host etter & Alibali, 2019).

The dynamic object (the robot) incorporated into th e activity provoked the affordance of emergent dynamic gesturing by the learner. In this kind of a ctivity, where learners are tasked with programming  a Sphero robot to navigate a triangular path, learners find opportunities to walk on the path, embodying both the robot and the geometric shape of the path within their cognit ive schema. Through gestures, such as hand movement s and body swerves, to depict directions and angles, lear ners engage in embodied, eme rgent experiences that deepen their understanding of geometric shapes (Nathan & W alkington, 2017). Our observations revealed that le arners without prior knowledge of the Sphero robot's inter nal coordinate system or the geometric properties o f an equilateral triangle relied on embodiment and gesturing reasoning to interpret logical turns (angles) and directions the robot will travel on the triangle (Alibali & Nathan, 2012; Nathan et al., 2021). Integrated CT and mathematics activities involving programming robots to navigate geometric paths require learners to recognize and apply computation al and theoretical mathematics ideas in practical w ays. This entails understanding and bridging the gap bet ween abstract geometric concepts in theoretical mat hematics and their practical application in problem-solving activities. This highlights the power of in tegrating CT into mathematics lessons to build a conceptual understanding of how young learners reason  about complex mathematics concepts like angles when they program robots to navigate geometric shapes. CT robotics activities integrated into mathematics lessons integrate a wide variety of tools and scaffolds that provide opportunities for young learners witho ut programming or advanced logical-mathematical reasoning to use gestures and embodiment to support computati onal practices, complex cognitive interactions, and  engage in mathematical reasoning that can be observable through grounded embodied cognition lens. Like all studies, this work has its limitations. We focused our observations on just one student in a focal group, to understand how he used embodied gestures to reason about geometric shapes (equilateral triangle). Not all learners may have used embodied gestures to reason about shapes and angles as they programmed their robots to navigate geometric paths. We, therefore, cannot generalize or apply certain learning claims about young learners’ use of embodied gestures to reason about geometry in robotics programming tasks. Studies wit h larger sample sizes could offer valuable insights into the  role of embodied gestures in understanding geometr ic reasoning.

## Conclusion

Examining complex contexts in which participants le arn mathematics concepts like geometry in CT roboti cs contexts requires attending to learners' gestures a nd embodied actions. While geometric angles are int roduced as part of the upper elementary mathematics curriculum lessons, they present significant complexity once introduced into robotics programming activities. We presented instances where participants used gestures and embo diment to reason about geometric shapes (triangles). Our w ork lays the foundation for further research into t he role of embodiment and gestures in integrated mathematics, and CT-enhanced robotics tasks. Specifically, we aim to build on our findings to explore the discursive nature of learning with CT tools, such as robots, particularly when integrated into mathematics lessons. Our findings suggest that teachers can integrate CT and  math by using robotics tasks that involve movement, gesture, and physical tools. Encouraging students to act out paths and spot patterns supports both spatial reasoning and algorithmic thinking, making abstract concepts more concrete.
