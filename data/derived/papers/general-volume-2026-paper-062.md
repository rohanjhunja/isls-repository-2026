# Advancing a Learning Sciences Approach to Quantum Information Science in K-12

**Conference:** ISLS 2026

## Abstract

My research examines learning in complex, rapidly evolving technological domains, particularly quantum information science. Bridging multiple theoretical perspectives, I am currently pursuing two directions: designing learning environments that make ultra-complex STEM accessible to K-12 learners; and theorizing quantum computational thinking to identify concepts, practices, and perspectives that distinguish quantum from classical reasoning. Research agenda and theoretical framing In my research, I am committed to understanding how emerging technologies shape and support learning. I pursue this understanding both via conceptual inquiry and via the design of learning environments that help learners navigate complex, rapidly evolving domains. While some of my previous (e.g., Coelho & McCollum, 2025) and ongoing work involves empirical investigations using AI and NLP technologies to support teaching and learning across K-12 and post-secondary contexts (e.g., Coelho et al., 2025a), this submission focuses on my research in K-12 quantum information science (QIS). My scholarship is primarily grounded in learning sciences traditions that view learning as situated and mediated by material and social interactions (e.g., Cole, 1998; Moll et al., 2006; Pea, 1985, 1993a, 1993b; Rogoff, 2003). I adopt an integrationist stance (Danish & Gresalfi, 2018), deliberately examining learning across one or more interrelated planes (Rogoff, 2008), while critically attending to the analytic affordances and limitations of this approach and maintaining a commitment to understanding the dynamic interplay among learners, social interactions, and context. I also draw on complementary perspectives that have traditionally emphasized the individual – such as constructivism (Kafai, 2012; Papert & Harel, 1991; Wilensky, 2020) – and cognitive sciences (e.g., Bandura, 2010) as generative for understanding and supporting meaning-making, agency, and motivation. I utilize these complementary perspectives to inform my conceptual and empirical contributions to K-12 quantum information science education as described in the subsequent sections. Thereafter, I discuss future directions centered on studying and designing for quantum computational thinking. Current work When I began reviewing the literature on K-12 quantum information science education (Coelho et al., 2025b), I was surprised to find that learning sciences researchers, who have spent decades studying how students understand complex scientific concepts, were almost entirely absent from the conversations. This absence seemed particularly striking given that learning quantum information science requires learners to grapple with ideas that defy classical intuition and mathematical formalisms that create barriers to accessibility even at the college level, let alone in K-12 settings. These are precisely the kinds of challenges that learning sciences researchers have developed expertise in addressing: making complex, abstract concepts accessible through appropriate representational tools, scaffolding, and cultural practices that support new ways of thinking. And just as this expertise is most needed, national initiatives are pushing for quantum education to begin in middle and high school (NSF, 2020). While computer science and physics education researchers (e.g., Franklin et al., 2020; Singh et al., 2022) have begun important work in this space, the conversations have yet to substantially engage with learning sciences frameworks and methods that could complement and extend these disciplinary efforts. Our review also showed that both theoretical grounding and design conjectures in K-12 QIS education research remain limited. Many initiatives simply ask, “Does it work?” in a Thomas Edison exploratory framework (Stokes, 2011), without interrogating why or under what conditions learning occurs, risking innovations that engage learners but do not reliably support deep understanding. My position paper (Coelho et al., 2025d) argues for a bidirectional opportunity that neither field has fully recognized. QIS education could leverage learning sciences expertise in design-based research to iteratively develop quantum learning environments. This includes principled approaches for creating new representational infrastructures (Wilensky, 2020) that make quantum phenomena accessible while preserving mathematical integrity, as well as participation structures that allow learners to collectively make sense of quantum phenomena, negotiate meaning through shared representations, and transform their participation in disciplinary practices (Lemke, 2001; Pea, 1992; Rogoff et al., 1995). But equally important, and perhaps more provocative, is what q uantum information science offers the learning sciences: a domain that pushes us to reconsider our theories of conceptual change, representation, and learning at the boundaries of human intuition. To put these conceptual arguments into practice, my recent empirical work (Coelho et al., 2025c) investigates how collaborative game making (Fields & Kafai, 2018) leveraging distributed expertise (Pea, 1993) can support students in developing initial mental models of quantum information science concepts and practice while cultivating curiosity, confidence, and identity – early pathways toward a “competent outsider” stance (Sandoval et al., 2025). We found that designing quantum-themed board games created accessible entry points supporting participation without requiring advanced formalism, though supplementary social and material support will also be incorporated in future iterations of the work. Throughout this process, groups mobilized diverse expertise and resources to materialize quantum ideas, illustrating how even quantum-adjacent activities candrive motivation, sensemaking, and participation in ultra-complex STEM domains. Future work Building on my prior work in K-12 quantum information science education, I have begun to articulate a tentative framework for quantum computational thinking (QCT), extending classical computational thinking to the distinct concepts, practices, and perspectives of quantum information science, while attending to the ethical, societal, and justice-related dimensions of access to quantum technologies (Coelho et al., 2025e). This exploration identifies key concepts (e.g., randomness, interference, superposition), practices (e.g., designing probabilistic algorithms, leveraging amplitude amplification), and perspectives (e.g., understanding computation as materially grounded, designing under indeterminacy) that distinguish QCT from classical computational reasoning, while also emphasizing equitable participation and the social and ethical stakes of access to quantum technologies. Conceptually, this work considers how engagement with QCT might prompt learners to develop non-classical intuitions, reason under uncertainty, and approach computation, abstraction, and scientific problem-solving in fundamentally new ways. Looking forward, my K-12 research agenda focuses on two interrelated directions. First, inspired by the STEM novice/expert research literature (Bransford et al., 2000; Erickson et al., 2006) and Science-Technology-Studies investigations of laboratory life (e.g., Latour, 2005; Latour & Woolgar, 1986), I aim to ground a QCT framework in empirical studies of how quantum information scientists think and work, examining the concepts they use, the practices they enact, and the perspectives they bring to reasoning with quantum phenomena and technologies. Second, I plan to build on these insights by designing and iteratively refining K-12 learning environments that scaffold engagement with QCT, leveraging new representational infrastructures and collaborative practices that invite students into developmentally appropriate, quantum-adjacent forms of authentic inquiry. Such activities may include designing games, music, or visualizations that incorporate principles like amplitude amplification or probabilistic reasoning, enabling learners to make abstract quantum ideas tangible, expressive, and personally meaningful. While practical quantum computers remain on the horizon, progress toward them is uncertain and uneven (Eisert & Preskill, 2025). They are not expected to replace classical systems; instead, future systems will likely integrate both paradigms (Preskill, 2018). The field is already shaped by substantial financial investment to address the enormous hardware challenges. Yet an even greater obstacle lies in identifying problems for which quantum approaches provide decisive advantages, beyond those canonical examples discovered decades ago. How we approach quantum reasoning and learning today will influence not only who gets to participate in shaping this emerging field, but also what kinds of problems we even recognize as solvable. First, inspired by the STEM novice/expert research literature (Bransford et al., 2000; Erickson et al., 2006) and Science-TechnologyStudies investigations of laboratory life (e.g., Latour, 2005; Latour & Woolgar, 1986), I aim to ground a QCT framework in empirical studies of how quantum information scientists think and work, examining the concepts they use, the practices they enact, and the perspectives they bring to reasoning with quantum phenomena and technologies. Second, I plan to build on these insights by designing and iteratively refining K-12 learning environments that scaffold engagement with QCT, leveraging new representational infrastructures and collaborative practices that invite students into developmentally appropriate, quantum-adjacent forms of authentic inquiry. Such activities may include designing games, music, or visualizations that incorporate principles like amplitude amplification or probabilistic reasoning, enabling learners to make abstract quantum ideas tangible, expressive, and personally meaningful. While practical quantum computers remain on the horizon, progress toward them is uncertain and uneven (Eisert & Preskill, 2025). They are not expected to replace classical systems; instead, future systems will likely integrate both paradigms (Preskill, 2018). The field is already shaped by substantial financial investment to address the enormous hardware challenges. Yet an even greater obstacle lies in identifying problems for which quantum approaches provide decisive advantages, beyond those canonical examples discovered decades ago. How we approach quantum reasoning and learning today will influence not only who gets to participate in shaping this emerging field, but also what kinds of problems we even recognize as solvable. The learning sciences contribution, then, is not auxiliary to quantum computing’s development; rather, it is central to its realization. Bandura, A. (2010).
