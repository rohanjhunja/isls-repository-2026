# Identifying Productive Reflection in Ill-Structured Problem Solving

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Ill-structured problems are common in the workplace but difficult for undergraduate students to learn. Within engineering, one ill-structured problem is engineering estimation, which involves determining an approximate value for a physical quantity in a system. Researchers have developed multi-phase modeling-based learning environments to support the learning of estimation. Although this software scaffolds the steps involved in estimation for students, it is still an open question as to how students engage in these learning processes. In this work, we analyze the interaction sequences of students during the engineering estimation process and how they relate to performance. High performing students engage in significantly more patterns of evaluation and planning concurrently with modeling, compared with lower performing students. These findings highlight the importance of additional interventions to trigger the evaluation and planning processes among learners.

## Introduction and Related Work

Consider this problem: “ You have to design a light aircraft of maximum take-off weight of 15000 kg. It should be able to take off in 1000 m and less than 30 seconds. Its air speed at 40000 ft above sea level should be about 750 km/hr. Estimate the power of the engine you need to  design such an aircraft. ” This is an example of an illstructured problem as it has multiple solutions, so lution approaches, uncertainties and evaluation cri teria (Jonassen, 2000). Such ill-structured problems are common in engineering when there is incomplete information about the exact operating conditions and the features of the machinery (Linder, 1999). In these situations, experts solve problems by understanding the problem space, making assumptions and scoping the problem, generat ing possible solutions and selecting one, and implementing and monitoring the solution (Jonassen, 1997). Monitoring their progress and intermittently evaluating their goals is an integral part of the expert problem-sol ving process (Kothiyal, Murthy & Chandrasekharan, 2016). Student s have difficulties with ill-structured problems be cause solving them requires students to regulate their cognition by planning and evaluating their goals, and monitoring their progress (Shin et al., 2003). Scaffolding the se processes can help students learn ill-structured  problem solving (Ge & Land, 2004). Researchers have developed learning technologies to scaffold these problem-solving processes (Tawfik et al, 2018), but less is known about how students use these tools for effective learning. For scaffolds to be effective at supporting student  learning, we need to understand whether and how students engage with these scaffolds. Previous work  has begun to investigate these learning processes and researchers have found that successful students are able to connect task activities to domain knowledge (Wen et al., 2018), follow incremental approaches (Blikstei n et al., 2014), and use more cycles of testing and  pausing (Perez et al., 2017) compared with less successful students. These examples support that students who engage in more reflection while problem solving may have high er learning. However, it is an open question of how  these results may transfer to another ill-structured problem-solving context, as the frequency of reflection required may vary for different kinds of problems. Therefore, an analysis of students’ learning processes is necessary to better understand the interplay between problem-solving and reflective activities within a scaffolded ill-str uctured problem-solving context, and how that relates to learning. Such an analysis can support our understanding of when to intervene in the learning process to effectively support student learning. The goal of this work is to investigate the differential sequential action patterns of students who succeed vs those who do not at engineering estimation probl em-solving, which is an ill-structured problem wher e engineers need to determine an approximate value fo r a quantity (such as power required by the aircraf t in the example above). To investigate the sequences of act ions that students engage in during learning, we us ed the modeling-based estimation learning environment (MEt tLE) which divides the learning of estimation probl em solving into six phases (Kothiyal & Murthy, 2018) with scaffolds to support every phase of the problem solving such as question prompts and hints (Ge & Land, 2004). We address the research question, what are the sequential action patterns of learners as they learn estimatio n problem-solving, and how do they relate to perfor mance? Specifically, we are interested in how learners eng age in problem understanding and reflective actions  during problem solving and revisit previous phases. We hypothesize that students who have higher performance would interplay more frequently between modeling and refl ection activities, and revisit previous phases more  as they reflect on past actions.

## Methods

Learning Activity MEttLE structures engineering estimation problems into six tasks (three modeling tasks, a calculation task, a final evaluation task and a reflection task) with the modeling tasks being further divided into three subtasks, and some subtasks having multiple steps (Figure 1). The thre e modeling tasks, based on the expert estimation pr ocess (Kothiyal, Murthy & Chandrasekharan, 2016), are called functional, qualitative and quantitative modeling. Within each modeling task, students have model creation, e valuation and planning subtasks (details and screenshots of the software are available in Kothiyal and Murthy (2018)) that include questions for triggering the metacognitive processes of monitoring the model, evaluation and planning such as “While estimating power for a design, would you determine the maximum, average or instantaneous power required? Why?”. The functional and qualitative model creation together constitute problem understa nding. The final evaluation task includes questions  for triggering the evaluation of the calculated estimate. The problem is considered complete when the estimate passes given evaluation criteria. After solving the problem, students complete a reflection task which includes questions asking them to reflect on the estimation process. MEttLE is an open-ended intervention in which students cando a task/subtask at any time, use the affordances as they see fit and do the (sub)tasks as many times as  they wish. During the process, all actions are logged by the system.

## Participants and Data Collection

We analyzed data from a field study in an engineering college with 72 second year students (50 mechanical and 22 electronics engineering) who solved a problem in  MEttLE as part of a workshop. The average age of t he learners was 20 years. The study began with informed consent being gathered from all participants. Afterwards, participants individually solved one problem in MEttLE, namely, “You are participating in an electric car race in which you are required to design an electric car of weight 5kg with wheel diameters of 4'' that can traverse a track of 50m in less than 5 seconds. Estimate the e lectrical power needed to achieve this performance.” While solving, students were not allowed to use the inter net but could use all the resources in MEttLE and a sk the researcher for technical help. As a posttest, participants individually solved an estimation problem on paper similar to the one in MEttLE, without any additional resources or help. We collected logs of participants' interactions in MEttLE, including entered data with timestamps. Owing to technical issues, we were able to recover the logs of only 22 participants, which are used in our analysis.

Analysis Approach We graded the posttests using the rubric for assessing estimation given in Kothiyal & Murthy (2018). For every criterion that was satisfied, we awarded one point, for a total of three points. Participants who scored three points were classified as high performing students and all  others were classified as low performing students.  We had three learners in the high performing group and nineteen learners in the low performing group. To identify interesting behaviors that are associated with the engineering estimation process and can be used to differentiate high and low performing students, we used differential sequence mining (DSM) (Kinnebrew ICLS2022 Proceedings 1074 © ISLS et al., 2013). DSM identifies sequences of actions that are common within a group of students and by comparing these action sequences between groups, we can better understand how they are associated with performance. To perform the DSM, we used the log data from MEttLE to get all student actions and coded each action as the step the student was working on to align with the flow s een in Figure 1. For example, the functional model creation subtask is split across two steps and any student action in step 1 is coded FM1 and any action in step 2 is coded FM2. Thus, the action coding corresponds exactly to the problem-solving step the student is at. Some steps have multiple questions that require inputs leading to s equences such as “FE2 -> FE2 ->FE2”, which indicate  either that a student entered responses to different quest ions on the page or revised their previous response. These sequences of coded student actions serve as the inp ut to the DSM algorithm that identifies the most fr equent patterns. We implemented the algorithm using the LASAT tool (Mishra et al., 2019) with the s-support set to 0.5, which means that the algorithm returns all patterns that are 1) present in at least 50% of the teams in at least one group, high or low performing and 2) whose frequenc y of occurrence in a team (i-support) was statistic ally significantly different between the two groups.

## Discussion and Conclusion

In this work, we investigated the estimation learni ng processes of students, a kind of ill-structured problem commonly encountered in engineering education. Spec ifically, we used differential sequence mining with  data from students working with the MEttLE learning software to investigate how high and low performing students use reflection and revisiting actions in their engineering estimation learning process. Experts tend to spend more time exploring and re-representing the problem, and intermittently evaluate their work (Wankat & Oreovicz, 2015; Kothiyal, Murthy & Chandrasekharan, 2016), and we found similar problem-understanding and reflection patterns with our high performing students. These actions are promising to find within the engineering estimation process, as these behaviors are known to be productive for ill-structured problem solving (Wankat & Oreovicz, 2015; Shin et al. 2003; Jonassen, 1997). More concerning is th e fact that these same actions were present to a si gnificantly lesser extent with low performing students as these  actions were explicitly scaffolded inside of MEttL E. The students were provided with question prompts at fre quent intervals to trigger the problem-understandin g (functional and qualitative model creation) and reflection (model evaluation and planning) processes. However, ICLS2022 Proceedings 1075 © ISLS only high performers used the scaffolds and perform ed problem-understanding, evaluation and planning. These results are interesting because it is not that low performing students tried to engage in these types of actions and still did not perform well; this makes it much more  likely that the lack of problem-understanding and reflection actions may be directly connected to the low performance. It may then be the case that providing scaffolding in an open-ended intervention is not enough. More meta-cognitive scaffolding may be needed to guide students to do these problem-understanding and reflection actio ns at regular intervals until they are able to buil d these processes into their implicit problem-solving process.

Although there are several limitations to our stud y, the work shows promising results in understanding the impact of students’ problem-understanding and reflective actions and the role that learning scaffolding plays in this process. First, we did not find that students engaged in reflective quantitative modeling and final evaluation, and cycles of modeling, evaluating and planning, wh ich may be due to students not having enough time. Additionally, we had a small sample size of 22 students and a skew with 3 high performers and 19 low performers. All of these factors may have had an impact on our results. However, these results add to the current literature by both replicating the importance of problem-understa nding and reflective actions within ill-structured problem solving, and extending the literature by showing th e limitations of scaffolding this process without e xplicitly providing support for students who may not have the skills to know when and how to engage in these productive problem-solving processes. Future work is needed to explore how additional guidance should be incorporated to ensure that students do the problem-understanding, evaluation and planning tasks.
