# Collaborative Lore Development as a Mediational Bridge Between Material and Playworlds

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study examines how playful, collaborative development of project-related lore can mediate learning in a making-focused STEM learning environment. We present a case study of two intersectionally-marginalized seventh-grade girls who initially distanced themselves from STEM-related materials and practices but came to create a robotic character, “Mr. Evil French Cheeto”. To understand the evolution of their participation, we employ a Vygotskian perspective on play, extended by theories of storytelling, ima ginative world-building, and identity-resources. Through a microethnographic analysis of video and audio data from a ten-week in-school maker program, we explore how lore — the evolving narrative about the character and his world —generated iterative exchanges between the girls’ imaginative playworld and the constraint-laiden material world. Our findings suggest that collaborative lore development can be a promising avenue for supporting agency, authorship, and problem-solving, and may have the potential to open up expansive pathways for equitable participation in STEM-related learning environments.

A central design question facing the learning sciences is how to design learning spaces that invite young people into meaningful participation in STEM practices, especially youth from historically marginalized groups. We report here on two low-SES seventh-grade girls of color who shift from initially distanc ing themselves from engineering materials and practices, to designing and building a sophisticated robotic character. We employ the construct of playworlds to analyze the mediated means through which youth and program facilitators collaboratively contributed to the construction and development of their project. Drawing from video and audio data across a ten-week maker program, we consider how the participants’ lore (emergent and evolving imaginative narratives) within the playworld shaped and was shaped by their work. We examine how the co-construction of a playworld and its lore mediated the girls’ engagement with tools and practices in the space: that is, how the development of the project played into the lore development, and the lore development shaped the construction of the project.

Making and the so-called “maker movement” have been heralded as a potential force for democratization and expanded participation in STEM, largely through positioning learners as creators of knowledge through their design and construction of personally meaningful artifacts (Blikstein, 2013). Equity in making is far from guaranteed – scholars highlight that learning environments must address not only access to tools and practices but also a hierarchy of epistemic knowledge related to whose ways of knowing are  valued (Calabrese Barton & Tan, 2018; Vakil, 2018; Vossoughi et al., 2016). Although making practices are widespread across racial, ethnic, and socio-economic lines (Gutiérrez et al., 2017), school-based making contexts hold promise for expanding access to maker toolsets and disciplinary STEM discourses. Notably, school-based making is often geared towards meeting disciplinary goals and is reformatted to fit institutional structures and constraints (Lemée & Blikstein, 2025), and school-based programs risk losing the complexity and “essence” of making when their approach is highly structured or assessment-driven (Bevan, 2017). Yet, when handled with care, they can invite youth assets and ingenuity into conversation with disciplinary ideas and tools (Godhe et  al., 2019; Heredia & Tan, 2021). A core question is how to create environments that support this convergence, subverting cultural narratives that suggest maker tools and practices are “not for” marginalized youth in the space, flattening hierarchies among teachers and learners, and opening space for heterogeneous assets, pathways, and everyday ingenuities, as entry points for engaging with STEM-rich subject matter in their own way (e.g., Calabrese Barton et al., 2021; Carlone & Mercier, 2023; Gravel et al., 2021).

1832 Play and storytelling To preview our results, we found that the focal girls in our study engaged in extensive and playful world-building as part of their maker practices. In anticipation of sharing those findings, we consider research on play and storytelling in STEM learning environments. Playful, hands-on experiences (including those within makingcentered learning)  have been shown to provide powerful entry points into engineering practices for intersectionally marginalized girls (Calabrese Barton, Tan, & Greenburg, 2017) and can create opportunities for learners to experiment with materials previously seen as incompatible with their identity (Carlone et al., 2015).  Lindqvist (1995), synthesizing Vygotsky’s theories on play, art, culture, signs, and aesthetics as they apply to learning and development, coined the term “playworlds” – co-constructed imaginary worlds that can offer opportunities for participants to explore ideas, relationships, practices, and identities through narrative. In our context, one important emergent form of play (and building of playworlds) occurred through storytelling. Storytelling can benefit a wide range of STEM skills, but more research is needed design and report on interventions for diverse populations (Cabrera et al., 2025). Within the space of storytelling, we introduce the idea of lore  to describe emergent and evolving shared narrative s between actors in the classroom space. Lore refers to a specific type of storytelling, originally in reference to an oral storytelling tradition, but more recently describing forms of fantasy worldbuilding. Hergenrader (2011) found collaborative worldbuilding promising for scaffolding creative writing and game design. Lore can consist of character arcs, storylines, jokes, etc., that emerge from playful, imaginative, collaborative worldbuilding, and it can act as a mediational substrate or bridge between the perceived available resources in the playworld and the physical world.

## Theoretical framework

We see learning as a relational process that is mediated by exposure to and engagement in cultural practices, relationships, tools, and symbols (Vygotsky, 1978; Rogoff, 2003). Models of mediation include both material tools (e.g., motors, batteries, craft supplies) and symbolic resources (e.g., relational and ideational identityresources, lore-development, and imaginary play) (Carlone & Mercier, 2023; Holland, 2001; Nasir & Cooks, 2009). Each learning environment is associated with a different subset of tools and resources that can be both explicitly and implicitly featured.

Our analysis considers how the girls’ imagined and physical worlds were co-constructed and co-evolved in response to one another. For example, how the lore about the character influenced the design and construction of the project and how the material constraints of constructing the robot led to changes in the narrative about the character. Amidst this reciprocal movement between the playworld and the material world, playful lore development became a bridge that connected the two dimensions, creating opportunities for mutual engagement. Specifically, we see lore as a substrate that can uncover identity resources for expansive participation (Nasir & Cooks, 2009). The playworld can be designed to be free of the figurative and actual constraints present in the physical world. Actual barriers  are primarily in respect to the available tools, materials, and skill sets available in the space, which are governed by cultural norms (Holland, 2001) which can often be [unknowingly or intentionally] couched in the pervasive “matrix of oppression” (Collins, 2022). The term "figurative barriers" refers to ingrained stories about what one believes one can and cannot do in a space (Doty, 2024).  When a facilitator positions themself as a player in the playworld or a collaborator positions the student as an expert in the playworld, the perceived hierarchy can flatten, and new opportunities may emerge. We conceptualize lore as a mediational bridge between material world and playworld – lore can hold translational and generative power.

## Context, data, and analysis

This paper is part of a larger analysis of how historically marginalized seventh-grade girls engaged in learning and play within a robot art-making program. The program took place over the course of eight weeks, amounting to ten ~50-minute class periods. According to our partnering teacher, funding cuts had eliminated arts education opportunities at the middle school, and her students had not been exposed to engineering education. Our program was designed to provide access to these domains by supporting students in designing, building, and coding interactive art pieces. We provided a loose prompt  – “make an interactive art piece for a collaborative peace garden”—and provided materials such as microcontrollers, servo and hobby motors, LEDs, sensors, actuators, cardboard, and assorted craft and art supplies.  Each session followed a consistent structure: a brief opening activity, presentation of engineering and design practices, time for individual and group planning, extended time for hands-on building and coding, and a reflection exercise. The classroom was facilitated by the first author, the regular classroom teacher, and undergraduate mentors co-enrolled in a university practicum course. Our data corpus includes weekly video and audio recordings of seven groups of girls (2– 4 participants per group), ethnographic fieldnotes produced by the lead mentor and undergraduate mentors (following Emerson, 1833 Fretz, & Shaw, 2011), and written reflections submitted by students at the end of each session. The current analysis centers on two focal participants, Rebekah and Yozaima (pseudonyms), who worked on a single project together. We employed a microethnographic approach (Garcez, 1997), attending to fine-grained interactional detail of how participants engage with one another, their environment, and sensemaking practices. We foreground here a set of pivotal moments that illuminate how the girls participated in and shaped their experiences in the program. We highlight the playfulness of the interactions and the ways that the collaborative student-mentor lore building contributed to the iterative design of the girls’ project. We present three episodes to illustrate how Yozaima and Rebekah’s playworld emerged and evolved through reciprocal growth in and movement between imaginative and tangible domains. The episodes can be seen as examples of how relational dynamics can shape trajectories of learning, engagement, identity, and authorship. Episode 1: The birth of Mr. French Cheeto On the first day, the prescribed activity was making a “spinning thing” – a crafted artifact atop a motor and battery. Instead of engaging directly with the prompt, Rebekah and Yozaima crafted a figurine out of the art supplies, whom they named Mr. French Cheeto (hereafter, Mr. FC). When Author 1 asked, “Are you going to make him spin?” they said, “No, we are going to keep working on him.” Author 1 responded by saying, “Ok, cool!” In this episode, we see the girls’ first iteration of what would become the focal character in their playworld. The girls elected to work exclusively with craft supplies. When asked if they were planning to “make him spin” using batteries and a motor, they declined. Possible interpretations of this choice could be that the girls were not ready to work with the motors and batteries and/or they did not see spinning as a property of Mr. FC. The facilitator’s response signaled that it was acceptable for the girls to respond to the prompt in their own way, positioning them as authoritative on their vision and the authors of their work. The craft supplies provided material resources for the girls to engage with, while the facilitator’s endorsement of their vision and authority can be seen as offering relational and ideational identity resources. Episode 2: Material and time constraints shape lore development In the second episode, we see material and time constraints in the physical world in relation to changes in Mr. FC’s characterization and storyline. The body of Mr. FC was made from a piece of orange paper that the girls had rolled into a tube. The color and shape were described as a “cross between a French fry and a Cheeto.” They had added a servo motor to the design, but Mr. FC ’s slender body made it hard to conceal the motor. The girls tried out several different ways to hide the servo motor, but we describe only two here. One iteration involved slotting the servo motor box into the upper opening of the paper tube body. In this iteration, the arm extended out from the top of his head with the servo box concealed in the tube. Rebekah remarked, “I like that!”. Following Rebekah’s remark, Yozaima said, “no, no, no, no, no” in synchrony with the back-and-forth movements of the servo motor. Following this, Yozaima came up with a new solution that involved cutting out a space in Mr. FC ’s body in which she could house the servo motor box. Without communicating her new idea to Rebekah, she began trying to pierce a hole in Mr. FC ’s chest with a pair of scissors. In response to seeing this, Rebekah gasped and exclaimed, “why are you stabbing him?” Yozaima replied, “I’m trying to make a hole,” then abruptly ceased the “stabbing” and said, “I’m scared … What if it doesn’t work … I did something wrong … I don’t know what to do, bro.” After this interaction, the girls called Author 1 over for help. Yozaima explained their process. In response, Author 1 suggested trying to make “a cape or something” to cover the servo motor. Yozaima and Rebekah took up this idea and proceeded  to make a paper cape for Mr. FC. During the process of making the cape, the girls developed a new storyline for Mr. FC. At the end of class, the girls beckoned Author 1 over to relay the new developments. Yozaima shared, “So we changed it; he’s not curling his mustache no more. He’s an evil villain who say ‘no!’” This episode shows how actions and constraints in the material world (the size of the servo motor versus the size of Mr. FC’s body) led to several problem-solving iterations that ultimately led to the changes to the narrative around the character within the playworld.  The head-arm iteration produced a fruitful reenvisioning of the possible attributes of Mr. FC’s playworld characteristics. We see this moment of reimagining as a reading of the material world (the physical movement) through the lens of the playworld (Mr. FC as villain). When the servo motor was placed to the side of Mr. FC (in the location that a human arm would extend from) the action of the servo motor was close enough to their initial plan of making the hand stroke his mustache. Upon placing the servo motor on Mr. FC’s head, Yozaima saw new potential meaning in the arm movement. Instead of seeing only a 1834 stroking motion (as originally planned) she saw that the same motion could be interpreted as a “no” in the form of wagging a finger back and forth. The envisioning of the servo motor arm movement as “no” combined with the addition of the cape resulted in Mr. FC being recast as Mr. Evil French Cheeto, a villain who says “No!” Episode 3: Lore shapes project construction In this episode, we see how Yozaima and Rebekah made a representation of their newly developed lore about Mr. Evil French Cheeto (Mr. EFC) in the physical world. During the second to last session, Author 1 asked the girls, “What’s the first thing [Mr. EFC] will do?” Yozaima replied, “[He will] free all of the Hot Cheetos from the store…so they don’t get eaten!” Author 1 replied, “Is there going to be a Hot Cheetos rampage on the streets?!” Both Rebekah and Yozaima respond in unison, “Yeah!” Following this conversation, the girls proceeded to make a diorama of the scenario. The diorama featured depictions of store fronts on fire surrounded by hordes of Cheetos and French fries carrying torches and pitchforks.

In an effort to gain more insight into the girls’ playworld and build on the lore developments from the previous week, Author 1 asked about the backstory of Mr. FC’s villain backstory. Author 1 aimed to mediate the development of new lore by offering what Reggio Emilia refers to as “fantastic hypotheses” —guiding questions that aid in storytelling (Rodari, 1988, as cited in Lindvist, 1995). In response, Yozaima excitedly shared how Mr. EFC wants to protect his kin. The final project took shape as a diorama  that depicted the lore that the girls had developed with Author 1. The girls made a clear physical representation of the “rampage” with their use of pitchforks and torches.

We see this episode as a pivotal moment where the development of lore drove the girls' work in the physical space. We note that, while the lore development was collaborative both between the girls and between students and the adult facilitator, the girls retained authorship of the lore at all times. The facilitator could ask questions and offer wonderings, but only the girls had the authority to declare what was canonical. So too did they retain authorship of the material manifestations of that lore. In this study, we examined how the co-construction of lore and material practices shaped two girls’ participation in an in-school maker program. Across the process of creating of Mr. (Evil) French Cheeto (in both the playworld and the material world), the girls cyclically iterated on their emergent playworld and their material project. Lore functioned as a bridge of reciprocal mediation between the playworld (place of limitless possibility) and the physical world (place of both figured and real constraints). Lore facilitated the generation of ideas in the playworld and provided means for translating the ideas into participatory action in the material world. Lore also helped to deliver ideas from the playworld into the material world and provided an avenue fo r material constraints to be reframed as opportunities for developing the narrative in the playworld. L ore offered the girls opportunities to translate their imagination into action and reconnect to creative agency when faced with technical difficulties. I t also provided a rhetorical space in which to work with a facilitator, while maintaining the girls’ authority and authorship. Imaginative artifacts, such as narratives, characters, and backstories, were integral to girls’ process of designing and iterating upon their project. Across the episodes, the girls cyclically and playfully wove together imaginary storylines, crafting skills, and engineering and design materials and practices. Girls with intersectional positionalities often face a disconnect between representations of STEM and their own aspirations, values, and emergent identities. This study illustrates how collaborative, playful learning experiences have potential to leverage student assets and foster expansive views of what is considered legitimate participation in STEM. The bidirectional exchange of information from the playworld to the tangible world highlights the generative and translatory potential of these spaces: challenges faced in the tangible world led to adaptations in the playworld, and developments in the narrative of the playworld led tonew design choices. These findings contribute to the body of literature that holds that identifying and supporting expansive forms of participation as legitimate can expand what STEM practices can look like and who feels they are welcome to participate. We see collaborative lore development as a means for uncovering heterogeneous assets that may have been held back if the girls saw their assets as not applicable to the activity. Like Dorothy’s trip to Oz (Baum, 2000) or teachers’ exploratory ventures into imagined, idealized worlds (Gutiérrez & Vossoughi, 2010), journeys to and from playworlds hold promise for transformation. While we were fortunate in this case to notice and support emergent lore and travels into a playworld, future research will investigate the potential to explicitly design for and support lore development as pedagogical practice.
