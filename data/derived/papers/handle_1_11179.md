# Individual and Group Contributions and Authorship of Objects in Collaborative Knowledge Creation

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In the study of object-centered knowledge creation, epistemic objects, technical objects, and boundary objects have been explored in terms of their mutability and role in knowledge creation and collaboration. This paper offers a new taxonomy that enables exploration of conceptual development and authorship of the objects by distinguishing between four types of objects—individual, group, synthesizing and cooperative. Beyond simply individually-versus group-created objects, synthesizing and cooperative objects enable insight into how individuals participate in joint knowledge creation. The synthesizing object, for example, is produced by an individual based on group conceptual development within a collaborative project. The new object types are useful for educational design and in research on collaborative learning and related forms of generative collaborative work, including in professional and interdisciplinary research spheres. Attention to the complexities of authorship in the stages of development augments analysis of epistemic and boundary objects.

## Introduction

Knowledge creation conceptualises learning as the social construction of shared knowledge objects that are the focus of activity  (Paavola & Hakkarainen, 2005). Equated with knowledge building, knowledge creation is “deliberate, conscious action” (Bereiter & Scardamalia, 2014, p. 35) to generate knowledge that is new or newly applicable to a situation. Importantly, the processes of knowledge creation in collaborative ac tivities and how to support object-oriented work are not well-documented or understood (Damşa & Muukkonen, 2020). Interest is evident in knowing better how individuals contribute and develop conceptually within shared knowledge creation, and how individual learning and group work ‘co-evolves’ (Cress & Kimmerle, 2018). This includes how what a group produces is “taken up int o the individual understanding of community members ” (Stahl, 2013, p. 3) and how to support the interplay between the creativity of the individual and that of the group (Zhou, 2012). Questions such as these provoked us to investigate how individual and  group conceptual  and material development connect.

Questions of individual and group use and creation of objects are relevant in any collaborative knowledge work in the professional and academic spheres as we ll as in formal and informal learning. Although universities embrace collaborative problem-, project-and practice-based learning (Boud, 2012; Hmelo-Silver et al., 2018) to develop student skills for collaborative knowledge work, they are not nece ssarily well equipped to support this (Karlgren et al., 2020). Pedagogies for collaboration in university courses are still in fo rmation (Rowe, 2019). Studies of the processes and objects used and created by teams contribute to a richer understanding of collaboration in practice.

The importance of objects to analysis of knowledge co-creation prompted us to consider the typology of objects, including the features and dimensions used to def ine them and their use. Drawing on empirical findings, we discuss in detail  twonew types of objects, the synthesizing object  and cooperative object, which combine individual and group contributions. In addition, we argue that greater attention to who produces objects and the complexities of how knowledge is developed and inte grated by groups and individuals  will assist in better understanding and supporting knowledge co-creation processes. Objects and object-centered epistemic practice Key to tracing and understanding knowledge creation are the objects teams create and the activities that center on them. How group “interactional resources” are "taken up” by individuals (Stahl, 2013, p. 3) is investigated through empirical research into the use of objects in group activity. Knowledge co-creation might occur through activities that involve the whole group,  but also  through parallel work by  individuals, connected by  metacognitive awareness of how individuals and the group contribute to common goals (Rowe, 2019). Objects are used by people to achieve goals; they act toward and work with them  (Star, 2010) in the process of realizing those goals. The purpose of activity is, then, made sense of through objects (Kaptelinin, 2005) and the “collaborative, systematic development of common o bjects of activity” is central to knowledge creation (Paavola & Hakkarainen, 2005, p. 536). An object may be  an online video tutorial, a design, a hypothesis, or a piece of writing. Objects  are usually expressed in physical or digital  artefacts, recorded and shareable. Objects are, importantly, also  conceptual, and incorporate some level of  students’ understanding of what they aim to produce as a group, what is the objective of the activity (Kaptelinin, 2005; Markauskaite & Goodyear, 2017). Problem solving is not abstract and disembodied: it happens through  engaging with the world  (Kirsh, 2009), interacting with other people and the objects we work on and with. Material objects that are e.g. visible, readable, or audible, are more open to interpretation or change. Less tangible conceptual objects can also be under development: A theory is also an object (Star, 2010). To illustrate, objects may be: visible and bounded, for example, a Wikipedia page used as a source of information; mutable and in development, for example, that same page undergoing editing, or a house design being sketched; or conceptual, and not so visible or bounded,  for example, ideas about project goals  being formed through group discussion. As shown by the example of the Wikipedia page, which might be accepted as stable knowledge object or else identified as incomplete and in need of revision, the usage and type of an object will c hange with the focus of work and  in different circumstances (Ewenstein & Whyte, 2009; Nicolini et al., 2011).

Material objects like a wiki page are a “public” knowledge, “available to be worked on and used by other people” (Chen & Hong, 2016, p. 268) or “shared for manipulation and transformation by the participants  of the activity” (Kuutti, 1996, p. 27). Research has been urged into how objects are developed, their features, what they afford, how they “affect learners’ actions” (Damşa et al., 2010, p.  182), how participants attribute meanings and how the roles of objects change in “the common effort” (Nicolini et al., 2011, p. 627). Used consciously towards meeting collaborative goals, material objects assist in explicating meaning and motive (Engeström & Toiviainen,

2011) and create a record that persists beyond the here and now. Such shared objects instantiate group memory

and facilita te thinking. Different types of objects have been identified by scholars of epistemic practices (cf. Akkerman & Bakker, 2011; Knorr Cetina, 2001; Nicoli ni et al., 2011; Star, 2010) and classified, using their function in collaborative work and level of mutability, into epistemic, technical and boundary objects. The epistemic object or “knowledge object” (Knorr-Cetina, 1999) is the key object type in knowledge creation. Epistemic objects instantiate questions and ideas i n formation and therefore are always incomplete, in the process of being formed, emergent and under study  (Ewenstein & Whyte, 2009; Knorr Cetina, 2001; Muukkonen et al., 2010). People produce them and make sense through them of their ideas and goals (Engeström & Blackler, 2005). Examples include a design being sketched or project goals being set. A technical object might be used as  a tool, procedure, or reference in knowledge work, and does not undergo change or investigation (Ewenstein & Whyte, 2009).  A technical object might be an instrument and established steps for  DNA sequencing  (Rheinberger, 1997), or an application quick guide. It may need to be interpreted, but its form, message and function remain the same. A boundary object spans and links disciplines, sites, professions, or communities and can be interpreted differently within each (Akkerman & Bakker, 2011; Ewenstein & Whyte, 2009; Star, 2010). For example, a house schematic will serve different purposes for a mason or electrician. A boundary object allows for negotiati on of diverse meanings and may be worked upon across domains—a mutable epistemic object—or at other times a type of ‘black box’ that is set and unchanging, though interpreted within separate domains in different ways (Akkerman & Bakker, 2011). A boundary object, then, can cover a range of purposes and levels of mutability. Object type can be fluid depending on how it is used (Ewenstein & Whyte, 2009; Nicolin i et al., 2011). A research report is an epistemic object as the authors analyze data and decide how to communicate findings. The same report, finished, is a technical object used as a reference in follow-up studies or a boundary object in, e.g., a policy decision. An architect is working on an epistemic object when designing a house, the completed plans of which are used as a boundary object to communicate to the different building trades where to add, e.g., plumbing or a window. It might revert to an epistemic object if problems arise during building and plans need to be revised. Object typology considers whether objects are under development (mutability), what roles they perform and their material and conceptual dimensions. Less attention is paid to authorship and how individual or group contributions are made to and integrated within objects. Starting from empirical observations, outlined below, we addressed this gap by focusing on the producers and production of objects and the interplay of individual and group contributions. We propose further object types that extend analysis of objects in knowledge co-creation.

## Methodology

Through exploration of situated project work, we aimed to answer two questions:

1)  ‘What objects are created by individuals within group projects?’ and

2) ‘What role do these objects have and how are these objects formed and used?’

This paper draws on findings from seven ethnographic case studies of undergraduate universi ty student group projects—3-5 members each to a total of 27 participants. The projects allowed students some freedom in the conceptual and material production of shared objects. Human ethics approvals were received (The University of Sydney, protocol 2014/065 and Macquarie University, protocol 5201400241) and participants gave informed written consent (their names are changed). The cases were drawn from one undergraduate course in education, with a group digital object and presentation on a chosen topic weighted at 30%, and one in engineering where a project report and presentation on an engineering scenario were weighted at 85%. Data collection captured student activities in physical and digital spaces (Nerland, 2012), including video records of in-person work, to gather as complete a record of group activity as possible, covering students’ interactions, and use and creation of objects. Analysis included transcription of 36 hours of recorded interactions, including gestures and how space, tools and objects were used by students, covering the interactional or “relational practices” between students and how they used and created objects or their “representational practices” (Johri, 2011, p. 213). Transcriptions were descriptively coded to develop an understanding of what was happe ning at points and over time, and to assist in developing a narrative of each project across communications, conceptual development, and use of objects. Importantly for the development of new perspectives on object  authorship, analysis included  the development of  diagrams that linked  together ideas, activities, objects, and students and traced iterative development over time  for each project  (see Figure 4, Spence, 2015). This allowed us to visualize who was initiating action, for example; where and by whom ideas were proposed or progressed, on both a micro, in the moment, and week-by-week timescale. The diagrams clarified who was involved in conceptu al and material development and who created the objects at the center of knowledge co-creation, including what preceding work and concepts they built upon. This showed much that did not fit simple ‘group’ or ‘individual’ authorship. Empirical examples In this section we briefly outline the  two notable empirical examples on which we base d our conceptual work especially the development of the concept of synthesizing objects.  In the cases, substantial objects were produced by individuals who took group ideas and added further perspectives and arrangements. We include examples of these objects and others from the seven cases to illustrate the empirical foundation for introducing the dimensions of conceptual development and object authorship.

Example 1: Storyboarding in education In this case, the student team of four decided to produce a video as their project output. Over several weeks, they discussed its goals and related ideas. One member, Blake, after an initial discussion,  edited together short video excerpts based on group ideas and shared the resulting video. Over time, others shared quotes or further images and videos to illustrate ideas. Towards the end of the project time, another member, River, suggested they needed to take practical steps  to finalize the video, and brought  to the next meeting a  script and  printed storyboard, organized in chronological illustrated panels, that River had created in their own time. These organized the ideas and objects that the students had contributed into a form that made sense to River, who had some basic filmmaking experience. The storyboard and script organized and converged concepts and conversations, expressing them so that timings and coordination  of the elements  were explicit. The printouts were the focus of that meeting’s discussion, eliciting some minor changes before providing the final blueprint for Blake to edit the final video. Example 2: Village planning in engineering This group project had the task of providing a housing plan for 2000 people in a fictional aid project. Of the four in the team, two core members, Alex and Cameron, worked consistently together on conceptualizing the ir solution. Alex took on both the drawing of a prototype house plan and a diagram of how houses might be laid out in the new village.  Based on an initial group discussion about materials and other elements of th e house design, Alex brought a free-hand sketched house plan to a meeting. Alex and Cameron used this and Alex’s later digital version to discuss details of the design of the hou se, and its use by villagers, with Alex making modifications towards a final design. During their conversation, they drew preliminary ideas for terracing the houses, leading to conversations about the overall layout of the village. In another collaborative session, Alex started a diagram of the village layout, which he was dissatisfied with,  as its grid pattern reminded him of a prison. At intervals, he would stop and confer with Cameron on the progress of the diagram. Cameron reminded Alex of a previous conversation where they considered more circular suburban layouts and a central focus for community life, pulling up online images to support the idea. Afterwards, Alex worked alone on a new version of the village layout  that incorporated Cameron’s ideas and that Alex further extended with research on optimal orientation for housing. Other objects The above examples showed objects that an individual had voluntarily taken ti me and effort to create towards progressing a group project. The objects incorporat ed concepts and artefacts contributed by multiple t eam members, and in the engineering case, were central to grou p discussion. The objects, however, were shaped by the individual using their experiences and capabilities, filling a gap they identified in the project workflow. Different combinations of group and individual contributions and production roles were evident in other objects in each of the seven cases. For example, students: provided individual contextual summaries of source material; took meeting minutes; as a group created a mind map of concepts while talking; worked separately on sections of a final report based on an overall structure and division of tasks. In another example, students wrote and edited for the length of the project  within a group document, both synchronously and asynchronously, each with a designated section but offering comments and suggestions to other team members. The complexity of how individual and group contributions were made and in tegrated was impossible to describe using the exist ing classifications of objects and required further conceptual work.

## Discussion

In this section, we outline the taxonomy focusing on twonew types of objects and discuss them in the context of existing object types and their usefulness in research and education. Figure 1 sets object types against dimensions of who contributes to material and conceptual development and who directly produces objects. Although separated into quadrants, the object types  are on a continuum. For example, there may be some separate individual contributions used within a  mostly group object. In the top left quadrant of Figure 1 would fit objects that are fully discussed, developed, and produced together within a group. In the bottom right quadrant would be objects that are conceptualized  and produced by an individual. We  focus here on the quadrants we have designated synthesizing objects and cooperative objects—the spaces in which the distinctions between group and individual work are not clear-cut. Figure 1 Mapping Production and Conceptual Contributions to Objects Characteristics of a synthesizing object We outline five main characteristics of a synthesiz ing object to clarify it as a distinct type that ha s at its core the contribution of the group and production of the individual within the processes of knowledge  co-creation. The object type was initially identified in the storyboard/script and house planning diagram s outlined above. The use of ‘synthesizing’ conveys the act of bringing toget her ideas from an array of contributors to create a  new, more coherent, organized, or richer fusion/synthesis. A synthesizing object:

1) Is created by an individual, or potentially a small sub-group, to fulfil an identified need in collaborative

work. The individual tends to elect to do the work, potentially connecting it with personal capabilities.

2) Is based on the conceptual work of the group. The synthesizing object is founded in shared knowledge

work and incorporates group-developed ideas and related artefacts.

3) Substantially expands, adds to, (re)organizes or adjusts perspective on collective ideas.

4) Is in artefact form. Because of its ‘public’ role within the group, the synthesizing object is not just

conceptual but is instantiated. That means it has material form and may be adopted and adapted by others.

5) Typically transforms mode or format. For example, it might put a conversation into text or diagram,

place concepts in a mind map, or combine images, video, text, and other contributed items into a storyboard.

A synthesizing object is founded in, represents, and adds to group ideas. When it is presented to th e group, it becomes subject to review, modification, and adoption. In a fluidity of object types related to their role in activity that changes over time (Ewenstein & Whyte, 2009; Nicolin i et al., 2011), synthesizing objects might be discussed, changed, improved, or developed further as group objects. A synthesizi ng object might also be adopted in the end as a technical object, used without modification as part of the group’s final output, for example, a village layout used as an illustration within a r eport, or a component of r esearch results. A synthesizing object might be used to produce a boundary object (e.g. a storyboard), where an author from one discipline or profession takes concepts and artefacts from a multidisciplinary or interprofessional project and creates an object using that discipline’s methods. The intention being that the produced object then be used by other disciplines or professions in collaborative work.

Characteristics of a cooperative object The cooperative object includes relatively less joint, integrative  conceptual development, although knowledge work is directed towards a shared output.  Findings identified objects with  individually-allocated elements, assembled with little discussion. In comparison to the synthesizing object, a coopera tive object relies more on individual conceptual development, towards achieving collective goals. A cooperative object:

1) Is created by individuals working separately on parts of a common object. The individual tends to

contribute in an area or topic in which they have relative confidence or to which they are assigned. Collaborators may make limited suggestions about others’ contributions.

2) The cooperative object prioritizes individual knowledge work within shared goals and purpose.

3) Makes no or minimal changes to individual conceptual contributions in joint production of a collective

material object.

4) Like the synthesizing object, it is in artefact form because of its ‘public’ role.

5) The object may be available consistently to collaborators, for example a shared online document,

making contributions visible in real time. Alternatively, each may produce their part separately, with the group later assembling the various parts.

Considering, again, the fluidity of object types, some cooperative objects might be closer to group objects if collaborative conceptual development occurs regardless of a division of labour. One case group used a shared document for the length of the project  to write their report, adding ideas during meetings and giving each other feedback on their allocated sections. Another case group only discussed ideas in a limited way despite also having set up a shared document, added their allocated sections in the day before it was due and did not integrate ideas. Objects in terms of contributions and producers Distinctions can be made through objects’ role in or motive for activity: epistemic objects for general creation of knowledge; technical objects for utili zing stabilized knowledge; boundary objects for nego tiating or enabling knowledge at the intersection of domains; synthesizing objects for individual contributions within collaborative knowledge creation; and cooperative objects for ass embling individuals’ separate parts into a whole. Epistemic and boundary objects might be wholly collaborative, wholly individual or a mix  of individual and group contributions and production, with aspects that are synthesizing or cooperative. Types are fluid and overlap: one might describe a ‘synthesizing boundary object’  (more below). Objects can be elements within other objects: a group epistemic object, e.g. a presentation slide-deck, assembled collaboratively,  might incorporate technical objects, such as a table from a website, and synthesizing objects, such as a summary diagram by one team member. How contributions are produced, evolve, and are integrated has not been prominent in object definition and research. Authorship for objects may be ascribed to individua ls and/or groups, and it is an argument of this paper that a focus on how  and by whom  contributions are made, put together and integrated in objects is a key part of understanding knowledge creation. Synthesizing objects are a product of both group co-creation and strong individual contribution and creative production, while cooperative objects tend towards coordination of individual contributions. The value of synthesizing and cooperative objects is in directing focus to the intersection between group and individual knowledge work, adding that dimension to epistemic and boundary objects. A synthesizing object is mutable and in development, so is a form of epistemic object, and could be used to similarly describe the development of a boundary object. A synthesizing boundary object might incorporate the collaborative work from multiple domains and then express a ‘take’ on that shared conceptual development from the perspective of one domain. To illustrate this mix of synthesizing and boundary objects, consider the example of architectural planning. An initial consultation between client and architect  is the start of the design process, with multiple design changes by the architect from suggestions or demands in subsequent client meetings, and in consultation with engineers, materials specialists, and trades at each stage of design and implementati on.  It is pertinent to ask, to what degree are ideas group-formed, or from individuals or discipline representatives; how is the object developed collaboratively and individually and how does it perform its role over time? Why a new typology of objects?

We warrant here how  the proposed taxonomy could enrich the analysis of collaborative learning and inform educational design. In the discussion below we focus on the concept of synthesizing objects. As research concept Knowledge creation is an area that requires investigation of its mechanisms and practical implementation (Damşa & Muukkonen, 2020). What team members do with objects in creating kno wledge is key to understanding that process. For an overarching epistemic object such a s a shared project or task, collaborators assemble and make interim and constituent objects, combining a range of object  types. A typology of objects that pays attention to goals, affordances, development over time and authorship facilitates detailed analysis of how that work is done. The authorship-based classification of objects, by specifically focusing on the interplay of collaborative and individual contributions  and knowledge integration, differentiate within the wider type of epistemic object, adding new dimensions and refinement to it and to t he boundary object type. Authorship descriptions and examples extend vocabulary and ways of thinking about object role in knowledge co-creation to considering how and by whom they are developed.

For the synthesizing object type, ‘synthesizing’ refers to “put[ting] elements together to form a coherent or functional whole; reorganize elements into a new  pattern or structure”  as well as planning, generating, and producing something novel (Anderson et al., 2001, p. 68). Coherence, finer representation and (re-)organization, are among the key processes in which the producers of synthesizing objects  are likely to engage. F rom the cognitive perspective, these processes are closely associated with  the learning of complex knowledge (Chi & Ohlsson, 2005) and complements a sociomaterial approach. Synthesizing objects are a valuable representation of how the individual takes on or co-opts knowledge creation for themself. The objects are material evidence of how individuals personalize the interactional resources created by the group (Stahl, 2013). A model that combines individual and shared epistemic agency (Damşa et al., 2010) can encompass the four types of object authorship by acknowledging the contributions and interdependency of a team and its separate members in the production of objects. A synthesizing object expands analysis of problem exploration and inquiry by identifying where group ideas are integrated through individually-produced material objects. Conceptual and material a uthorship can sit wholly within one practice domain  or combine and integrate knowledge  across boundaries, shedding light on the processes at work on boundary objects in interdisciplinary and interprofessional knowledge creation.  As a focus of educational research, an analysis of authorship dimensions  can reveal  the processes of and connections between collective and individual development. A design concept Students are expected to work together on projects and develop transferab le skills in teamwork, which includes knowledge-centred collaborative problem solving (Graesser et al., 2018). Towards this, “collaborative dexterity” should be assessed, in conjunction with teaching collaboration instead of leaving it to chance as an  “inherent quality” of students (Rowe, 2019, p. 10). Our study did not research design for learning, and our design conjectures are subject to  further translation and empirical testing, although the multiple occurrences of the different authorship types in our study suggest that they will not be anathema to either designer or student. A question that arises is how to support the appropriate range of shared and individual development and production of objects. This could be useful i n project-based learning, where students can often miss the early step in collaborative problem-solving of “Exploring and Understanding” (Graesser et al., 2018, p. 69). Modelling the use of group, individual, synthesizing, and cooperative objects may encourage both shared conceptual development and prompt individuals to identify and utilize specific capabilities and independent thought, focused on producing artefacts. For example, individuals could contribute to a team project using synthesizing objects, without resorting to a siloed division of labour in which each works in isolation. Equally, a strongly collaborative group need not complete every task in tandem: individuals can expand and improve upon group ideas. Synthesizing objects in the cases prompted the justification of ideas to the group, identified as a key part of problem-based learning (Hmelo-Silver et al., 2018). In contributing to the shared effort, individuals may benefit from giving and receiving  informal peer review, practice in which assists in the development of evaluative judgement, that is, the ability of students to judge the quality of their own and others’ work (Boud et al., 2018). The dimensions of authorship offer a construct for how students can collaborate and contribute as a group as well as individuals within that group: it need not be simply division of labour or wholly group-driven. Design for effective team-based projects could be enhanced by considering the use and development of individual, group, cooperative and synthesizing objects in knowledge creation.

## Limitations and further research

The foundation for authorship dimensions of  objects came from a nalysis of seven student projects, which were sufficient to outline object type but not confirm frequency or generally identify conditions under which they are likely to be created. This suggests a need for design research into especially synthesizing and cooperative objects in the promotion of effective knowledge co-creation. The cases in the study were of projects at a particular time in undergraduate studies and warrant further resear ch to extend the range of examples and contexts.  Data collection and analysis focused on objects and observable actions and dialo gue in group sessions. However, the evident role of individual recognition of a need and then choice in creating objects point to the importance of team roles, personal resources, and relationships, inviting further study. Beyond undergraduate education, these objects can contribute to resear ch in other levels of education and in professional and a cademic research settings  by providing focus on the individual versus group contributions to a shared endeavour. For example, the presented synthesizing objects were firmly situated in  the creation of material outputs  in the form of  housing plans and a video. Synthesizing objects might also be found in more conceptual knowledge work.

## Conclusion

In the interest of deeper understanding of how individuals take up and work with ideas in knowledge co-creation, this paper has argued that analysis of objects of activity should pay attention to who contributes to conceptual and material development and when, and who acts directly as producer of objects. We have provided a taxonomy of object types using elements of authorship, including conceptual and material contributions and direct production.

The study showed how the use of the object can switch from, e.g., a group to a synthesizing object and back again, and how ostensibly group-authored outputs are built from objects that combine individual and group, conceptual and material, authorship. It contributes to a sociomaterial framework, outlining how dimensions of authorship in objects can extend research and potentially support design for learning and working with objects. It is helpful to consider who is working on ideas at what stages of projects, and how groups or individuals integrate and contribute to conceptual development work and to object production. We invite further investigation of how synthesizing, individual, group and cooperative objects may be used as tools for analysis and design.
