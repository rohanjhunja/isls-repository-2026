# Object Trajectories: What Happens to the Things Students Make

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
What happens to student-made artifacts once making ends? We have rich accounts of fabrication, but rarely follow objects out the door. We introduce object trajectories as a lens for tracing the social and epistemic lives of artifacts after creation. Drawing on constructionism, material culture, and critical theories of visibility, we develop three dimensions: perceptual seeding, recognition, and visibility, and argue tha t following objects reveals forms of learning that stopping at the moment of making cannot reach.

In most classrooms, making has a clear ending. Student work moves through rapid cycles –produced, assessed, briefly displayed, and artifacts disappear quickly after. Essays get forgotten, LEGO builds are disassembled, and cardboard projects get recycled. Mo st student-made artifacts have a thin afterlife. What is lost is not only the object itself but the ongoing relationship between a learner and something they made: the chance to return to it, repair it, show it, and find that it still matters. We rarely lo ok at what happens next. Yet, occasionally something escapes. Some student-made things become keepsakes, reused tools, remembered failures, or social currency among peers. These afterlives are sites of learning that current frameworks are not built to see. Constructionism, the tradition most invested in student-made things, reminds us that people learn best when they build artifacts that are meaningful to them (Papert, 1980; Resnick, 2017). Papert’s emphasis was always on what the object becomes, which Resni ck extended further, framing making as sustained engagement. This tradition has also embraced remix, curation, and selection as legitimate forms of making (Kafai & Peppler, 2011) and, over the years, has expanded to encompass engagement, creativity, and agency (Blikstein, 2013; Halverson & Sheridan, 2014) –yet the emphasis has remained on making itself. Constructionism has often foregrounded process over product to resist reductive assessment (Bevan, 2017). A necessary move, but one that has meant that artifacts tend to drop out of analysis once the making ends. Papert gave us objects-to-think-with, we suggest we alsoneed objects-to-follow.

The keychain phenomenon (Blikstein, 2013) is one symptom of what gets missed. Making can produce trinkets, artifacts whose learning value seems thin at the moment of fabrication. But an object dismissed as a trinket in the makerspace can acquire a dense social life the moment it leaves. What looks like shallow making from inside a classroom may look very different when followed “out the door”. These afterlives, the kept thing, the gifted object, the social currency passed between peers, are sites of learnin g that current frameworks are not built to see. This paper introduces object trajectories as a conceptual lens for tracing those paths, and suggests that following objects extends constructionism beyond the moment of making by revealing continuities of learning through circulation, re-encounter, negotiation, and changing value across contexts. Object trajectories Constructionism foregrounds artifacts as part of how learning happens (Papert, 1980). Material culture extends this by showing that meaning and value are not fixed at the moment of making but shift as objects move across contexts and audiences (Appadurai, 1986; Kopytoff, 1986). Where constructionism centers the making event, material culture decenters it, insisting that what an object becomes matters as much as how it was made. Critical theories of visibility add a third layer. Being seen is not neutral, and the conditions under which artifacts are shown, protected, or withdrawn shape what learning remains legible and to whom (Glissant, 1997; Scott, 1990). Together, these traditions motivate object trajectories  as a way to follow what students make and what those things become. We treat artifacts as relational mediators (Latour, 2005), while also recognizing that their material properties actively shape the relations they enter, inviting some engagements and resisting others (Ingold, 2013; Sennett, 2008). This is not a claim about object agency in a post-humanist sense, but about how material qualities participate in shaping what becomes possible.

We define object trajectories as the social and epistemic journeys of student-made artifacts: the paths through which they are circulated, displayed, stored, repaired, revisited, hidden, gifted, or discarded, and the ways these movements reshape what they mean and do. Rather than moving in a straight line, the same object may loop through homes, backpacks, hallways, and peer networks, each turn altering its meaning and value (Kopytoff, 1986; Turkle, 2011). The learning made visible through object trajectories lies in the practices that unfold around 2749 artifacts after making ends: the sensemaking, reinterpretation, re-encounters, attachment and valuation, repair, explanation to others, and the social negotiations around objects (Rogoff, 2003). Following objects helps us understand learners through how they revisit, circulate, protect, and revalue them over time. Three dimensions grounded in existing scholarship make a trajectory legible. Perceptual seeding, the sensory and affective engagement that makes an artifact matter enough to carry home, repair, or return to, draws on Ingold’s notion of making as correspondence and Sennett’s (2008) account of how tactile attention produces care. Researchers can look for this in moments of unprompted attachment: a student carrying something home, remaking it without being asked, or describing it in detail long after the making event. Recognition, the social and institutional acknowledgement of value, flows unevenly across audiences and contexts, shaped by power and dominant norms (Bernstein, 2004; Vossoughi et al., 2016). Critically, recognition is negotiated by learners who may actively seek peer recognition, redirect value away from institutional audiences, or refuse to display altogether. Evidence of recognition includes waitlists, peer requests, gifting decisions, and the terms students use to explain why something matters.  Visibility, whether an artifact is seen, shared, or deliberately protected, is not always benign (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2006). Drawing on Scott (1990) and Glissant (1997), we argue that invisibility can be strategic and protective. Students may withhold work from institutional view to preserve its meaning or social function. Researchers can look for toggling: an object present in one context, absent in another, and ask whose interests that movement serves. The vignette that follows is an illustration, not empirical data, written to show how the dimensions work in practice.

The Dinosaur’s Trajectory This is the story of a plastic dinosaur. Small, plastic, jointed, and easy to print. The kind we use to test extrusion and hand out as trinkets. In the lab, it barely registered. But a child saw it, shiny and articulated, and asked to help make one. She chose the file, picked a color, renamed it, loaded the filament, and waited. The segments clicked when they moved. She turned it over in her hands, testing the joints, noticing the flex. Perception hooked first, through the sensory details Sennett (2008) ide ntifies as the foundation of care. The learning was underway: noticing, attending, forming attachment to something she had shaped. Then she slipped it into her pocket and took it home. At home, her sister saw it and understood that it could be made again. The dinosaur became a verb: reprinted, requested, remixed, passed along. The learning continued in explanations, in decisions, and in the labor of replication. At school, it crossed a rule: no toys from home, and so it moved differently, in backpacks, pockets, and pencil cases. To peers, it circulated openly; to adults, it was strategically invisible. Visibility toggling by audience and intent, its survival depending on opacity (Glissant, 1997). Among peers, it gathered a different kind of value. There were requests, preferences, negotiations, and a waitlist. Red for one child, gold for another; a snake variant; a dragon. She mapped who got what, in what order, with what justification, negotiating fairness with a seriousness that adults reserve for budgets. Recognition was actively managed; she sought peer recognition, set the terms of distribution, and kept the object out of institutional view where it would have been reframed as rule-breaking rather than valued in its own terms (Kopytoff, 1986). The negotiation, curation, and social work were real, sustained, and entirely outside our institutional scope. What mattered was not that the dinosaur was special in itself. Its importance took shape along the way: a biography made in motion. It gathered meaning the printer did not intend and the institution did not see. Tracing the object’s trajectory surfaces forms of learning that an analysis focused on the moment of making would not capture. Object trajectories extend constructionism beyond the bounded project toward the longer lives through which artifacts continue to mediate learning. The dinosaur illustrates this: an object dismissed as a “keychain” at the moment of fabrication can become a dense site of learn ing once it is followed out the door. What students learn by making does not end when making ends.  The framework problematizes visibility as a simple epistemic good and foregrounds learner agency in how recognition is sought, redirected, or refused. The larger project this work is part of asks: what conditions allow a produced thing to become a meaningful artifact –not whether something was made, but what it becomes once it enters the world. Object trajectories is the analytic tool we are developing to pursue that question, following artifacts across the settings and relationships that shape what they carry and what they leave behind. The next steps are empirical: applying object trajectories in field settings using tools designed to track movement over time, such as custody logs, photo diaries, repair notes, and peer-circulation traces. These methods reposition the learner as an active meaning maker whose changing relationship to the artifact is itself evidence of learning. We suggest not stopping at what students learn by making, but asking what happens to the things they make.
