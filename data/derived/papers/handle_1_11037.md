# Exploring Narratives about People and the Environment in the Context of Resilience Education

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper offers a conceptual contribution for the study of environmental learning, considering which stories about people and the environment might be told through a lens of resilience. Grounded in our research project and broader literature, we introduce narrative themes that may emerge from an interdisciplinary resilience framework: collaborative communities and agentic hope for people and the environment. We also propose narrative tensions for justice and global socio-environmental challenges that should be considered.

Offered as a hope-inspiring and adaptation-centered framework for education on socioenvironmental challenges (e.g., climate change), resilience education has been approached from many angles, from trauma-informed pedagogy to disaster preparedness and more.  We use an analytic framework of narratives and counternarratives (e.g., Andrews, 2002) to introduce a conceptual contribution for the study of environmental learning, exploring the following question: what stories about people and the environment might be told to learners through a lens of resilience? First, we briefly introduce our interdisciplinary approach to resilience education, undergirded by the pillars of personal, community, and ecological resilience. Then we introduce central narrative themes and tensions that may emerge from an interdisciplinary resilience framework like this one, grounded in data from our research partnership with teachers and broader literature. We propose two narrative themes: collaborative communities and agentic hope for people and the environment. We propose that, despite its affordances for counter narratives, a lens of resilience may  risk minimizing the severity of environmental challenges and backgrounding justice; therefore, thoughtful attention to the design of resilience-centered learning environments is important. Weaving together personal, ecological and community resilience in education Nature Together with a team of middle school teachers, we have been engaged in co-designing an interdisciplinary resilience-centered curriculum that interweaves personal, community, and ecological resilience. Definitions of resilience vary across fields from ecology to psychology (e.g., Krasney & Tidball, 2009), but generally, the project approaches resilience as the ability to recover from or adapt to challenging circumstances. This approach builds on research in sustainability education, psychology, ecology and other fields. Increasingly, interdisciplinary approaches to resilience are being recognized as necessary for complex social and ecological challenges, and we propose that these pillars may support more holistic resilience learning. Narratives and counternarratives We use the concept of narratives and counternarratives to explore what stories about people, the environment, and their interrelationship may be depicted through a lens of personal, community, and ecological resilience. Narrative refers to how ideas are socially shared and depicted, such as through literature, art, research, curriculum, conversations, and more (e.g., Andrews, 2002). These socially shared stories shape what society considers “natural” and inform our understanding of how things are meant to be. Dominant narratives “offer people a way of identifying what is assumed to be a normative experience”  (Andrews, 2002, p. 1). Counter narratives, in contrast, provide alternatives to dominant stories. These stories push back on more typical narratives, expand our imaginations, and provide additional ways to understand how things could be (e.g., Romero & Stewart, 1995). Fruitful counter narratives from a lens of resilience Collaborative communities Historically, dominant narratives surrounding humans and the environment tended to center settler colonial notions of land ownership, exploitation of people and the environment, competition among and between species 2401 incl uding humans, rugged individualism, and neoliberal notions of individual responsibility (e.g., Korteweg & Oakley, 2014; Ritchie, 2011). Associated counternarratives, sometimes grounded in or overlapping with Traditional Ecological Knowledge, offer alte rnative perspectives on nature and humans that foreground interdependence, collaboration, and reciprocity (e.g., Bang & Marin, 2015; Kimmerer, 2013) Resilience offers one lens for showcasing counter narratives that highlighting the role of collaboration within and among species, from orcas collaborating to hunt to trees sharing nutrients through mycorrhizal networks. Agentic hope Historically, and to some extent currently, narratives in education have often approached socioenvironmental challenges with “doom and gloom” messages, using scare tactics to propel people into action (e.g., Tzou & Bell, 2015). These practices can foster fear and limit learners’ sense of agency. The efficacy of this approach has been challenged (e.g., O'neill & Nicholson-Cole, 2009) and given rising levels of climate and ecoanxiety (e.g., Pihkala, 2020), it is critical that the impact of such fear-based narratives on learners be considered. A body of research has recognized the need to foster hope in the face of grand environmental challenges (e.g., Ojala, 2012). Resilience education can showcase the agency of individuals, communities, and ecosystems, through narratives of individuals and communities that rise to support one another during hurricane recovery. This type of counternarrative can challenge the idea that environmental or social challenges are too big or overwhelming for humans to address and withstand.

Narrative tensions from a lens of resilience We briefly introduce two narrative tensions. First, while fostering genuine hope in the face of grand socioenvironmental challenges is important, resilience narratives risk diminishing the scope and seriousness of these challenges. How do stories of personal, ecological, and community resilience also address the severity of global climate change? Second, while stories of resilience can inspire our sense of agency, they also risk talking over narratives of injustice. The movement for social emotional learning in schools for example has been critiqued when it places a burden on individual kids to use tools to cope with hardship without acknowledging the systemic injustices that cause stressors (e.g., Gregory & Fergus, 2017), from poverty to racism. Stories can showcase resilience in the face of adversity, but how can learning environments be designed such that systemic contributors to adversity are simultaneously addressed in ways appropriate for young learners?
