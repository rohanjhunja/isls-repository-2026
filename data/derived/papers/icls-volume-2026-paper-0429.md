# Child-Adult Interviews as Sites for Relational Transformation

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Teaching is inherently relational, yet structures of schooling often create distance between teachers and children, making it difficult to authentically listen and find connection. We explore new configurations of adult-child interviews in math and scienc e with a focus on creating spaces for listening and mutuality. We find that these configurations provide opportunities for prospective teachers to be amazed by children’s brilliance, to reconsider their teaching practice, and to engage in playful, reciprocal exchanges with children.

Listening is central to the work of mathematics and science teaching and learning. Listening helps teachers learn how their students are making sense of concepts and it is a core part of building relationships grounded in trust (Bartell, 2011; Berland et a l., 2020; Franke et al., 2009). Yet so much gets in the way of listening to children in United States schools, from tight time constraints to lack of teacher autonomy over curricular decisions to ever increasing pressures to raise performance on narrow measures of “student achievement” (Yoon & Templeton, 2019). As much as elementary teachers may want to listen closely to the children in their classrooms, it is not always easy to do so within the constraints of schooling. In this comparative case study in two elementary teacher education programs, we examine how science and math interviews with children can be a space for prospective teachers to not only better understand children's reasoning, but also to foster relationships with one another, to disrupt divisions between adults and children, and to nurture a sense of connection in the human experience of disciplinary learning (Scott & Philip, 2023). We ask, What more can prospective teachers learn-about children, the discipline and their teaching practices-in these new math and science interview configurations?

## Theoretical perspectives

Creating spaces of relationality that foster a sense of mutuality among teachers and children is a difficult task, given the power differentials that are built into the roles of teacher and student in a classroom (Philip et al., 2022). Children’s expression of ideas are “influenced by the implicit and explicit cues of adults who place value on dispositions, discourses and actions deemed important and significant (Jackson, 1990) - arguably, subtle gestures of power” (Yoon & Templeton, 2019, pg. 80). Simultaneously, the education research field often treats children and adult learning as siloed, instead of seeing the ways their learning is co-constructed and intergenerational (Machado et al, 2024; Vossoughi et al, 2021).

In this paper, we explore how interviews might be reimagined with a focus not only on learning more about children’s math and science reasoning but also with the aim of creating opportunities for prospective teachers and children to find a sense of shared humanity within the discipline where joy, playfulness and discovery can be shared. Interviews are a well-established tool for hearing children’s ways of making sense of the world, traditionally implemented for diagnostic, formative, and summative assessme nt purposes (Carpenter & Franke, 2004; Ginsburg, 1997; Russ, Lee, & Sherin, 2012). In contrast, we seek to understand what might become possible when prospective teachers are supported to let go of adult stances to listen to children on their own terms, engaging in interviews that support reciprocity and relationality instead of evaluation and extraction.

Using comparative case study methodologies (Yin, 2009), we look across mathematics and science methods courses in two US elementary teacher education programs, where the authors served as the primary educators in teacher credential programs. In both elemen tary methods courses, we used an interview assignment where prospective teachers worked one-on-one with children in their teaching placements using scientific and mathematical tasks, prompts, and materials to better understand children’s sensemaking practices. We emphasized mutuality throughout the interview process, with teachers beginning with questions about the child’s interests, hobbies and neighborhoods (Lanouette & Taylor, 2022), and then giving time for children to ask them questions. Data sources i nclude prospective educators’ originally scripted interviews and their written reflections on the interviewing process. Using thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2006), we examined written reflections across the two cases to identify patterns and themes and to explore their prevalence. Through this process, we identified 2716 three central themes that occurred most often in both cases and then chose instances from each case that exemplified the themes. Pseudonyms are used for prospective teachers and the children they interviewed. In future analyses, we intend to further invest igate how these themes differ across the math and science cases and to iteratively refine our interview assignment in response to these analyses. Across math and science interview reflections, three themes emerged most frequently for prospective teachers: 1) amazement at children’s capabilities and ingenuity, 2) insights into children's experiences in school that helped teachers imagine new possibil ities for math and science teaching, and 3) playful, reciprocal exchanges between adult and child. Due to space constraints, here we focus on theme 2 of new insights into teaching. The structure of the interviews and the emphasis on listening rather than “teaching” offered prospective teachers a rare opportunity to gain insight into how children experience disciplinary learning. By listening to children-not telling or evaluating-prospective teachers were able to more fully appreciate children’s experiences in math and science classrooms and to begin to consider limitations of current schooling routines and practices. For example, in her mathematics interview written reflection, Ta lia highlighted how the interview experience shifted her thinking about what was possible for her students: After assessing this student, I realized that these children could do mental math. I was surprised because I had been told that most of these students were barely learning basic math skills and number recognition. Then, to experience this, wow, it was such an incredible experience. Here, Talia extended her surprise beyond a strategy on a given problem to a broader insight that the reports she had been receiving from her mentor teacher were underestimating children’s capabilities. She went on to connect this observation to pedagogy, commenting, “I learned that students could show their knowledge, it is just a matter

## of allowing them to use different learning methods.”

In a reflection about a science interview about water cycles, one prospective teacher, Ella, had a similar realization to Talia. She noted how the interview process had prompted her to reconsider what children could do: Should I do this interview again, I think I would have more faith in my age group. 4th graders are extremely intelligent, inquisitive, and self-sufficient. Going into this interview, I was worried that what I was asking would be too much and too difficult for a 4th grader to articulate, but Milla definitely proved me wrong in that regard.

Listening closely to children as they shared their own perspectives, approaches, and ideas offered Talia and Ella a window into what else might be possible in the math and science classroom beyond what they were seeing. Our initial findings suggest the potential for reconfigured interviews to open new possibilities for teachers to recognize the brilliance of children in science and math, to consider changes to their own teaching practice, and to experience a discourse structure that moves adult and child closer together in playful ways. Children have important things to tell educators about disciplinary learning, about math and science teaching, and about their experiences in schools. In this study, when prospective teachers were supported to listen with curiosity and generosity, children found ways to tell teachers that they have worthwhile knowledge, ideas, and strategies to bring to math and science learning but that their capabilities are often vastly underestimated. And children drew prospective teachers’ attention to the ways classrooms must be intentionally designed to make space for children to share their ideas on their own terms in their own forms of expression. We offer these findings with the hope that we as adults (as teachers, teacher educators, researchers, and caregivers) can become better at knowing when to stay quiet so that we can hear all that children are trying to tell us. In light of this year's ISLS theme on partnering with purpose, we see these subtle but intentional shifts in interviewing children as offering the possibilities for new adult-child partnerships in co-learning within the disciplines of math and science. Through a cross program comparative study on two divergent coastlines, we see these changes as simple but powerful ways to retain hope and humanity during these challenging times in the US and globally, and to counter educational approaches that presume children as less than, incomplete or in need of fixing (Machado et al, 2024), especially within STEM disciplines steeped in patterns of exclusion (Martin, Gholson & Leonard, 2010; Morales-Doyle, 2019).
