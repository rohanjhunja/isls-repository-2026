# “The Heart of the Matter”: Addressing College Composition Problems of Practice in a Research Practice Partnership

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
A research-practice partnership (RPP) made up of college composition instructors and researchers came together to address a problem of practice facing writing teachers – engaging students in exigent rhetorical situations and real audiences. In this paper we use case study methods to describe the ways that RPP members collaborated in a professional learning community to address this problem in college composition by adopting human-centered design as a pedagogical tool. Through thematic coding, we noted the different moves participants made and how those moves productively guided practitioners in tackling and designing instructional experiences that responded to the problem of practice. We also note the ways in which these moves also aligned with another RPP goal – fostering mutually beneficial relationships. These findings suggest that professional learning communities, especially those within an RPP, provide an effective frame for addressing problems of practice in mutually beneficial ways.

## Introduction

Research-practice partnerships (RPPs) have become instrument al in bridging the gap between educational research and classroom practice, allowing practitioners and researchers to collaboratively address rea l-world problems of practice in education and foster mutually beneficial relationships (Coburn et al., 2013; Henrick et al., 2017). This paper adds to this research by presenting our work on an RPP aimed at addressing  a persistent issue in college composition: creating writing tasks that hold genuine exigence and authen ticity for students to foster increased student engagement and a broader conceptualization of writing (Tham, 2021a). We drew upon Coburn et al. (2013)’s framing of RPPs and Clark et al.’s (2023) framing of professional learning communities (PLC) to bring together composition instructors and researchers to explore human-centered design (HCD) as a pedagogical approach to address our core problem of practice. HCD emphasizes the potential of complex rhetorical situations, or “wicked problems,” to enhance rhetorical engagement in college composition (Leverenz, 2014, Marback, 2009). By incorporating H CD into college composition pedagogy, instructors c ould create assignments that engage students in solving real-world challenges, thus providing an authentic, exig ent context for writing.  To make sense of how the RPP took up this work, we asked: 1) What moves or actions do members take in their role as part of a PLC? 2) How do these moves support PLC members’ work in addressing the identified problem of practice in college composition? By addr essing these questions, this paper contributes to the growing body of research on RPPs and their potential to address problems of practice in higher education.

## Literature review

In adopting an RPP to address a key problem of prac tice in college composition-exigence and authenticity in writing tasks-we pull from two bodies of literature: 1) RPP literature, s pecifically their role in addressing problems of practice and building mutually beneficial partnerships; and 2) human-centered design as a pedagogical practice in writing instruction in college composition. Working in partnership with practitioners RPPs have much to offer education researchers who w ant to impact real change in classrooms. RPPs invol ve “long-term, mutualistic collaborations between practition ers and researchers that are intentionally organize d to investigate problems of practice and s olutions” (Coburn et al., 2013, p. 2). In successfu l RPPs, learning is multidirectional, and members leverage their individual expertise (Farrell et al., 2022). In a systematic review of RPP literature, Sjölund et al. (2023) found that members adopted a variety of roles tonegotiate their collaboration within diversely composed RPPs. Researchers adopted roles like inquiry facilitator or design advisor, a nd practitioner roles included co-inquirers and designer pilots. Sjölund and colleagues (2023) also raise the need for more research on the outcomes of RPP collaboration, which our work addresses. In addition to improving educational systems, RPPs could also address college instructors’ desire for sustained professional development  and collaboration. Instructors seek learning commun ities to address their problems of practice, yet there are few chances for this in higher education (Hairon et al. 2017; Clark et al. 2023). Collaboration in higher education is challenging to  implement due to the specialization of faculty (Wa rd & Selvester, 2011). To address the need for collaboration around problems of practice, higher education spaces could leverage the PLC approach often found in K-12 settings. PLC members “engage in professional development in a collaborative, interactive and ongoing way, in contrast to traditional models of professional development which is developed by outside experts and delivered to teachers” (Cann, 2021). PLCs often focus on problems of practice to improve student learning (McLaughlin & Talbert, 2010). As Cl ark et al. (2023) found, the PLC model fosters innovation in teaching and addresses the desire for  collaboration and camaraderie in higher education.  Our work builds on the PLC work that Clark and colleagues (2023) did, highlighting the benefits of this approach in higher education to build sustained, collaborative spaces for college composition instructors. Connecting HCD and writing HCD is a problem-solving approach that centers the human-factor in designing solutions to what many scholars call “wicked problems” (Brown, 2008). HCD involves five spaces or stages – understanding the problem space and users/stakeholders, synthesizing user and conte xtual data, ideating possible solutions, prototypin g, and implementing. Among others, Goldman and Zielezinski (2022) document how to bring HCD into educational spaces, highlighting the ways it fosters inquiry and problem-solving. Within writing studies, Purdy (2014) describes the symbiotic relationship between design thinking and writing processes, seeing much overlap between the two. For instance, HCD’s understanding and synthesis work aligns with the prewriting and research work that often takes place during the initial part of one’s writing process. This overlap highlights the ways that writers are d esigners of compositions, opening the door for HCD as a pedagogical approach in college composition. F or instance, Marback (2009) encouraged composition instructors to bring wicked problems into their assignments. Wicked problems offer college composition students opportunities to work with rhetorical situations that have authentic purposes and real audiences. Tham (2021a, b) demonstrated the potential in integrating HCD into both first-year composition (20 21a) and technical writing (2021b). “[Design thinking] gives students opportun ities to practice their developing expertise in res earch – problem formulation, data collection, data analysis, and report of findings … design thinking makes writing a key part of the problem-solving process rather than just an artificial exer cise that is forced upon the students as a course requirement” (Tham, 2021a, p. 19).  Finally, HCD ’s wicked problems have the potential to “increase student engagement in any writing they’re asked to do” (Leverenz, 2014, p. 3).  Part of this engagement stems from the amount of agency they have over their process and the products they make when responding to a wicked problem rhetorically (James & McCarthey, 2024). For these reasons, the RPP sought to explore ways to integrate HCD as a way of addressing their primary problem of practice.

## Methods

We adopted a case study methodology since this approach allowed us to tell the story of the RPP’s work as a PLC. Merriam and Tisdell (2015) define a case as a bound ed system within a specific context that allows lea rning to happen and allows for what Geertz (1973) calls “thi ck description.” Here, the case is the RPP-a group of six college composition instructors and two researchers – and it is bounded by the work we did during the summer of 2024 as we learned about, wrestled with, and designed instruction that integrated HCD into writing pedagogy.

## Context and participants

This work began with a partnership between a design  thinking center and college composition instructor s at a large Midwestern land-grant university. We formed the RPP with six composition instructors across several English department programs – including professional and technical writing  (Mort* and Joe), rhetoric and composition (Katrina, Kelly*, and Liz), and writing pedagogy (Dana) – and two researchers (Carrie and Andrew). Mort and Kelly are pseudonyms while the rest consen ted to be identified by their real names.  Instructor participants adopted two roles – co-designers of instruction and “inquiry translators” while researchers adopted the role of “expert inquirers” and guided instructors as “design advisors” (Sjölund et al., 2023).

## Data collection

To address our research questions, we collected two  types of data – observational and artifactual. During the summer of 2024, we met with all participant partner s in June and August. During the first week of June, we led two workshop sessions that lasted a pproximately 1.5 hours each. The first workshop provided an overview and rationale for bringing HCD into college composition. The second focused on the practical side of bring ing HCD into the composition classroom. We then held PLC meetings two times a week during the last two weeks of June and the first two weeks of August in smaller groups. We divided into two groups to meet the differing schedules of participants. The Blue Team consisted of participants Dana, Liz, and Mort and Katrina, Kelly* and Joe made up the Green Team. Carrie and Andrew joined both teams. Each meeting lasted approximately 1.5 hours. We video-recorded the sessions to capture events and collected the work participants brought with them. This included initial ideas, assignment descriptions, syllabi, instructional plans, and their PLC meeting notes.

## Data analysis

We began our analysis through an inductive approach. “Coding inductively is entering the analytic enterprise with as open a mind as possible – a “learn as you go” approach that spontaneously creates original codes the first time data are reviewed” (Saldaña, 2021, p.41). After each PLC session, one of our team created a qualitative memo in the form of a contact summary sheet (Miles & Huberm an, 1984) of key themes and concepts from the sessi on. We (the research team) then watched the video record ings of all 12 PLC meetings that occurred across June and August to brainstorm potential codes. Based on these initial reviews, we returned to the literature and refined our codes based on RPP literature (Coburn et al., 2013;  Henricks et al., 2017). This informed  the final codebook for our second round of analysis. We ultimately identified three categories of codes: partnership a nd relationship building (e.g., sharing personal stories, member ch eck-in), participant moves in addressing problems of pr actice (e.g. suggesting ideas, asking clarifying questions, validating ideas), and content focus in addressing problems of practice (e.g., talk that considered students or th at mapped HCD onto instruction). After a norming pr ocess, we coded video and artifact data to help us make sense of the case, focusing on the moves participants made and how those moves aided participants’ abilities to address the problem of practice through HCD.

## Findings: Sharing the case

Tackling the problem of practice In the June meetings, each participant took on the role of “focal” participant and bought ideas for how they wanted to use HCD to address the problem of practice. For instance, Mort described wanting an assignment that more authentically considered audience. “The heart of th e matter is: this is an inside-out project. It doesn’t really have an audience...The way this gets taught and the way it was designed, it’s an exercise in group writing...there really is not an authentic audience.” (Green Team PLC, 6/1 8/24, 14:24-15:10) Joe wanted to engage his students in research that considered local problems and developed proposals with real solutions based on hands-on research. Carrie set expectations at the start of each meeting after engaging in relationship building conversations such as asking members about their weekend plans. She then turned the meeting over to the focal participant, who built background for the rest of the group by sharing course and project information. For example, Kelly provided an overview of her multimodal composing course, so all member s had a basic understanding to work with her ideas. Building background also included sharing an  overview of the course assignments, describing vis ions the instructor had for their course, and how they wanted to change the course in response to the problem of practice. While building background continue d throughout the session, participants shifted from passively engaging with ideas – whether through listening or reading materials the  focal participant  brought – to more interactive moves. These  included suggesting ideas, asking probing or clarif ying questions, sharing experiences or resources, and thinking aloud about the course o r assignment. In making these moves, participants focused on many concepts including mapping on of HCD, offering  pedagogical strategies, and con sidering students’ needs and prior knowledge. Joe, for instance, consistentl y used each PLC session to better understand the el ements of HCD, asking clarifying questions such as how journe y maps worked or how a focal participant took up th ese practices in their project development. Carrie often provided suggestions to map HCD onto different assignments. For example, she mapped out how long it would take for Mort to complete  the HCD process, sketching out the time needed for each space. This helped Mort decide that stopping at data synthesis would work best. Probing questions provided participants with a n indirect way of offering suggestions. “I wonder if” frequently signaled these types of questions. Andrew, for instance, asked Dana about her mapping project to consider ways it might impact students’ uptake of the final assignment. “I wonder  if that, that exordium, or whatever, if you could or should match that up to the last thing they are doing?” (Blue Team PLC, 6/25/24, 55:1955:45). This encouraged Dana to consider ways to connect early and late semester projects. By the end of the first round of sessions, participants felt supported and expressed their gratitude for the ide as the team generated. For instance, Mort ended his session saying “Holy crap... this is fantastic. Yeah, there is a heart of the matter that we really got to. Like, I think we did it from the inside out” (Green Team PLC, 6/18/24, 1:08:31-1:09:00). Iteratively designing solutions Between the sessions in June and August, each of th e practitioner participants used the time to contin ue to think through assignments and prototype their courses, as signments, or projects. Unlike the first round of sessions that focused more on brainstorming and thinking through the potential each idea had to address the core pro blem of practice, these sessions primarily centered on feed back and revision. The focal participant provided an overview of their projects or course, share d documents for the rest o f the team to review, and guide d us in the kind of feedback they wanted. For instance, Dana presented her course syllabus and sought the team’s feedback on whether she accomplished her three goals using an a nalogy of braiding hair with three strands. She wan ted to know if her three strands were adequately represented. “I’ve got this strand for the content of the class and sort of overarching goals, and then the strand of, like, of  how to support their writing, and then trying to w eave in this other of human-centered design. And I’m like, I feel like, like, u nsure about, like, if I struck the appropriate balance” (Blue Team PLC, 8/13/24, 21:19-22:29). In this way, Dana sought validation that she was heading in the right direction, and Carrie provided validation by acknowledging the presence of HCD. Participants’ suggestions moved from ideation of id eas toward refining them. In Joe’s second session, he shared with the team his outline of his project,  which focused on engaging his students in research ing issues of safely getting around campus via different modes  of transportation. In sharing his plan for identif ying users, the PLC team entered a dialogue of suggesting that refined the list of “extreme users” - users who do not describe the norm in a given context. Carrie suggested broadening the extreme users to include “someone who’s just new” to our city and “are international.” Katrina chimed in suggesting that Joe consider these as “two separate” groups “because then you have the constraint of having dif ferent sign systems or whatever compared to someone  who’s from the U.S. and just in a new part of the country ” (Green Team PLC, 8/13/24, 25:59-27:32). This refinement occurred in different ways across the six August sessions. In Katrina’s session, the Green Team helped revise the assignment sheet for Katrina’s final project so tha t students would more explicitly consider the audie nce and venue of their proposed exhibits. The Blue Team offered Mort ways to incorporate mapp ing of different stakeholders as an activity to help students consid er the different audiences they were engaging with in their research project. By the end of the sessions, all participants had concrete ways to revise and finali ze their first prototype for a test run in their fall courses. Sim ilar to the first sessions, the y left expressing feelings that the collaborative nature of the PLC sessions set them in the right direction.

## Discussion and implications

The formalized, collaborative nature of PLCs, and R PPs more broadly, played a vital role in addressing  the problem of practice participants faced in developin g exigent and authentic writing tasks in college co mposition. Participants engaged in a give and take dynamic to refine their ideas in response to feedback and coll ective problem-solving that was mutually beneficial for all participants (Coburn et al., 2013, Henricks et al., 2017). The opening moves that Carrie engaged in helped set  a clear purpose and  expectations for PLC sessions. The opportunity for focal participants to guide the ses sion put them in charge of the PLC session instead of the researchers and gave them the agency to focus on wh at mattered to them (Coburn  et al., 2013). The focal participants presented their ideas to the group, pr oviding background on their courses, assignments, a nd visions for integrating HCD. As they built background, other participants used their moves to support ideation, co-design, and refinement of potential solutions to the problem of practice. By suggesting, clarifying, probing, and validating, focal participants navigated the inclusion of HCD i nto their courses, assignments, and projects, each bringing in their own expertise to support each other (Farrell,  2022). The discussions that members of the PLC eng aged in reflected the iterative nature of co-design, where ideas were continually reworked and evaluated. These moves and their impact reinforce Ormel et al. (2013) assertion that RPPs foster innovation. This study demonstrates that PLCs specifically, and RPPs generally, are an effective means of fostering effective collaboration in higher education. These collaborations are essential for addressing the cha llenges of teaching and improving pedagogy in college composit ion. Instructors in this study were able to refine their instructional design through collaborative feedback  to work toward designing assignments that better m et their students’ needs. PLCs offer a space for these educators to collaborate, share ideas, and receive ongoing feedback to address problems of practice in mutually benefic ial ways. The theoretical implications of this work extend the literature on RPPs and PLCs in higher education. Th is study adds to our understanding of how RPP  members collaboratively navigate PLC sessions  to address problems of practice. Additionally, this study underscores the potential of PLCs as a tool for pedagogical development. Future research on PLCs and RPPs in higher education is needed to examine the long-term impact of this work, particularly on students’  learning and attitudes toward writing. Our ongoing study will address this, but more studies are necessary to provide additional insights.
