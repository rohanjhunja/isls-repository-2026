# Creating a Science Classroom Culture where Teachers and Students Care about Learning Science Together

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Reform-oriented science classrooms emphasize students acting as epistemic agents and working collaboratively to make sense of ideas. Establishing classroom cultures in which students adopt such efforts can be challenging. We consider how one teacher initiated such a classroom culture and investigate how she invited students to join her in the development and maintenance of that culture. Results suggest that student partnership in community-oriented classroom culture can support students in sensemaking about science.

## Introduction and conceptual background

Science education reforms encourage classroom envir onments in which students construct knowledge through collaborative engagement in the science and engineering practices (National Research Council, 2012). This occurs as students ask questions about phenomena, identify how to answer those questions in ways that make sense t o them, and collaboratively develop science knowledge (Damşa et al., 2010; Stroupe, 2014), but such expectations are uncommon (Makar & Fielding-Wells, 2018). Although prior  research describes how reform-oriented classrooms might look (Engle, 2012; Reiser et al., 2021), teachers’ actions to establish and maintain such cultures that include student adoption of these practices require further study to make these environments more prevalent. Classroom culture involves multiple factors such as behaviors, procedures, and expectations (Carlone et al., 2011). Three elements of classroom culture from prior research are group orientation toward learning, group orientation toward interaction with peers, and teacher orientation toward students and instruction  (Cavanagh & Waugh, 2004). Cavanagh & Waugh (2004) argue that teachers can use their authority to initiate culture and that the ways teachers share decision making  with students influences that  culture. We focus on how one teacher invited her students into  developing and maintaining a classroom culture centered on two constructs we see as central to making sense of science ideas: students’ epistemic agency and a commitment to a collective enterprise. Students with epistemic agency  can actively direct knowledge-building work  (Berland et al., 2016).

When students take up epistemic agency, teachers can then help them negotiate ideas to develop shared knowledge in a commitment to a collective enterprise (Reiser et al., 2021). While research documents the value of epistemic agency and commitments to a collective enterprise, more work is needed to understand how teachers create and foster a culture in which both constructs exist and support one another. In this paper, we ask, “In what ways can a teacher initiate, develop, and maintain a classroom culture centered on student epistemic a gency and a commitment to a collective enterprise in ways that support student adoption of sensemaking in science?”

## Methods

Our study focuses on one teacher  in a large suburbandistrict in a west-coast state, whom we refer to as Ms. Seaborn, and her 7th-grade science students from the 2021-2022 and 2022-2023 school years. Our data include teacher interviews and surveys, classroom video, teacher stimulated recall interviews, and student interviews. We coded data for epistemic agency and commitment to a collective enterprise because we identify these constructs as the way Ms. Seaborn attempts to shape  group orientation toward learning and peers (Cavanagh & Waugh, 2004). We then organized data into evidence of initiation, development, and maintenance of the classroom culture to investigate Ms. Seaborn’s orientation toward students and instruction (Cavanagh & Waugh, 2004).

## Results and discussion

Teachers can use classroom culture to encourage students to adopt roles such as acting as epistemic agents or engaging in collaborative work  (Carlone et al., 2011; Cavanagh & Waugh, 2004). The evidence from Ms. Seaborn’s classroom suggests several key factors fo r building such culture. First, co-construction and enactment of classroom expectations seems to promote student buy-in. Although Ms. Seaborn initiated classroom culture with predetermined expectations of “respect, equity, commitment to community, and figuring things out together,” she almost immediately invited students to develop their adoption of these expectations through “social contracts.” These contracts, created by students, include student-determined expectations such as “encourage, participate, best effort, talk it out, and contribute.”  Second, these expectations were clearly and regular ly emphasized  to maintain classroom culture. Ms. Seaborn consistently facilitated attention to classroom expectations (e.g., “I want someone to take a risk. Encourage your group member s. Help each other. Why are we doing this experimen t today?”). Students also held others accountable to classroom expectations (e.g., students reported that they “help others out [with] the social contract.”  Specifically, students engage with one another with  questions like  “What did you mean by…” and “Can I have some help [with this idea]?”). Finally, expectations centered around sharing ideas and working together to build science knowled ge. The phenomena, or the science content, provided  the purpose for students to share and work together (e.g., Students indicated [whole group discussion] is “a good time to be confused,” “build on others’ ideas,” and “potentially change your way of thinking.”). While Ms. Seaborn did not explicitly communicate that she wanted a classroom culture in which students take agency over the knowledge-building work or that engaging in science practices  requires a collective enterprise, the students communicated that they fel t their class was “student-led,” that “the class” figures things out for themselves, that “whatever [they] say matters,” and that Ms. Seaborn was there to “support”  them. Ms. Seaborn brought her students into enactment of this classroom culture by including them in the development and maintenance of that culture and asking them to uphold their community expectations. The result was a culture in which students could experience a reform-oriented science learning environment and were motivated to do so.

## Conclusion

Science education reformers call for teachers to sh ift more responsibility for knowledge-building work to their students. Ample evidence documents the characterist ics of classrooms in which students have embraced s uch participation, but more work is needed  to sufficiently support more teachers in making the se shifts. The current study shows how bringing students into the developm ent and maintenance of classroom culture designed for the purpose of supporting students to act as epistemic agents and collaboratively build science knowledge is a specific act teachers can perform to advance reform-oriented classroom experiences. Future work should investigate more about the variety and nature of such classroom environments to further develop this work.
