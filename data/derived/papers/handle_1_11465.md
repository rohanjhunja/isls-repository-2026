# Culturally Revitalizing STEAM Learning as a Space for Ecologically Situated Identity Work

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study examined the experiences of Indigenous American youth and adults participating in a culturally revitalizing science, technology, engineering, arts, and math (STEAM) learning workshop. Specifically, we examined how this workshop provided space for participants to explore, develop, and express their individual and collective identities through their creation of ribbon skirts and ribbon shirts, incorporating electronics and digital embroidery, and pottery beads painted with locally sourced, earth-based pigments. We analyzed video recordings, interviews, field notes, surveys, and participant-created artifacts, collected as part of a participatory design study. Drawing on situated, sociocultural, and ecological theories of learning, we identified five types of identities expressed and developed through design work during the workshop: individual identities, family identities, tribal identities, Indigenous community identities, and dual or hybrid identities. We also identified aspects of the workshop design, such as intergenerational, community based, and participant-driven learning that supported the exploration and expression of these identities.

## Background

Over the last decade, ‘m aking’ and the ‘maker movement’ have gained significant momentum, with ‘makerspaces’ and ‘fab labs’ becoming commonplace in many communities  and schools. Making encourages participants to integrate STEM (science, technology, engineering, and math) with art  (turning STEM into STEAM), to explore ideas, learn technical skills, a nd create new products  by using digital and tangible technologies (Sheridan et al., 2014). Enthusiasm for making has been driven largely by its potential to democratize access to the tools and skills of STEAM professiona ls (Honey & Kanter, 2013; Maric, 2018; Martinez & Stager,

2013) and its potential to provide space for the mobilization of young people’s intere sts and practices through

new forms of activity  (Barajas-López & Bang, 2018; Barron, 2006; Calabrese Barton  et al., 2016; Halverson & Sheridan, 2014; Ito et al., 2010; Sheridan et al., 2014). However, this potential hinges on our ability to address documented inequities in access and participation (Buchholz et al., 2014; Blikstein & Worsley, 2016) by structuring interaction and meaning-making in makerspaces to equitably afford a diversity of purposes, relationships, and identities (Barajas-López & Bang, 2018; DiGiacomo & Gutierrez, 2016; Vossoughi et al., 2016). Like many educational spaces, especially in STEM, making has been critiqued for being dominated by white men, excluding the interests and ways of knowing of women and Blac k, Indigenous, and other People of Color (Blikstein & Worsley, 2016; Lewis, 2015). It has privileged the creation of products over people and relationships (Vossoughi e t al., 2016). The maker movement  has also largely ignored the history of human making that extends far beyond  the contemporary era of making with digital techno logies, devaluing old—that is, traditional—technologies in favor of new ones (Barajas-López & Bang, 2018). Consequently, there has been a push to design making activities t hat are more inclusive and attend to culture as a part of their design. In Indigenous communities, this has taken the form of incorporating Indigenous epistemologies, intergenerational learning, and con nections with the more-than-human world (Marin & Bang, 2018). In response to the cultural and linguistic genocide caused by settler-colonialism in Indigenous communities, such efforts have been intentionally conceptualized as  culturally revitalizing (McCarty & Lee, 2014). The goal of these activities is not just to accept, connect to, or sustain the cultures of Indigenous youth and communities, but to actually reinvigorate them by challenging dominant narratives about what STE AM is and what it should be. For example, Kafai, Searle, Martinez, and Brayboy (2014) engaged Indigenous American youth in creating an  electronic textile (e-textile) quilt representing local plants used throughout tri bal history. This connected the tools and technologies of STEAM to Indigenous practices and ways of knowing. Similarly, BarajasLópez and Bang (2018) engaged Indigenous youth and families in a summer STEAM program involving the making and sharing of claywork to explore nature-culture relations and elevate Indigenous practices and technologies.

One of the arguments for such approaches to STEAM l earning is that they nurture the identities of Indigenous youth. However, the mechanisms through which that happens and what those identities entail has not been directly explored. As Sch uettpelz (2024) explains, for Indigenous Americans, identity development and identification are complicated by the history and contemporary politics of settler-colonialism. This history has included the dissolution of tribal families, communities, and cultures and the imposition of criteria for who can legally identify as a member of a particular tribe. This has led to a historicopolitical reality that creates particular complexity for the identification and identity development of multiracial and urban Indigenous Americans, who navigate dual or hybrid identities while straddling two or more cultural communities. It also creates complexity for Indigenous Americans attempting to honor and revitalize historical cultu ral identities and practices as they imagine futures for themselves and their communities involving varying degrees of hybridity. For example, during the planning of the current project, the second author relayed a story about two young artists from the Meskwaki Nation who said they didn’t think their art was ‘Na tive art,’ because it didn’t draw in traditional or stereoty pical ‘Native’ art forms. She responded, “Of course your art is Native art. You a re Native.” What the young people were grappling with here is the harm imposed by the cultural misconception that Indigenous culture is part of our past but not our present or future and that it rema ins constant, rather than evolving with the Indigenous people living here today (Dunbar-Ortiz & Gilio-Whitaker, 2016; Jentz, 2018). This project, which we’ve called The Future is Indigenous, supports Indigenous youth in navigating this landscape of identity development  through engagement in culturally  revitalizing STEAM making activities. It examines the question: H ow do these activities provide space for participants to explore, develop, and express their individual and collective identities?

## Theoretical framework

To frame this investigation, we draw on sociocultural, situated, and ecological approaches to learning and identity development. For example, from Cole (2007), we draw the notion t hat learning and development are proleptic processes of becoming within cultural communities, embedded in both cultural and historical context. From Lave & Wenger (1991), we draw the idea of communities of practice, aiming to design an environment that embraces cultural knowledge and creates a n intergenerational community for engaging in hybri d practices integrating STEAM with Indigenous artmaking  and ways of knowing. From Wenger (1998), we  also draw the idea that, “Because learning transforms who we are and wh at we cando, it is an experience of identity. It i s not just an accumulation of skills and information, but a process of becoming” (p. 215). Finally, from Bronfenbrenner (1977) and Bronfenbrenner and Cici (1994), we draw the idea that humans learn and develop in interaction with multiple, nested layers of ecological context through which t hey live their lives. These scholars identify specific layers of context or systems  that impact learning and development, including: (1) the microsystem —a specific learning environment, such as school or home; (2) the mesosy stem—the specific combination of microsystems through which a given individual lives their life; (3) the exosystem—social structures, such as local governments, media, or more distant family, which impact an individual’s mesosystem but are not  directly a part of it; (4) the macrosystem—larger structures of norms, laws, or values that impact interactions in the other systems (e.g., culture, social norms, political and economic syste ms); and finally (5) the chronosystem—which represents how all of these systems change over time.  Thus, to understand identity development, we must look not only to the immediate context but to the impact of these nested layers of context. These nested layers of context are made salient for learning and identification  (or not) in any given learning environment. For example, the ways in which participants position themselves and one another in social interaction is influenced by socially and culturally available categories derived from participation in nested layers of context (Varelas et al., 2012). As an individual deploys these positional lenses through situated interactional practices, they author themselves and shift the ways in which they are reflexively authored by others (Azevedo & Mann, 2022). However, the social, cultural, and material contexts of specific learning environments differently afford or constrain this sort of authoring as well as the social and cultural categories made available  for participants’ individual and collective identification.

## Method

Our study drew on community-engaged forms of design research, such as participatory design research (Bang & Vossoughi, 2016) and social design-based experiments (Gutiérrez  et al., 2020). It took place in the Midwestern United States and involved the co-design of a six-week, weekend workshop  by a European American, female learning scientist (Ramey); an Indigenous, female artist (Velasquez); a European American, male director of a local, community Fab Lab (Cheyney); and a European American, female anthropologist (Beck). Our team was assisted by two co-facilitators: one Indigenous woman and one European  American woman. Together, we guided participants through the making  of  ribbon skirts and ribbon shirts, integrating contemporary technology tools of digital embroidery and wearable electronics. We chose these garments because of their past and present use among the Indigenous peoples of the Great Plains and among urban Indigenous communities. We also guided participants through ma king and painting clay beads with locally sourced, earthbased pigments used in Indigenous pottery  making. The workshop culminated with a fashion show attended by 142 people, where participants modelled and discussed their designs for friends, family, and the public. As a space designed for intergenerational learning,  we invited youth, parents, and other adults to the workshop. Consequently, we had 21 participants, age 7 to 72, from 13 tribes: Apache, Chippewa Cree, Cherokee, Choctaw, Fox, Inupiaq, Meskwaki, Odawa, Plains Cree, Sac, Salteaux, Shawnee, and Yaqui. We documented their experiences and learning using video-ethnography (Goldman et al., 2006; Pink, 2001). Data consist of video captured by stationary and wearable cameras; retrospective, semi-structured interviews (Charmaz, 2006); surveys; field notes; and photographs. Our team analyzed this data using interaction analysis (Jordan & Henderson, 1995) and reflexive thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2006; 2019) in consultation with one of the Indigenous workshop participants, who also works as an intercultural research and design consultant (Cota), and two graduate research assistants: a German woman (Schamberger) and an Iranian woman (Baradaran Shoraka). Drawing on our theoretical framework, we analyzed data for evidence of identity work being done during the workshop. Specifically, we focused our attentio n on participants’ ribbon skirt and ribbon shirt de signs and how they described these designs and their experiences in interviews and during the workshop. Our analysis was framed specifically by Bronfenbrenner’s conception of multiple nested layers of ecological context and yielded five types of identities expressed and developed through design work during the workshop: individual identities, family identities, tribal identities, Indigenous community identities, and dual or hybrid identities. In what follows, we detail examples of each and show how the context of the workshop supported the expression of identities.

## Discussion

Our findings illustrate how workshop participants explored, developed, and expressed multiple layers of identity, often literally woven together, through their activities during the workshop and the symbolism embedded in their final designs. Drawing on Bronfen brenner’s ecological systems theory, we can see how the different identities expressed are drawn from different ecological layers. For example, the workshop context itself represents a particular microsystem, in which choice allowed for individual expressi on, while intergenerational learning alongside family members made family identity salient. The diverse group of participants who were from different tribes but who came together to share in their Indigenous comm unity identification also made both tribal and Indigenous community identities salient. Simultaneously, family identities were connected to home microsystems, while tribal and Indigenous community identities reflected the influence of multiple exosystems on identification. Marigold’s description of “walking in two worlds” a s an urban Native highlights the role of each indiv idual’s mesosystem in supporting identity development. Othe r participants expressions of shared tribal or Indigenous culture also reflected the influence of macrosystem s, as did the ways in which some participants positioned their identities and designs in relation to historicopolitical aspects of Indigeneity (e.g., Dahlia’s st ory about wearing ribbon shirts to fight for Indigenous rights in the 1970s or Rosy’s discussion of the identity politics of being a biracial Indigenous American).  Finally, across participants ’ designs, we saw the impact of the chronosystem in the integration of old and new technologies and of traditional cultural forms with new interests and expressions. These findings are consistent with prior research d emonstrating the ways in which the arts support identity development and self-expression (Halverson & Sheridan, 2014) and further our understanding of the ways in which makerspaces and STEAM learning might empower participants. They provide specific guidelines for the design of making activities to support identity work in ge neral, and in Indigenous or other marginalized communities in particular, including: (1) incorporating choice to support se lf-expression of individual or hybrid identities; (2) incorporating collaborative, intergenerational and/ or family learning to support development of identities related to family and tribal  or cultural groups; (3) community-based learning to allow members of a particular community or cultural group to learn with and from one another and to develop shared identities; and

(4) connecting traditional tools and practices tonew technologies to support participants in developing identities

that are grounded in cultural past and present while also looking to and imagining their Indigenous futures. Finally, the fact that many younger participants focused on individual or family identities, while older participants more often referenced broader tribal, Indigenous community, or dual/hybrid identities, suggests a trajectory of identity development beginning in specific micro systems and the n spiraling out to  broader understandings of identity in relation to more and more nested layers of ecological context. This is consistent with current approaches to instruction in fields like social studies, where young learners often start with learning about their local community then move to learning about broader state, national, and global contexts (Iowa Department of Education, 2017). It also suggests the type of supports for identity developm ent that learners of different ages might need and benefit from to keep exploring and understanding who they are and who they could be in relation to the world around them.
