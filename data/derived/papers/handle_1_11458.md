# Thematic Issues: Three Design Models to Address The Challenge of a New Interdisciplinary Course

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
The increasing complexity of societal challenges requires interdisciplinary approaches in education to cultivate critical thinking, adaptability, and a holistic understanding of global issues. In response, the Quebec college system’s Social Science program introduced the Thematic Issues course (TI), a single-instructor course designed to engage students in interdisciplinary inquiry through instructor-selected themes and three prescribed social science disciplines. To navigate the challenges of teaching across disciplines in an unstructured framework, instructors formed a Faculty Learning Community (FLC) to collaboratively refine course design and instructional strategies. This paper examines three TI course models: the serial model, offering compartmentalized insights; the spiral model, promoting cumulative, integrated learning; and the jigsaw model emphasizes collaborative synthesis by combining specialized disciplinary perspectives into a cohesive understanding. By analyzing these models, this practice-centered study highlights the pedagogical affordances and constraints of single-teacher interdisciplinary instruction.

## Context

As societal issues grow more complex, interdiscipli nary education is increasingly recognized as a way to help learners make sense of them. Curricula must prepare  students for real-world challenges by fostering critical thinking, adaptability, and transferable skills (Peiqi & Xionghu, 2003). This paper presents a practitioner-centered perspective on the development of a multidiscipline  course aimed at fostering interdisciplinary thinki ng. The course was collaboratively designed through the efforts of nine instructors (practitioners) at a large urban college in Quebec, part of the province’s unique 2-year post-secondary CEPEP system (1). These individuals were tasked with developing and teaching the first cohort of a 45-hour, third semester Social Science course called Thematic Issues (TI), which prepares students for a self-directed interdisciplinary project in their final (fourth) semester. As a core course within the program, the TI is offered in 20 sections annually, serving approximately 500 students. Operating as a Faculty Learning Community (FLC; to be defined shortly), the nine instructors brough t expertise from six disciplines: psychology, anthropology, philosophy, religious stu dies, sociology, and business administration. These instructors had no experience working within an explicit theoretical framework to prepare for the design of the interdisciplinary course.

Beginning in late May 2024, the instructors held in itial meetings of the FLC before pausing for the summer, during which independent course planning took place. Bi-weekly meetings resumed in Fall 2024, leading to the course's first large scale offering that semester. This piloting phase is currently ongoing (Winter 2025) and will extend into Fall 2025. Early design decisions were driven by pragmatic rea soning and practitioner insights. However, one FLC member––the first author —was simultaneously involved in a research-practice partnership focused on interdisciplinary instruction, allowing for the integration of research-informed insights of this process. Inasmuch, her role is characterized as that of boundary spanner (Akkerman & Bakker, 2011), bridging theory to practice and enriching the FLC’s pedagogical approach to interdisciplinary course design. The faculty learning community Drawing on the Cox model of Faculty Learning Commun ity (Cox, 2004), an FLC is a structured, collaborat ive group of educators who engage in an ongoing, schola rly, and collegial process to enhance teaching, learning and professional development (Richlin & Essington, 2004). This participatory approach offers several advan tages including fostering interdisciplinary collaboration, enhancing teaching practices and building a suppo rtive professional network that drives innovation in peda gogy. Participating in an FLC not only enhances ind ividual teaching practices but also fosters a sense of community and professional growth among instructors (Cox, 2004). The FLC participants consisted of instructors who were either assigned to teach the TI course or who were involved in its initial design. Course development began with a shared understanding of the course competencies. Over the summer, each instructor independently designed their course based on their interpretation of the  course competency and learning o utcomes. In Fall 2024, during biweekly FLC meetings, instr uctors collaboratively refined their course designs, addre ssing challenges and aligning their approaches befo re implementing them in their classrooms.

As noted earlier, the central challenge faced by this FLC stemmed from the nature of the TI course itself. With its open framework and minimal prescriptive gu idance, the course placed the responsibility on ind ividual instructors to independently design innovative peda gogical approaches that integrate diverse disciplin ary perspectives. This task w as further complicated by the fact that, like many post-secondary educators, the instructors lacked formal training in teaching and learning (Ramolula, & Nkoane, 2024) —what is often referred to as the Scholarship  of Teaching and Learning (SoTL). Moreover, the cour se required them to engage with content and perspectives beyond their own disciplinary expertise. These were just some of the issues the nine FLC members grappled with during their many meetings, w hich ultimately shaped the course design described in the next section.

Course design The initial results of the FLC were five proposals for the TI course design, these were subsequently refined to three models—Serial (Figure 1), Spiral (Figure 1), and Jigsaw (Figure 2)—each representing one of possible ways of addressing the interdisciplinary teaching challenge. These models were developed through instructors' experiential insights and collaborative brainstormi ng sessions. In large part, the FLC  instructors’ pragmatic practices and considerations of feasibility were the driving decision makers to settle on choos ing these three designs as the models to be piloted. Once decided by the FLC, the first author brought t hem to the research-practice group for deeper study, leading to the identification of feature s that suggest alignment with known learning sciences theories and instructional strategies. Case 1: Serial model The serial model (Figure 1) follows a low-integration multidisciplinary structure, where students explore themes sequentially, with instructors acting as content experts (Jacobs, 1989). In this model, each discipline is presented independently, allowing students to engage with multiple perspecti ves before drawing comparisons. This compartmentalized approach provides clarity but lim its synthesis, positioning students as passive obse rvers. While some instructors viewed this structure as a ba rrier to interdisciplinary learning, others found it beneficial for students unfamiliar with interdisciplinary work, as it offers a structured introduction before deeper integration. Case 2: Spiral model The spiral model (Figure 1)  adopts a high-integration interdisciplinary approach, reinforcing  cumulative understanding through iterative learning  cycles, reminiscent  of Bruner’s (1960) spiral curriculum  approach. It engages students in recursive knowledge  construction, aligning with authentic complexity of real-world issues (e.g. mental health and wellbeing, climate change).

This course design begins by establishing foundatio nal knowledge of a central theme. It then progressively incorporates additional layers, exami ning how various factors —such as cultural contexts, developmental stages, and systemic influences —shape and inte ract with the core concept. These elements are explored as interconnected components that contribu te to a broader understanding. Each stage builds up on the previous one, creating a cohesive framework that en ables students to apply their earlier learning to  increasingly complex ideas.

Figure 1 The Serial and Spiral Models The serial model (left) presents disciplines sequentially, examining themes in isolation before drawing connections, while the spiral model (right) interweaves disciplines, revisiting core themes at increasing depths for a cumulative and integrated understanding.

Case 3: Jigsaw model The jigsaw model (Figure 2), rooted in the cooperative learning framework (Aronson, et. al 1978; Johnson, et al., 2000), structures the TI course into two distinct phases. In the first phase, spanning the first 10 weeks of the semester, students are introduced to disciplinary concepts sequentially, much like in the serial mode. This phase establishes the foundational understanding of the course material. The final five weeks, the second phase, adopts a Jigsaw approach, where students are divided into groups to explore a specific social science discipline’s perspective. This model engages students in collaborative synthesis, r equiring them to actively contribute while instructors balance facilitation with content expertise. The Jigsaw model fosters student agency and interdependence, deepening critical thinking through peer teaching (Aronson et al., 1978). Figure 2 The Jigsaw Model This figure begins with a serial presentation of religion, introducing foundational concepts through three distinct components of the scientific study of reli gion. This is followed by an example showcasing the anthropology team's focused analysis, demonstrating  their exploration of religious practices. The exemplar represents the discipline-specific deep dive, a critical step in the collabor ative integration process that defines the jigsaw model.

## Conclusion

Designed as a foundation for interdisciplinary learning, the TI course guides students in examining contemporary issues from multiple disciplinary perspectives, hel ping them develop the analytical skills needed for integrative thinking. The development of the TI course models emerged fro m ongoing collaboration between a practitioner FLC and a research-practice team, mediated by a facilitator who acted as a boundary spanner. Using an FLC approach, nine instructors collaborated to identify  key challenges in interdisciplinary instruction and develop practical solutions for the TI course design. These discussions, informed by insights from the facilitator––working with the research team ––led to the identification of three pedagogical mode ls—serial, spiral, and jigsaw —each offering a distinct approach to the challenge of interdisciplinary learning. Importantly, they demonstrate that the grounded and pragmatic solutions generated by practitioners can also be  justified by the learning sciences research. In doing so,  these models could act as boundary objects that allow both the FLC and the research-practice groups to examine the implications of the course designs on interdisciplinary learning. Results from the initial pilot of the TI course revealed that most instructors adhered to the seria l model, likely due to its simple structure. However, two instructors from the FLC took the opportunity to experiment with the more interdisciplinary approaches –one implementing the spiral model and the other the  jigsaw model. The instructor who opted for the spiral model highlight ed its effectiveness in fostering  knowledge integration. In reflecting on the experience of working within this  model the instructor stated that it allowed her to  reflect more "authentic real-world situations" into her class activities (Participant 2, personal communication, [2025]). By examining core themes from different disciplinary p erspectives, it helps students integrate the knowle dge in meaningful ways that resemble solving authentic problems. The Spiral model was praised for its effectiveness in fostering deep understanding and sustained engagement. The instructor also noted that it required significant time to plan the curricular activities. The other instructor, who adopted the jigsaw model,  reported high student engagement. However, during an FLC discussion, conc erns were raised about the challenge of managing th e integration of the six disciplines within the exerc ise (recall Fig 2) disciplines within this model. D espite the challenges, he was encouraged by the results of the  jigsaw model and proposed exploring modifications to improve its feasibility, including a more purposefu l selection of disciplines to enhance the coherence  of the activity.

From the perspective of the practitioner, these three TI course models offer flexible feasible frameworks that can be adapted to various institutional and di sciplinary contexts and offer valuable insights for  educators seeking to foster integrative learning across disciplines. The Serial Model, with its discipline-specific sequencing, may be most suitable for institutions where interdi sciplinary coordination is limited but can serve as  a first step toward integration. The Spiral Model, which builds on iterative engagement with interdisciplinary them es, requires sustained collaboration but offers a scala ble approach for programs aiming to deepen integrat ion over time. The Jigsaw Model, emphasizing cooperative learning, is highly adaptable to settings that prioritize studentcentered learning and active engagement. From the perspective of learning sciences, these models are opportunities for research to examine how they function across different educational contexts and their impact on student learning outcomes, allowing us to better understand their effectiveness in fostering interdisciplinary thinking. Additionally, further i nvestigation could explore the extent to which the models align with constructivist and social constructivist princ iples, identifying potential affordances within their designs that may enhance interdisciplinary learning. From a facilitation perspective, the FLC approach proved to be a valuable mechanism for supporting interdisciplinary course design. It enabled the dev elopment, refinement, and implementation of the thr ee course design models while fostering a collaborative space for pe dagogical innovation. The role of the facilitator w as particularly crucial in maintaining a focus on inte rdisciplinary learning objectives. A key challenge involved balancing instructional design priorities with the traditionally content-driven orientation of higher education instructors. As this project continues, ongoing analysis in collabo ration with the research-practice group will further examine the effectiveness of these models and refine their implementation, particularly within the context of an FLC.
