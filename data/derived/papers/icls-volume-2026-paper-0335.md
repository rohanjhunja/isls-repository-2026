# Designing “With” Schools, Not Just for Them

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Many education technology(edTech) product rollouts underperform because tools are designed for schools instead of with them. In this practice paper we describe a three-month pilot where an edTech product manager, developer, and designer worked from the school, sat in classrooms, and co-created a Portfolio Analyzer with the Principal, teachers, and students. We adapted Design-Based Implementation Research (DBIR) within a Research –Practice Partnership (RPP) to run short improvement cycles: observe in class, try a change, check it in class, write it into school policy with a scope to review and iterate. The pilot saw strong use in Grades 5, 7, and 9 (~60 students, 10 teachers) with clear improvements to day-to-day usability and review flow, leading to a whole-school rollout decision. We shar e a simple, repeatable pattern for other K-12 schools and edtech teams.

Adoption—not just innovation —remains the central challenge in educational technology for schools. In India and elsewhere, many promising tools struggle to sustain use because they are designed for  schools rather than with them. End-users are rarely engaged early or continuously in the design process, technology access is uneven, and infrastructure is often unreliable. Moreover, most edtech companies lack “lab schools” that allow for rapid, classroom-based iteration. As a r esult, even tools with strong technical features fail to embed meaningfully in daily practice.

Our prior experiences reflected the same pattern: pilots began with enthusiasm but soon stalled. Products were shaped by developers’ memories of schooling rather than the realities of today’s classrooms, and lacked mechanisms for genuine, in-class feedback. These difficulties are widely documented in research showing that limited end-user participation during design leads to weak trust, poor contextual fit, and fragile implementation (Coburn & Penuel, 2016; Farrell et al., 2021). Conversations with multiple  founders echoed this theme —design cycles that ended before real adoption began, and minimal collaboration with teachers or school leadership once pilots started.

To address these recurring issues, we formed an embedded, co-located partnership between an edtech team and a K– 12 school. Instead of designing from a distance, the product manager, developer, and designer worked within the school, sat in classrooms, observed lessons, and made small, visible changes alongside teachers and students. The product at the center of this pilot —the Portfolio Analyzer —allowed students to upload their project work and reflections, teachers to provide granular feedback, and school leaders to track students project progress in specific subjects across grades.

In this practice paper we present a process of how to design: a classroom-embedded, co-creation practice guided by Design-Based Implementation Research (DBIR) within a Research –Practice Partnership (RPP) framework (Coburn & Penuel, 2016; Farrell et al., 2021). By sitting in classrooms, closing feedback loops within days, and writing decisions directly into policy, this partnership achieved full-school adoption within three months. While DBIR and RPPs are often multi-year endeavors, our case demonstrates how their principles can be compressed into short, iterative cycles that still leave behind durable artifacts such as policies, roles, and “howto” guides. Theoretical foundations Research–Practice Partnerships (RPPs) are long-term, two-way collaborations between researchers and practitioners that aim to improve educational practice in authentic settings. They emphasize shared goals, co-ownership of methods, and the development of organizational capacity rather than tool-specific proficiency (Coburn & Penuel, 2016; Farrell et al., 2021). Within such partnerships, Design-Based Implementation Research (DBIR) offers a complementary approach focused on tackling persistent problems of pra ctice through iterative cycles of: co-design, real world use, refinement. Crucially, DBIR treats implementation —policies, roles, and workflows—as part of the design itself, rather than an afterthought (Fishman et al., 2013). In this work, the RPP framework provided the governance and shared ownership necessary for sustained collaboration, while DBIR supplied the method for continuous, evidence-informed improvement. The 2167 combination ensured that design decisions were negotiated in context, tested rapidly, and embedded within institutional practice. We translate the principles of RPP and DBIR into a co-located, classroom-embedded cocreation practice—one where product teams  and educators work side by side in classrooms, observe use in real time, and iterate daily with students and teachers. This approach aligns with co-creation and participatory design scholarship, emphasizing design with users rather than for them (Sanders & Stappers, 2008; Han et al., 2023). By grounding design in lived classroom experience, we extend these theoretical models into a practical, scalable pattern for K–12 edtech partnerships.

Rapid DBIR within RPP Our implementation followed a co-located and co-created model. A small product trio: comprising a product manager, developer, and designer worked from within the school alongside the principal, teachers, and students. The principal and section heads brokered trade-offs between pedagogy and technology, while student realities, such as device access, language proficiency, and workflow determined design priorities. Each cycle followed a consistent rhythm: diagnose, ideate, build a minimal viable change, test in class, collect feedback, iterate. Issues were logged during classroom observations, and fixes were deployed within hours or days. This rapid turnaround transformed abstract “edtech versus pedagogy” debates into grounded, evidence-based decisions observed directly in context. The stance was intentionally student-centered: prompts, labels, and workflows were designed first for student clarity, then simplified for teachers and administrators. Concretely, the Portfolio Analyzer allowed students to upload diverse forms of work—images, documents, presentations, or links (Drive, Canva, video)—and respond to structured reflection prompts such as Basic Info, Role & Originality, Skills & Reflection, Process & Challenges, Documentation, Learnings/Transfer, Gratitude, and Review & Submit. Teachers viewed dashboards, left per-question and overall feedback, requested revisions, and approved submissions. School leaders monitored progress across grades. The technical stack combined Next.js (Vercel) for the frontend and Supabase for authentication, data, and storage, supporting secure, role-based access for students, teachers, and administrators. This rapid, classroom-embedded DBIR cycle demonstrated how close proximity between developers and educators accelerates both learning and trust. Each iteration moved the product from a “technology-first” solution to an authentic classroom tool co-shaped by its users—a shift that ultimately enabled whole-school adoption. Implementation details The pilot took place at a philanthropic lab school, Shikha Academy, in Mumbai, India, serving students from lowincome backgrounds and affiliated with the Cambridge curriculum. The initial cohort included approximately 60 students across Grades 5, 7, and 9, supported by 10 teachers. Following the pilot, the school approved a scale-up to roughly 300 students and 35 teachers.

Implementation unfolded over three structured phases as follows:

1.  Problem definition (June 20–28, 2025): The team documented real classroom workflows, device

constraints, and success signals.

2.  Setup and planning (June 29–July 19): The first version of the Portfolio Analyzer was deployed, initial

reporting needs were configured, and pilot logistics were agreed upon—grade selection, a pilot lead, fortnightly review meetings, and a three-month test window.

3.  Classroom testing (July–September 2025): Beginning with a demo and hands-on session, teachers

requested improvements such as per-question comments, rich text, subject lists, and notifications. A short test validated submission and review flows before classroom use began. Across three DBIR cycles (August 1 –September 12), the team stabilized and refined the product based on classroom observations. The first cycle prioritized mobile reliability, introducing auto-save and draft restore to eliminate data loss. The second cycle focused on precision in feedback, adding per-question comments, gradeappropriate wording, and clearer Save/Submit placement. The third cycle improved review efficiency with new filters (subject, month), confirmed admin roles, and postponed noncritical features like notifications and large file uploads.

Each issue was acknowledged within 24– 48 hours, with fixes typically shipped within a week and re-verified in class. The process clarified roles across all stakeholders: Principal – set goals and timelines, Pilot lead

– coordinated feedback and communication, Product lead and developer – scoped, prioritized, and implemented

changes, Teachers – conducted reviews and validated usability, Students and student monitors – created portfolios, supported peers, and communicated issues, Administrators – oversaw progress and data access. A key policy outcome stated that teachers should provide initial feedback within ten working days and final approval within five, with five submission windows per subject each year. Data ethics and privacy were ensured through 2168 leadership oversight, secure role-based access, and student choice over whether their approved portfolios were made public.

## Case study: Co-design from a classroom

By the end of the three-month pilot, the co-design approach produced clear evidence of improved engagement and usability. All 60 students created at least two portfolio entries, generating over 120 submissions in total. Ten teachers used the system regularly, providing both per-question and overall feedback. The revision loop: submit, review, revise, approve worked as intended, and administrators were able to monitor progress across grades in real time.

Informal feedback showed that students preferred this tool to the previous solution (Google Sites) because it allowed them to document their process, receive targeted feedback, and publish polished portfolios they could proudly share. Teachers valued the new item-level feedback structure, which reduced repetitive corrections and clarified expectations for students. The centralized dashboard also made it easier to track submissions and follow up on pending work.

Two sample vignettes In the first, teachers requested per-question comments during the demo phase. The earlier single feedback box made it difficult for students to identify what to fix. With the Principal’s approval for a short trial, the development team implemented per-question feedback within a week and removed the redundant end-page comment box. The change reduced back-and-forth communication, and students began revising exactly where teachers had indicated. The school later incorporated this feature into its official portfolio policy, and teacher training now includes examples of short, precise feedback comments.

In the second, students reported losing text on mobile devices when refreshing or accidentally closing the browser. The team paused new feature development and prioritized reliability —adding auto-save and draft restoration, and moving the Save/Submit button from the top to a more visible position at the bottom. After this change, reports of lost work dropped to zero. Updated student guides now reassure users that “Your work autosaves,” and teachers highlight this during orientation sessions. Design lessons Across the three-month collaboration, five key lessons emerged that shaped both the process and the outcomes of co-designing in classrooms. These insights are not prescriptive checklists but grounded principles that any edtech– school partnership can adapt.

Mobile-first reliability is non-negotiable-In resource-constrained classrooms, where students often access tools through shared or low-end mobile devices, technical stability determines trust. The pilot reinforced that basic reliability—auto-save, draft restore, and clear action buttons—must come b efore any advanced feature. When the application performed predictably, students persisted longer and engaged more confidently, reducing frustration for both learners and teachers.

Precision in feedback improves learning and efficiency-Item-level comments transformed the feedback process. Teachers could pinpoint specific areas of improvement rather than leaving broad notes that students found difficult to interpret. This change reduced unnecessary revision cycles and gave students a clea rer sense of progress. The insight underscored that design decisions in educational tools must support the granularity of authentic teaching practices, not simplify them for convenience. “Policy is product.” -  An unexpected but powerful realization was that sustainability depends on codifying successful practices into school policy. By setting explicit turnaround times for feedback and approval, and establishing clear submission windows, the school transformed a  digital tool into a living workflow. Clear rules reduced uncertainty, prevented delays, and lightened the support burden on teachers and administrators. In effect, policy design became a parallel act of product design—each reinforcing the other. Stabilize before adding features-Throughout the pilot, feature requests emerged continuously, but not all were equally urgent. The team learned that prioritizing stability and ease of use before layering new functionality kept the system lean and reliable. Deferred “nice-to-haves” such as notifications or large-file uploads were addressed only once the core experience—submission, feedback, and review —was fully dependable. This approach prevented feature creep and protected teachers from unnecessary complexity. Co-location accelerates learning and trust-Working within the school enabled immediate, evidence-based decision-making. Observing classroom use in real time meant that technical and pedagogical discussions could converge around shared observations rather than assumptions. The proximity allowed the team to move 2169 through the cycle— observe, decide, fix within days. As a result, improvements were grounded in lived classroom experience rather than external hypotheses (Fishman et al., 2013; Sanders & Stappers, 2008). While the pilot did not include formal pre-and post-tests, its next phase aims to build on these lessons through systematic data tracking. Metrics such as on-time submissions, feedback turnaround (≤10 days for first feedback, ≤5 days for final approval), number of revision rounds, and reflection completeness will provide quantitative insight into process improvement. A forthcoming version of the Portfolio Analyzer will also link student reflections to life-skills rubrics and, once stable adoption is achieved, explore AI-assisted analysis to support reflective learning at scale.

Relevance for others Although frameworks like Research– Practice Partnerships (RPP) and Design-Based Implementation Research (DBIR) are well established in the learning sciences, practical models that translate these ideas into short, repeatable routines for schools remain scar ce (Coburn & Penuel, 2016; Fishman et al., 2013). This pilot offers one such model: a pattern of rapid, embedded collaboration that can be adopted even by small teams working within everyday school constraints. Our approach drew inspiration from agile software methods (Okeke, 2024)— co-location, continuous feedback, and iterative improvement —but adapted these principles to the rhythms of schooling. Weekly routines were intentionally simple: one classroom observation, one review meeting, continuous small fixes, and about two hours of hands-on teacher training. These touchpoints balanced improvement with manageability, ensuring that design never outpaced classroom capacity. The artifacts that emerged from the process —policy templates, role descriptions, feedback and triage rules, and quick-start guides—were deliberately designed for reuse. They enable other schools or small edtech teams to replicate the core model in approximately three months. Even more important than the artifacts themselves are the habits  they cultivate: shared observation, open communication, and collective decisionmaking. These are the true indicators of a sustainable partnership. The case also highlights practical boundaries. Co-location can intensify the workload for small product teams if every issue is treated as urgent. The key is to batch fixes weekly, defer noncritical features, and maintain a shared “Now / Next / Later” board that aligns priorities across the school and development team. Each change should be verified in class before release, protecting against drift between intention and use. Attention to ethics and infrastructure is equally vital. In low-resource settings, device access and connectivity cannot be assumed. Favoring lightweight file formats, protecting draft work, and planning for intermittent internet connectivity proved essential to maintaining student trust. Leadership continuity was another focus: by documenting roles, maintaining an admin panel, and embedding policies into school routines, the system became resilient to staff turnover.

Ultimately, this case underscores a larger lesson: many edtech initiatives fail not because they lack features, but because they lack collaborative implementation. A co-located, student-centered DBIR-within-RPP stance makes the process itself the innovation. It demonstrates that even small teams candesign with  schools— producing tools that grow from the classroom outward rather than being imposed from above. Similar work in Indian classrooms, such as contextual handwriting assessment tools (Vyas et al., 2021), reinforces that authentic collaboration and iterative refinement remain central to creating educational technology that truly fits its context.
