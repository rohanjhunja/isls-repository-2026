## Conceptual framework

The conceptual framework of this study is grounded in literature about ambient identity cues, defined as symbols in the environment  that indicate who belongs  (Cheryan et al., 2009; Kim et al., 2024, 2025). For example, the presence of posters of famous White scientists and the lack of posters of scientists of color in a science classroom may cause students of color to perceive that they do not belong in science. The theory of ambient identity cues can be further understood with reference to the sociocultural theory of identity, according to which i dentity is formed through interactions between a person and mat erial, relational, and ideational identity resources in the social context (Nasir & Cooks, 2009; Nasir et al., 2020; Penuel & Wertsch, 1995). In the STEM domain, stereotypical conceptions that only certain groups are fit for STEM careers have been present (Dunlap & Barth, 2023; McGee, 2016, 2018). These stereotypes can be reified through ambient identity cues  (Cheryan et al., 2009). Previous research has shown that stereotype endorsement of minoritized groups can lead to a lack of interest in the domain and a sense of exclusion (Cheryan et al., 2009; Dunlap & Barth, 2023; Master, 2021). Domain interest is known to be positively connected with academic choices and achievement (Schiefele, 2009). For children to freely dream about their future selves, it is important for researchers and practitioners to provide an environment where they can sense belonging in multiple disciplines. Based on this consideration, this study focused on exploring children’s reactions to ambient identity cues in the STEM domain with consideration of their stereotype awareness, stereotype endorsement, and interest in the STEM domain. Moreover, while previous studies on ambient identity cues emphasized how learners’ domain interest changes based on belongingness, this study additionally takes situational interest into account. Situational interest is a focused state associated with enjoyment or curiosity, which can be triggered by cognitive conflict or surprise (Hidi, 2000; Renninger, 2000; Schiefele, 2009). Since this study tries to explore children’s reactions to ambient identity cues in classrooms, children’s moment-to-moment reactions may also be understood more in depth within the framework of situational interest and identity resources.

The following question guided this study:  How and why do children react to ambient identity cues in STEM classrooms?

## Method

The data in this study come  from a larger research project, the goal of which was to explore how elementary learners and teachers interpret ambient identity cues and why. Videos with equitable ambient identity cues that the teacher identified through codesign were integrated into elementary STEM lessons. 74 participants in grades 3 to 5 were observed in STEM classrooms. The data sources include teacher-identified ambient identity cues in the videos, classroom observations, and student surveys. The survey collected participant demographics, Role Identity Surveys in STEM (Paul et al., 2020), which also included children’s STEM interest, and two revised items on student awareness and endorsement of STEM stereotypes (Nasir et al., 2017). For this study, one video and data from four participants in grade 5 were analyzed to explore how children interact with ambient identity cues in a specific context. One video was selected as a focal case based on the number of teacher-identified ambient cues. The selected video was a video that explains the life of optical engineers and an inclusive company culture  (KLA Corp., 2023). This video included adult figures with multiple racial cues and STEM-related objects, which teachers identified (see Figures 1 and 2). Among the 15 students who watched the video, four students were selected as participants based on the richness of observation data (i.e., amount of observation notes; see Table 1).

## Data analysis

Thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2006, 2021) using NVivo 14 was conducted to analyze children’s responses while watching the selected video. Participants’ overall observation and survey data were used to either calibrate the response level or to understand their actions related to the context. To understand the context clearly, participants’ observation data were all coded using the coding scheme. The coding scheme (see Table 2) used for this study was the version created in a larger research project based on a conceptual framework, collected data, team discussion, and pilot coding with a partial dataset. Survey data was used as a reference to trace the participants’ identity and interest related to STEM.

2042 Since we used the coding scheme from a larger project, our data analysis method would be classified as ‘codebook thematic analysis’ based on Braun & Clarke (2021)’s classification.  Still, this study was made as a separate study from the larger project to check whether new perspectives and themes emerge from a different scope of data. Therefore, we went through both inductive and deductive coding to use our experience from previous data analysis and to check any newly emergent findings. Moreover, while we agree that calculating interrater reliability is useful in many contexts (O’Connor & Joffe, 2020), we also agree that methods of  assessing quality of qualitative research candiffer by context  (Braun & Clarke, 2021). Considering the limited amount  of data used in this study  and the purpose of revisiting data from a  new perspective, we decided to be open to differences in how data could be coded (Braun & Clarke, 2021; Patton, 2014) but tried to recognize the different interpretations, revise the coding scheme, and develop themes through in-depth discussion. To enhance rigor in research during this process, we triangulated researchers involved in data analysis (Patton, 2014). The selected data were coded using the coding scheme. After coding, researchers revisited the coded data multiple times and discussed whether the data was coded based on the coding scheme and whether there were any emerging insights throughout the coding process. Throughout  the discussion, t he researchers took detailed notes to advance sensemaking of data and  discussed the notes. Coded data were revisited multiple times during the discussion. After generating preliminary themes based on the discussion, the researchers revisited the selected video, observation notes, and survey data again and finalized the themes through in-depth discussion. Finding 1: Children exhibited salient reactions to ambient identity cues that were not identified by teachers.

When children were watching the selected video, they showed excitement when an Asian female adult looked through the glass with a dialogue recalling her childhood experience where she decided to become an optical engineer (see Figure 1). Observation notes specify how two children responded to that moment. For example, we could see Mia’s excitement based on the observation note that “s he was also excited for the scene in the video when the leaf was burnt by the light and glass (Observation notes about Mia)” and Warren’s engagement through observation note that mentioned “h e talked about how the light burnt the leaf with the glass  (Observation notes about Warren).” This burning leaf  cue was not specified in the teacher's identified ambient cues, which implies a possibility that children may interact with ambient identity cues in an unexpected way from the perspective of adults. This also aligns with previous research (Kim et al., 2024). Further research is needed to fully understand which ambient identity cues students react to and why.

Finding 2: Children reacted to racial and gender-related ambient identity cues only when actions associated with disciplinary objects were also presented. While children reacted to the ambient identity cues embedded in the Asian woman  recounting the experience of using glass to burn a leaf leading her to pursue optical engineering (Finding 1), children did not react to other people seen in the video (see Figure 2) even when they had more relatedness with other people in terms of race or gender. The difference between the Asian woman and other figures in the video was how they were presented in the video. The other figures  appeared in an interview format, where they were presented o n one side of the screen, describing what they do as an optical engineer and what their company culture is like. After one person’s dialogue, scenes of the company (e.g., equipment, chips, conference, meeting) were shown and another person appeared to share their dialogue. It was evident that the main content of the video was about optical engineering 2043 and the company, not one person’s story as a protagonist. Racial or gender identity cues were used mainly to portray an inclusive company culture rather than empowering certain groups of race or gender. The static presence of people-related ambient identity cues might not suffice  for participants to connect possible ambient identity cues with themselves. While it is conceivable that adding ambient identity cues related to children would be enough to help them sense belongness and interest, how it is presented seems to be the key for ambient identity cues to get even noticed. In this sense, it would be beneficial  for children to see STEM discipline related actions of people with racial or gender identities with which children can resonate. Finding 3: Children’s reactions reflected their situational interest rather than individual interest.

Surprisingly, c hildren’s reactions to ambient identity cues did not align with perceived stereotype awareness, stereotype endorsement, and STEM interest indicated in the surveys (see Table 1 and Figure 3). How focused the participants were when ambient identity cues were presented in the classroom w as not related to their STEM interest in the survey. This applied to their stereotype awareness and endorsement. For example, Warren actively reacted to the video (Finding 1), but his survey responses indicated his STEM interest was lowest among the four participants. Annie showed the highest STEM interest among the four participants but did not appear to be interested in STEM content in the classroom based on her reaction. Annie “kept watching the video without any reaction or behavioral change ” (Observation notes about Annie).  All four students responded in the survey that they were aware of stereotypes in STEM, but their reactions to the videos were different from each other. Austin was the only student among four who did not respond as endorsing stereotypes in STEM, but there was not much engaging reaction that stood out from Austin.

Stereotype awareness, stereotype endorsement, and interest in the domain may not necessarily determine how children perceive belonging in a domain-related environment based on contextual ambient identity cues. Children’s reaction to ambient identity cues might be a more contextual phenomenon associated with situational interest. Still, further research on  ambient identity cues, situational interest, and children’s reactions would be needed to explore possible connections.

## Implications for research and practice

Despite the small number of participants, this research shares important findings that (a) ambient identity cues may need to be presented along with actions using STEM discipline-related objects for elementary children to make their own meaning of ambient identity cues, and (b) it is critical to take children’s perspectives to understand how they interpret ambient identity cues.
