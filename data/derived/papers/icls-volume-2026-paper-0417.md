# Grafemos: A Harmonious Partnership to Drive Educational Innovation and Decolonize Complexity Education Leveraging Learning Engineering

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
The Grafemos complexity education project promotes equitable STEM by blending Indigenous Mayan knowledge with non-computational modelling in rural communities. Over two years, it nurtures culturally responsive educators through Mayan weaving epistemologie s, enhancing self-efficacy, growth mindsets, and community innovation. Using the nested learning engineering process (LEP), authentic assessment, and collaboration, Grafemos develops local leaders to sustain inclusive, decolonizing complexity education, transforming their region into a hub of innovative educational practice.

Computer-based modelling helps young learners understand emergent phenomena such as traffic jams and flocking birds (Danish, 2014; Goldstone & Wilensky, 2008; Wilkerson-Jerde & Wilensky, 2015). However, in rural developing countries such as Guatemala, opportunities and professional development are scarce, and internet-dependent methods exacerbate educational inequities (Antoninis et al., 2023). Grafemos offers a noncomputational, epistemology-based project, co-created with Mayan teachers and supported by t he Ixkoj Ajkem Council, to promote decolonized complexity education (Lam-Herrera, 2022). Research background Grafemos stems from over 20 years of collaboration with a Guatemalan Indigenous community, creating a physical modelling environment and pedagogical framework with the Ixkoj Ajkem Council (Lam-Herrera, 2022). It helps decolonize complexity education by blending Maya-Kaqchikel epistemologies with Western methods and critiquing AI-driven technocentrism (Antoninis et al., 2023). Case studies on issues like alcoholism, minors' sexual reproduction, and river degradation illustrate Grafemos' role in fostering mul ti-level reasoning, ethical debates, and community-based learning (Levy & Wilensky, 2008; Russ et al., 2008; Bang & Marin, 2015; Cajete, 2000; Keogh et al., 2018).

## Theoretical framework

This work develops teachers' skills across cognitive, interpersonal, and intrapersonal domains (Lyndgaard & Kanfer, 2024). Cognitive skills include problem-solving and digital literacy, interpersonal skills include communication and leadership, and  intrapersonal skills include self-belief and self-regulation. Learning is effective when these are integrated and supported by curricula, technology, and social capital. A main goal is cultivating accurate self-efficacy (SE) (Bandura, 1992; Dweck, 2002); therefore, we follow Lyndgaard et al.

(2024) and focus on Adaptive Management SE, Academic SE, and Transfer SE, which connect learning to future

goals. Scaffolded training reduces barriers and builds confidence, persistence, and collaboration, emphasizing effort and resilience in line with Dweck’s growth mindset (2002). This is supported by using Elkhoury’s (2023) authentic assessment framework. Thus, Grafemos employs authentic, culturally responsive assessments that honour prior knowledge, foster feedback literacy, and promote belonging. Activities like community modelling, digital storytelling, and memory cycles deepen reflection and agency (McDermott, 2021; Pashler et al., 2007). Guided by a Learning Engineering approach (Goodell & Kolodner, 2022; Kessler & Totino, 2024), this work uses iterative, human-centred design and data-driven decisions to develop a decolonizing STEM hub in Guatemala, supporting scalable innovation globally (Globalization SIG, IEEE, n.d.). Grafemos is a design-based project that, as its initial step, uses the Grafemos Activity©. The project partners with—and is accountable to — the target community while supporting the decolonization of STEM education. To guide innovation and scaling, the project follows a Nested LEP, which emphasizes learners’ “time, path, pace, place, choice, and voice” (Goodell & Kolodner, 2022, p. x; Kessler & Totino, 2024). Led by a Guatemalan scholar in Canada and colleagues in Guatemala, Grafemos is shaped by logistical constraints, a commitment to cultural and ethical responsiveness, and love. All teachers in training are from the target community, many belong to the 2680 Ixkoj Ajkem Council, and the initial group of eight women educators (M = 45.9, SD = 11.7) draw on both formal and informal education. Despite historical barriers to schooling (Farriss, 1984; Quijano, 2000), they bring extensive Indigenous knowledge and experience. Ethics oversight is provided by the Council and AFEDES (Women’s Association for the Development of Sacatepéquez). Within the Nested LEP, data at the individual, community, and program levels are used to monitor learning, refine practice, and build evidence for scale. This iterative, data‑driven approach—grounded in learning sciences and instructional design and infused with Mayan perspectives—aims to improve teaching and advance educational justice and community self‑determination.

## Ongoing work and preliminary results

Technocentric STEM models, though influential, depend on privileged resources and thus reproduce inequities for Indigenous and rural communities such as those in Guatemala (Antoninis et al., 2023). In contrast, Grafemos’ design-based approach, grounded in Indigenous perspectives and developed collaboratively, enables participants to co-create innovative practices and engage with complexity education regardless of digital access. Activities are iteratively refined using the Nested LEP (Kessler & Totino, 2024). Over 24 months, the training combines online instruction, workshops, and activity design with reflection and storytelling (Lam-Herrera, 2022), sustaining high engagement and zero attrition despite health and social crises. The Nested LEP includes three levels: Project (Top), Community (Group), and Intervention (Individual). At the project level, data-driven strategies remove barriers and foster an inclusive environment that promotes systemic problem-solving, respect for Indigenous knowledge, and the integration of Mayan cultural practices – especially textile art – into STEM education. This is realized by preparing active community members to become Grafemos Facilitators. Participants report growth in reading, vocabulary, and oral communication and are already acting as agents of emergent change. At the group level, we examine how the eight teachers develop culturally relevant models of local issues to foster trust and shared purpose. They engage in a variety of learning activities to build the knowledge needed to lead professional workshops. Structured reflection points help them envision their im pact beyond the classroom  (Lyndgaard et al., 2024). We draw analogies from weaving, community realities, and everyday life to connect experience to learning. After each class, participants complete a group questionnaire with open-ended questions and a self-reported confidence level. They report high satisfaction and describe post-class debates in which they recognize their progress and shared sense of purpose. This is vital, as half of the teachers are Mayan knowledge keepers with limited formal schooling w ho report feeling welcomed and valued rather thandefined by a perceived lack of formal education. This underscores the importance of multiple forms of assessment, rehearsal, and discussion. At the individual level, we assess self-efficacy and support a growth mindset with process-oriented feedback  (Dweck, 2002; Lyndgaard, 2024; Lyndgaard et al., 2024).

Participants produce artifacts such as narratives and videos on topics including instructional design, complexity education (the Grafemos Activity©), project planning, and data collection. Multiple assessments document their progress. For example, participants lead ~40-minute lessons on design principles using provided printed materials and slides, followed by constructive peer critique. This structure has proven effective: the training environment is warm, trusting, and intellectually generative  (Elkhoury, 2023). Within the complexity education component, participants enact the Grafemos Activity©. They bring written stories and short videos portraying complex community situations and reason through the elements and interactions in each narrative. We record classes and analyze selected segments using the same framework as in the initial Grafemos work. An early illustrative case is the story of the Barriletes del Día de Todos los Santos  [The Kites of All Saints’ Day]. A teacher shared a three-minute video documenting a community gathering in an open field. She identified the kite, the holder, the thread, the design, and the colours as key elements  (MchR, Russ et al., 2008) and explained how the thread symbolizes a connection to heaven when the kite is flying. Attaching a written message to the kite and letting it rise was understood as a way to send a message to departed loved ones, in her case, her son (Cultural and Personal Connections  [CPC], Lam-Herrera, 2022). She emphasized that this day is special for her, for families in the community, and for Guatemala as a whole, and that it is a time to send messages to the spiritual world. Her account shows how this cultural practice can be described at multiple levels (LR, Levy & Wilensky, 2008), revealing deep interconnections among emotion, culture, and complex systems thinking. Future steps Future project phases will co-create Mayan-grounded professional development for local teachers, focusing on ethical data practices and participant protection. By training educators in both formal and informal settings, the project will expand affordable w orkshops – especially for rural Mayan teachers – while charging non-rural and international participants to sustain the work.
