# Dignity-Affirming Music Pedagogy: Reframing Music as Relational Design in Learning Spaces

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In K-12 education settings, music is often framed as an enrichment activity, overlooking its deeper role in shaping dignity-affirming learning spaces and in organizing interaction among community members. We examine music as interactional infrastructure, asking how it makes dignity observable in learning environments. Using Rhythmic Interaction Analysis (RIA), we analyze a drum circle interaction to examine how participation unfolds through rhythmic turns. We trace how vulnerability, attunement, and agency emerge as participants respond to one another, illustrating how dignity can be made visible through coordinated, embodied interaction. Problem / reframe Within K-12 education settings, music is often invoked to motivate participation, support memorization, or improve testable skills (Bhide, Power, & Goswami, 2013; Governor, Hall, & Jackson, 2013; Ashley & Timmers, 2017; Kumar et al., 2022). Such framings risk eclipsing music’s relational, historical, and identity work (Blacking, 1973; Small, 1998; Robinson, 2020; Hess, 2019). At the same time, dignity-affirming education (DAE) calls for learning environments that center learners’ full humanity—supporting vulnerability, agency, and meaningful participation through relational practice (Espinoza, et al., 2020; Vossoughi, et al., 2018; Keifert, et al., 2021). This perspective shifts attention from outcomes alone to the conditions under which learners are recognized, supported, and able to contribute. We take up this shift by approaching music as an epistemic and affective partner that co-organizes learning. In this view, meaning-making unfolds as a collective process of “rhythmic attunement” —people sensing, responding to, and adapting with one another, much like players in an ensemble. Grounded in DAE, ethnomusicology, and sociocultural theories, we ask: How can music-infused pedagogy—listening, speaking about music, and making music together —cultivate learning environments that affirm students’ dignity and agency while expanding relational forms of participation? Conceptual orientation: Dignity as interaction Rhythm has long been understood as a structuring feature of social interaction, shaping how participants coordinate attention, timing, and response (Erickson, 1982; Bassetti & Bottazzi, 2015). We examine how rhythmic interaction organizes participation itself. As participants coordinate through rhythm, they make space for one another’s contributions —creating conditions where participation can be recognized, supported, and sustained. From this perspective, dignity is not an abstract quality of a learning environment, but something enacted in time, as participants recognize, respond to, and sustain one another’s contributions. We ground this work in dignity-affirming education, which frames learning as a relational practice enacted through everyday interaction. To make this construct analytically tractable, we focus on three observable dimensions: vulnerability—moments where participants take risks (e.g., initiating a contribution); attunement—moments of alignment (e.g., synchronized timing or coordinated response); and agency—moments where participants shape the direction of activity.

## Method: Rhythmic Interaction Analysis (RIA)

To trace participation in both spoken and musical exchanges, we extend conversation analysis (CA) to sonic interaction (Pomerantz & Fehr, 2011). In CA, turns are the basic unit of interaction—how contributions begin, unfold, and are taken up. We treat rhythmic actions —such as drumming, movement, and embodied response — as turns in a musical conversation. We refer to this approach as Rhythmic Interaction Analysis (RIA). This approach makes visible how participation develops over time, including moments where risk is taken, recognized, and sustained.

## Context

Data come from HubSpace, a multi-year, community-based participatory program serving middle school youth. Music-making—particularly collective drumming —is a central practice through which participants explore expression, coordination, and shared activity.

## Finding: Dignity is enacted through rhythmic interaction

We illustrate this through a drum circle interaction in which participants take turns contributing rhythmic phrases. Earlier in the day, one participant (d5) had been among the quieter voices in the verbal group interaction. When she is invited to initiate a solo, her initial contribution is hesitant and low in volume. As others respond—through head nods, foot tapping, and rhythmic mirroring—her playing becomes more stable and pronounced. These embodied responses signal immediate recognition without interruption, allowing her to continue. In this moment, vulnerability (risking entry) is met with attunement (aligned response), supporting continued participation. Notably, these responses occur within seconds of her initial contribution, creating a narrow window in which her participation is recognized and sustained.
