# Supporting Children’s Systems Thinking about Climate Change

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This case study examined two Grade 5 climate-learning scaffolds: teacher-guided causal mapping and water-cycle-based feedback loop instruction. Analysis of classroom video, student artefacts, and interviews showed that causal mapping expanded one-step claims into mechanistic chains but rarely produced loop closure or delays. Feedback loop instruction supported recognition of cyclicity and reinforcing loops, yet students still struggled with balancing loops, time lags, and energy-budget reasoning. Rationale and background Systems thinking equips learners to interpret complex, dynamic phenomena by tracing interdependencies, mechanisms, and feedbacks (Ben‑Zvi Assaraf & Orion, 2005; Hmelo‑Silver et al., 2017). Climate change is a paradigmatic “system problem,” marked by nonlinear causality, delays, and reinforcing loops (Roychoudhury et al., 2017; Sterman & Booth Sweeney, 2002). Yet research shows that elementary students often identify components and simple cause –effect links but struggle with multi‑step causal chains and feedback loops (Evagorou et al., 2009; Mambrey et al., 2020; Tripto et al., 2016). Despite growing emphasis on systems thinking in policy and curriculum, there remains a practical gap: classroom‑tested, developmentally appropriate scaffolds that help younger learners move beyond linear reasoning (Gilissen et al., 2020; Leung et al., 2024). This study responds by co‑designing with teachers two complementary, curriculum‑aligned interventions —causal mapping and feedback‑loop instruction—and examining how each supports students’ systems thinking in the context of climate change. Conceptual framework This study builds on the Components –Mechanisms–Phenomena (CMP) framework (Hmelo-Silver et al., 2017), which guides learners to recognize system components (C), infer mechanisms (M) that connect components, and explain emergent phenomena (P). To deepen causal reasoning, the study integrates insights from Ben-Zvi Assaraf and Orion’s (2005) hierarchical systems thinking model, which progresses from identifying system components and simple relations, to recognizing dynamic feedback loops and predicting future s ystem states. Drawing on prior findings that young learners’ systems thinking develops nonlinearly and contextually (Mambrey et al., 2020; Liu & Hmelo-Silver, 2009), this study designed two distinct instructional pathways: (1) Causal mapping is expected to lengthen and clarify causal chains (e.g., “cutting trees for a mall → fewer sinks for CO₂ → higher atmospheric CO₂ → intensified greenhouse effect → warming”); and (2) Feedback-loop instruction leverages the familiar water cycle to foreground cyclicity and transfer those ideas to climate feedbacks (e.g., ice– albedo feedback). ## Conceptual framework

This study builds on the Components –Mechanisms–Phenomena (CMP) framework (Hmelo-Silver et al., 2017), which guides learners to recognize system components (C), infer mechanisms (M) that connect components, and explain emergent phenomena (P). To deepen causal reasoning, the study integrates insights from Ben-Zvi Assaraf and Orion’s (2005) hierarchical systems thinking model, which progresses from identifying system components and simple relations, to recognizing dynamic feedback loops and predicting future s ystem states. Drawing on prior findings that young learners’ systems thinking develops nonlinearly and contextually (Mambrey et al., 2020; Liu & Hmelo-Silver, 2009), this study designed two distinct instructional pathways:  (1) Causal mapping is expected to lengthen and clarify causal chains (e.g., “cutting trees for a mall → fewer sinks for CO₂ → higher atmospheric CO₂ → intensified greenhouse effect → warming”); and (2) Feedback-loop instruction leverages the familiar water cycle to foreground cyclicity and transfer those ideas to climate feedbacks (e.g., ice– albedo feedback).

## Participants and context

Two elementary science teachers and their Grade 5 classes (Class A: 17 students; Class B: 16 students; ages 10 –

11) from a public school in Eastern Canada participated. Both teachers had experience with inquiry‑based

instruction and co‑designed lesson sequences with the research team.  Each intervention ran for six weeks (one 90‑minute session per week).

• Class A (Causal Mapping Intervention). Lessons centered on tracing multi‑step chains from concrete,

local actions (e.g., driving gas‑powered vehicles; cutting trees to build a shopping mall) to broader 2797 climate outcomes. Students constructed causal maps collaboratively and iteratively revised their diagrams with teacher prompts.

• Class B (Feedback‑Loop Intervention). Lessons began with the water cycle (already in the elementary

curriculum), emphasizing cyclicity and interdependence. The teacher then introduced climate feedbacks (e.g., glacier melt → reduced albedo → increased absor ption → further warming → more melt), supporting students to identify loop structure and effects over time. This study adopted a qualitative multiple‑case study design (Yin, 2014), enabling in‑depth, situated analysis of learning in two intervention contexts. Data sources included video‑recorded classroom observations (whole class and small groups), student artifacts (causal maps, loop diagrams, written explanations), and t eacher interviews (post‑intervention) and student small‑group interviews (eliciting explanations with prompts).

Using CMP‑informed coding (Hmelo‑Silver et al., 2017), this study  analyzed evidence of:  (a) Components identification; (b) Mechanisms articulated (verbs/processes linking C→C and/or C→P); (c) Phenomena explained (e.g., warming, mitigation). Targeted codes were added for chain length (number of explicit steps), presence/closure of feedback loops, references to time delays, and reciprocal causality.

## Preliminary findings

Causal mapping extended causal chains and made mechanisms more explicit At baseline, most explanations were limited to one-step claims (e.g., “cars cause pollution”). Through mapping and teacher prompts (“What happens next?” “Because…?”), students produced multi-step chains that connected local actions to global outcomes (e.g., “cutting trees → fewer trees to absorb CO₂ → more CO₂ in the atmosphere → stronger greenhouse effect → higher temperatures”). Mechanism language (e.g., “absorb,” “trap,” “reflect”) entered students’ talk and maps over time, aligning with CMP-M gains. However, even extended chains tended to remain open-ended (rarely looping back), and time delays were only occasionally mentioned. Feedback-loop instruction fostered recognition of cyclicity and loop closure Starting with the water cycle allowed students to generalize “what goes around” to climate contexts. Many students articulated or diagrammed reinforcing loops (e.g., ice –albedo feedback) and used cyclical language (“keeps making it warmer,” “goes back agai n”). Several could close loops verbally before representing them graphically, suggesting linguistic scaffolds outpaced representational precision. Students were less confident describing time lags or balancing loops (e.g., negative feedbacks), and mechanis tic depth varied (e.g., “less reflection” named but not tied to energy budget language). Across classes, difficulties remained with time delays, interacting feedbacks, and quantification (e.g., relative contributions of mechanisms). Explanations also tended to foreground immediate human concerns, consistent with prior reports of anthropocentric framing in young learners. This study shows that elementary students candevelop sophisticated aspects of systems thinking when instruction is deliberately scaffolded. Causal mapping helped students elaborate multi-step causal chains and name mechanisms, while feedback-loop instruction cultivated recognition of cyclicity and loop closure —both crucial for understanding climate dynamics (Roychoudhury et al., 2017; Sterman & Booth Sweeney, 2002). Findings add to evidence that systems-thinking development is context-dependent rather than strictly hierarchical (Liu & Hmelo-Silver, 2009; Mambrey et al., 2020).

The study contributes to the field by providing  classroom-validated, developmentally appropriate scaffolds for Grade 5 systems thinking in climate contexts, responding to calls for concrete, early-grade instructional models (Gilissen et al., 2020; Leung et al., 2024). It also offers an analytic approach (CMP + chain/loop coding) and a set of practical design principles teachers can adopt. This is a small, two-class case study in one setting; future work should examine scalability and integrate epistemic tools (e.g., dynamic visualizations) to support time delays and multiple interacting loops (Evagorou et al., 2009; Hmelo-Silver et al., 2017).
