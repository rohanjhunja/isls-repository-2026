# Artifacts to Support Teacher Learning? Rethinking and Forging New Design Principles

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Understanding and developing teacher expertise has long been a challenge, especially in the teaching of mathematics. Expertise in teaching requires developing multiple forms of knowledge, instructional practices and commitments, and dispositions and practices to learn in and from teaching. Challenges to accomplishing these different goals have led tonew visions for incorporating and leveraging artifacts of classroom instruction—including analog, technologically advanced, and conceptual models—as levers to understand and support teacher learning. Research often privileges asking if artifact-rich learning environments accomplish the desired outcomes, with less attention to how they support teacher learning. In this symposium, we examine different approaches for situating artifacts in pre-and in-service teacher learning contexts. By examining the situated use of artifacts across settings, we gain a nuanced understanding about, and offer design principles for, the productive use of artifacts to improve mathematics teacher learning.

Understanding and developing teacher expertise has long been a challenge, especially in the teaching of mathematics (e.g., Cobb & Jackson, 2021). This challenge has led to a shift from traditional approaches aiming at the implementation of fixed “desirable ” practices to approaches that acknowledge the complexity of teacher learning (Grossman et al., 2009; Horn & Garner, 2022; Kennedy, 2016). This shift also invited new approaches to study teacher learning, expanding from the innovation used to accomplish the desired learning outcomes to also considering how and under what conditions those outcomes are achieved. An important outcome of this work has been the investigation and development of design principles to guide teacher learning, that can enable innovations to be adapted in different contexts (Kang & van Es, 2019). Attention to design is particularly important in professional learning settings that incorporate artifacts—including analog and technologically advanced tools, as well as conceptual models —as levers to understand and support teacher learning (Danish et al., 2021). In this symposium we seek to advance research both on design and on teacher learning, by illuminating detailed and nuanced accounts of how artifacts are taken up and used in different contexts, resulting in advancing design principles for generative use of artifacts for mathematics teacher learning. We adopt situative perspectives to teacher learning, which recognizes that learning is situated in the social and physical contexts in which it takes place, and that these settings are also historical and cultural in nature (Greeno, 2011; Putnam & Borko, 2000). Thus, teacher learning is necessarily tied to the particular setting and contexts in which teachers learn, the tools and participation structures that organize learning, and the histories and cultural practices of the local learning context, which are all embedded in the community of practice in which it takes place (Horn & Garner, 2022; Kazemi & Hubbard, 2008; Lave & Wenger, 1991). Two tenets of this perspective are important to our theory of teacher learning. One is that material (e.g., video) and conceptual (e.g., 2251 language) artifacts, teachers’ prior experiences and histories of participation, and the rules and norms that govern participation work in concert to shape interactions to advance learning and practice (Cole & Engeström, 1993). The second is that the affordances of the tools are situated in particular contexts and are realized through their use in the learning setting (Kirschner, 2002). This perspective enables researchers to consider not only if a learning environment achieved the desired outcomes, but how  the different features worked together through their enactment to result in teacher learning.

As a set, the four papers in this session advance research on teacher learning and designs for professional development by illuminating how artifacts are situated and put to use to support teachers’ developing knowledge, dispositions, and practices for responsive and ambitious mathematics instruction (Wilson et al., 2019). Specifically, the first paper describes whether a structured lesson planning protocol, as artifact, supports teachers using their classrooms as sites for teacher learning and documents the nature of knowledge teachers learn through its use. The second paper positions frames as tools for teacher learning and investigates if and how preservice teachers develop new frames to inform their reflection on practice. Analysis of teacher educators’ use and preservice teachers’ learning of these frames leads to refining design principles for teacher education. In this way, the first two papers shed light on how the object of learning gets accomplished through interaction with the artifacts. The next two studies shift from what is learned by engaging with the tools to how features of the system support learning. The third paper explores how teachers take advantage of opportunities to learn when using a web-based, multi-dimensional professional development space (as artifact). An important finding is that teachers with different commitments and backgrounds leverage the potential affordances of the web-based learning environment in ways that have implications for their professional learning. Finally, the l ast paper adopts a practice-based lens to identify routine practices facilitators use to leverage the affordances of artifacts of classroom instruction in teacher education.

Together, these papers reveal that developing expertise in mathematics instruction requires exploiting multiple forms of knowledge and practice, and that whether and how such learning occurs is contingent on the locally designed professional development ac tivity, as well as teachers’ own experiences and histories (Kazemi & Hubbard, 2008). These findings suggest that design principles for teacher learning can be generative for implementing and adapting professional learning artifacts for mathematics teaching expertise. Paper 1: Exploration of teacher learning from teaching through lesson plans Yasemin Copur-Gencturk Sebnem Atabas Teaching as a profession involves various forms of on-the-job learning opportunities, some of which include external supports for learning, such as professional development, and others that occur without such support, such as teaching itself. Classroom instruction has long been recognized by both scholars and teachers as a powerful resource for teacher learning (e.g., Enthoven et al., 2023; Hoekstra et al., 2009), which we conceptualize in this study as the activation and development of the knowledge teachers draw on in their practice (Grossman, 1990). Yet relatively little research has systematically investigated whether and how learning through teaching occurs in the absence of external support.

Much of the existing work on this topic has focused either on specific components of teaching—such as curriculum materials or pedagogical practices introduced by researchers to examine their potential as learning opportunities (Remillard, 2000) —or on measuring teachers’ knowledge at different points in their careers as indicators of professional growth (Copur-Gencturk & Li, 2023; Hiebert et al., 2017). Although these approaches have provided valuable insights, the design of such studies limits researchers’ ability to confidently attribute teacher learning to the instruction itself. Specifically, when researchers deliberately manipulated components of teaching (e.g., Remillard, 2000), they demonstrated that teachers could develop knowledge through classroom instruction. However, it remains unclear whether similar learning would have occurred without the resources or practices introduced by the researchers. Likewise, evidence of teachers’ knowledge increasing over time suggests 2252 the possibility of learning through instruction but does not eliminate alternative explanations, such as participation in professional development or coaching (e.g., Hiebert et al., 2017). These limitations highlight the complexity of studying learning through teaching. Teachers work in highly diverse contexts, with different resources, curricula, and students, making it difficult to identify a consistent approach to studying what they learn  from their teaching experiences. Moreover, finding a shared artifact that captures teachers’ learning across varied settings poses a challenge. To address this challenge, we explored the use of structured lesson plans as a common artifact to both support and study teachers’ learning through teaching. Specifically, we adapted a lesson plan template from Hiebert et al. (2017) that included prompts for teachers to document (a) which representations they planned to use and why, (b) what student struggles they anticipated, (c) what strategies they expected students to use, and (d) what formative assessment data they planned to collect. Teachers developed 2-day lesson plans, and we conducted structured interviews (a) before they began teaching the planned lessons, (b) after implementing the first day’s lesson to capture what they had learned from that experience, and (c) after implementing the second day’s lesson to capture their learning from teaching the second lesson. The interview questions focused on teachers’ knowledge development related to the main task used to introduce the concept, the student strategies and struggles they anticipated, their planned responses to those struggles, and their approaches to monitoring students’ learning. The preteaching interviews aimed to capture teachers’ baseline knowledge before instruction, whereas the postteaching interviews focused on identifying shifts or changes in their knowledge. Collecting data before and after instruction allowed us to more confidently attribute observed changes in teachers’ knowledge to the teaching experience itself. For example, when teachers reported students’ struggles they had not anticipated, we coded this as evidence of learning through teaching. In contrast, when teachers reported nonew insights into students’ struggles, we coded this as an instance in which no learning through teaching occurred.

We also examined the conditions under which teacher learning occurs. Because tasks play a key role in promoting higher order thinking and deeper understanding, the cognitive demand of tasks influences the extent to which students generate diverse ideas, strategies, and rich interactions around content (Stein & Smith, 1998). These factors, in turn, can shape the kinds of knowledge teachers develop through teaching. Lesson structure may also influence teacher learning because teacher-led instruction provides fewer opportunities than student-centered instruction for teachers to develop knowledge, given that it allows less room for students to explore their own ideas and strategies (Sengupta-Irving & Enyedy, 2015). Another potential factor is teachers’ own under standing of the content, which affects how they interpret students’ strategies and make sense of their thinking (Copur-Gencturk, 2021). Therefore, we focused on three factors that could shape teacher learning: (a) the cognitive demand of instructional tasks, (b) the lesson structure, and (c) teachers’ understanding of the content. Our study included five teachers in Grades 3–6 (two who self-identified as male and three as female; two White, two multiracial, and one Black). Each teacher created three 2-day lesson plans and participated in nine pre-and postlesson interviews. Two researchers coded the data by using categories aligned with knowledge for teaching (e.g., Copur-Gencturk & Tolar, 2022). Interrater reliability was substantial, with a kappa coefficient of.71.

Our findings showed that teachers developed knowledge through teaching, particularly in pedagogical content knowledge, followed by general pedagogical knowledge. Importantly, we also identified the conditions under which this learning occurred. Teachers using cognitively low-demand tasks tended to develop general pedagogical or contextual knowledge, whereas those using cognitively demanding tasks gained more knowledge related to students’ mathematical thinking and instructional responses to student needs. Similarly, teachers with a strong understanding of the content developed deeper insights into students’ mathematical thinking and effective instructional strategies. Taken together, our findings provide evidence that classroom instruction can serve as a powerful source of professional growth. The cognitive demand of instructional tasks, teachers’ depth of content understanding, and the degree to which lessons are student centered shape both the type and amount of learning that occurs. These results suggest that lesson plans and structured interviews can effectively capture teachers’ learning through teaching and that creating opportunities for teachers to use cognitively demanding tasks, strengthen their content knowledge, and center instruction around students’ thinking can foster continuous onthe-job professional learning. Paper 2: Examining frames as an artifact in preservice teachers’ mathematics rehearsals Marta Kobiela, Zhikun Zhang, Enista Sharonne Chiniah, Xinyue Chen University teacher education programs have long experienced the challenge of supporting preservice teachers (PTs) to bridge theoretical knowledge from their university coursework to their classroom teaching practices 2253 (Grossman et al., 2009). One attempt to bridge this divide has been through rehearsals. A rehearsal is a simulation in which a PT practices teaching a lesson to their peers under the supervision of a teacher educator (TE); the rehearsing PT or the TE can interject (pause the teaching) to elicit or provide feedback on a problem of practice (Lampert et al.  2013). Prior research has often focused on opportunities for learning based on the content of rehearsal feedback (e.g., Ghousseini, 2017) or on PTs’ general perspectives of their learning (e.g., Rawlins et al., 2020). Yet, we know little about how PTs int erpret TEs’ rehearsal feedback and how their interpretations differ from what TEs intend to communicate.

In this paper, we examine the role of interpretive frames (Goffman, 1974) as an artifact in preservice teachers’ (PTs’) learning from rehearsals. Drawing upon Werstch (1998), we conceptualize a n artifact to be a non-tangible cultural tool associated with power and authority that mediates thinking and learning. Frames are the interpretive lenses that one uses to make sense of an event (Goffman, 1974), thus mediating how events are seen, interpreted, and influenc e learning opportunities (e.g., Horn & Little, 2010). Research suggests the importance of teachers establishing shared frames during professional development to support their collective interpretations of problems of teaching practice (Horn & Little, 2010). Such shared frames can include “concepts, principles, and terminology” (Horn & Little, 2010, p. 209)  as well as culturally ingrained beliefs about students or the nature of knowledge (Alvidrez et al., 2022; Russ & Luna, 2013). Based on this, we considered a frame to be an important  conceptual artifact in PTs’ learning during rehearsals. Thus, we  asked: 1) What frames do PTs and TEs use to interpret rehearsal feedback? 2) How do those frames shape how PTs and TEs describe feedback? We conducted a qualitative case study that was part of a larger design-based research study within the context of an elementary mathematics methods course in Eastern Canada. The design-based research study aimed to study and support both PTs’ learning duri ng rehearsals and TEs’ learning to facilitate rehearsals. One of our main design principles (Bakker, 2019) prior to the study was that PTs should have opportunities to develop shared frames to support alignment in their collective interpretations of problems of practice. To enact this principle, PTs were introduced to shared frames of core principles (e.g., children are sense makers) and practices (e.g., asking pressing questions to learn more about students’ thinking) (Lampert et al., 2013). We had a similar design principle for TEs learning: Because collective interpretations of problems of practice benefit from shared frames, TEs should learn to focus their feedback on shared frames. In addition, to prepare for the rehearsals, PTs were provided with a lesson plan that directed them to use certain practices (e.g., represent the student’s strategy). To support TEs, our research team facilitated 3 meetings in which they watched videos of rehearsals from prior years and reflected on how they would provide feedback, focusing on different problems of practice, including those related to core practices.

For this paper, we focus on the cases of 2 TEs (TE-A and TE-B) and 2 PTs from each of their sections (PT-A1, PT-A2, PT-B1, PT-B2). Data included 60-minute video-elicited interviews with each participant. Participants from the same section viewed the same video excerpts of rehearsal interjections and explained what the TE wanted PTs to understand and why. We then conducted a frame analysis (Goffman, 1974), drawing upon existing dimensions of teachers’ frames: a) epistemic —nature of disciplinary knowledge or practice (e.g., Russ & Luna, 2013) and b) positional—perceived roles of individuals (e.g., Alvidrez et al., 2022). Our analysis revealed three findings. First, participants most frequently drew upon frames of principles and practices of teaching and p erspectives of learning  and least frequently upon epistemic frames (Table 1). Mathematics frames were used more often by TE-B, PT-B1, and PT-B2, perhaps because the rehearsal they viewed involved more complex mathematics. Second, although PTs and TEs often used similar categories of frames, the nature of those frames often varied, even when interpreting the same feedback. For example, TE-A drew upon an epistemic frame that mathematics involves reasoning and thinking to justify why she had encouraged the rehearsing PT to press on the student’s thinking to help them link their answer to the mathematics concepts: “We want students, kids, [to] develop different reasoning, different thinking, about the mathematical concepts, to foster their reasoning and understand the math.” In contrast, when PT-A1 watched the same feedback, they instead drew upon an epistemic frame that mathematics is answer-oriented. PT-A1 saw the feedback as conveying that teachers must draw students’ attention to the answer: “We [teachers] have to analyze what is the most important  information, which in this case is to say the answer.” Of note, although all PTs drew upon frames related to teaching principles and practices, they differed in the extent to which they drew upon the course core principles and practices as a frame. Sometimes PTs used principles and practices that resembled the core practices, but using different language, what we called informal course principles and practices. Other times they used completely different principles and practices, what we called other principles and practices. In contrast, TEs more consistently drew upon core principles and practices, what we called formal course principles and practices. Finally, as illustrated by the prior example, differences in frames were related to differences in how PTs and TEs described the TE’s feedback and explained why it was important. Tiffany Reyes-Denis, Nigel Bosch, Sarah Burns, Hana Kearfott, Cheryl Moran, Meg Bates, Joseph Cimpian, Michelle Perry Improving mathematics teaching and learning is a national concern (U.S. Department of Education, 2022), especially when it comes to foundational mathematics (Ma, 2020). However, we cannot improve teaching without first attending to teachers’ learning and professional development (PD). Lave and Wenger’s (1991) classic work on situated learning—and Webster-Wright’s (2009) and Roth et al.’s (2017) more recent interpretations of situated learning, in the context of situated teacher learning—provides a useful framework for understanding learning in communities of practice and, in particular, how individuals develop professional knowledge. This process reflects that learning is situated, takes place in the negotiation of participation, and highlights the overall relational interdependence of agent, world, activity, and professional learning. Given the distributed nature of online communication and communities, asynchronous artifacts are increasingly becoming a central component of formal and informal learning, where legitimate peripheral participation takes place. Especially since the massive shift to online learning caused by the pandemic —including teacher professional learning —making online learning here to stay (e.g., Aguilar et al., 2021), it is important to understand how teachers can lear n from these online professional learning communities to support their professional growth and to uncover effective principles to apply to the design of online PD settings. Online learning opportunities give teachers a great deal of control over what, when, and where they learn. This agency is related to educator success (Shaha & Ellsworth, 2013) and student performance (Magidin et al., 2012). However, at the same time, flexi bility means that teachers might use online platforms in ways that differ from the originally anticipated (and designed-for) use cases. A better understanding of which teachers interact with online learning platforms, and in what ways, would help the effective design of those platforms. One such platform is the University of Chicago’s Virtual Learning Community  (VLC), with over 68,000 registered members, designed to support teacher learning and practice in elementary mathematics classrooms. The VLC offers a variety of free online tools and resources, including mathematics lesson videos, discussion boards, and PD modules for teachers. These online tools and resources on the VLC are the artifacts available for supporting teacher learning, and we explore which of these artifacts teachers chose to learn from. Given the unique advantages of online PD, the present study examines how elementary mathematics teachers use the VLC to improve their teaching. Specifically, this study examined whether teacher behaviors on the VLC clustered in ways that suggest distinct use cases, whether those behaviors were associated with particular teacher characteristics, and what that implies about design principles that could benefit future iterations of online PD platforms. We analyzed the behaviors and characteristics of at least 1,377 VLC users (depending on the analysis) using a combination of supervised and unsupervised hierarchical clustering. Specifically, we considered behaviors—consisting of various interactions teachers had with artifacts on the VLC —and characteristics — consisting of information that teachers self-reported when they registered as a member on the VLC or when they updated their profile. These characteristics included teaching experience, perspectives on math teaching, and other individual differences.

Teachers were considered more similar to each other if they exhibited similar behaviors, both in terms of what they did and did not do on the VLC. In particular, we measured 57 variables that measured, for example, whether a user ever navigated via the homepage, whether they did so regularly, whether they accessed specific types of resources available on the VLC, and so on. Given thes e variables, we then fit hierarchical clusters via 2256 scikit-learn (Pedregosa et al., 2011), which reveals groups of users who are most similar to each other but does not clarify why they are similar. Thus, we subsequently used the clusters themselves as the labels or outputs of a supervised decision tree-based model predicting which cluster each user belonged to, so that we could analyze the tree to determine which behavioral characteristics best defined clusters. Specifically, we used a χ² test for each decision made by the supervised model to discover which  behaviors significantly affected the distribution of cluster assignments. Significant decision nodes (p <.05 with Bonferroni corrections) were kept and transformed into human-readable rules, which were then interpreted by four independent human coders and discussed as a group for consensus. behaviors and characteristics than those who used the VLC for other use cases, such as to find instructional tools and other resources to use in their classes or in their lesson planning. Specifically, teachers who primarily used PD are passive users (e.g., visit the site less often and access fewer pages), do not find teaching math enjoyable, and are less experienced than those who did not use the PD. In contrast, teachers with other use cases are generally more active users, have more teaching experience, and tend to engage in other parts of the platform, especially resources. The flexibility of the VLC therefore results in novice-specific use cases, which the data also show do not lead to future discovery of other affordances the VLC offers for teacher learning. We can use these findings to make sense of how teachers navigate professional learning websites and structure their learning, and to enhance virtual learning platforms. For instance, teachers that use the PD modules could benefit from more agency within the modules, which has been associated with increased enjoyment of the learning experience (Luo et al., 2019) and enhanced professional learning (Lieberman & Pointer Mace, 2008). Furthermore, given that PDs are often perceived as idiosyncratic and irrelevant  (Lieberman & Pointer Mace, 2008), this agency may lead non-PD users to capitalize on the module sections most relevant to them. Additionally, adding navigation and search guidance might help the novice passive users take advantage of the online resources.

In conclusion, online PD websites can be used in different ways. Understanding how they are used, and by whom, can help teacher educators and website developers to build in supports to help teachers navigate these spaces most advantageously. Future research should provide additional insights into exactly what those supports look like to maximize teacher learning from online PD websites. Specifically, we propose that online PD platforms could benefit from incorporating a broadening use  design principle, wherein teachers are gradually explicitly encouraged to explore more capabilities of online PD platforms so that they become more confident and well-equipped to leverage the flexibility of such platforms. Our current and future work in t his area implements this principle through interventions on the VLC designed to target the specific use cases teachers are coming from and help them to engage in other, possibly more-productive ways, on the VLC. Paper 4: Iterating as a core practice for developing preservice teachers’ inner witness for equity Ethan Rubin Elizabeth van Es Teacher noticing, which encompasses how teachers attend to, interpret, and interact with the learning environment to support student learning (van Es & Sherin, 2021), is often shaped by narratives and ideologies that marginalize culturally, linguistically, and racially diverse learners (Louie et al., 2021). To make these narratives visible and disrupt their influence on instruction, research calls for teachers developing insight into themselves as noticers, what research refers to as teachers’ “inner witness” for responsive and equitable instruction (Williams et al., 2020). The course integrated approximations of teaching that featured classroom artifacts (e.g., videos, lesson materials, student work samples) and facilitated collaborative analysis to expand candidates’ noticing and disrupt dominant narratives related to mathematics teaching and learning, such as narratives about what it means to be smart, who is allowed to demonstrate smartness in the classroom, and how instructional practices position some students as more capable than others. Building on previous research (Rubin & van Es, 2023), we investigate how specific design choices, as they are embodied and enacted in activity, promoted preservice teachers’ learning to notice for ambitious and equitable instruction. In doing so, we aim to contribute to emerging research documenting teacher educators’ practices for using artifacts to support teacher candidates’ learning to advance responsive and equitable instruction (Santagata et al., 2021).

We conceptualize teacher education as a complex activity system that spans multiple contexts and involves a variety of material and conceptual tools (Kang & van Es, 2019). Among these tools, approximations of teaching, such as video and student work, enable candidates to investigate instructional practice by spanning across contexts, affording access to key elements of classroom activity within the relatively bounded space of university-based coursework. Approximations of teaching afford preservice teachers the opportunities to examine 2257 the details of instructional events and teacher decision making, thereby supporting new insights into teaching practice and developing alternative visions of teaching and learning (Grossman et al., 2009). Though approximations of teaching show promise, whether and how their potential is realized is contingent on how they are situated and used within complex learning ecologies. Examining patterns of their use can lead to identifying design principles and instructional practices that can ensure their productive use across contexts. We draw on design-based research and situated learning theory to understand how approximations are enacted in preservice teacher education to support preservice teacher learning (Danish et al., 2021). These perspectives recognize that the social organization of the learning setting will shape the utility of approximations of instruction for learning, including how they are situated and carried out in routine activity. Our study aims to unveil how teacher educators facilitated collaborative analysis of appr oximations that positioned candidates to interrogate and expand their visions of teaching and examine how their vision could be enacted in the classroom (Rubin & van Es, 2023; Louie et al., 2021). Specifically, the approximations of teaching that teacher educators used included videos of classroom teaching, samples of student work and associated lesson plans, transcripts of classroom interactions, and teacher candidates’ documentations of critical moments from classroom observations. Data include three teacher educators’ course planning notes, course materials such as videos, handouts, and slide decks, and artifacts of candidates’ work. Through an iterative process of qualitative analysis of course planning artifacts (Bingham, 2023), we first enumerated the broad learning goals and the material embodiments in the course design. Next, we articulated conjectures that motivated the inclusion of each embodiment based on the forms of activity they could provoke for candidates, which illustrated how embodiments were expected to support particular learning goals (Sandoval, 2014). This process of retrospective conjecture mapping identified participation structures centered on approximations that unified the course design. We used the conjecture map as a framework to analyze candidates’ responses in order to gain insight into the nature and evolution of their noticing, which were documented in course artifacts such as class notes and assignments. By comparing our conjectures with candidates’ reflections and insights, we highlighted facilitation practices that supported generative engagement with approximations.

Our analysis identifies iterating as a core practice for using approximations to support candidates’ learning to notice. This practice served to illustrate teacher noticing as situated in classroom interactions, provoke candidates to consider multiple interpretations for classroom events, and expand candidates’ conceptualizations of learning. Iterating was enacted in three main forms throughout the course activities. First, the course involved iterative re-viewing of the same approximation, which positioned candidates to notice more detail and nuance in seemingly mundane and brief interactions. This practice supported candidates in appreciating the complexity and multidimensionality of teaching and learning, emphasizing the importance of cultivating one’s noticing. Reviewing a given approximation in subsequent course sessions also helped candidates build an awareness of how their noticing was changing and developing over time. Second, the course provided different conceptual tools across iterative viewings, which facilitated collaborative analysis and expanded candidates’ visions of teaching. By applying a new conceptual lens or framework each time they reviewed an approximation, candidates became aware that their initial interpretations were not complete or neutral, but rather reflected one of many possible visions of teaching. Moreover, these collaborative analysis activities illustrated how theories are embodied in practice and that it is possible to change and expand how one attends to and interprets events. Finally, the course design encouraged participants to generate alternative interpretations by prompting them to re-narrate events from multiple perspectives over the course of iterative viewings. In group activities that invited participants to share diverse interpretations and insights with one another, re-narrating events played an important role in candidates’ expanding their inner witness by capturing multiple points of view. This study reveals how the practice of iterating  can enable preservice teachers to learn to notice from a responsive and equitable teaching perspective and engage in ongoing learning from teaching. These findings suggest that iterating can serve as a design principle for designing teacher education that  leverages artifacts of teaching to engage preservice teachers in approximations of practice. Moreover, future research on the use of approximations of teaching can examine if and how iterating is taken up in the enactment of this pedagogy of teacher education and how it interacts with preservice teachers’ learning to notice. Such questions might examine how iterating hones and expands teachers’ noticing to particular features of classroom interactions to deve lop a vision of equitable and responsive teaching, while also supporting teachers’ adopting alternative perspectives and interpretations to recognize the complexity in accomplishing this vision of instruction in practice. Future studies of this sort will contribute to calls for research to specify how artifacts of mathematics teaching and learning candevelop teachers’ classroom noticing (Santagata et al., 2021), while also supporting research moving from single case findings to understand how designs support learning through design principles and practices (Sandoval, 2014).
