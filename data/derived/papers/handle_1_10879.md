# Including Diverse Image Schemas in Knowledge Building 
for STEM Learners Who are Visually Impaired

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
To foster epistemic agency for visually impaired (VI) learners in STEM education for Knowledge Building (KB), educational support that suits diverse image schemas may be necessary. By better understanding diverse image schemas of VI people, which are often overlooked in educational environments where the needs of sighted learners are prioritized, this study identifies challenges to overcome for KB to function equitably as a social infrastructure.

## Introduction

Knowledge Building (KB) recognizes scientific knowl edge as a process of improving ideas rather than standardizing them around discovered truths, and th erefore emphasizes how students express their epist emic agency as dynamic knowledge creators (David Cohen, 1988 as cited in Caswell & Bielaczyc, 2002). Additionally, rotating leadership roles can encourage democratic and symmetrical knowledge advancement that includes diverse members of communities (Scardamalia, 2002). However, KB for visually impaired (VI) learners has rarely been discussed. For VI learners, the focus has traditionally been on individualized learning rooted in behaviorism and cognitivism and translating learning content into braille, speech, and tactile images (Dell et al., 2015), which risks assuming image schemas, “relatively simple structur es that constantly recur in our everyday bodily experience,” of sighted learners (Lakoff 1987, p. 2 67 as cited in Geeraerts et al., 2005). For VI lear ners, unfortunately, many school systems prioritize needs of sighted learners in an environment where knowle dge and skills are expected to be acquired through individu al effort rather than through social learning, whic h may intensify the learning difficulties of VI students with normal cognitive abilities (Mc Dermott, 2001, Han, 2020). This study provides insight into how image schemas of VI individuals seem to differ from those seeming ly assumed by creators of tactile images translated fr om graphics used in STEM education for sighted indi viduals. We connect our findings to McDermott’s (2001) research on the social context for learning to better to include VI learners who have been overlooked in KB by recommen ding: (1) improving the accessibility of collaboration-based software such as Knowledge Forum for VI learn ers, (2) exploring multimodal methods that deviate from the assumptions of visual perception, and (3) transforming VI students' role as passive containers emphasized by static theory into active and dynamic participants in learning through collaboration.

## Methodology and framework

Five VI participants were recruited for case studies to examine VI learners' perceptions of tactile images translated from graphic materials frequently used in STEM educ ation. After providing informed consent to our Rese arch Ethics Board approved procedure, we employed a qualitative  and inductive research approach to provide additional insights regarding participants' experie nces that may otherwise be elusive in controlled ex perimental studies. The context of the pandemic limited our research to individual intervention studies with adult participants, including college students. Over six-months, we tested tactile graphics (some with brail le labels), reliefs, 3D models, text-to-speech and hybrids of these (using a pen-based audio label reader instead of braille) to determine how effectively each conveys spatial-topological properties (Coppin et al., 2016) of STEM content. Our findings informed how we improved and retested prototypes. O ur study provided insight into how image schemas of  a visually dominant culture (such as foreshortening, outline drawings, shadows and even color) can make their way into tactile graphics, conflicting with schemas of VI perceivers. Furthermore, we situate this study w ithin the context of finding ‘points of contact’ to support l earners in multiple environments to extend VI stude nts’ individual learning into KB-based collaboration (Lave, 1998a, b, as cited in McDermott, 2001).

## Analysis and findings

3D models significantly improved comprehension for learners with visual impairments compared to raised  line graphics; auditory label hybrids made both more comprehensible. Overall, difficulty in navigation, confusion due to complexity, and a need for additional speech explanations were common. However, perspective, texture, color, and braille understanding showed significant differ ences depending on the individual's visual memory, braille learning, and device use experience. ## Implications

Multimodal accessibility that considers diverse sch emas can provide a richer KB environment by includi ng VI learners whose new perspectives are encountered by their sighted collaborators. This study can contrib ute meaningful clues for future studies on how VI learners expand their conceptualization and learning.
