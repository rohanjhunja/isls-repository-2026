# Investigating Linguistic Landscapes to Empower Multicultural Community Building in the University and Beyond

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Students often interpret their communities as the school context, however, exploring the wider range of their communities, especially in the context of seeking culturally diverse and multicultural environments is crucial. Here we investigate the Linguistic Landscape (LL) of Buffalo NY, which is home to many universities. The goal is to foster critical thinking and multicultural competence, to empower linguistically diverse students to recognize their language and cultural heritage, and to promote equity for all students.

## Introduction

On university campuses, international students can feel isolated and domestic students, both from in-and out-ofstate can remain in siloed communities. This may be exacerbated by common perceptions of community that end with the university campus rather than extending to  the wider community where a school is located (Gay ton & Fisher 2022). However, intentionally connecti ng to the multicultural assets of a local environme nt can help alleviate these issues for students from all backgrounds. Here, we discuss the concept of linguistic landscapes and how investigating a local linguistic landscape could serve as a step toward multicultural community building and wider, more productive engagement between universities and the communities around them. The concept and role of linguistic landscape (LL) The linguistic landscape (LL) represents the cultur al, economic, sociological, and psychological domai ns of individuals who live in certain locations and areas. Studying the LL of an area can highlight important information about the people who live in that area, such as which language ideologies are at play, which languages have power and prestige, and which are marginalized (Cenoz & G orter, 2008). A changing LL opens up possibilities for broader accepted and legitimized racial and linguis tic identities and related language varieties, which allows greater critical attention to be paid to ownership of English and to representations of the language’s  various speakers within the broader social imaginary (Motha, 2014). Outlining the LL of a given place provides a starting place for students to consider the language and dia lects present in their own community, their relatio nship to the landscape, and the connections they have with the place where they live. It can also help students to recognize the range of communicative resources students all possess to engage and support multicultural communication. Here, we focus specifically on the LL of Buffalo, NY.

Buffalo, New York’s linguistic landscape (LL) Buffalo, located in the northeastern quadrant of th e United States, is a racial-ethnically and linguistically diverse place, which has neighborhoods with significant populations of speakers of languages other than English (Dixson & George, 2020). According to the U.S. Census burea u 2020, 32.6% of people who speak a language other than English speak Spanish. After Spanish, there is a st riking range of languages spoken including Arabic (9.5%), Chinese (6.2%), Bengali (3.9%), French (3.9%), Poli sh (3.7%). One way of understanding an area’s linguistic landscape is by studying the street and business si gns visible from the sidewalk. This provides insigh t into the informative and symbolic functions of signs, indica ting the linguistic ideologies at play and the imp acts these ideologies may have on speakers of languages other than English as well as reflecting society’s percep tions of English and non-English languages (Cenoz & Gorter, 2008). For examp le, Buffalo’s Hispanic Heritage District is a representative area o f Spanish speaking populations, where street and bu siness signs showcase familial ties to the original places people and their languages come from, revealing that a language other than English is used within the community to maintain heritage and seek to promote a primary language within the English-dominant community (Dixson & George, 2020). However, some si gns containing Spanish languages displayed what appeared to be incorrect grammar, which could be in terpreted as the sign-makers not fully engaging with  the linguistic features of Spanish (Dixson & George, 20 20); alternatively, it could also indicate distinct  dialects or literacies of the community. A LL can continuously change and grow: displays can change, dynamic power  and attitude towards a culture and language can also change accordingly. Thus, explo ring a local LL can support building a community with critical multicultural awareness. Empowering multicultural community building By investigating a given area’s LL, this paper argu es that universities and educational institutions c an empower and facilitate multicultural community building, and promote critical engagement of learners around th e impact of their words and the range of their knowledge (Pa ris & Alim, 2014). Multicultural community from an educational perspective, contains five dimensions (Motha, 2014), including highlighting perspectives f rom marginalized and minorities groups in course content, supporting students' critical analysis and uplifting different backgrounds and ways of knowing, naming forms of op pression and ways to address them, critically implementing practices toward educational equity, a nd committing to transforming institutional culture s. Therefore, critical attention is needed to genuinel y engage with these dimensions to help students and  faculty in schools to commit to equity and transforming instit utional cultures (Motha, 2014). Critically analyzin g multicultural communities means to analyze the ways  in which power circulates in relation to culture -- the resource distribution, the relationships of economi cs and power (Motha, 2014). Based on the demographi cs and the general LL of Buffalo discussed above, we can attend to the relationships of economics and power, where the government embraces multicultural dynamics and cele brates the diversity of Buffalo, where the communit y respects and builds on their primary culture and language, and at the same time, absorbs and adds new and existing linguistic features  unique to the area. We can, however, also notice th e dominance of English, where English language always features above and bigger while oth er languages are below, or signs which have only En glish language in certain locations. By explicitly noticing these power dynamics and linguistic features, students claim agency of their own critical language awareness (Me tz, 2018) and gain a more comprehensive understandi ng of the place they live in, including cultural, ethnic, economic features and language usage.

## Implications for universities and educational programs

Attending to linguistic landscapes provides not onl y an opportunity for both domestic and internationa l students to explicitly notice and contextualize their social life with language components in their surroundings, but also to seize learning opportunities around them in authentic learning spaces with the community to support their learning process (Dieterle & Dede, 2007; Lave & Wenger, 1991; Sharples et al., 2007). The implementation of LL practices in the class can include textual, graphic or even 3D formats such as individual journals of students observing local LL, electronic posters or pictures that students co llect from school-organized trips or personal experiences, or a co-constructed bump map with students’ artifacts. At t he institutional level, this attention also support s faculty and administrators to better address the needs of a ll students, especially those who are linguisticall y diverse and ethnically minoritized. We suggest that it is necessary to notice the local LL around a certain location oneself, for both students and faculty to critically engage with multicultural awareness, and to build multicultural communities with a practice that can be incorporated into daily  routines and beyond the school community. In futur e studies, we will draw a more comprehensive picture of LL that can provide  further insight about the language use and community environments present in Buffalo with an e ye toward principles for designing more responsible, authentic, and productive connections between campus and the surrounding city.
