# Disciplinary Conversations as Material-dialogic Pedagogy

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
To centralize disciplinary relationships, we use a critical posthumanist approach towards material agency to conceptualize a conversation as an iterative cycle of attention and responsive action. By characterizing disciplinary conversations as those conversations that are shaped by the discipline and contribute to shaping the discipline, we focus on how conversations both shape relationships and are shaped by relationships.

## Introduction: Turning from disciplinary practices to relationships

Over the last decade,  disciplinary standards have begun to  reflect a turn towards framing disciplinary growth in STEM as the repetition and stabilization of situate d cultural practices (NGSS Lead States, 2013). However, a developing issue involves an even further turn: towards disciplinary relationships as a potential focus of analysis (Krishnamoorthy et al., 2021).

To turn in this direction, we use a critical posthumanist (Murris, 2020) approach towards material agency to conceptualize an agent in conversation when they are engaging in  iterative cycles of attention and responsive action. If listening is any type of direction of at tention, and speech is any type of action; we consider an agent to be in a conversation (with materials, theory, other s, and self) exactly when they are iteratively spea king and listening. This posthuman conception of discourse gives us a way to notice the impacts and changes of disciplinary relationships by examining material-discursive moments of speech and listening. Conversations are emergently shaped by existing relationships; conversations also iteratively shape future forms of relationships (Rogers, 2011). We characterize disciplinary conversations as those conversations that are shaped by the discipline and contribute to shaping the discipline. This perspective binds practices of observation, measurement, creative insight, care for others, and empathy within the concept of disciplinary listening.

Using a postqualitative approach of narrative inqui ry (Garroutte & Westcott, 2013; Rosiek & Snyder, 2020), we explore how a relational turn changes the space of narratives for disciplinary behavior. By considering disciplinary listening, we surface ways that iterative feedback and attention to one’s embodiment can inhere in disciplinary practice. Considering disciplinary con versations, we find disciplinary continuity between students’ material interactions, their shifting interpersonal  relationships, their disciplinary identity, and th eir embodied sensations and affect. We found that reconceptualizing disciplinary practices as posthuman conversations helped us to see and boost relational themes of STEM that are  typically constructed as peripheral to disciplinar y knowledge production. In particular, practices of listening closely to understand the impacts of one’s actions can be framed as a basis for disciplinary inquiry.

By developing this paper for the ISLS annual meeting, we hope to connect with other scholars working to bring learning sciences research into better alignment with relational ontologies. We see something in common between the careful listening done by a restorative  justice practitioner (paying close attention to who has been harmed and what they are asking for), the careful listening done by a teacher (paying close attention to the thoughts and resources of their student), and the careful li stening done by a scientist (paying close attention  to the ways materials and theory push back on their understandi ng). We frame disciplinary listening as a practice of paying attention, noticing, and trying to understand an agent more deeply—similar to Dominguez’s concept of reciprocal noticing (Dominguez, 2019, 2021). And we explore a framework of disciplinary conversations as a form  of material-dialogic pedagogy (Hetherington et al., 2018; Hetherington & Wegerif, 2018). Contemporary research: Reciprocal noticing and material-dialogic pedagogy Higinio Dominguez historicizes teacher noticing (Dindyal et al., 2021) as a type of relational care, and develops the notion of reciprocal noticing (Dominguez, 2019, 2021). Dominguez uses relational ideas from Indigenous Knowledges and agential realism to theorize reciprocal noticing as “a relational practice through which teachers and students exchange roles as knowers by reciproca ting each other's noticing as they study mathematic s concepts” (2019, p. 75). In these two empirical articles, Dominguez explores reciprocity among learning partners as they notice, sense, and engage in sense-making. He positions this perspective of reciprocal noticing as located “not in the minds of individual teachers or students, but in the space and time that these vibrant bodi es cocreate with other vibrant nonhuman bodies” (2021, p. 53).

The relational mobilization  of knowledge is resonant with  Bakhtiniandialogic pedagogy. From a Bakhtinian perspective, speech “is always cast in t he form of an utterance belonging to a particular s peaking subject, and outside this form it cannot exist” (Ba khtin, 1986, p. 71). With this orientation, verbal and nonverbal communication are types of utterances between autho r and audience that exist in a larger, living, dyna mic conversation with previous and subsequent utterance s. Futhermore, “knowledge is a phenomenon that emerges through dialogic interactions” (Hetherington & Wegerif, 2018, p. 28). Hetherington, Hardman, Noakes, and Wegerif argue that the established theoretical grounding  for “dialogic pedagogy” is compatible with the relational ontology and posthuman material agency found in agential realism and resonant with Indigenous Knowledges (2018). Based on their study of a professional development program for science teachers, Hetherington and Wegerif (2018) found that teachers can find dialogic pedagogy difficult in general, and need support from pedagogical framing and gui dance. They also found that teachers did not spontaneously consider materials being part of the dialogue (regardless of whether they were think ing about dialogic pedagogy). Drawing from Barad’s agential realist formulation of relati onal ontology and material-discursive practices (Barad, 2007), these authors extend dialogic pedagogy to explicitly include material agency using the term material-dialogic pedagogy (Hetherington et al., 2018; Hetherington & Wegerif, 2018). They argue that a material-dialogic approach could support a justice-oriented dialogic approach, emphasize the role of materials, and lead science teachers to “consider h ow their choices of materials and communication too ls intraact in dialogue with themselves and their pupils in generating emergent learning” (p. 40). Understanding how to engage in material-dialogic pedagogy  and how “materials  might enter into dialogic learning is not obvious and requires a new theory if it is to make sense” (Hetherington & Wegerif, 2018, p. 29). In this paper, we explore material-dialogic pedagogy by considering Dominguez’s notion  of reciprocal noticing as a form “listening” that is aligned with the relational ontology of agential realism.

## Study context

The disciplinary setting for this work is an underg raduate computational physics course taught at a sm all liberal arts university in the Northeastern  region of North America. One of the five projects o f the course was to model the behavior of a physical construction made from e veryday craft materials —string, magnets, wooden hoops, tape, dowels, and cardstock. With the materials on display at a communal table, the students were invited to “make an oscillator.” After constructing and refining an assemblage of materials, the students collected vid eo data and used tracking software to create a data set of posi tion coordinates. The students then designed and co ded a computational model of the system.

Modes of inquiry Rosiek and Snyder (2020) use the term narrative inquiry to refer to approaches that acknowledge the agency of methodology, results, analysis, ontological innovat ions, etc.  N arrative inquiry searches for “modes of representation that include performative elements t hat draw attention to how the narrating process coc onstitutes the writer, reader, and focus of study” (J. L. Rosiek & Snyder, 2020, p. 1157). We orient this research as narrative inquiry, and we are explicit about this postqualita tive (Murris, 2020) approach to keep the intra-action (Barad,

2003) of the framework with our meaning-making at the forefront of my methods.

As a part of a larger collaborative design-based research project (Cobb et al., 2003), our data was initially collected with the methodology of Interaction Analysis (Jordan & Hende rson, 1995) in mind. Our research team videorecorded classroom activities throughout the oscillator project, wrote field notes, and interacted with students with in situ interviewing and rapport-building conversations. Our analysis involved repea ted and collaborative review; and the creation o f content logs, transcript, and analytical memos. T hroughout our data collection and review, our strengthening perspective of the centra lity of discourse in shaping disciplinary relations hips also heightened our attention towards scholarship in critical discourse analysis (Rogers, 2011). Our research team was surprised by how many relational moments surfaced during the interview of two group mates: Emerson and Ashley. We chose to use their interview as a site for deeper analysis. Consistent with both critical discourse analysis and Interaction Analysis, we engaged with our data iteratively and collaboratively to reflect on moments in the transcript that repres ented different kinds of speech and listening. The first author generated memos in between lines of dialogue. The moments were reviewed, sorted thematically, and repeatedly refined collaboratively. The first author  then wrote broader analytical memos that summarized  Emerson’s conversations and shifts in disciplinary relations.  The memos were then iteratively refined by our rese arch team into accounts of Emerson’s disciplinary conversations. We chose to theorize three scales of relational engagement for these accounts: extra-personal conversations and relationships with mater ials and theory, inter-personal conversations and relationships with other peers and instructors, and intra-personal conversations and relationships with embodiment and identity.

Operationalizing disciplinary conversations In this short paper, we share only a short summary of the memos that we have written. Our narrative inquiry is an exploration of how a change in the framing of STEM might also change the space of stories that are pos sible to tell about disciplinary development and pedagogy. W e are investigating: What forms of listening might be understood to inhere in  STEM? What disciplinary conversations can we notice? Does it make sense to consider ‘developing a stronger disciplinary relationship ’ to be the instructional goal of a course? If that w ere to become our goal, how might we think about what it means to strengthen a disciplinary relationship? Throughout the project, Emerson’s charisma, facility with coding, and experience with Physics led other students to look towards Emerson for leadership. They played a significant role in the trajectory of  the group’s work, but we also noticed that they deemphasized their authority at times and distributed power across the group. Emerson worked hard, and their projects grew more sophistic ated throughout the course. In the view of the instructor and research team, their experience and growth in our designed learning environment was as positive as we could hope. We begin with the assumption that Emerson’s relationship to computational physic s grew stronger through the course. We understand Emerson’s discipl inary relationship to be iteratively linked with Emerson’s disciplinary conversations. As an attempt to visualize Emerson’s disciplinary re lationships, our narrative inquiry explores  the many disciplinary conversations that we can infer that Emerson engaged in throughout the project.

Summary: Noticing the disciplinary conversations within disciplinary practice With respect to extrapersonal conversations with materials and theory, Emerson listened and responded to many agencies to better understand the interplay of the physical phenomenon, the equations of motion of a d oublependulum system, and an implementation of the Runge-Kutta algorithm. Emerson’s work involved conversations with material specificities of their oscillator, the tracking software, the “janky” realities of their computer, as well as theoretical specificities of the equations of motion, computational algorithm, and programming language. When asked if this course had changed the way Emerson thinks about themselves as a student, Emerson at first suggested that they “know it did, but I just don’t really thi nk that I can put it into words.” With an in vitation to articulate more, they shared that the oscillator project “kind  of sparked this thing in me —where all I wanted to do was build.” They continued, “I started viewing myself a s learning as ‘a mechanical engineering kind of ind ividual.’ And I started hanging out in mechanical engineering spaces a lot.” As Emerson described them, they experienced these extrapersonal conversations as fun and reward ing—contributing to their disciplinary work as well as  their account of their identity shift.

Regarding interpersonal conversations, Emerson expl icitly stated that attunement to other members’ wellbeing is a necessary condition for disciplinary work. Emerson and Ashley also described trading tasks, sharing intermediate progress, and finding complimentary ni ches. Emerson noted that robust team support and close complementary relationships (where everyone’s ideas are appreciated) were some of the “most important things” for disciplinary work. Emerson describes choosing t o “shut down everything” to rep air the relationships so that they continue to facilitate “crazy tons of work.”  Emerson also reported that their relationship to the concept of a professor changed as a result of the interactions t hey observed and experienced with the instructor. Emerson noticed that the instructor’s performance of care f or their disciplinary learning involved extend ing agency for students to “go ahead”  as they chose  and to then return for help when needed.  Emerson attributes learning “a whole new branch of ways to solve problems” to the extended agency enabled by the instructor. The agency enabled by the instructor allowed their group to iteratively find and follow their developi ng disciplinary sense of what types of inquiry were interesting to them. And Emerson described that their disciplinary growth was the result of following what they found to be interesting. We interpret this as a type of intrapersonal conversation. Paying attention to one’s embodied co nfiguration of affect might be seen as listening to  oneself. And taking actions to maintain a particular embodied configuration can be seen as speaking to this aspect of your emotions (considered temporarily as an agent). In this way, iteratively paying attention to what is interesting, and intentionally acting to pursue that interest, can b e seen as an intrapersonal conversatio n. Emerson’s group described building a double-pendulum system because it was “more interesting” a nd “chaotic.” At the initial conditions they modeled, an idealized double-pendulum does not, in fact, exhibit sensitivity to initial conditions in the sense o f chaos theory. But because of the double-pendulum system’s connection to the development of chaos theory, this group wove the descriptor of “ch aotic” into their narrative of the oscillator’s beh avior. Their intrapersonal conversations and attunement to epistemic affect (Jaber & Hammer, 2016) was an important element of their sustained disciplinary inquiry.

Noticing Emerson’s conversations with these agents is a way to understand Emerson’s deepening relationship to disciplinary ideas and practices. They strengthened their relationships with the ideal  physical system of a double-pendulum, with computational practices like the Run ge-Kutta algorithm as well as specifics of coding in C and Python, with the system of equations of motion, with peers and mentors, with epistemic affect, and with their disciplinary identity. We characterize Emerson’s disciplinary growth as em erging from these conversations between the agencies of their materia ls (their oscillator), theory (the idealized double-pendulum system, chaos theory, and programming techniques), affect (determining what they found to be interesti ng), and each other (working collaboratively).

## Conclusions

Reflective practice and listening  closely to understand the impacts of one’s actions can be understood as first necessary steps towards repairing relationships and finding restora tive forms of disciplinary learning (Everett, 2020; Winn, 2018). We explored a speculative framing of listening as a basis of disciplinary inquiry. Considering Emerson’s disciplinary conversations, we see disciplinary continuity between Emerson’s material interactions, their shifting interpersonal relationships, their disciplinary identity, and their embodied sensations and affect. By considering disciplinary relationships, we can (1) better understand varying ways and degrees that actions can be disciplinary, (2) understand disciplinary development from a relational perspective that is less compatible with individualism and meritocracy, and (3) recognize a continuity between the listening practices that inhere in science and the practices of listening that also propel restorative justice.
