# Speculative Futuring for Understanding River Socio-Ecology

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study investigates how Speculative Futuring (SF) can create space for high school students to critically engage with environmental challenges and envision alternative futures for their communities. Our findings show that students drew on multiple form s of knowledge such as lived experiences, and scientific information to shape their understanding around addressing socio-environmental challenges such as water quality, and accessibility of the natural resources. Artifacts developed during SF reflected their understanding of current socio-ecological systems while envisioning possibilities for the future.

Community-based learning focuses on building learning around learners’ local contexts, recognizing the epistemic knowledge of the community, positioning students as active participants who engage with the social, political, historical, and scientific dimen sions of their environments (Bang & Medin, 2010; Morales Doyle, 2017; Gyles, 2024). Speculative Futuring further extends this approach by creating space for critical and creative engagement with present and future socio-ecological contexts, shifting learni ng from fixed outcomes to sites of imagination and critique (Bang & Vossoughi, 2016; Bendor & Lupetti, 2025). Embedded within community contexts, SF enables students to draw on lived experiences and cultural knowledge to reimagine social and ecological fut ures. Building on this, the present study examines how a SF session within a participatory design-based implementation research project (Fishman & Penuel, 2018) supports students in conceptualizing and imagining the socioecological systems of the local ri ver, Narmada. We ask: How does student participation in speculative futuring support their conceptualizing of the socio-ecological systems of the River?

## Theoretical framework

Indigenous knowledge systems, feminist ethics of care, and speculative futuring reframe science and engineering education as relational, reciprocal, and contextually grounded. Indigenous epistemologies emphasize interconnectedness among humans and more-than-humans, centering reciprocity, care, and responsibility (Bang et al., 2018). While feminist ethics of care conceptualizes care as an active, collective practice of maintaining and repairing shared socio-ecological worlds (Tronto, 1993; Puig de la Bellacasa, 2017; McGowan & Bell, 2022). Together, they position care as both epistemological and practical, shaping how knowledge is created, shared, and enacted within lived contexts. Building on this, SF offers an approach that critically and creatively engages students to explore socio-ecological systems by imagining alternative futures (Bendor & Lupetti, 2025; Helgason et al., 2019). Collectively, these frameworks challenge dominant views of science as objective and detached, instead positioning learning as a relational process grounded in care and reciprocity with both human and more-than-human worlds (Bang & Medin, 2010).

## Context, data, and analysis

Situated within a larger project co-designing a high school environmental engineering curriculum with teachers, students, researchers, and environmental educators across 11 sessions focused on the Narmada River in Southern California, this study explores a  SF session in which participants constructed future river models using creative materials (e.g., LEGO, pipe cleaners). Drawing on audio recordings and artifacts, it analyzes how students made sense of the river. Analytical memo writing and grounded theory coding (Corbin & Strauss, 2008) showcased that students conceptualized the river as an interconnected, living system grounded in care and interdependence. Reimagining socio-ecological relationships between humans and more-than-humans As students envisioned the future of the river, they approached it from multiple dimensions, considering ecological balance, community roles, and relationship between humans and nature. In group 3, Gabby drew upon her personal experiences to conceptualize her vision for the river. She expressed: “As a runner, I would want more shade along the runner path so it's not hot. And people could go down the river for fishing if they have a fishing license. There can be a park next to it where children can run instead of having a fence around the river.” Gabby’s contribution highlights how lived experience and a connection with place informed her design thinking. As a runner who regularly moves along the river path, she envisioned more trees for shade and proposed removing fences to make the river more accessible. Her suggestion reflects a desire for the community to engage directly with the river rather than observe it from a distance. Building on this, group 3 collectively developed an idea of a digital board for people to visualize information on the riv er’s water quality. Gabby agreed to the idea and reflected on why she believes people are currently avoiding the river: “I believe a lot of people don’t really go down the river, they think it's polluted and, I guess, looks so dirty. If there is water quality information, more people will not be scared to come down to the river.” Her comments demonstrate how she recognized that community engagement is not only shaped by physical design but also perceptions of environmental conditions. Through these discussions, Gabby and her peers emphasized that reimagining the river’s future involves both rebuilding social relationships of care, awareness, and participation with the environment. Overall, the group 3 final design envisioned a river system where relationships between humans and more-than-humans in the river ecosystem were reciprocal and interactive. The river, in these instances, is not seen as a passive object of engineering intervention, but as a connected socio-ecological ecosystem.

This study showcases how speculative futuring supported students in reimagining their local environments not merely as engineered systems, but as living, interdependent ecosystems shaped by the environment and community relationships.
