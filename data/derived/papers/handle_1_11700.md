# Co-Designing to Foster Collective Agency

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
While research has pointed to participation in co-design having the potential to increase feelings of agency, understanding how co-design teams build collective agency is valuable. We describe a visual analysis of interactive boundaries that shape collective agency within co-design, offering examples of how collective agency emerged and was built during initial co-design.

Objectives While agency is often discussed in relation to acti on and engagement of individual learners and how th ey navigate learning environments (Severance et al., 2 016; Yip et al., 2017), we question the ways agency surfaces when groups of individuals engage in colla borative design (co-design). Collective agency (Gehring & Marx, 2023) notions are relatively new to the lea rning sciences; historically agency has primarily b een attributed to individuals interacting across systems of power. We define collective agency as movement toward agreed upon goals by groups working to shift tradit ional power structures in their interactions. We us e collective agency to extend understandings of how co-design teams engage together. We ask: A) What are the boundaries of context and activity that result in o bservable collective agency in co-design and how do these boundaries support the enactment of collective agen cy?; B) How can representations of these boundaries support facilitators planning and implementing iterative co-design?

## Theoretical perspectives

We conceptualize agency as enactment of identities in relation to sociocultural and sociopolitical con texts (e.g., Engeström et al., 2020; Gutiérrez et al., 20 19) and define collective agency as dependent upon the enactments of collective identities within education. Collective agency (Gehring & Marx, 2023) purposefully shifts from individual action within co-design to focus on non-normative approaches. Previously, we proposed a typology of boundaries that constrain or guide cr itical acts of co-design that can result in collective agency (Tierney & Stamatis, 2024). These include systemic power, identities and contexts, and disciplinary activities, roles, and tools.

## Methods

This paper focuses on a community-based project to co-design a youth and community art exhibit at an urban Latine museum. The initial co-design meeting took place with a group of twelve in itial stakeholders—three educators (one Latine, two white), six muse um professionals (five Latine, one white), and thre e researchers (one Latine, two white). Data was drawn from meetin g transcripts, fieldnotes, and artifacts. In previous work (Tierney & Stamatis, 2024), we examined how individual agency in co-design is bounded by histories of power and privilege, context, roles of members, and the goals of the project. Here, we sought to operationalize these boundaries in the design and iteration of our  co-design team. We posited that collective agency woul d emerge and be most observable when each of the boundaries had been attended to intentionally. Our analysis began with iterative rounds of coding, seeking to c onfirm or disconfirm the co-design boundaries we had initially established. As initial boundary codes em erge, we also questioned how to visualize boundaries as working together to support collective agency. Exam ining the coded boundary frequencies throughout analysis, we recognized the meeting could be separa ted into sections within which different codes occu rred with varying frequency. Radar charts graphing frequency of multiple codes bounded by time, supported us to visualize how boundaries of collective agency were engaged across the meeting (see Figure 1).

## Results

We summarize the three sections of our initial desi gn meeting and offer examples of ways collective ag ency emerged and was bounded across the meeting. We use radar charts to explore the potential for visualizi ng collective agency as shaped over time. We explore the possibility of these tools for co-design across projects. The initial design meeting took place at the museum and was designed in three sections: Background Building, Site Tour, and Initial Design. We began w ith researchers facilitating introductions and introducing the project proposal, goals, and budget. In his  introduction, Odim, a 22-year-old teacher who had engaged as a student in the pilot study, began by sharing his experience in the previous project. He explained he  was excited to see what was possible when community mem bers were invited to share their sto ries. His introduction set the tone for others and focused th is portion of the meeting on acknowledging co-designers’ identities, accompanying historic power, and the context and background of the project. Figure 1 Collective Agency by Meeting Segment The second part of the meeting was a site tour led by the museum professionals  to introduce the museu m’s exhibition on immigration. Lydia, the director of the museum, and Umberto, the museum’s lead curator, led a conversation about the possibilities of youth parti cipation in the exhibition. Umberto explained he sa w two models for the youth exhibition. In one version, ea ch young person who created a piece of art was incl uded, in the other, a smaller group of young people to ma ke curatorial decisions that did not need to includ e every young artist. Lydia jumped in and offered permission to treat the entire project as “research” where the text of the exhibit explained what we were learning from th e young people’s art, rather than highlighting the art and artists themselves. However, Umberto pushed back, explai ning both during the site tour and again in the subsequent design activity, “I’m thinking of this as a professional exhibit, and I believe they [the young people] are up for that.” In the design section of the meeting, several partners pointed out that had Lydia, rather than Umberto, been insistent on the professional nature of the ex hibition, there would have been much less freedom i n the designs of the programming. However, because  Lydia, whose role included more institutional power, voiced flexibility, co-designers felt excited by Umberto’s charge to creat e a professional exhibition. Theresa, an exhibit designer, responded to the potential tensio n by explaining, “As we settle into constraints, it becomes easier to design.” Across the first two sections of  the meeting, discussions of identity and power aro se intentionally and organically, surfacing the ways t hat different designers approached the project and offering opportunities for clarification. However, as we examined the bounda ries of collective agency within our co-design, we noted the need to develop co-design norms that could create space and procedures  to navigate potential tensions. The radar charts offered a concrete visual to discuss with our partners and to determine the best ways to develop these norms in our future design sessions.

## Significance

In this work, we examine collective agency in relation to boundaries that constrain and support choice, power, and action during co-design sessions. The construct of collective agency, and the radar charts we developed as visual analytic tools, have the potential to off er new ways of understanding and acting upon collaboration by examining how power, privilege, and identity are  leveraged toward goals. We seek to contribute to theorizing and co-design practices of collective agency. We examine h ow collective agency emerges in co-design, while acknowledging that it does not negate individual agency but instead builds upon it. If agency is the confluence of choice, power, and action, then n otions of collective agency push us toward reimagin ing power structures toward educational justice.
