# Acting, Sensing, and Writing: Making Sense of Self-Authored Data

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Data literacy is increasingly becoming an essential ability in our age of information. Core skills to be data literate include reading, writing, and interpreting data. When reading and writing data, the interpretation of data requires considerations of authorship as part of the sensemaking process. In this paper, we interpret data interactions with respect to authorship, which we operationalize as acting, sensing, and writing data. We highlight what that means for reading self-authored data. With data from a summer program, we examine youth’s sensemaking across a four part activity, wherein they use a jump rope sensor, recreate it with a new sensor, and then create their own version of a jump counter. This analysis presents emerging relationships between different configurations of author and reader and how those structures afford different sensemaking opportunities with data.

## Introduction

There are countless data-driven systems that are used to produce and collect data from human actions as the world enters an age of information. The ability to make s ense of data – across the layers of how it is collected, transformed, translated, and used – is a fundamental skill to navigate the current landscape of personal and global data (Wolff et al., 2016). Data literacy builds acr oss information, statistical, and computational competencies, so that learners can understand, find, interpret, manage, and use data (Prado & Marzal, 2013). Developing accessible ways of engaging learners in recognizing and unders tanding these life cycles is essential in navigatin g critical data futures where learners can agentically interac t with data. In this project, we exp lore various approaches to the production of “self-authored” data to deepen conceptualization of data literacy practices of writing and reading in relation to authorship. We discuss how expanding the modalities through which learners engage in data writing and reading can afford an expanded repertoire of di fferent kinds of data sensemaking. We present the d esign of an embodied data activity where youth compete in ju mp roping using a digital jump rope, try to “trick”  the jump rope, visualize jump data, and create their own digital jump rope. The activities enabled learners to make sense of data in ways that are situated in their lived experience and to make sense of data in ways that involve their bodies, different technologies, and their social connection s. Through a lens of authorship, we explore and pre sent how authorship is co-constituted across context, actions, participants, and sensors – and how this shapes and affords varied data sensemaking opportunities.

## Background

Here, we use data literacy to refer to “the ability  to understand, find, collect, interpret, visualize, and support arguments using quantitative and qualitative data” (Deahl, 2014, p. 41). “Literacy” as a term surfaces the processes of acting and sensing as they relate to reading and  writing data. Reading data may involve understandi ng, interpreting, and supporting arguments, while writi ng data involves finding, collecting, visualizing, and interpreting data. Interpreting in both emphasizes the non-neutrality of data and how it  appears in all interactions with data. This aligns with critical data literacy approaches to data that emphasize the need to be aw are of ideological, historical, political, and sociocultur al dimensions of data abilities (Louie, 2022). We argue that by examining the actions and sensing processes that lead into writing and reading, we can support critical data literacy learning for critical data futures (Selwyn, 2021). In this examination, we position youth as “authors,  architects, and interpreters of data” (Stornaiuolo, 2020; Bhargava et al., 2015), so that they feel empowered to inter act with data. In this next section, we consider how data au thorship can be a framework for better understandin g the relationship between data actions, se nsed observations, readings, and writings for us to  reach more agentic practices with data.

## Theoretical framework

We use data authorship to describe the interconnect edness between the tools, practices, and people tha t inscribe and write data; and the ways these data are read and comprehended. To conceptualize authorship, we operationalize data generation and productio n as acting, sensing, and writing data. We present examples that highlight specific perspectives on the production p rocesses of data and how that relates to reading da ta. We use the term sensing – instead of terms like observing – to highlight the similar roles played by senses like sight and technological sensors like movement, light, and con tact sensors as bridged in our study. This commonal ity also expands the ways we think of action and data as mul tilayered networks of people and tools.  These connections highlight how interpretation and “conclusions” are often inseparable from the network of factors that shape all stages of data interactions. We situate this proces s in personal data literacies to focus on learners’  interactions with data that is personal to them – as writers, sensors, readers, and more. We set the stage for our study around learners making sense of their personal embodied data around athletic movements. We synthesize these frames to investigate how being (and partnering with) the act or, sensor, and writers of data shape diffe rent ways of understanding and relating to data for young learners.

Acting, sensing, and writing data for reading Data generation is the production or creation of data with an intention in a context. It involves people sensing and acting, which are necessary for the data to exist. Data c ollection is the process of organizing, storing, and managing data, so that it is accessible at another time. It involves writing data, where people decide how to represent an action based on how it was sensed. Nav igating between acting and writing, there is also u sually a tool or sensor that is sensing. Sensors translate actions into some type of text, which can be later viewed. Through these interactions, data comes into being and peopl e become authors and managers of data. Understandin g these relationships helps us recognize how data processes are not neutral – situated by a person’s knowledge and context (Wilkerson & Polman, 2020), and shaped by authors a nd the tools used for collection (Hardy et al., 202 0). Learning experiences that include the firsthand exp erience of acting, sensing, and writing data offer learners insight into the impact of their choices through the production and writing process. Writing data through embodied activities creates a unique relationship and enable s learners to recall and interpret through their fe lt experience and senses (Lee et al., 2021b) impacting their reading of the data. Authorship and interpretation Acting, sensing, and writing constitute authorship.  Data authorship refers to the individual or group responsible for the creation of a specific set of data, who man age subsequent uses of the data by considering priv acy and consent processes (Lee et al., 2 021a). Authorship of data impacts the perceived pow er and social capital of that information (Selwyn, 2021). People bring in their story, historical and social narratives, and political environments as part of their interpretations of data analysis. Approaching critical interpretations and representations of data requires understanding personally situated aspects of the data, culturally significant parts, and soci opolitical systems of power (Lee et al., 2021a). The significa nce of these layers goes beyond the types of questi ons and analyses created, because it configures the validit y of knowledge from data and the trustworthiness of  sources (Lee et al., 2021a). As such, the relationship betw een people and data within interpretations reflects  the trustworthiness of the information as people become readers of data stories. Data storytelling involves interpretation in the reading of data by not only w riters of data, uniquely exemplified when youth aut hor data stories about their personal practices (Stornaiuolo, 2020). Through such authorship, youth have personal relevance and context for the data, which affect how they tel l these stories. Personal relevance surfaces consid erations of privacy, often invisibilized in interpretations of pre-collected data. As data writers, youth relate their  personal knowledge to their data as legitimate sources of kn owledge to their data stories and as readers, they critically evaluate information shared with them (Stornaiuolo, 2020).

## Methods, data, and context

To center ownership and agency in the processes of sensing, acting, and writing data, we created an activity where youth experience a sequence of different sensing and writing practices around the central goal of counting jumps with a jump rope. This activity took place during 1 day of a 3 hour session as part of a six-week summer program for youth between 13-15 years old. First, we tested and recreated a smart jump rope with a micro:bit – a popular, easy to program microcontroller with a variety of s ensors to record data around movement, sound and light. In this embodied data activity, youth first compete getting the most jumps with or without the smart jump rope. Next, they “trick” the jump rope by maximizing the count without necessarily jumping. After this, youth visualize jump data using the micro:bit, and write code to create their own digital jump counter. The design of this activity relates to existing critical data literacies approaches by offering a lens into the many choices made around t he data that is produced and collected during the activity. Ackn owledging the choices made within the activity supp orts sensemaking around the often-hidden interpretations within data analysis, which may promote future questioning and critique of data, especially when youth produce data themselves. Our data includes photos, student artifacts, and re flective memos. Using a grounded theory approach (Charmaz, 2006), the authors iteratively reflected on this data to identify how the activity sequence contrasted ways youth made sense of the different kinds of data. The earlier mentioned categories of actor, sensor, and writer emerged from these reviews, which we use to frame a nd highlight how the activities displayed different  aspects of sensemaking through these changing authorship configurations.

## Findings

We present our data as vignettes spanning the four phases of the activity – counting jumps, “tricking” the rope, collecting data from the micro:bit, and recreating a digital jump counter – highlighting instances of when youth actions varied from our planned activity, highlighting novel aspects of data authorship enacted in the study. Trusting the process: From action to writing to reading In small groups, youth tried to get the most jumps in a minute, and they recorded their number on a piece of paper. Here, youth were the actors jumping rope to generate data. The groups used different methods to count and write their data. Some relied on t he number that the device counted. Some groups manu ally counted the number of jumps. We asked youth to be the “final” writers of the data, by writing on their paper. This was follo wed by a reflection and data reading period to compare how m any jumps people  did across groups. One group assumed that the activity required consecutive jumps, rather than the total number of jumps possible within a minute, which impacted how they wrote and generated their data. G roups jumped from 10 to 174 times, among students w ho participated. This revealed surprising ways that in terpretation was embedded and invisibilized in the process of writing the counts of jumps. Youth interpreted and understood successful jumps through their shared understanding of individual jumps, and sometimes even the contextualized activity of jumping rope itself, which often includes the challenge of maintaining consecu tion in jumping. This writing is produced through a coordination of their sight (a visual sense) and the contextual knowledge they bring to bear. Youth relying on the device trusted the digital sensor’s ability to count jumps, which reflects the smart jump rope’s inclusion as a data co-writer. The consecutive jumping group’s decisions surface d the typically invisibilized aspects of reco gnizing a jump. But no one questioned the mechanics of how the smart jump rope assesses a successful jump. Des pite working in a different manner (compared to sight), it is engaged as a trusted partner since it maintai ns the connection between action (jumps) and expected writing (increase in count). Sensing: Recontextualizing actions and writing In the same groups, we asked youth to push their un derstanding of how the smart jump rope worked and w hat it wrote by asking them to get the highest number poss ible using whatever method they wanted. For 20 minu tes, youth swung, shook, and spun the rope to  increase the count on its handle. The device played  the same role as a co-writer of jump counts, but the action that generated the data was vastly different from the first part. During this data writing, two groups accidentally unscrewed the  handle from i ts battery, which erased their count. Youth panicked at the thought of having to start over, so  we asked them to give us their best estimate of th eir score, which we added to the final count on their sensor. This resurfaced themes of trust, and makes salient how the smart jump rope is a co-writer, and not a singular source of truth. It showed youth that data writing does not only occur at one point in time, nor is it only a part of data collection. It comes up in transforming and reshaping data. For these g roups, they had to organize and update their data b ased on two points of collection, which differed from the other two groups that recorded one number. After 20 minutes, we collectively read the data we collected. This data reading included a breakdown of the different actions that went into the data writing across different groups. Youth developed strategies about how to mov e the smart jump rope in order to optimize the numb er of “jumps” that it counted. Some strategies included swinging the rope between two people, twisting the rope by the handle in small circles, holding the rope and rubbing only the handle. Their strategies reflected their sensemaking about the device by focusing their attention on the rope in relation to the handle with the sensor. Writing with a new sensor This section introduced a new sensor, the micro:bit, so that youth could consider the affordances of another sensor and how its interpretation of a jump may differ. The micro:bit records time, acceleration, pitch, roll, and magnetic force. It also allows you to create your own counts  based o n these features. This was different from the smart jump rope, which only had one feature and it did not reveal how it decided what counted as a jump. The facilitator loaded code on the micro:bit to log readings from the micro:bit’s accelerometer. Then, the facilitator and a student jumped for 10 seconds with the micro:bit on their w rist. While they were jumping, the rest of the grou p was counting the number of jumps visually. They did 15 and 21 jumps, respectively. As a whol e class, we reviewed the data to make sense of it in relation to the number of jumps we counted. The data written by the micro:bit was reshaped by interface as a line graph. We examined this graph to see i f we could identify a pattern in the data related to the jumps. The youth began counting the numbe r of peaks on the variable with the largest y value s, which happened to be acceleration strength. The number of peaks could be counted in a way to match the number of jumps. We used this pattern to create a rule (or  model) for deciding what counts as a jump. We decided that if acceleration strength was over 2000, then we would count that action as a jump. This process of making sense of acceleration data recontextualized the writing process of the smart jump rope. By  introducing a new sensor and co-writer, we highlighted new ways of writing the same under lying activity of jumping rope. Returning to the original familiar action, showing new ways of writing data about it highlights how different kinds of interpretation can be involved in assessing similar activities.

Anticipating data reading: Designing a new way for writing data We invite youth to create their own digital jump (r ope) counter. We suggested creating a version that enables them to “win” in a way that others cannot easily imitate even while doing the same overall ta sk. We provided them a starter code utilizing the agreed upon threshold of 2000 for acceleration strength as a jump counting metric. They extended this code integrating data reportings  from different sensors. We saw youth center the affordances of the micro:bit to reshape the “desired” action being measured. For example, one group included sound in input and output. This offers a system that needs an extra noise to count a jump, providing the coders a “secret” access to scoring bonus points; and also in surfacing cros s-modal senses of what can be used to judge a jump. We reflected that in specific settings, jumps can also  be counted through the presence of specific sounds  (often the sound of the jump rope hitting the floor), and does not always need players to think about converting their visual modes of judgment to accelerometer based measures. This is a new authorship configuration, where youth create and shape their data co-writers (through code) and use that to write data about their own and each other’s activities. They are able to shape the relationship between act ion and data in even more dynamic ways than simply when they are investigating and “hacking” a fixed system (the smart jump rope itself as in phase 2).

## Discussion

As youth jumped, their movement acted on the smart jump rope, and together both sensed and co-wrote the data. The activity was designed to push the relationship that youth had with the jump rope’s sensing and jum p identifying abilities. Earlier, the group understoo d readings from the smart jump rope to accurately a ssert “I/you jumped [number] of times”.  However, by moving the sensor in ways that were dif ferent from jumping rope and noticing that the number was still increasing, youth recognized the sensor’s interpretation as a distinct part of the data writing process. Youth questioned the jump rop e as a source of “truth” and saw that the way data was produced with the sensor could be disconnected from  the action behind the data generation. This unsett ling revealed the interpretation process of reading self-authored data as a reconstructive act to understand  the actions that produced the data (Lee et al., 2021b). Moreover, reconciling personal data through active reflection upon the relationship between embodied actions and objective  data supports data literacy understandings (Ching et al., 2016). This understanding connects to various aspec ts of personal data literacies (Pangrazio & Selwyn,  2019), which highlight the value of being aware and critical of the limitations of technologies and data. We propose that activities like this offer unique kinds of co-authorship practices, since youth are writing data with a technological co-writer. While this is familiar to different debugging and tinkering practices in making and computing (Blikstein & Worsley, 2016), this req uires a different kind of trust and relationship in  the writing of personal data, where youth can embrace and expand the context of their data. And, when youth shape the (co)writing mechanism of their collaborative authorship (through code), youth formulate their embodied and sensed knowledge through a computational restructuration, which creates opportunities for youth to think abou t their movement through novel representational forms (Wile nsky & Papert, 2010). This constructionist app roach to creating a data writer offers inroads for youth to think about the data that makes up and can count towards a jump. The youth’s consideration of collaborative forms of  authorship with the technology lends itself to col laborative authorship between  community members, where data production requires i nterpretation across stakeholder’s acting and writing processes (Clegg et al., 2023).

This project expands notions of authorship, where youth shape what action is paid attention to, and how it is reported. Across authorship configurations, p eople have varying degrees of personal relevance and context regarding data, which they can apply to the way they tell different stories. One limitation  to our analysis is that we relied on artifacts and memos from the interactions to conceptualize authorship throughout the activity, rather than video. Future work may explore this and how we  can reconnect the ways sensors work to our senses when reading and writing data. Can this open space to reflect on the connections between acceler ometer data and felt senses of movement and balance, or how such actions can be interpreted and written about through sound? These cross-modal aspects of sensing and writing data especially open exciting avenues to think about engaging youth and learners in accessible forms of data writing and visualization, for instance data sonification (Vi nes et al., 2019). We highlight authorship as a lens to describ e different relationships between acting, sensing, and writing, as key processes underlying sensemaking with data.
