# The PS-I Squared Model: Integrating Failure-driven and Success-driven Scaffolding

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Much research has examined Problem-Solving prior to Instruction (PS-I). Recent studies have reported the effectiveness of failure-driven scaffolding during this problem-solving phase. However, concerns remain that such scaffolding may negatively affect stu dents’ confidence. This study proposes the PS-I Squared Model, which integrates failure-driven and success-driven scaffolding. In a lesson on vector dot products for high school sophomores, we compared the effects of the PS-I Squared Model, PS-I with success-driven scaffolding, and PS-I with failure-driven scaffolding. The results indicated that both the PS-I Squared Model and failure-driven scaffolding were more effective than success-driven scaffolding for promoting conceptual knowledge acquisition. Furthermore, the PS-I Squared Model appears to offer a viable approach to addressing the concerns associated with failure-driven scaffolding.

In recent years, the field of learning science has demonstrated through numerous studies the effectiveness of an instructional strategy called “Problem-Solving prior to Instruction (PS-I)” for acquiring conceptual knowledge (Kapur, 2016; Loibl et al., 2017). PS-I consists of two phases: a problem-solving phase where learners tackle complex problems containing unfamiliar concepts, and an instruction phase where they receive explicit instruction on these concepts (Kapur & Bielaczyc, 2012).  Furthermore, research has begun to enhance the effectiveness of PS-I in conceptual knowledge acquisition by supporting learners during the problem-solving phase of PS-I. However, attempts to enhance PS-I effectiveness through scaffolding have not demonstrated sufficient results. Sinha and Kapur (2021) conducted a meta-analysis comparing lessons using PS-I with lessons that added scaffolding during the problem-solving phase of PS-I or implemented alternative activities instead of problem-solving. The results  reported that the presence or absence of scaffolding did not affect the effectiveness of acquiring conceptual knowledge. In response to this challenge, Sinha  et al. (2021) examined the effectiveness of explicitly guiding learners toward failure during the problem-solving phase of PS-I, providing them with opportunities to learn from failure. They refer to this intervention of guiding learners toward fai lure as failure-driven scaffolding, contrasting it with general scaffolding that supports problem-solving toward success. Conversely, scaffolding that explicitly guides problem solving toward success is termed success-driven scaffolding. Sinha et al. (2021) compared the effects of success-driven scaffolding and failure-driven scaffolding on university students enrolled in a data science course. Consequently, the report indicates that the group receiving failure-driven scaffolding significantly outperformed the success-driven scaffolding group in the quality of their inferences on the post-test. Based on these insights, this study proposes the PS-I Squared Model, which integrates failure-driven and success-driven scaffolding, as a more effective instructional design  and examines its effectiveness (see Figure 1). The research question is as follows: RQ:  How does the PS-I Squared Model affect the acquisition of conceptual knowledge compared to PS-I, which involves failure-driven scaffolding and success-driven scaffolding? ## Participants

Participants were 103 second-year high school students (3 intact classes) from a national high school in Japan. Each class was assigned to one condition (PS-I Squared, failure-driven, success-driven), without random assignment, and all lessons were conducted by the same teacher. The intervention was conducted by the first author in September 2025.

## Procedure

The topic of this intervention was the vector dot product. The intervention was conducted over three days. On the first day, a questionnaire survey on learner characteristics (10 minutes) and a pre-test (10 minutes) were administered. One week later, over two days, lessons were conducted for each of the three classes: a PS-I Squared Model lesson, a PS-I lesson with failure-driven scaffolding, and a PS-I lesson with success-driven scaffolding (60 minutes each). During this period, a questionnaire survey (5 minutes) regarding students' awareness of knowledge gaps was administered. Finally, a questionnaire survey (10 minutes) on engagement in this practice and its content was administered, followed by a post-test (20 minutes). Prior to the main analysis, we compared pretest scores across the three classes using a Kruskal-Wallis test and found no significant differences (p >.05).

We conducted an analysis using a general linear model with post-test scores as the dependent variable and pre-test scores, two dummy variables based on the PS-I Squared Model, and the interaction term between pretest scores and the dummy variables as independent variables.
