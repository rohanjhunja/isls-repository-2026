# Experiencing and Envisioning STEM futures: How Conditions of Learning in STEM Educational Settings Supported the Links Four Latinx Young Women made to the Future

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper examined how four Latinx women interested in pursuing STEM-Health pathways made meaning of possible futures over three years while they participated in a summer STEM-Health program (HSTEP) and while taking science and math AP and Honors courses in an urban STEM magnet high school. I developed a conceptual framework about how young people make links to the future in connection to STEM learning by drawing on social psychological and sociocultural perspectives from the learning sciences. I found that the types of relational and pedagogical supports each context made available shaped conditions for learning about STEM and, in turn, shaped how participants linked learning experiences to the future. This analysis helps us consider why it is important to design learning environments to experience and envision futures in STEM connected to the goals of STEM education.

## Introduction: Conditions for making links to the future in STEM

Significant research in the learning sciences is tied to efforts to reimagine the goals of STEM education and how those goals align with ethical and political commitments to create robust conditions for learning that center young people’s past, present, and future (McKinney de Royston & Sengupta-Irving 2019; Warren et al., 2020). Some of this research examines how young people experience STEM futures socially and how futures can be modele d in connection to peers and educators in the present and across time (Sengupta-Irving et al., 2023; Vossoughi et al., 2020). There remains a large gap in our understanding of the role that env isioning and experiencing future s in STEM plays on the experiences of women of color, particularly how it supports the pursuit of future STEM pathways. In this paper, I draw on research from sociocultural perspectives of the learning sciences and social psychology to develop a conceptual framework to examine how young people link their experiences to the future based on the conditions for learning and experiencing the future made available in STEM settings. I draw on the zone of proximal development to demonstrate how possible futures can be modeled thr ough engagement in socially mediated activities in the present moment (Vygotsky, 1978). Through prolepsis, I conceptualize how adults have the power to envision what is possible for young people and their futures (Cole, 199 8, p. 184; Sengupta-Irving et al., 2023). I draw on social psychology to capture how young pe ople make meaning of the future by leveraging future identities as meaning-making tools and how future identities are  dynamically constructed in the moment (Oyserman, 2015; Oyserman, D., Destin, M., & Novin, S). This framework helped me examine how the types of relational and pedagogical  supports made available across both contexts shape d the links participants made to futures in STEM.

## Methods

This paper examines the learning experiences of four Latinx women for three years and across two contexts, the summer Health Science Teaching Excellence Program (HSTEP)  and while taking math and science courses in a large Midwest urban magnet high school known here as Metro College Prep (MCP). HSTEP provides participants with a “health science curriculum addressing traditional external factors (e.g., barriers and support) through handson, student-centered, and interactive learning experiences'' (Rocha et al., 2022, p.4). A new health/medical theme is selected each summer. For participants, emergency medicine, pediatrics, and public health w ere the central themes. MCP was selected as a partnering high school becaus e the school’s neighborhood was designated as a ‘health professional shortage area’ due to low access to medical professions. All four participants identified as Mexican American and were enrolled in the same cohort for all three years. Participants joined the program because of their int erest in STEM-Health pathways. HSTEP enrolls 5 to 6 scholars each summer. This cohort also enrolled an Asian male scholar who was not selected as part of this study. I explicitly center the lived experiences of Latinx women to gain insig hts into the phenomenon of constructing possible futures from the perspective of minoritize d women of color who remain underrepresented in STE MHealth careers. Methodological process In this paper, I adopt an interpretive methodological approach (Erickson, 1986; Vossoughi & Gutierrez, 2014) to understand how participants make meaning of their experiences in connection to the future across two learning environments. I take an interpretive approach to me an that the central focus of the study is “the loca l meanings that happenings have for the people involved in the m” (Erickson 1986. p.121). The meanings participant s make about their learning and educational experiences be come the foundation for our understanding and theorizing of complex human becoming and possible futures. Furthe rmore, to move away from normative conceptions of human becoming, an interpretive approach asks resea rchers to notice and illuminate tensions and contra dictions to provide a more complex understanding of people and cultural practices (Vossoughi & Gutierrez, 2014).

## Data collection and analysis

This paper draws on secondary data from a larger longitudinal qualitative researc h study analyzing the experiences of minoritized high school students who participated in HSTEP. As part of this research study, semistructured interviews were conducted at the end of each four-week summer intensive. Additionally, every week during the four-week summer intensive, participants were assigned to journal about their learning experiences in the program. For this study, I employed open coding, which is an interpretive ap proach informed by grounded theory that is useful for exploring themes that eme rge from the data (Charmaz, 2006). My data analysis resulted in four phases  that led me to understand the association between linkin g present experiences to the future and what factors shaped conditions of learning about STEM in  both contexts. First, I reviewed all interviews and reflections cross-sectionally using an inductive approach to understa nd what emerged from the data. Second, I conducted a longitudinal analysis of the data to un derstand how experiences might be different and sim ilar between participants. This analysis led me to develop two categories: a. how participants experienced and b. how they envisioned the future. Third, I narrowed my sc ope and examined how participants described their learning experiences across both contexts to understand how futures were  experienced and envisioned in connection to what they were learning about STEM and/or STEM-Health. Fourth, I examined differences and similarities in the types of support both contexts offered which allowed me to understand how a range of relational and pedagogical supports shaped how participants experienced and en visioned the future.  In this paper, I leverage my  cultural intuition (Delgado  Bernal, 1998) and positionality as a methodological tool that  allowed me to capture the complexity of the narratives of Latinx women withou t romanticizing their experiences. My identity as a firstgeneration immigrant and high school graduate, and the power and privilege I hold as a Latinx woman of  color researcher informed my analysis of participant experiences.

## Findings

How relational and pedagogical supports shape links to the future. Across both contexts, I found that relational and pedagogical supports shaped con ditions for learning about STEM-Health and in turn shaped how participants linked t heir experiences to the future. By relational support, I mean the relationships participants formed with pee rs, teachers, and mentors that supported their link s to the future. By pedagogical supports, I mean the availab le artifacts, concepts, opportunities, and resource s that each context made available for thinking about the future  in connection to the subject matter or dis cipline. Based on student descriptions, relational and pedagogical su pports were framed as either generative or as the absence of generative supports. I found that within HSTEP, participants described generativ e relational and pedagogical supports that offered opportunities to experience a nd imagine possible futures in STEM-Health in the present. This included socially mediated experiences with me ntors and hands-on opportunities to learn about medicine. On the other hand, in HS generative supports meant developing bonds with teachers (relational)  and teachers providing follow-through and clarification about the subject matter (pedagogical). I found that generative supports in the context of HS were described as  support to do well in classes  which is different from HSTEP which is connected to future identities and expansive possible futures in STEM. Generative relational and pedagogical supports During her second year in HSTEP, Amanda described learning how to bandage and suture wounds using the skin of a banana. She noted, “...and this stood out to me the most because it was hands-on and it kind of gave me more experience, more this surgery, like that.” Later the interviewer asked Amanda if surger y was something she was interested in pursuing. Amanda responded, Yes, it’s like I am all over the place. I am lookin g into a lot of different things. This is why I like how each summer is a different topic. So I am looking into OB-GYN, surgery is something I would like to do, even though I struggle a lot wi th those bananas. My mentor is helping me with that. Because she specializes in minimally invasive gynecology. Amanda’s experience learning how to suture a banana  through “hands-on” opportunities (pedagogical), and the additional support provided by her mentor to practi ce the skill (relational), provided an opportunity to tie this educational experience to a possible future as a surgeon. Though Amanda struggled with suturing, the support she received from her mentor provided an opportunity for Amanda to reframe her struggle in a generative way. Here Amanda described generative relational and pedagogi cal supports tha t created opportunities for  linking present experiences to the future connected to becoming a surgeon. Relational support s included making one-on-one connections with mentors with medical specialties in their fields. Pedagogical supports included experiences such as shadowing doctors and taking part in hands-on activities. Additionally, in addition to descriptions of generative supports, participants highlighted the important ways HSTEP structured socially mediated opportunities so that participants could envision and experience possible futures in health care in the present (Vygotsky, 1978). We see this when Amanda described how hands-on activities provided an opportunity to experience  how to suture. The support Amanda received from her mentor helped her reframe a struggle into a possible future as a surgeon. This connects to research about practice-linked identities (Nasir, 2012), which argues that relational, material, and ideational resources have the potential to shape co nditions for learning, understanding one’s place in  the world, and experiencing possible futures in practice with others. Absence of generative relational and pedagogical supports During HS I f ound that  the overall nature of supports, which included both  generative and the absence of generative supports, created less meaningful opport unities to experience possible futures or ideas abo ut oneself as a doer of science. When asked how she felt about her science class during her first year in HS, Love responded, I think I did alright. I feel that I could have don e better. I feel like our teacher wasn’t as —I thought he tried. He tried to get us into the class but one of the students wasn’t really—how can I say it—didn’t like to do what the teacher said, so it woul d disrupt the class, which made me not able to focus as much, and since the teacher ha d all his attention too —it was not only that one boy but it was a group, he would take long to g o through his powerpoint, and I couldn’t take as much notes, and I wasn’t a ble to understand as much. I needed help from my fr iends, but it was alright, but I could have done better.

Love’s experience taking science during 9th grade w as challenging due to disruptions experienced in th e classroom that made it hard for her teacher to get through the course material. Descriptions of an abs ence of generative relational and pedagogical supports  included limited opportunities to learn and underst and the class material from her teacher, such as when her teacher  had a hard time getting through a power point with out disruption (pedagogical), and her teacher having to split his attention between s tudents, making it difficult for Love to connect with her teacher (relational). On t he other hand, Love also described access to genera tive relational and pedagogical supports from friends th at provided additional opportunities for making sen se of the material. The combination of these supports made Lo ve feel she “could have done better” in class. The quote suggests that Love was connecting not being able to  do well in class to an absence of generative rela tional and pedagogical supports.

In the follow-up question, we observe how the absence of generative relational and pedagogical supports in Love’s science course shaped how she linked experiences to the future. When asked how she felt about taking science next semester, Love responded, Hopefully, I get a good class, or at least be able to focus. Or go up to my teacher and ask him questions personally. I guess freshman year was whe n I wasn’t as up to going as much to my teachers, that was a problem to me too. So hopefull y, this year, I wo n’t be shy. I can go up to my teacher and ask him a question or if I have a problem. Though the interviewer prompted Love to think about  the future, here we get a sense of how Love drew o n her experiences in 9th-grade science to imagine what it would be like to take science as a 10th-grader. Love described two links to the future that were framed as hopes for how her experiences could be different next year. First, Love hoped she would “get a good class.” Through the use of the word  “good,” Love described the conditions she hoped to experience in the future that would be an improvement from her current experience. Second, Love stated “or” and proceeded to hope she would “at least be a ble to focus.” The use of the word “or” implied how, in the absence of generative support, Love would need to be the one to create better condition s to support her learning. Love stated several actions, such as the need to “f ocus,” not being “shy,” and taking the initiative to go up to her teacher and “ask him a question.” These actions sig nal agency and how Love actively thought of ways to learn and participate in class. Yet, Love’s use of the words “I won’t” and “I wasn’t” also showcases how she interpreted her struggles in science class as a problem that wo uld be up to her to fix  alone. This is in contrast to Amanda’s experiences in HSTEP overcoming struggles connected to suturing through support from her ment or. Here we also observe that the links Love made to the future  were about improving conditions of learning for herself. This connects to research suggesting that people make meaning of the future based on the fit between a future identity and the context (Oyserman, Destin, & Novin, 2015). The links participants made to the future in HS reflected the constraints and absence of generative supports they experienced in the classroom.

## Discussion

These findings expand our understanding of how cond itions of learning in STEM settings can support and/or constrain how young people make links to the future  in connection to STEM pathways.  Within HSTEP, I found that generative relational and pedagogical supports  helped create robust conditions for learning about STEM-Health that supported experiencing possible futures  in the present such as shadowing doctors and takin g part in hands-on activities such as suturing. Additionally, the a vailability of support  from expert mentors provided opportunities for social mediation connected to pra ctice. Though not fully explored in this paper, links to the future included exploring ethical commitments connected to future careers in STEM-Health. In HS, I find that an absence of generative relational and pedagogical su pports resulted in links to the future that  participants framed as hopes for improving their learning conditions, which they framed as something that would be up to them to fix alone. These learning conditions left little space for participants to envision themselves as doers of  science and math. An important implication and contribution of this work is the need to design STEM learning experiences that align with efforts to reimagine STEM education that support expansive links to possible futures in STEM.
