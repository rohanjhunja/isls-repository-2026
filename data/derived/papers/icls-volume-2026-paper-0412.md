# The Influences of Alternative Onboarding Approaches in a Puzzle Video Game on Learning, Problem-Solving, and Player Experience

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Onboarding shapes how players learn, yet empirical comparisons of theoretically grounded approaches remain scarce. We tested three onboarding variations in Baba Is You— Tutorialized (direct instruction), Safe Problem-Solving (visually guided), and Increased Impasse (productive failure) —against the original game with 122 participants across three sessions. Despite being perceived as the least difficult, the Safe condition produced significantly lower mechanical understanding. Behavioral and experiential differences converged in subsequent sessions, carrying implications for tutorial design in game-based learning.

Onboarding—how a game’s introductory levels introduce players to its mechanics—is a constant design challenge with real pedagogical stakes. As games are often used in learning settings (Gee, 2005), identifying which onboarding methods promote deeper unders tanding of the mechanics becomes essential, as these early moments shape not only whether a player continues or quits the game (White, 2014), but also how well they understand the basics on which the game is designed. Although practitioners have extensive practical knowledge— including approaches ranging from explicit text-based instructions to implicit environmental guidance (White, 2014) — empirical comparisons grounded in learning theory within the context of games remain limited (Endresen & Nathan-Roberts, 2018; Cao & Liu, 2022; Andersen et al., 2012). Following Design-Based Research principles (Sandoval & Bell, 2004), we designed three different onboarding conditions for Baba Is You (Teikari, 2019), a commercial puzzle game where players rearrange text-based rule tiles to solve levels. The Tutorialized  condition delivered direct instruction (Rosenshine, 2009) through in-level pop-up text and post-level summary signs. The Safe Problem-Solving condition reduced the risk of failure by making solutions visually apparent and removing most possible impasses. T he Increased Impasse condition applied productive failure (Kapur, 2008) and impasse-driven learning approaches (VanLehn, 1988), deliberately directing players toward common stuck points. The Original game served as a baseline of well-ordered problems (Gee, 2003). These learning approaches and much of our previous work with the game informed these alternative designs (Authors, 2022, 2024, 2025).

## Method

We recruited 122 undergraduates at a large Midwestern university, all new to the game. Groups did not differ significantly in prior game expertise or age. Three one-hour sessions were conducted via Zoom: Session 2 took place three days after Session 1, and Session 3 was held one week later. In Session 1, participants played the seven introductory levels of their assigned condition and then completed a 12-item multiple-choice quiz along with surveys measuring difficulty, enjoyment, curiosity, and engagement (Cronbach’s α =.70 –.93). Sessions 2 and 3 used unmodified original game content for all groups as a measure of how well the initial levels prepared them for the rest of the game.

The quiz included six direct identification questions (e.g., explaining what the STOP rule does) and six inference questions that required reasoning about mechanical interactions in specific game states. Behavioral log data (undo counts, rule manipulations, restarts, wins) were analyzed as per-minute rates to account for differences in time spent on the seven introduc tory levels across conditions (Safe = 7.96 min; Tutorial = 13.49 min; Original = 13.33 min; Impasse = 20.48 min). Between-group comparisons used Kruskal-Wallis tests (ε² effect sizes), while within-group changes were examined with Bonferroni-corrected paired t-tests (α =.0125 for four-group correction).

Mechanical understanding. Quiz scores differed significantly across conditions (H(3) = 16.76, p <.001, ε² =.139). The Safe Problem-Solving group (Mdn = 9.5, M = 9.23, SD = 1.48) scored lower than all others: Increased Impasse (Mdn = 10.0, p =.003), Original (Mdn = 10.0, p =.005), and Tutorialized (Mdn = 11.0, p <.001); no other pairwise differences were significant. Item-level analysis revealed the Safe group deficit emerged from inference 2665 items—on the key item asking which object could become the win condition, only 46.7% of Safe players answered correctly compared to 70 –90% in other conditions (p_BH =.022). All 16 incorrect Safe responses were due to choosing the most visually salient object rather than analyzing the rule-tile layouts. On direct-identification items, the Safe group performed similarly to the other groups. Additional inference items showed consistent descriptive trends in the same direction, though these did not reach significance after correction. Problem-solving behavior.  Undo chain rates during onboarding varied significantly (H(3) = 45.69, p <.001, ε² =.381): Increased Impasse was highest (M = 3.84/min) and Safe Problem-Solving was lowest (M = 0.61/min), with Tutorial (0.95/min) and Original (2.21/min) in between, confirming the conditions produced their intended behavioral profiles. Across Sessions 2 and 3, no significant group differences were observed on any log metric.

Player experience.  Between-group comparisons of mid-session difficulty revealed significant differences (p <.001): the Increased Impasse group reported the highest difficulty (Mdn = 24.0) and the Safe group the lowest (Mdn = 17.5). Post-session, all groups converged (p =. 767). Within-group analyses showed that the three groups reporting the lowest initial difficulty experienced the steepest increases when transitioning to unmodified content: Safe (d = 1.37), Tutorialized (d = 1.48), and Original (d = 1.04)—all p <.001. The Increased Impasse group showed a small, non-significant increase (d = 0.40, p =.037 uncorrected). Regarding enjoyment, three groups showed significant post-session declines (Original d = 0.72, Safe d = 0.76, Tutorial d = 0.94; all p <.001) while the Increased Impasse group did not (d = 0.33, p =.084). Curiosity and engagement showed no significant between-group differences at either time point. The Safe Problem-Solving condition resulted in the lowest level of mechanical understanding and the largest difficulty jumps at session transitions, challenging the assumption that structuring early levels to be free of frustration and visually clear is safe for player learning (White, 2014; Endresen & Nathan-Roberts, 2018). Players seem to have completed early levels by visually following patterns rather than reasoning about underlying rules, consistent with Miller’s (2023) “giant’s staircase”: the disconnect between onboarding and full-game difficulty leaves players unprepared and causes enjoyment to decline at the transition. Notably, the Tutorialized and Original groups spent nearly identical time in Session 1 (13.49 vs. 13.33 minutes), yet the Tutorialized group scored descriptively higher (Mdn = 11.0 vs. 10.0), suggesting what players do during onboarding matters more than how long they spend. While problem-solving behaviors converged across groups in Sessions 2 and 3, this convergence gave no window into whether the knowledge gap revealed by the quiz had closed—the game normalized what players did, but behavioral data alone cannot tell us whether their understanding followed. This dissociation illustrates why behavioral data alone can be insufficient as a proxy for learning in game-based contexts, and why mechanic understanding requires direct assessment. The Tutorialized and Increased Impasse conditions achieved similar mechanical understanding through opposite methods —explicit instruction and deliberate struggle — showing multiple viable paths to deep understanding, provided players actively process the mechanics. Designers have flexibility in choosing onboarding strategies without long-term harm to engagement: those focusing on mechanic retention should prefer Tutorialized or Increased Impasse; those seeking the smoothest difficulty progression should use Increased Impasse; and those wanting low early friction without sacrificing understanding will find Tutorialized to be the most practical choice.

## Poster discussion and visual support

This poster invites discussion of: (1) when smooth onboarding undermines mechanical learning; (2) how behavioral data can conceal knowledge gaps in game-based learning research; and (3) how productive failure and direct instruction frameworks are applied in commercial puzzle game design. Visuals will include side-by-side level screenshots illustrating the four onboarding designs, boxplots of quiz scores and mid-session difficulty by condition, and a within-group change summary (difficulty and enjoyment) across the session transition.

## Limitations

Sample sizes of approximately 26 –30 per condition limited the power for some post-onboarding comparisons.

The study used a single commercial puzzle game with unique mechanics, and the findings may not apply to other genres. The quiz was carefully designed but not formally validated. Behavioral log metrics capture players' actions, not the quality or intent behind them; ongoing think-aloud and screen recording analyses will complement the reported patterns.
