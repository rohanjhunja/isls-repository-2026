# Infrastructuring STEAM Learning: Building Interconnected STEAM Learning Infrastructure for Preservice Teachers and K-12 Students

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This design-based implementation research project sought to understand and improve science, technology, engineering, arts, and math (STEAM) learning infrastructure for K-12 students and preservice teachers in one local ecosystem in the Midwestern United States. Drawing on Actor-Network Theory and infrastructuring, we analyze the process of creating a STEAM workshop series for preservice teachers and an elementary afterschool STEAM club, co-facilitated by these preservice teachers. We identify specific elements of higher education and K-12 infrastructure that supported or impeded implementation.

## Background and objectives

In recent years, there has been a push away from conceptualizing and structuring STEM (science, technology, engineering, and math) as a set of siloed disciplines  and toward  integrated-STEM or STEAM (STEM plus arts/design) instruction (Bybee, 2010; ITEA, 2007; NGSS Lead States, 2013). However, for integrated-STEAM instruction to be facilitated effectively, educators need preparation. Most preservice teachers (PSTs) don’t receive practical exposure to STEAM tools or teaching methods. As a result, they lack understanding of what STEAM is, why it is important, and how best to teach it (Portz, 2015). There are few formal STEAM curricula, and those that do exist are often expensive. This means that to teach STEAM, teachers need to design their own lessons. This takes time they are rarely afforded and understanding and interest they have not had opportunities to build (Estapa & Tank, 2017). Thus, educational infrastructure  must be considered. Penuel (2019) proposed ten elements of infrastructure that influence K-12 education: (1) teacher professional learning; (2) standards;  (3) curriculum materials; (4) assessments; (5) instructional techn iques and routines; (6) building-and district-level policies; (7) school schedules; (8) instructional support  roles and positions; (9) organizational routines; a nd (10) personnel evaluation systems. We propose three more: (A) technology tools and materials; (B) formalized goals and strategic priorities; and (C) physical spaces. We also argue that higher education brings its own system of infrastructure.

To address these elements of infrastructure, Penuel (2019) proposed infrastructuring as a form of design research involving the creation of tools to improve practice, develop theory, and build knowledge (Edelson, 2002), focusing on creating conditions for educators to make innovations into working infrastructures. We use this approach to improve STEAM education for PSTs and K-12 students based on our understanding of infrastructural supports and barriers. We draw on prior approaches to educator learning that attend to infrastructure, including research on engaging PSTs as facilitators of the Fi fth Dimension afterschool program (Cole, 2009; Cole  & The Distributed Literacy Consortium, 2006)  and research on the design and implementation of ST EAMbassadors, a professional learning program for community college students to become  out-of-school time (OST) STEAM mentors (Pinkard et al., 2021). We integrate these approaches by engaging PSTs in learning to facilitate STEAM through participation in workshops and co-facilitation of a STEAM afterschool club. In doing so, we have two objectives: (1) better understand infrastructural supports and barriers to STEAM for PSTs and K-12 students; and

(2) design and test a model to prepare PSTs to facilitate STEAM while providing K-12 STEAM opportunities.

## Method

Design: The beginning To create the conditions for STEAM learning through infrastructuring, our project aim s to redesign existing systems to generate effective and sustainable “working infrastructures for organizing learning activities” (Penuel, 2019, p. 2) in both K-12 and higher education. This took the form of a Design-Based Implementation Research (DBIR) project (Penuel et al., 2011). We attended to elements of existing infrastructure  in making the design adaptive to local constraints  as we also sought to understand those constraints. In collaboration with  K-12 and higher education practitioners, we engaged in multiple cycles of iterative refinement, informed by data. When the first and second author started this project, we had just been hired as a faculty member and makerspace facilitator, respectively, at a large university in the Midwestern United States. We had the shared goal of supporting PSTs in learning about STEAM  through hands-on making and facilitation activities. This aspect of our approach was informed by situated learning theory (Lave & Wenger, 1991), as was the desire to create a community of practice of STEAM educators based on design principles (see Table 1) from research (e.g., An, 2020; DiGiacomo & Gutiérrez, 2016; Hilppö & Stevens, 2020; Izadinia, 2013; Kafai & Fields, 2018; Quigley et al., 2017; Paradise & Rogoff, 2009; Ramey & Stevens, 2019, 2023; Stevens et al., 2016; Vossoughi & Bevan, 2014). In Fall 2022, we began conversations with colleagues in the College of Education  and a local school district  to determine needs, assets, supports, and barriers. As the substance of these conversations and how the y shaped implementation is the subject of our findings, we will skip ahead to  explain the final form of implementation first. In Fall 2023, we began implementation of a STEAM workshop series for PSTs. We have since implemented two more iterations of this workshop series in Spring and Fall 2024, as part of an established program that allows stude nts to explore areas of interest not covered in the required PST curriculum. All PSTs are required to complete one workshop track of their choice, involving six 60-90-minute workshops. In our STEAM Track, participants choose six of eight 90-minute workshops. These included workshops on STEAM facilitation; STEAM learning on a budget; vinyl cutters and subtractive manufacturing; 3D modeling and printing; electronics; robotics and coding; making with AI; and fitting STEAM into the classroom. Consistent with DBIR, workshop design was refined over the three iterations. All students enrolled in a track also complete 10 hours of volunteer work  before they student-teach. Previously, these volunteer hours  did not need to  relate to their track. For the STEAM  track, we asked that participants complete their volunteer hours by co-facilitating an elementary afterschool STEAM club, in order to gain experience facilitating STEAM learning while b uilding community STEAM learning infrastructure. Our partner school was selected, because it had: (1) makerspace tools  currently being underutilized; (2) a librarian interested in STEAM; (3) an existing culture of afterschool clubs; and (4) a high percentage of students from lowincome households (67 percent) and underrepresented groups (72 percent). Starting in Fall 2023, the afterschool STEAM club met for 2 hours per week in three 5-8-week terms per year. It was open to students in grades 3-6.

## Data collection and analytic framework

Throughout iterative cycles of implementation, refinements were informed by data on implementation processes and outcomes as well as student and PST learning. For the current analysis, our research questions were: (RQ1) What are the infrastructural supports and barriers to STEAM teaching and learning?; and (RQ2) How might we engage PSTs in STEAM learning, while building K-12 STEAM learning infrastructure? Over the course of the  2023-24 and 2024-25 school years, 40 PSTs enrolled in our workshop track. Of those, 12  agreed to participate in research. Simultaneously, 47 elementary school students participated in the afterschool STEAM club, with 18 participating for more than one term. Of these, 20 agreed to participate in our research. With these participants, we collected vid eo data using stationary and wearable cameras. We also collected photographs of artifacts and qualitative post surveys with both PSTs and elementary school students. We collected field notes in workshops, club sessions, and our planning and partner meetings. We also saved emails and planning documents. Our analysis here focuses on these notes and documents. In the analysis, we focused on infrastructure that supported or impeded implementation. It was guided by Actor-Network Theory (ANT), which treats organizations as dynamic networks of  actors that come into and out of association with one another (Latour, 2005). ANT drew our attention to tracing associations between actors over time (Callon, 1986), attending to the work they did to stabilize the network and to moments of translation— the processes of change through which the network assembled and extended itself (Fenwick & Edwards, 2010). We examined interessement—actions by which actor s entice others to join a network (Callon, 1986) and how actors served  as obligatory passage points  (Callon, 1986; Latour, 1987) —ideas or tangible entities position ed within a network so that they must be passed through. We discuss enrolment—a process whereby entities become engaged in new identities and behaviors  (Callon, 1986; Latour, 1987) and distinguish between intermediaries— which “[transport] meaning or force without transfo rmation”—and mediators—which “transform, translate, distort, and modify the meaning or the elements they are supposed to carry” (Latour, 2005, p. 39).

## Findings: The middle

Infrastructuring preservice teacher education From our initial planning conversations and meetings with university stakeholders, it became clear that successful implementation of STEAM learning for PSTs would require creative infrastructuring. Our initial vision was to create a class on STE AM facilitation. However, we were told by two STEM education faculty, who would function as obligatory passage points  for such a class,  that creating this class would not be possible because of the curricular requirements for PSTs. One of the facult y also expressed skepticism about the value of STEAM. Thus, our unsuccessful interessement of faculty and curricular time required a different approach. At this critical moment, the third author and another colleague  told us about the workshop track program. We met  with the program director (an obligatory passage point) and pitched a STEAM workshop track. The director, who was seeking new workshop proposals, was excited by our interessement to STEAM learning. However, she mediated implementation by requesting some translations. We had hoped to have students attend workshops in a specific order and to assign pre-readings and post-assignments. However, she told us that neither of these ideas aligned with the program structure. This led to a translation: We could require attendance at the first and last workshop, but otherwise, flexibility would be necessary. The second translation was that we could offer optional readings and assignments, but we could not require them as homework.  A third translation was that workshops could be no longer than 90 minutes  and should meet in the evenings so as not to conflict with  other classes. One final translation that was requested was to offer virtual participation, but we rejected this translation because of the hands-on nature of STEAM. Once we agreed on workshop structure, the director asked that we submit a written proposal detailing the content and learning objectives of each workshop. Ultimately, we traversed this obligatory passage point and enrolled new actors. However, these actors served as mediators, translating implementation. We alsoneeded to assemble technology tools and materials. Drawing on the second author’s prior experience as a higher education makerspace facilitator and the first author’s experience supporting STEAM learning in K-12 schools, we assembled a list of tools and materials that would support authentic STEAM practices and resembled what PSTs might encounter in school s. This process of enrolling material actors to the network aligned with the mandate given to the second author: to set up a makerspace for the College of Education. However, before tools and materials could be purchased, they had to be approved  (another obligatory passage point). In Year One, this meant submitting a list directly to the techno logy director and departmental executive officer, who quickly approved all  purchases. However, in subsequent years, tools and materials had to be requested through a different, competitive process, through which faculty and staff apply for funds students pay as a technology fee. Many, but not all, of our requests were granted, thus mediating implementation. Finally, we needed to find a physical space to house our equipment. Initially, the second author was told by his supervisor that a room might be available within the College of Education building. However, it quickly became clear that a room with the necessary space and ventilation was not available. An alternative space in a nearby building was proposed, with the caveat that the space wouldn’t be ready until 2026. Thus, an interim location was needed. A suitable space was identified by one of the assistant deans, in a university owned building seven miles (11 kilometers) away. This was a 20-minute drive by car or nearly an hour ride via the free campus shuttle. This presented an obstacle to access by students without cars, leading to low attendance in early workshops at the makerspace and another translation. We began running many of the workshops that used smaller, mobile tools in the College of Education building and only hosted two per semester at the actual makerspace. Again, new infrastructural actors had been enrolled, but not without translating or mediating the form of implementation. Infrastructuring K-12 STEAM learning Next, we needed to address K-12 infrastructure. Prior to design, the second author had talked with administrators and teachers in a local K-12 school district, who indicated STEAM education needs mirroring those observed by the first author in districts around the country. This district, like many others, had invested in STEAM kits and makerspaces. However, materials were often sitting unused or underused—to the detriment of students’ STEAM learning. This community need provided an interessement for local school stakeholders to join the network. We wanted to learn more about local actors, assets, and barriers, so we set up several conversations with school stakeholders, including two district-level administrators who oversaw science and technology, respectively. These administrators connected us with the librarians  at two schools whose libraries housed small makerspaces. The librarians shared that they were using the makerspace tools in limited ways during library class but expressed frustration that they weren’t seeing broader use by other teachers. From these co nversations, we gathered that barriers to use included: (1) instructor knowledge of what to do with the materia ls; (2) instructor time to design activities; (3) instructional minutes during the school day; and (4) accountability to state standards and standardized tests. We also learned about the material and technology assets in the schools and that one of the schools, an elementary school, had an established culture of one-day-per-week, trimester long, afterschool clubs. We decided that this school’s afterschool club program would b e a good place to seed OST STEAM learning. We co-designed a STEAM club with the school’s librarian, then attempted to recruit PSTs in the STEAM track to spend their volunteer hours co-facilitating the club. However, in Fall of Year One, only two of the over 20 students in the track volunteered as facilitators. For some, the barrier to enrolment was a scheduling conflict. Others did not feel urgency to complete volunteer hours, as students have until their third year to complete these hours. Consequently, the first and second authors continued co-facilitating all afterschool club sessions with the collaborating librarian in a high-touch approach to implementation. By Winter and Spring of Year One, there were occasional PST volunteers, but it wasn’t until Fall of Year Two that we consistently got four PSTs to attend each club session. One positive consequence of this slow enrolment of undergraduate students was that it led us to recruit different actors, graduate students, to support implementation. Thi s included roles as participant observers and activity designers. In particular, we invited graduate students with STEAM backgrounds, including the fourth and fifth authors, to design and lead STEAM activities leverag ing their expertise. While this helped stabilize the network, it also translated the form of implementation, as these actors served as mediators. Finally, while the school was able to assemble some necessary tools and materials, our team assembled and enrolled many university tools and materials. This exposed elementary students to higher-powered tools and allowed us to facilitate activities resembling those used with PSTs. However, activities were translated to address the needs of young learners, including using largel y picture-based instructions. A final translation was that any computer-based activities had to work on Chromebooks, i.e., they could only use a web app or Google app. This is a common problem in U.S. K-12 schools, where a desire to provide every student with a computer is balanced with pressure to minimize costs. This has led to a proliferation of low-powered computers and the phasing out of higher-powered ones. Here, it meant that the enrolment of computers mediated or translated implementation.

## Discussion and conclusions

These findings illustrate how an ANT lens can enhance our understanding of the processes of implementation and infrastructuring. ANT calls our attention to specific roles that human and non-human actors play in supporting or impeding implementation, including how actors might  serve as obligatory passage points, be enrolled in  the network, or mediate implementation. Our findings improve our understanding of the infrastructural supports and barriers to STEAM in K-12 and higher education. For example, Penuel (2019)  identified teacher professional learning as an important element of K-12 infrastructure; our study illuminates higher education’s infrastructural supports and barriers to providing PST STEAM education. These included: (1) standards and curricula for teacher preparation; (2) faculty and staff perspectives on STEAM; (3) co-curricular programs such as the workshop track program; (4) accompanying policies; (5) instructional and organizational routines; (6) class schedules; (7) physical space; and (8) technology tools and materials. Because we implemented within the workshop track program, we were not accountable to assessments, beyond student  satisfaction surveys (where feedback was overwhelmingly positive). However, we also found ourselves constrained in the types of measure s we could use to evaluate program success, because assessment was explicitly discouraged. In K-12 schools, by working in the OST space, we avoided dealing with some elements of infrastructure  identified by Penuel (2019), such as assessments and standards alignment. However, the other infrastructural elements identified by Penuel were consequential, and we identified still others of import, including (A) technology tools and materials; (B) goals and strategic priorities; and (C) physical spaces. Finally, this project provides a viable model for engaging undergraduate PSTs in STEAM teaching and learning while also meeting a communit y need for young learners’ STEAM opportunities. By attending to the elements of infrastructure involve d in building this sort of program in our context, we draw attention to the types of supports and barriers oth ers may experience, as well as the need for infrastruct ural or systems-level thinking to support and sustain implementation of STEAM or other educational innovations.
