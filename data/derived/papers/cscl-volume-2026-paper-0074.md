## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
While the emergence and evolution of ideas are central to collaborative learning, these processes often remain invisible in digital environments. Social annotation (SA) embeds collaboration within shared texts, forming a distinct ecology where reading, ann otation, and discussion occur as a single, integrated workflow within one representational space. This study introduces the Idea Creation Cycle in Social Annotation (ICCSA), a theory-informed and analytics-operationalized framework that models how ideas mo ve and transform through four recursive phases: sensemaking, anchoring, interactive elaboration, and re-anchoring. Using multimodal SA traces, ICCSA captures the temporal trajectories of ideas, examining how cognition, artifacts, and interaction coevolve within one representational space. A proof-of-concept analysis of undergraduate course data reveals distinctive transition patterns and highlights re-anchoring as a key mechanism of knowledge advancement. Together, these findings position ICCSA as a bridge between CSCL theory and actionable learning analytics, advancing the understanding and support of idea development in text-embedded collaboration.

In collaborative learning contexts, the emergence of new ideas is critical for knowledge advancement. However, the processes through which ideas emerge and take shape typically remain invisible in conventional classroom practice. Social annotation (SA) off ers a unique opportunity to make these processes visible by embedding highlights and comments directly into shared digital texts, allowing learners to externalize their thinking in situ and engage in dialogue around shared anchors in specific textual passages (Kalir & Garcia, 2021; Novak et al., 2012). Accordingly, SA has been widely adopted in higher education, particularly in online and STEM settings, to support both individual comprehension and collaborative knowledge construction (Zhu et al., 2023). SA is not merely a strategy to increase participation during reading; rather, it intertwines reading, textual anchoring, and peer discussion into a single, text-embedded workflow, which can be viewed as a distinct computer-supported collaborative learning (CSCL) ecology where cognition, artifacts, and interaction coevolve within one shared representational space (Zhu et al., 2020). In this ecology, ideas emerge at the intersection of private comprehension and public inscription. Students select key passages, articulate interpretations or questions in the margins of text, and invite immediate uptake through threaded dialogue. Despite this tightly integrated configuration of sensemaking, anchoring, and interaction, current research often treats these layers separately or reduces analysis to surface indicators focusing on what or how learners contribute (e.g., annotation quantity). While prior studies provide valuable analytical insights into participation and idea creation by proposing conceptual frameworks with operationalized analytic metrics (Zhu & Chen, 2023) and delving into specific reply patterns (Chen & Chen, 2024), those approaches rarely capture how ideas move and transform across these layers of activity in SA.

This study addresses these gaps by introducing the Idea Creation Cycle in Social Annotation (ICCSA), a theory-informed, analytics-operationalized framework that conceptualizes SA as a recursive process of sensemaking, anchoring, interactive elaboration, and re-anchoring/advancement. ICCSA conceptualizes learning as a socio-material cycle in which cognition, artifact, and interaction continuously shape one another, and operationalizes this process through multimodal indicators integrating temporal, semantic, positional, and network dimensions. Specifically, this study (1) operationalizes each ICCSA phase and branching outcome through measurable indicators, (2) constructs an integrated analytic pipeline that identifies phase transitions and idea trajectories, and (3) validates the empirical distinctiveness of these patterns through a proof-of-concept application using undergraduate course data. By consolidating dispersed metrics into a unified, transition-oriented model, ICCSA reframes SA as a first-class context for CSCL theory and provides actionable analytic instruments for modeling and supporting idea development in text-embedded collaboration. The Idea Creation Cycle in Social Annotation (ICCSA) The Idea Creation Cycle in Social Annotation (ICCSA) is a process-oriented framework that models how ideas emerge, evolve, and advance within text-embedded collaboration. ICCSA positions SA as a distinct socio-material 469 ecology in which cognition, artifact, and interaction coevolve within a single, integrated workflow. In this ecology, students interpret shared texts, anchor their insights to specific passages, and build on peers’ ideas through threaded dialogue. ICCSA seeks to make these often-invisible movements of ideas empirically visible by linking cognitive, textual, and social layers of collaborative meaning-making to multimodal traces generated by SA learning platforms. Rooted in collaborative discourse and knowled ge-building traditions in the CSCL field, ICCSA conceptualizes learning as a process of refining and improving ideas through socially mediated inquiry (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2014; van Aalst, 2009; Weinberger & Fischer, 2006). ICCSA extends these perspectives by showing how idea movement can be observed in text-anchored contexts where reading, annotation, and how material representations shape and sustain collective cognition (Suthers et al., 2010). In SA, the text itself functions as a shared artifact that coordinates comprehension and dialogue, while anchored comments make ideas public and durable, allowing others to elaborate, contest, or recontextualize them. Through this lens, ICCSA fuses cognitive and social processes that are often studied separately into a recursive cycle of externalization and transformation.

Additionally, temporal and sequential perspectives on CSCL (Popov et al., 2017; Rosé et al., 2020) further inform ICCSA’s phase-transition view of idea development. These approaches emphasize that learning unfolds as interdependent actions whose order and timing shape meaning-making opportunities. Rather than treating annotations as isolated events or aggregated participation metrics, ICCSA models ordered sequences of actions that trace the life cycle of an idea, from private cognitive preparation (sensemak ing), to public textual externalization, to social elaboration and subsequent recontextualization. Through this view, ICCSA advances recent analytical efforts that model collaborative discourse through networked perspectives, such as the AttentionInteraction-Creation (AIC) (Zhu & Chen, 2023) and Social Semantic Network Analysis (Oshima et al., 2019). While these models reveal structural and relational patterns of SA (Zhu & Chen, 2023) or examine temporal dynamics of idea networks in broader CSCL contexts (Ohsaki & Oshima, 2021), ICCSA shifts the analytic focus from structural connectivity to temporal transitions across phases of idea work, situating these dynamics in the distinctive ecology of SA where the text itself organizes participation. By integrating  behavioral (e.g., reading durations), textual (e.g., semantic continuity), and social (e.g., thread branching) indicators, ICCSA establishes a conceptual-behavioral-analytic bridge between CSCL theory and computationally observable traces. This enables empirical modeling of how ideas are initiated, elaborated, and re-anchored over time within the ecology of text-embedded collaboration.

ICCSA delineates four recursive phases of idea creation (sensemaking, anchoring, interactive elaboration, and re-anchoring), each representing a distinct coupling of cognition, artifact, and interaction within SA ecology. Learners first make sense of text (sensemaking), externalize ideas as epistemic artifacts (anchoring), refine them through social negotiation (interactive elaboration), and recontextualize them intonew spaces (re-anchoring). Each phase generates distinct but interlinked behavioral and sem antic traces, enabling analytics to capture transitions (e.g., anchoring → elaboration → re-anchoring versus anchoring → dormancy). Through this process view, ICCSA connects micro-level annotation behaviors to macro-level trajectories of idea development, providing a framework for both theoretical explanation and computational modeling of collaborative sensemaking in SA.

Phase 1: Sensemaking.  Sensemaking precedes visible contribution and captures how learners allocate attention, interpret text, and prepare to externalize ideas. The text functions as a cognitive scaffold, prompting the identification of ambiguities or connections that later bec ome public annotations. Drawing on theories of joint attention and preparatory comprehension (Chi, 2009; Wise et al., 2021), ICCSA operationalizes this “invisible” cognitive work through behavioral indicators such as reading duration, scrolling coverage, and highlight density before annotation (see Table 1). These traces reveal learners’ investment in comprehension and the establishment of shared attentional context, which is an essential foundation for subsequent anchoring. Phase 2: Anchoring. Anchoring marks the transition from private understanding to public articulation. In this phase, learners externalize emerging ideas by attaching them to specific textual passages through highlights and annotations, creating visible, text-bound epistemic artifacts that can be taken up by others (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2006; Suthers, 2006). The highlighted passage and its associated annotation together function as a boundary object linking individual interpretation with collective discourse. Conceptually, anchoring is defined by how ideas are first made visible and situated within the shared representational space. Accordingly, analytic indicators focus on two dimensions: (1) the specificity of text linkage (captured through highlight span length and anchor precision, reflecting how tightly an idea is localized to a textual segment) and (2) the form of initial externalization (captured through annotation length, syntactic completeness, and epistemic stance such as questioning, which characterize how learners position their contributions at the moment of anchoring). Together, these indicators capture how ideas enter the collaborative space and enable subsequent elaboration. 470 Phase 3: Interactive Elaboration.  Interactive elaboration forms the social core of ICCSA, where anchored ideas are tested, refined, or restructured through dialogue. In this phase, learners engage with others’ contributions by building on, questioning, or contesting prior annotations, ref lecting transactive discourse and dialogic engagement (Weinberger & Fischer, 2006; Stahl et al., 2014). Compared to anchoring, which focuses on initial inscription, interactive elaboration captures how ideas evolve through interaction over time. Analytic indicators focus on two dimensions: (1) the structure of interaction and (2) the semantic progression of ideas. Structural indicators include thread depth, branching factor, and reply latency, reflecting the extent, responsiveness, and sustainability of dialogue. Semantic indicators capture how ideas develop within threads, including semantic cohesion, stance shifts, and the introduction of new perspectives. Through these threaded interactions, ICCSA identifies distinct traje ctories of idea development, including divergence (where peers introduce novel perspectives and expand conceptual space), convergence (where dialogue consolidates shared understanding), discarding (where ideas are refuted or abandoned), and dormancy (where anchors receive little or no uptake), revealing how collaborative engagement shapes ideas beyond their initial articulation. Phase 4: Re-Anchoring/Advancement. Re-anchoring represents the recursive culmination of the cycle, where learners revisit and extend prior ideas across time and contexts. In this phase, ideas are not only elaborated within a single thread but recontextualized through new annotations that connect back to earlier contributions, either within the same text or across texts. This process operationalizes idea advancement in knowledge-building terms, reflecting the continued refinement and reuse of ideas over time (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2014; Stahl, 2017). Analytic indicators focus on the continuity and integration of ideas across contexts. Semantic continuity captures the degree to which new annotations relate to prior ones, indicating reuse or transformation of  earlier ideas. Cross-text linkage and re-anchoring latency reflect how ideas travel over time. Network-based indicators such as bridging centrality capture how learners connect otherwise separate discussion clusters. Together, these measures reveal how id eas accumulate, integrate, and advance, highlighting the role of learners in sustaining knowledge development within text-embedded collaboration. The course w as designed around weekly readings, during which students collaboratively read, annotated, and discussed shared scholarly articles using a digital annotation platform named Perusall. The dataset comprised de-identified Perusall exports containing reading times, highlight spans and positions, annotation texts, timestamps, and reply structures, enabling reconstruction of students’ multimodal engagement across cognitive, textual, and social layers of discourse. For analytic consistency and comparability, we se lected the same Week 5 reading used in both the 2022 and 2023 course iterations. This midpoint reading provided a balanced volume of activity, stable instructional context, and structured pagination that allowed precise data alignment. The proof-of-concept focused on three analytic objectives: (1) operationalizing each ICCSA phase through measurable multimodal indicators; (2) constructing an integrated analytic pipeline to detect temporal transitions among phases; and (3) examining cross-year differences to validate the empirical distinctiveness and robustness of the framework. Each ICCSA phase was instantiated using theoretically grounded metrics derived from Perusall logs: temporal (e.g., reading and inter-annotation intervals), textual (e.g., annotation l ength, semantic continuity), interactional (e.g., thread depth, reply latency, cohesion), and network-based (e.g., bridging centrality, cross-thread linkage). These multimodal traces were processed through a unified analytic workflow to generate comparable indicators across cohorts.

Across both cohorts, ICCSA captured the full life cycle of idea activity from preparatory reading to anchoring, elaboration, and re-anchoring. Descriptive contrasts between the 2022 (n = 87) and 2023 (n = 85) classes highlighted both continuity and variation in idea dynamics. In the  sensemaking phase, 2022 students exhibited longer pre-annotation reading times (M = 14.77 min) and broader coverage, suggesting deeper preparatory engagement than the 2023 cohort (M = 2.38 min). The  anchoring phase similarly sho wed richer externalization in 2022, with longer annotations (M = 185 words vs. 125) and greater interpretive distance between annotations and source text (0.70 vs. 0.61). During interactive elaboration, the 2022 cohort demonstrated more sustained discussion (thread depth = 1.48 vs. 1.06) and faster uptake (reply latency = 1.47 days vs. 2.55), aligning with higher dialogic engagement. Finally, re-anchoring patterns suggested stronger integrative movement in 2022, reflected in slightly higher semantic continuity (0.78 vs. 0.76) and greater network bridging (0.002 vs. 0.0004). Together, these indicators illustrate how ICCSA can identify distinct temporal and structural dynamics of idea development even within the same instructional design. This study introduced ICCSA as a theory-informed and analytics-operationalized framework for examining how ideas emerge, evolve, and advance within text-embedded collaboration. The proof-of-concept analysis demonstrated ICCSA’s potential to capture the temporal and structural dynamics of idea development, revealing how cognitive, textual, and social layers interact throughout the collaborative process. By linking these layers to multimodal traces, ICCSA helps move learning analytics toward modeling the processual movement of ideas across initial sensemaking, anchoring, elaboration, and re-anchoring. Theoretically, ICCSA contributes to CSCL theory by framing SA as a socio-material ecology where cognition, artifacts, and interaction coevolve within a single representational space. While prior frameworks of knowledge building and artifact-mediated cognition examined these dimensions separately (Zhu & Chen, 2023; Chen & Chen, 2024) or broader CSCL contexts (Ohsaki & Oshima, 2021), ICCSA unites them into a rec ursive cycle of externalization and transformation, elucidating how ideas transition from private comprehension to public inscription, from individual interpretation to collective negotiation, and from situated discussion to cumulative knowledge advancement. The inclusion of re-anchoring extends knowledge-building theory by identifying how learners revisit and repurpose earlier insights to sustain inquiry over time. Methodologically, ICCSA introduces a phase-transition approach linking CSCL theory with measurable multimodal analytic indicators. SA data, traditionally analyzed via structural metrics, are reconceptualized as traces of idea trajectories. Behavioral, textual, and social dimensions jointly represent cognitive investment, epistemic stance, and dia logic exchange (e.g., reading time and coverage reflect sensemaking; annotation length, stance, and text-annotation alignment capture anchoring). The proof-of-concept invisible in static engagement metrics, offering a shared analytic vocabulary that bridges conceptual and computational models. Together, by linking CSCL constructs to empirically observable patterns in learners’ annotation activity, ICCSA provides both a theoretical account of idea dynamics and a basis for analytics-based modeling, supporting the growth and continuity of ideas within the evolving ecology of text-embedded collaborative learning.
