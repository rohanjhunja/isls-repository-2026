# Making Decomposition Visible in Early Elementary Computational Thinking

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
We investigate how decomposition emerges in early elementary computational thinking (CT) through a microgenetic analysis of two first-grade students engaged in eight mathematics–coding lessons using a coding robot toy. Rather than presupposing large, open-ended problems for decomposition, we foreground unit s—what students use as a unit when interpreting problems. Findings show (a) prior intuitive units (e.g., “go-and-return”) that diverge from those of the robot’s syntax, (b) shifts toward robot-aligned units (move one grid unit; rotate 90° in place), and (c) formation of composite units by grouping recurring sequences of atomic units via a function tile. We conceptualize early CT decomposition as changes in the unit in students’ problem solving, offering a developmentally appropriate lens grounded in observable activity.

Computational thinking (CT) has attracted widespread attention (Wing; 2006; Grober & Pea, 2013), prompting extensive research on the components that make up CT (e.g., Tikva & Tambouris, 2021; Weintrop et al., 2016). Decomposition is frequently identified as a key component across this literature (e.g., Shute et al., 2017; Zeng et al., 2023). However, the literature has mainly analyzed decomposition at the secondary level and beyond. In recent years, CT education has increasingly extended to early-grade classrooms  (e.g., Bers et al., 2014), including emerging analyses of decomposition at this level (e.g., Clarke-Midura et al., 2023; Lee et al., 2023). Given that CT is highly contextual (e.g., Brennan & Resnick, 2012; Weintrop et al., 2016), it is important to extend this line of research with rich empirical evidence to articulate how decomposition emerges in younger learners’ contexts.

In this paper, we seek to document how decomposition surfaces in young students’ problem-solving, focusing on the forms it takes within first-grade, integrated mathematics–CT lessons using a robot-coding toy. Lit review: How “Decomposition” is framed and measured in CT Decomposition in K–12 CT frameworks Across K–12 CT frameworks, decomposition is routinely positioned as a core practice  or skill (e.g., Shute et al., 2017; Zeng et al., 2023). Various models operationalize this construct in different ways (Brennan & Resnick, 2012; Weintrop et al., 2016), resulting in variations in labeling and emphasis across contexts. Most definitions converge on the idea of breaking problems into workable parts  (e.g., Murcia & Tang, 2019). Prior studies on decomposition mainly examine the reasoning processes of secondary students in the context of open-ended tasks and trace how students identify and solve subtasks (e.g., Kwon & Cheon, 2019, on middle-school Scratch; Cheng et al., 2023). Such designs make decomposition visible at the subgoal or subtask level, often yielding clear analytic markers such as subtask articulation, subgoal coordination, and iterative refinement. Challenges in early-grade contexts Translating the “decompose a large, open-ended problem into smaller subtasks” lens from secondary education to early childhood is developmentally challenging. Firstly, tasks for young learners are necessarily constrained, which limits opportunities for gen uine top-down decomposition. For instance, in Rijke et al. (2018), students in Grades 1–6 created brief hand-movement sequences and were then asked to “break the sequence into parts (p.80)” and generate “as many decompositions as possible (p.80).” With the definition of decomposition in the secondary level context, such work is better characterized as partitioning a short, given routine rather than structuring a complex problem into coordinated subtasks: in an early-grade setting, the problem space is too small to support the kind of subgoal architecture typically analyzed with older learners. A second ambiguity concerns how decomposition co-emerges with adjacent practices. In Ehsan et al. (2021), a child’s plan—“I’m gonna build a fence and then a toy (p.452)”—was presented as an example of 1577 decomposition; however, this can be read primarily as sequencing toward a goal rather thandecomposition in the stronger sense used in secondary contexts. There is little sustained articulation of the internal structure of each “subtask,” and the reasoning intertwines with planning and ordering actions. In early grades, then, decomposition often appears entangled with practices such as sequencing, and coordination—making it analytically challenging to isolate “decomposition” as a standalone construct. Together, these challenges reveal a mismatch between how decomposition is defined and enacted in secondary education and the developmental realities of early childhood. Recognizing these constraints, prior work has proposed age-appropriate definitions that foreground part– whole coordination and construction from parts (e.g., Clarke-Midura et al., 2023). Building on this view, we treat early-grade decomposition as fundamentally contextual —shaped by tasks (which necessarily differs from secondary level tasks) and by its co-occurrence with neighboring practices. Accordingly, the present study offers an empirically grounded, fine-grained analysis of how first-grade students enact decomposition within integrated mathematics–CT lessons using robot-coding toys, highlighting the subtle, intertwined nature of decomposition in early-childhood contexts. To this end, our study is guided by the following research question: “What form of decomposition emerges in the context of first-grade integrated mathematics–CT lessons using robot-coding toys?”

## Participants

The data for this paper comes from a larger project (NSF- #2300357) on the integration of mathematics and CT in early childhood classrooms. In this project, we employed Design-Based Research (DBR) methods (Cobb et al.,

2017) to design and implement integrated Math-CT lessons for first-and second-grade students, and analyzed

their CT learning trajectories. For the present analysis, we focus on a pair of girls (pseudonyms, Ophelia and Lily) during eight 20-minute sessions in a school located in the western United States. Lesson implementation and video data A researcher –teacher led eight 20-minute integrated mathematics –coding sessions with two focal students. In these sessions, the students solved multiple pathway problems (e.g., creating a code to move a robot from a start to a target location on a grid map) using the Cubetto robot toy. Cubetto’s syntax system unitizes action, treating each colored tile as a discrete and atomic unit: green (move forward 5 inches), purple (move backward 5 inches), red (rotate right 90°), yellow (rotate left 90°), and blue (function). To capture the students' process, a second researcher video-recorded each session while writing a design memo. Students were also frequently asked to use the robot or their own bodies to show what a code would do. In total, approximately 3.5 hours  of video were collected for this group.

## Data analysis

We conducted microgenetic learning analysis (MLA) (Parnafes & diSessa, 2013) to capture students’ problem-solving at fine granularity (utterances, gestures, code placements, robot actions). Each session was segmented by task (i.e., code problem). For each new problem presented to the students, we (a) traced embodied demonstrations, including manipulations of the robot and body-based enactments, (b) documented how they explained what each tile or subsequence was supposed to do, and (c) noted accompanying gestures and verbal justifications that indexed their interpretation of the code. Drawing on these multimodal traces, we analyzed how decomposition surfaced in students’ activity and, through this analysis, inductively articulated three forms of decomposition: (i) simulating units of movements, (ii) reorienting to robot units, (iii) regrouping codes into composite units.  The following episodes highlight these forms of decomposition, thereby making visible shifts in the units students used when decomposing and interpreting the robot’s movement. Simulating units shows prior understanding of movements The first unit that emerged was based on students’ prior understandings of movement, which differed from the robot’s syntax. In Session 1, the researcher–teacher introduced the Cubetto robot and its four basic tiles to Ophelia. For the first ~10 minutes, Ophelia observed the robot’s movement for each tile; for the remaining 10 minutes, she was asked to create a code to move the robot from one point to another on a 3×3 grid. After approximately 5 minutes of observation, she was asked to enact the code Forward –Left–Backward–Right using her body (5:30– 6:00, Session 1). Her enactments diverged from the robot’s syntax: for Forward she took one step forward and returned to her initial position (Fig. 1); for Left, one step left and return (Fig. 2); for Backward, one step back and return; and for Right, one step right and return.

This indicates that by Session 3, their unit for rotation had shifted from one step right to an in-place rotation, yet they still coupled it with a return to a fixed forward orientation. Across subsequent sessions, their movements increasingly reflected the robot’s atomic units  without the automatic return.  These observations suggest that through interaction with the robot, the units they used to parse its movement gradually aligned with the robot’s syntax.

Re-chunking atomic units into composite units The second shift in students’ unit use was the adoption of composite units, whereby atomic tiles were regrouped into a new unit. In Session 6, the students were asked to program a square (3-by-3 grid). They first constructed a full program (Fig. 6a). Then, using the blue function tile, they chunked the pattern Forward– Forward–Left as a single composite unit and produced a new program comprising four invocations of this function (Fig. 6b). This re-chunking shows that the students not only aligned with the robot’s atomic units  but also reorganized the problem by repeating a composite unit. This suggests that their decomposition shifted again—from a sequence of atomic units to a sequence of composite units.

The findings show that as the two students interacted with the robot’s syntax, the units they utilized to interpret given problems shifted. Building on these results, we conceptualize decomposition in early CT as changes in the unit of students’ problem-solving reasoning—i.e., moments when learners re-chunk and reframe what counts as a workable part in their problem space. This framing clarifies the relation to sequencing: while closely related, sequencing primarily orders already-defined units, whereas, in early CT, deco mposition involves shifts in what counts as a unit for perceiving and structuring the problem. Thus, rather than ope rating at the level of large problems, decomposition for young learners can be examined at a micro level —focusing on the units they use to interpret and segment a given task. Viewing decomposition as shifts in the unit of reasoning offers a developmentally appropriate lens that makes it empirically visible and provides fine-grained indicators for studying or supporting young students’ CT engagement.
