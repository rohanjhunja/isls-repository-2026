# Alleviating Tensions between Culturally Relevant Pedagogy and Mathematical Practices of Proof

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Mathematical practices of proof are central to the study of mathematics. Engaging high school students in these practices may be challenging, however, as students often encounter difficulties in understanding their intricacies while teachers simultaneously struggle with conveying their nuances. Although the integration of culturally relevant pedagogy (CRP) in mathematics classes has promising benefits for students, the marriage of CRP and mathematical practices of proof is not devoid of tensions, as teachers often wrestle with harmonizing the norms and values underlying these two instructional praxes. This paper aims to alleviate these tensions by advocating for the establishment of equitable learning communities. By co-creating proof norms with their students, teachers can balance both the tenets of CRP and the disciplinary practices of proof to develop students’ mathematical proficiencies.

## Introduction

Mathematical proof serves as a fundamental and auth entic practice for mathematicians, playing a centra l role in their field (Davis & Hersh, 1998). For students, ma thematical proving cultivates the skills necessary for logical reasoning and effective argume ntation, while also allowing  students to develop conceptual understanding and procedural fluency in mathematics (NCTM, 2009). Despite these benefits, however, challenges ofte n emerge in the teaching and learning of proof in mathematics (Weber et al., 2020). Overarching these issues are the foundations of the disciplinary practices of proof themselves, as t he origins of mathematical proof exhibit a rigidity rooted in ancient traditions (Krantz, 2007) and emphasize argumentative structuring that tends to overlook non-dominant approaches (Reid & Knipping, 2010). While culturally relevant pedagogy (CRP) has been lauded as being influential in developing students’ mathem atical skills without centering dominant methodolog ies (Brown et al., 2019), mathemati cs educa tors may struggle to incorporate CRP into their cla ssrooms while simultaneously navigating perceived constraints ass ociated with the disciplinary practices of mathemat ical proving. This apparent struggle highlights the tensions that emerge from the need to promote m athematical rigor while simultaneously attending to the diverse inter ests and backgrounds of all students. To promote equitable outcomes, this paper proposes a pedagogical approach which utilize s the establishment of norms to mitigate the tensions that arise between CRP and the teaching of proof. Rationale for CRP while attending to proof In this paper, a mathematical proof is a logical ar gument consisting of a set of sequential statements  that flow from accepted definitions to a conclusion using modes of reasoning and representation agreed upon by experts in the field (Movshovitz-Hadar, 2001). As previously mentioned, however, stu dents historically struggle with proofs, and the difficulties of engaging students in the established practices of mathematical proof are compounded when attempting to integrate CRP into the classroom, given its three tenets that emphasize students’ cultural competence, critical consciousness, and academic agency (Ladson-Billings, 2021). While CRP advocates for drawing on students’ cultural practices for lea rning, proof is often taught in a top-down perspective that reinforces a specific way of doing things, and attention to both results in three emerging tension s. First, while CRP advocates for the use of students’ intuitive re sources to highlight their cultural competence, pro of is often taught in a way that minimizes the use of empirical  evidence and example cases (Harel & Sowder, 2007). While teachers seek to integrate students' unique cultural practices into the learning process and dismantle the dominance of mainstream culture, they may find it difficult t o simultaneously encourage students to accept th e dominant approach of using abstract generalization require d in mathematical proofs. Second, CRP teachers enco urage students to scrutinize and challenge the prevailing  sociopolitical forces that influence our world; however, mathematical proofs are often abstract with little to no perceived relevance to students’ lived experiences (Sears, 2012). While educators who embrace CRP in their cla ssrooms direct their attention towards fostering a critical consciousness in their students, striking a balance with teaching proofs may prove c hallenging, especially when aiming to foster students' enthusiasm for mathematics while also ensuring their engagement with the authenticity of its practice. Third and finally, implementing CR P entails a focus on academic excellence by fosteri ng student agency, but concurrently havin g students adhere to the established practices of mathematical proving  is often a significant challenge for teachers to overcome (Stylianides et al., 2016). While affording students voice and choice in the classroom is important in their learning and const ruction of mathematical identities, quality in students’ proofs is often conflated with using previously established disciplinary practices. Strategies to Address tensions between CRP and teaching proof One promising strategy to address the tensions betw een CRP and the teaching of mathematical proof in h igh school involves cultivating cultural and mathematical norms in the classroom. This involves a collaborative effort between students and teachers to establish the sociomathematical norms for engaging with proof regarding “what counts,” continually being referred to, revisited, and revised as needed (Yackel & Cobb, 1996). A multitude of perspectives must be embraced during this process, where both students and teachers need to contribute  to the development of a rich mathematical culture which values both dominant and non-dominant methods to highlight students’ cultural competence. Not only does this allow students to have voice and choice in how their proofs will be evaluated, but with the aid of the teacher, these norms can incorp orate the disciplinary practices typically expected in mathematical proving. The contexts of these proofs should include both abstract and social instances, allowing them to maintain the authenticity of mathematical practice while also enabling students to develop their critical consciousness to critically evaluate the world around them. Finally, students should construct proofs based on their shared ideas, allowing the contexts in which proof is explored to remain authentic and relevant to students' interests, affording them agency in their learning experiences as they pursue academic excellence. Taken together, these instructional strategies comprise a pedagogical approach to creating an inclusive learning environm ent in which the tensions between CRP and mathematical proof practices are alleviated.

## Conclusion

In conclusion, addressing the tensions between CRP and the teaching of mathematical proof is vital for  creating equitable learning communities. Future research sho uld delve into identifying the specific requisite c ontent knowledge, pedagogical content knowledge, professio nal development, and ongoing support necessary for mathematics educators to establish these types of learning environments in their classrooms. Finally, investigating how these recommendations fare within both the traditional and integrated sequenced classes can provide insights into their adaptability and effectiveness across various educational contexts.
