# Friendly Controversy: Synergistic Scaffolding for Practices of Providing Peer Critique

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Peer critique is essential for collaborative learning of science but presents challenges for students. Drawing on distributed and synergistic scaffolding, we designed multiple supports within a CSCL modeling environment, including integrated evidence and peer-feedback mechanisms, to help 6th-grade students develop peer critique practices. Through analysis of students’ comments and engagement patterns during critique activities, we found that students demonstrated progressive uptake of peer critique practices that the scaffolds were designed to promote, evident in more balanced, specific critiques over time. These findings inform the design of distributed scaffolds for supporting peer critique in science classrooms.

## Introduction

Peer critique is an essential collaborative learning practice that mirrors scient ific work, in which scientists evaluate, discuss, and help refine each other’s ideas  (Noroozi & De Wever, 2023). This practice is driven by identifying potential weaknesses in the evidence supporting cla ims or models (Ford, 2008). It supports students’ development of critical reasoning, knowledge construction, and epistemic agency, which can lead to learning (Henderson et al., 2015; González ‐Howard & McNeill, 2020). Despite the se benefits, providing peer critique presents multiple challenges for students. Specifically, the practice involves socioemotiona l complexity, with students often hesitating to participate due to fears of damaging friendships or receiving negative feedback (Borge & Xia, 2023). It inherently demands a high level of cognitive engage ment, requiring students to process content knowledge and apply appropriate criteria to evaluat e peers’ work (Chang, 2023). Additionally, students must balance constructive content with appropriate tone to ensure their feedback  is heard and acted upon (Tasker & Herrenkohl, 2016). These challenges underscore the need for thoughtful scaffolding to support students in developing productive critique practices for science learning.

To address this need, we designed multiple scaffold s drawing on notions of distributed and synergistic scaffolding (Puntambekar, 2015; Tabak, 2004). These scaffolds aim to help students appropriate the practices that can eventually become sustained norms within their classroom communities. We implemented the study within a scientific modeling context in a 6 th-grade classroom, supported by the Modeling and Evid ence Mapping Environment (MEME; Zhou et al., 2024; https://modelingandevidence.or g), a software tool enabling students to construct, revise, and critique models while exploring scientific phenomena. This CSCL environment thus creates an authentic context for peer critique. Through analyses of students’ MEME comments and scaffold uptake during critique processes, we identified how synergistic scaffolding supported the development of critique practices.

## Theoretical framework

This study is grounded in sociocultural theories, which view learning as occurring through interaction with others while participating in social practices within a co mmunity (Danish & Ma, 2022). From this viewpoint, learning manifests as changes in patterns of participation. As students learn new practices, their interaction patterns will demonstrate noticeable changes. In this study, we a im to document the interaction patterns as students engage in peer critique practices while interacting with different kinds of support. Here a practice refers to “normative ways of acting” within a cla ssroom community  that emerge and develop through ongoing interaction s, where participants collectively negotiate and construct norms of reasoning and communicating  (Cobb et al., 2001). In peer critique practices specifically, students and the teacher work together to establish shared under standing of constructive critiques. Through iterative participation in critique activities, we expect students will develop shared ways of evaluating peers’ work and providing constr uctive critiques. While such collaborative practice s are essential for learning, supporting students’ development and appropriation of critique presents challenges. Peer critique: Challenges and the need for support Peer critique has been well accepted in disciplines such as writing, yet its implementation in science education, particularly in K-12 classrooms, remains limited (Alqassab et al., 2023). Note that we use terms “feedback” and “critique” interchangeably in this study. Drawing on literature and our several years of design-based research on scientific modeling, we identified three major challenges students face when engaging in peer critique. Challenge 1: Socioemotional complexities of collabo rative critique. Collaboration is fundamental to inquiry-based learning environments (Hmelo-Silver & Jeong, 2022), yet  implementing collaborative  critique presents unique socioemotional challenges where students must manage both collaborative processes and critique practices. Students often hesitate to disagree with others, viewing critique as “a potential source of conflict” rather than an opportunity for learning (Henderson et al.,  2015). Although students can generate productive disagreements during peer critique, they somet imes prioritize social harmony over further disagreements (Zhou et al., 2024). Research indicates that peer critique between groups enhances science conceptual understanding, yet this practice remains uncommon (Tan et al., 2023). These challenges highlight the need for building clear collaboration norms that legitimize disagreements and critiques, frame critique as a valuable learning opportunity, and support students in managing socioemotional aspects of group critique. Challenge 2: Supporting shared criteria application  in model critique. Criteria are standards  for assessing whether an epistemic aim is achieved (Chinn et al., 2014). Applying criteria is essential for performing an effective critique and can enhance content-related feedback quality (Chang, 2023).  For instance, the criterion of evidence relevance helps students determine model validity by examining how relevant the evidence is to the model (Duncan et al., 2018). However, effectively applying criteria is complicated due to students’ varying levels of content knowledge and understanding of the crite ria themselves (Deiglmayr, 2018). This points to th e importance of engaging students in developing and c ontinuously refining their own class-shared criteria, rather than receiving them from teachers and researchers, which can promote shared understanding of criteria and build trust and commitment to using these criteria as students better understand their value (Murphy et al., 2025). Challenge 3: Providing constructive critique. Students often struggle with constructing substantive feedback and developing appropriate delivery tone (Tasker & Herrenkohl, 2016). While peer critique is fundamentally driven by identifying errors or weaknesses (Ford, 2 008), constructive critique requires multiple elements to be helpful for receivers. First, critique should go beyond problem identification to include actionable suggestions (Patchan & Schunn, 2015), which contrib ute to the quality of peers’ final work (Tan et al., 2023). Second, balancing positive and negative aspects is crucial, as it affects receivers’ agreement with the problem and their self-efficacy (Yu & Schunn, 2023). Third, specificity is essential. Comments should elaborate on what works or does not work and ideally provide justifications, rather than offering generic praise or vague crit icism (e.g. “I don’t like it”), helping receivers better understand issues (Tan et al., 202 3). These findings emphasize two key aspects of constructive critique:  maintaining content  specificity and including three structural elements: 1) acknowledging strengths of peers’ work (strengths), 2) identifying problems, areas for improvement, or questions (problems/questions), and 3) offering act ionable suggestions  (suggestions). These three elements create a balanced, specific, and action-oriented structure that informed our design of a useful framing of critique. Scaffolding and design principles Supporting students in developing peer critique practices requires careful scaffolding. Scaffolding assists students in accomplishing complex and difficult tasks before  they cando so independently  (Quintana et al., 2004). Scaffolding encompasses several key features: 1) human and symbolic mediation  – interactions with each other and tools that are designed to help structure learners’ actions in ways that help them engage in more expert activity,

2) achieving intersubjectivity – developing a shared understanding of a task’s goal, 3) ongoing diagnosis through

continuous assessment of learners’ understanding, 4) calibrated support contingent on learners’ changing needs, and 5) gradual fading as learners develop independence (Puntambekar, 2015). For helping complex practices like peer critique, scaffolds can be distributed across tools, agents (the teacher and peers), and activity structures in the learning environment  (Puntambekar, 2015). These distributed scaffolds could work synergistically, complementing and reinforcing each other to better address the same learning need (Tabak, 2004). Drawing on distributed and synergistic scaffolding approach, our design of scaffolds follows three key guidelines. First, CSCL scaffolds can promote productive  engagement with tasks and collaboration  through technology affordances, such as providing joint tasks  and shared resources. Scaffolds can afford to structure collaborative learning processes  through collaboration scripts and role taking to he lp students with challenging processes like discussion initiation, consensus building, and disa greement resolution (Hmelo-Silver & Jeong, 2022). Second, technological scaffolds can make expert thinking visible  through embedded guidance, including prompts, hints, and strategic questioning to help students appropriate scientific practices (Quintana et al., 2004). Importantly, pedagogical strategies and social support  should also be taken into consideration (Hmelo-Silver & Jeong, 2022). Following this comprehensive approach, we embed these supports across technology features, rolebased scripts, and expert guidance in both technolo gical and paper-based tools, complemented by teacher scaffolding, all integrated to help students appropriate peer critique practice s. To understand how our designed scaffolds support peer critique practices, this study addresses the following research questions (RQs): 1) How do students demonstrate uptake of peer critique practices, as evidenced in their comment structure and content?  2) What patterns of scaffold uptake characterize productive practices of peer critique? Designing scaffolds to support peer critique practices We designed a six-lesson inquiry-based unit exploring invasive species and developin g core ideas about ecosystems through scientific modeling practices. T he unit was anchored in a “New Fish” scenario inspi red by “Asian Carp,” which were locally relevant as  they began to appear as invasive species in Midwest  waterways. Students were tasked to investigate whether the New  Fish was invasive while iteratively modeling the effect of the New Fish on their ecosystem, based on supplied evidence depicting relationships between the New Fish and other species in the ecosystem. The curriculum included three key activities: a classroom norm-building activity, shared criteria development discussion, and structured gallery walks (discussed later). To support students’ engagement with the curriculum, we used MEME, a collaborative modeling tool enabling joint tasks through constructing, revising, and critiquing models to investigate scientific phenomena (see Figure 1). MEME enables students to interact with supplied evidence, their own models, and peers’ models. The integrated commenting system embeds class-shared criteria and prompts to facilitate students’ critique construction. ## Participants and instructional context

The study was conducted over a month at a rural public school in the Midwestern United States. The school serves a population where 36% of students are economically disadvantaged, 30% scored at or above the proficient level in math, and 51% achieved proficiency in reading. Among the two 6th-grade classes implementing the project, we focused our analysis on one class (n=17, aged 11-12 years) that had the full amount of time to complete the unit. The class was organized into five triads and one dyad. Their pre-interviews revealed that students had limited experiences in peer critique. The science teacher had two years of science teaching experience and 15 years in art education; the modeling practice was novel for him. The teacher facilitated all class activities including providing instructions, guiding whole-class discussions, and supporting small group work.  During each session, 2-3 researchers were present to observe and provide technical support as needed, with one researcher demonstrating MEME features to students.

The six-lesson unit (75 minutes each) was implemented as follows. On Day 1, students were introduced to MEME and modeling practice and developed collabo ration norms displayed on a poster. On Day 2, the c lass established shared criteria for good models: rea dable/organized, show all steps, use on-topic evidence, and supported by facts, up-to-date information. During Days 2-3, students worked in small groups to construct models using these criteria and initial evidence about the  New Fish ecosystem. Day 4 featu red the first gallery walk: the teacher introduced constructive critique, framed cr itique as a learning opportunity and emphasized its  role in helping others for model revision, demonstrated helpful and non-helpful critique examples to reinforce the threeelement structured comments, and connected construc tive critique to their school culture of “friendly controversy,” which was reinforced frequently. Students then spent 20 minutes critiquing peers’ models. On Day 5, students revised their models based on received critiques and new evidence. The unit concluded on Day 6 with a second gallery walk, final revisions, and development of a whole-class consensus model. Data sources and analyses For this study, we analyzed MEME models and comments, worksheets, screen recordings of small group activities (capturing verbal discourse and MEME interactions),  and video recordings of two groups, drawn from a larger dataset that included pre-and post-tests and interviews, and other classroom recordings. To address RQ1 abou t uptake of critique practices, we examined both structure and content of MEME comm ents made during gallery walks. Drawing from literature on useful framing of peer critique (as discussed in the theoretical framework), we coded the three components: strength, problem/quest ion, and suggestion, using a binary system: present  (1) or absent (0). Each comment received a score indicatin g how many of these elements were present, regardless of whether they were present more than once (0-3). For comment content analysis, we examined stude nts’ criterion selection in MEME (readable/organized, show all steps, use on-topic evidence, and supported by facts). Although MEME required  students to select one criterion per comment, we noticed that students sometimes addressed multiple criteria within a single comment. We therefore conducted additional analysis to identify other pre sent aspects beyond the selected one, assigning one point for each criteria addressed. We then tallied the frequency of all criteria across comments, both selected and ide ntified. Through repeated content analysis of the data, we also examined how comments evolved in terms of specificity and complexity across different criteria. Two researchers independently coded all comments and discussed discrepancies until reaching 100% agreement.

To address RQ2 about scaffold uptake  patterns and student development of critique practi ces, we conducted interaction analysis (IA; Jordan & Henderson, 1995) using screen recordings from all small groups and video recordings from one focal group. We analyzed how students used the  four designed scaffolds: the MEME commenting system, the criteria table  worksheet, the comment structure guide, and designed roles. The analysis involved five steps: 1) transcription of recordings  (about 220 mins), 2) segmentation of transcripts i nto critique episodes, 3) coding of scaffold uptake as apply (scaffold was used and discussed), copy/repeat (scaffold was used without discussion), ignore (scaffold was not used at all or explicitly rejecte d) (Vande Pol et al., 2019), 4) interaction analysis to identify patterns of scaffo ld use across groups, and 5) iterative review of screen and video recordings to ensure accurate coding and interpretation. We defined an episode as a critique process with a MEME model, from opening and reviewing it, to writing a comment, and exiting the model. Later return to a previously reviewed model for worksheet completion w as merged with the original episode, while brief model  interaction without discussion were not counted as episodes. Ea ch scaffold was coded once per episode, regardless of how many times it was referenced. We vetted our analyses through  three structured IA data sessions  with Learning Sciences Ph.D. students and ongoing whole-team conversations about the inferences while writing about them.

## Findings

RQ1: Students’ uptake of peer critique practices as evidenced in MEME comments The analysis of 31 MEME comments (Day 4: 18; Day 6: 13) reveals students’ progressive uptake of constructive critique practices through developing use of scaffolded structure. First, all groups showed progression in incorporating the three-element structure (strength-problem-suggestion) that we proposed (see Figure 2a). Even in the first gallery walk on Day 4, all groups alrea dy demonstrated initial uptake of the structure by including an average of 1.39 out of 3 possible elements in their comments. Their early uptake  predominantly focused on identifying problems or strengths in peers’ models,  with fewer suggestions (only 22.22% of comments included suggestions). For instance, students pointed out specific issues like: “It's not completely readable because of how they are overlapping and doesn't have any evidence. ” A few comments also demonstrated their uptake of supportive tone: “We see where you are coming but how does the new fish just existing make it bad ….” By Day 6, students more fully appropriated the three-element structure, achieving an average structure s core of 2.33. Notably, suggestion-containing comments increased dramatically to 82.62 %. Students  also balanced their critiques more effectively, as evidenced in comments like: “We like how much different types of information are in the model [strength] but we don't understand how  eating the algae immediately leads to them being i nvasive along with DIE pointing to invasive [problem/question]. Students consistently applied shared criteria for critique  on both days, addressing three main aspects: organization and clarity  (“readable/organized”), evidence use  (“use evidence ” & “ supported by facts ”), and completeness of steps (“show all steps”) (see Figure 2b). Model organization and clarity emerged as the dominant criterion, selected in 20 out of 31 comments. The specificity of comments evolved from Day 4 to Day 6. On Day 4, half of the  organization-focused comment s (5/10) vaguely commented on the overall structure (e.g., “very organized,” “it’s not that readable/organized”), while others demonstrated specificity by identifying  problematic areas (e.g., “ I like how the foods are listed but the healthy and  unhealthy parts are very unclear”) or provid ing reasons for their suggestions (e.g., “I think that the 2nd entity of new fish should be removed because you already have it and the same thing for other fish”). By Day 6, all 10 organization-focused comments showed specificity, with most (8/10) offering actionable suggestions for organization (“You could  try putting algae in with the food they eat”) and others explicitly identifying effective organization features (e.g., “I really like how you put before the new fish and after new fish”).

## Discussion and implications

Grounded in a sociocultural perspective that views learning as changes in participation patterns, our study reveals how students’ peer critique practices evolved through their interactions with designed scaffolds. A key finding is the emergence of balanced critique practices that demonstrate a clear developmental trajectory. Students initially focus on  identifying issues in peers’ model s, aligning with literature  that suggests peer critique is driven by identifying errors or weaknesses (Ford, 2008). However, they progress toward more balanced patterns that include both positive aspects and specific suggestions, reflecting  how normative ways of critique are negotiated and constructed within the classroom community. Previous studies primarily analyzed students’ final comments (e.g., Yu & Schunn, 2023), and our study extends this work by uncovering how students move from problem-focused critique to a more balanced approach over time. Understanding this trajectory provides important implications for teachers and designers in determining when and how to provide scaffolding. Moreover, Group 2’s progression from sequential to concurrent scaffolds use illustrates how distributed and synergistic scaffolding can facilitate increasingly sophisticated collaborative critique practices within a CSCL environment. The MEME platform made students’ evaluation processes visible to all group members, facilitating the development of intersubjectivity, the shared understanding of critique goals (Puntambekar, 2015). Through the MEME interface, models’ strengths and weaknesses became salient to the entire group, while the MEME commenting system  and integrated  criteria list and prompts guided students to apply specific criteria. The technological affordances were enhanced by physical tools–the paper-based criteria table and comment structure guide–that provided additional structure for balanced critique. What transformed this multi-modal scaffolding to truly synergistic was the social layer of support:  peer reminder s prompted attention to overlooked critique elements and ensured distributed cognition and co-regulation among group members. Teacher facilitation further strengthened this system through modeling construct ive critique practices, frequently reinforcing collaboration norms, and intentionally connecting critique activi ties to the school’s established “friendly controve rsy” norm. By explicitly bridging scientific practice s with familiar school culture, the teacher helped students navigate the socioemotional complexities of peer critique while engaging in collaborative discourse. Despite being limited to a single 6th grade classroom context, our study highlights several key strategies for designing future CSCL environments that foster constructive peer critique practices. First, future environments could include intelligent features that embody ongoing diagnosis and calibrated support ess ential to effective scaffolding (Puntambekar, 2015). For instance, systems could detect  when students offer only generic compliments (e.g., “your model is amazing”)  and prompt them to articulate str engths meaningfully. Similarly, when students focus on organization/readability of models, systems could guide them toward evaluating evidence fit and other epistemic criteria. Second, our comparison of Groups 2 and 5 reveals the need for differentiated scaffolds (Tabak, 2004). W hile Group 2 successfully leveraged synergistic scaffolding  for critique, Group 5 engaged in extensive verbal critique but did not translate this into specific written comments. This suggests that future designs could explore streamlined writing supports (e.g., structured drop-down menus) to capture key discussion points and strategies for division of la bor when managing multiple scaffolds.  Finally, since students largely ignored the designed roles–consistent with prior findings that scaffolds are often overlooked or treated as ends rather than learning tools (Ertmer & Glazewski, 2019)–embedding roles into meaningful narratives could make role functions more contextually relevant and purposeful within collaborative activities.
