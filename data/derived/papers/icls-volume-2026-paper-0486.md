# Can Paper be a Science Lab? Designing with “Low-Tech” Learning Tools in High-Tech Times

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper examines how analog media such as paper and ink can be designed as meaningful tools for science learning through Lab-on-a-Book, a portable, paper-based science kit with interactive pages. Grounded in constructionism, we argue that low-tech materials can provide accessible, modifiable, and multimodal entry points into scientific inquiry, particularly in contexts with limited access to digital technologies.

Educational innovation is often equated with high-tech digital tools, such as Artificial Intelligence (AI), Virtual Reality (VR), and other immersive platforms (Mallek et al., 2024); yet, access to these technologies remains uneven across the globe. The pandemic showed how learning opportunities remain shaped by material and infrastructural conditions, with some communities relying on low-bandwidth systems for schooling (McLean, 2025; McElrath, 2020). This digital exclusion becomes evident through social factors such as low income and limited access to internet services or hardware, especially among marginalized students (Phillips et al., 2020; Bell et al., 2022). What individuals can learn and how they learn it depend on the models and media available to them (Papert, 1980).

This work explores how low-tech materials (such as paper, ink, craft tools) can also serve as accessible “objects-to-think-with” (Papert, 1980), grounding inquiry in tangible and culturally familiar forms (Horn, 2013).

To address this, this paper examines the development of Lab-on-a-Book (Sotério & Blikstein, 2025), a portable paper-based science kit, and explores what it means to design low-tech tools in high-tech times. We chose a book format for three main reasons: (1) cultural familiarity (Horn, 2013): books are portable, personal, and easily annotated; (2) accessibility and sufficiency (Sotério & Blikstein, 2025): many middle-school experiments (e.g., pH, crystallization) do not require or have access to lab equipment (UNESCO, 2023); and (3) material affordances (Guerrettaz & Johnston, 2013): paper invites open-ended manipulation without the sense of waste. Rather than rejecting high-tech, we argue for the design of complex technologies with low-cost materials. “Low-tech” and “High-tech” High-tech in education generally refers to digital and computational tools such as AI, augmented and virtual reality, immersive platforms, and modeling software (Scully, 2024). These tools are often framed as pathways to innovation and future readiness, and they dominate both policy discourse and research agendas (U.S. Department of Education, 2016). Studies have also found that introducing technology in a limited way to previously technology-free environments can create a “low-tech” information environment  that supports and extends the learning process for those involved (Nedungadi et al., 2018). The emphasis on novelty and digital innovation can obscure the fact that learning is always mediated by the tools and contexts available to learners (Sun, 2018). Drawing on constructionism, we see the choice and design of “objects-to-think-with” not as add-ons, but as tools that directly shape how learners think and create (Papert, 1980). We rely on “low-tech” to refer to analog, tangible, and often everyday materials, such as paper, ink, yarn, wood, and craft tools. These are rarely foregrounded in contemporary discussions of innovation (Vossoughi & Bevan, 2014), yet research has shown their epistemic richness. For example, Horn (2013) argues that learning environments are more impactful when they draw on cultural forms, such as social practices that carry historical, social, or symbolic resonance, including solving puzzles or using paper-based activities. Peppler et al. (2022) argue that fiber crafts can function as “Mathlands,” embedding mathematical reasoning into familiar practices like knitting or quilting, and extend this work through the concept of materialized action, where proportional reasoning emerges from interaction with fabrics and tools. These studies emphasize that materials choices are not neutral, but shape affect, cognition, and community in learning. We explore how analog materials can inform hands-on science learning.

The case of Lab-On-A-Book 2886 Lab-on-a-Book illustrates how low-tech media may serve as constructionist learning tools. The book was designed as a microworld (Papert, 1980), where students can test hypotheses, observe outcomes, and refine their understanding directly within its pages. It includes more than printed instructions: some pages feature embedded materials such as chemical coatings or sticker channels for fluid flow, and others are intentionally left open for cutting, burning, or staining (See Figure 1). These varied formats indicate that the book is meant to be handled, altered, and tested as a working laboratory. The decision to use paper as the primary material was intentional, as paper is widely accessible and culturally familiar, lowering entry barriers through ease of use (Horn, 2013). By embedding inquiry within everyday acts such as reading, we aimed for cultural syntonicity – following Papert’s

(1980) notion that familiar media can make abstract science tangible and invite exploratory reasoning that often

resembles authentic scientific thinking more than certain school routines. Although each page is accompanied by a prompt, students do not have to follow that sequence; instead, they may choose their materials and edit the pages as they wish, preserving learner agency. Prompts are intentionally open-ended and function as invitations for students to pursue their own questions and variations.

Figure 1 Left to Right: Examples of a Prompt Page, an Embedded-Material Page, and a Hackable/Destructible Page Destructible pages, as shown in Figure 1, open up paths for free exploration and repurpose paper as a hackable material, inviting learners to cut, stain, fold, or burn to aid their conceptual understanding (Bagli & Serifoglu, 2022). For instance, on one page, students are asked to sprinkle vinegar and baking soda onto the paper to observe gas formation and acid-base reactions; on another, they are asked to singe the edge of the page to investigate combustion and material transformation; and on a third, they are asked to paint saltwater and observe the formation of crystals as it dries. Together, these activities turn the boo k into a playful laboratory where chemical change becomes visible and tactile, and where students can play with powerful ideas such as matter and reactions. These phenomena were selected for their conceptual richness, alignment with middle school science standards (National Research Council, 2012), and ability to be tested in homes or classrooms without specialized equipment. Additionally, the design foregrounds the affordances of paper, that is, its being absorptive, flexible, and disposable, as vital to scientific inquiry. Guerrettaz and Johnston’s (2013) ecological view of materials further supports this: paper is an active participant in the learning environment, influencing the learner’s interactions, discourse, and agency rather than being a passive information bearer.

## Implications and next steps

Lab-On-A-Book invites the learning sciences community to reconsider the power of “low-tech” materials in design, as they can reduce reliance on more fragile and costly scientific apparatus while being familiar to most students. While such materials have potential for engaging students in constructionist science learning, they are alsonecessary as persistent gaps in digital inclusion continue to restrict student participation (Khalid & Pedersen, 2016; Phillips et al., 2020; Bell et al., 2022). This design-based research study is in early design and testing cycles. Future research will examine how middle-school learners in under-resourced contexts in the US and abroad engage with Lab-On-A-Book and how the tool can be co-designed with educators to better support classroom use aligned with middle-school science standards.
