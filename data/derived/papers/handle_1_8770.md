# Studying the Development of Marginality in Collaboration: Methods for Examining the Interplay of Peer & Teacher Interactions

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Inquiry-based science learning environments emphasize learners’ engagement in scientific practices, yet we need to better understand how to promote equitable interactions to ensure all students benefit. Using Wenger’s (1998) concept of marginal non-participation, the present study examined patterns of interaction across teacher-and student-led phases of instruction for a focal group in which marginality was previously observed. Findings indicated reliance on ignoring off-task commentary across both phases of instruction, facilitating marginality for two group members.

## Introduction

When power is redistributed from teachers to students in inquiry-based science classrooms, peers shape each other’s participation in scientific practices and thus may play a role in mediating their teammates’ ability to identify with the discipline.  Further, as teachers retain the authoritative role of orchestrator and convey motivational messages, teacher discourse and instructional practices likely inform equity in peer interactions (e.g. Cohen & Lotan, 1995; Forman et al., 2017; Modrek & Sandoval, 2020). Yet, after accounting for the benefits of authentic inquiry, reform-oriented curricula can intersect with the question of equity in a potentially adverse fashion: extant research indicates small group learning can involve inequitable opportunities to learn and develop discipline-linked identities (e.g. Carlone et al., 2011). To address this problem, the current study employed microgenetic methods to examine the development of marginality inside collaborative groups in middle school inquiry-based science, with a focus on the interplay of teacher-student and peer interaction across teacher-led and student-led phases of instruction.

## Theoretical Framework

Marginality is an adaptation of Wenger’s (1998) concept of marginal non-participation and describes movement away from deepened, fuller participation in a community and its defining activities. From this perspective, learning has four facets: belonging in a community, engaging in that community’s practices, making meaning of one’s experiences with the community and its practices, and the process of becoming a member in said community of practice. Importantly, Wenger’s social theory of learning aligns well with science education reforms’ reconceptualization of proficiency in science, as proficiency now involves socialization into the role of a scientific thinker. The new indicators of proficiency include making sense of scientific explanations, producing and evaluating scientific evidence and explanations, understanding how scientific knowledge develops, and engaging in scientific practice and using scientific discourse. Inquiry contexts inform learners’ opportunities to be recognized as a member of community of competent thinkers in the science classroom, opportunities to practice norms of the discipline, opportunities to experience meaning-making about scientific concepts, and opportunities to become a scientific thinker or pursue a career in science.

## Methods

An intensive qualitative case study was conducted to clarify the role of teachers in tandem with peer interactions for constructing marginality. We used videorecorded observations and end-of-semester student interviews previously collected in a larger study: participants were selected from the classrooms of four seventh-grade science teachers in an ethnically-diverse, suburban middle school in the United States and all classrooms were participating in a series of inquiry science units over the course of a semester. Students used evidence to build, revise, and evaluate scientific models; they also engaged in both oral and written argumentation as part of the process. While features of the curriculum should support a shift of authority to students and offer increased opportunities for learners to engage in discussion and peer interactions, previous findings also suggest students peer interactions can be oriented toward constraining participation by groupmates, thus raising questions about the nature of exclusion in collaborative groups and inquiry science classrooms. The present study expanded analyses of a single group for whom group-level marginality-relevant interactions during student-led group ICLS2022 Proceedings 2170 © ISLS activity had already been coded and presence of marginality for at least one group member had already been established. We used a purposeful operational construct sampling strategy (Patton, 2015) to choose a group and class date, with the selected group having previously been identified as having episodes of marginality for more than one group member and the student-led period being of an uninterrupted and extended duration. We selected videorecordings of teacher-led instruction for the previously coded class period’s group activity. This included ~18 minutes of student-led activity and ~25 minutes of teacher-led whole-class activity. Semi-structured interviews with students in the selected group were reviewed to support interpretation of teachers’ role in constructing marginality. Analyses of all data sources were triangulated.

## Results & Discussion

The primary task the teacher assigned the class for the observed period was to discuss and evaluate the first in a series of pieces of evidence intended to support competing models of cell nuclei. The first ten minutes of the class period involved the teacher giving directions to th e class and checking in with groups around the clas sroom, stopping multiple times to ask different students outside the focal group to sit still. Prior to the groupwork phase of the period, the teacher offers positive evaluations and comments emphasizing speed of response to two of the four focal group members (e.g. “You’re fast, you’re amazing”; “Ooh, you two are really fast!”; “Your hand went up really quick, how would you rate Evidence A?”; calls on each of the two students to answer questions; evaluates the two students’ responses as correct). The teacher also asked one focal group member to return to his seat in one instance and ignored his raised hand in another instance, only for him to blurt out nominally on-task comments in the whole class discussion. Upon transitioning to the groupwork phase, the dynamic of two group members being on-task and two mostly being only nominally on-task continued. Three patterns were identified across the entire class period. First, the group as a whole and the teacher alike struggled with one student’s frequent nominally ontask comments, as these typically diverted the teacher or the whole group away from the primary task. The teacher and groupmates alike responded to this most often by ignoring the comments, but occasionally responding briefly before explicitly redirecting him back to the intended task.  Second, the tendency to ignore the nominally off-task comments eventually escalated to the group splittin g, with two on-task members avoiding interaction wi th the two off-task group members. Third, the failure to c hannel the first nominally off-task group member in to productive forms of engagement also created space for constructing the second off-task group member as marginal with respect to the peer group. In sum, within a class period, the teacher’s interaction with focal group members mirrored focal group members’ peer interactions. Th ese findings raise the question of whether group me mbers were specifically emulating the teacher’s approach to interacting with a potentially disruptive groupm ate, or drawing on other motivators.  Accordingly, additional analyses in progress will be presented. These will include more class periods for the same group and one additional group, with attention to interactions occurring earliest in the semester, norms of interaction developing over the semester, teacher and student talk reflecting motivational messages, and other instances of teacher intervention during groupwork phases.
