# A Comprehensive Strategy for Scaffolding the Development of Students’ Mathematical Proof Skills Across a Learning Progression

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
The core practices of constructing and critiquing proofs play a pivotal role in the authenticity of mathematicians' discipline, yet engaging high school students in these practices is notoriously challenging. Although research suggests various scaffolds to aid students in developing their mathematical proof skills, a unified approach for implementing all of them often eludes teachers in the classroom. This paper proposes a comprehensive strategy for developing students’ mathematical proof skills by integrating effective pedagogical approaches with a scaffolded trajectory. By strategically implementing hard and soft scaffolds across a learning progression, teachers can help bridge the gap between informal reasoning and formal mathematical proofs in pursuit of equitable outcomes for all students.

## Introduction

Mathematical proofs are foundational to the practice of mathematicians, being key products generated within their discipline (Davis & Hersh, 1998). For this reason, mathematics students are expected to engage in this practice to help them acquire the skill s needed for logical thinking  and persuasive communication in mathematics (NCTM, 2009). Nevertheless, there exist several obstacles to proof instruction in the realm of mathematics as teachers often struggle to scaffold appropriate learning progressi ons (Cai & Cirillo, 2014). While mathematics textbooks often include scaffolds to help students develop pr oficiency with proofs (Herbst, 2002), they are typi cally not sufficient on their own when it comes to progressin g students’ formalism once they are removed by the teacher (Nirode, 2018). There has also been a myriad of different scaffolds suggested by m athematics education researchers, but they often  lack synergy and thus usefulness in isolation (Angh ileri, 2006). These challenges highlight the need for a comprehensive strategy whe n teaching mathematical proof in the classroom to p romote equitable outcomes for all students  to be successful. In response, this paper proposes a n approach which utilizes effective scaffolding strategies and a learning progression for students engaging in mathematical proof. Scaffolding proof This paper defines a mathematical proof as a cohere nt and logical argument consisting of a series of consecut ive statements progressing from established definitions  to some conclusion, employing the modes of reasoning and representation acknowledged by experts in the disci pline (Movshovitz-Hadar, 2001). Students tend to struggle with constructing generalized proofs, unfortunately, as they tend to rely on the  use of empirical evidence and example cases (Harel & Sowder, 2007). While individual scaffolds have been suggested to as sist students in developing more abstract proofs, they often lack a unifying framework  and are thus insufficient by themselves (Nirode & Boyd, 2021).  In bringing this research together, one promising s trategy for teaching proof involves a scaffolded progression which gradually enhances the  formality of students’ proof techniques. In the fo llowing sections, we suggest the learning progression, hard scaffolds, and soft scaffolds that expand on this approach. Learning progression This learning progression entails guiding students from exploratory discussions to the structured prov ing of mathematical theorems over the course of a full sch ool year, with multiple opportunities to present, d efend, critique, and reflect on their own work, as well as the work of their peers. As studen ts advance through this trajectory, the complexity of their writing should increase with each phase of the progression. At the end of the year, students should engage in a project that allo ws them to delve into a theorem of personal interes t, crafting and defending their own proof before their peers to  showcase their mastery while being instilled with a sense of agency. In combination with scaffolds expertly tailored to their evolving needs  (Dove & Hollenbrands, 2014), students will develop their proficiencies with math ematical proofs as these scaffolds are gradually fa ded and removed over time by the teacher. We will first dis cuss hard sca ffolds, which are  static supports planned in advance, before moving on to soft scaffolds, which are dynamic supports arising in the moment. Hard scaffolds In the process of developing students’ proficiencie s with proof, we propose hard scaffolds in the form  of four phases that students should progress through once they are ready to move on. (1) In the informal reasoning phase, students should be asked to articulate their problem-solving approach, providing access to leverage their everyday ways of knowing. (2) In the persuasive argumentation phase, a structured fr amework should be utilized for students to fill in its missing components before progressing to the incorporation of transitional statements. (3) In the puzzle pieces phase, students should be provide d with statements and reasons using mathematical no tation (Cirillo & Herbst, 2012) and connect them using arrows to indicate proof flow before having to handle extraneous and missing pieces. (4) Finally, in the translational phase, students  should convert between different representations of proof (e.g., flow chart or two-column) before being expected to generate proofs from scratch. Soft scaffolds Throughout the year, teachers should also be provid ing soft scaffolds to assist students with engaging  in these proof practices (Saye & Brush, 2002). First, teachers should encourage students to take different approaches when they get “stuck” – whether by having them start from the beginning or end of a proof and working the other way when progress fails using one trajectory. Additionally, teachers must be well-prepared to extend support to struggling learners by offering helpful hints and p roviding supplementary transitional phrases as needed, as well as modeling similar problems with direct instructio n. Employing skillful questioning techniques is also crucial, and teachers should use probing questions to elucid ate how struggling students can progress  by having them explain the problem they are working on. Finally, r eorienting students’ perceptions of the given scena rio to something they are more familiar with can also help simplify the context to be more feasible to work with.

## Conclusion

In conclusion, implementing scaffolds and a learnin g trajectory is imperative in creating equitable le arning communities where all students are successful with proofs. These recommendations may necessitate professional development for teachers to ensure they are well-equipped to effectively integrate the suggested practices. Schools should also contemplate adopting a common language and nomenclature surrounding argumentation frameworks to facilitate smoother transitions between academic  subjects. Finally, future research is needed to evaluate and validate this approach through extensive piloting and implementation of the progression and its scaffolds.
