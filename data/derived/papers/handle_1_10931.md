# How Research Practice Partnerships Build Comfort and Strengthen Collaborations as Research Agendas Shift

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Setting a research agenda that is mutually beneficial for teachers and researchers is a challenging part of a research practice partnership. Our paper examines how our engagement shifted as our research agenda changed. Our findings show increased teacher contributions as our research agenda changed. We discuss how our collaboration shifted as the trajectory of our research agenda moved out of the researchers' hands and more into the teachers' hands.

## Introduction and background

A challenge of research practice partnerships (RPPs) is helping partners develop a mutually beneficial  research agenda that all partners are interested in and comm itted to pursuing. In some cases, teams set goals through brainstorming activities or collect data to identif y a problem of practice (Wentworth et al., 2022). W hile identifying an initial shared problem or practice i s critical, research agendas also evolve over time as collaborations uncover ne w problems, implement ideas, and learn together (Fa rrell et al., 2021). The process of setting a research agenda requires collaboration ac ross all who are participating. Collaborative design processes that develop mutuality across the partnership can increase teacher and researcher learning and designs that inform practice (Goldman et al., 2022). Collaborative design can he lp create mutually beneficial research agendas in RPPs, but we need to further understand how and und er what conditions effective collaboration happens as our research agenda changes. In this paper we examine o ur researcher-teachers collaboration over time in relation to our design goals to understand how our collaboration has shifted.

## Methods

Our project is a design-based research project set out to develop theory-building lessons for middle school classrooms (Swanson et al., 2023). We collaborate w ith middle school teachers in a rural community in the Intermountain West. In this paper, we focus on two science teachers, Ms. K and Ms. M. Over the course of three years, Ms. K and Ms. M iteratively designed and implemented theory simulations and curriculum building in their classrooms. Our goal of Year 1 was for each teacher to design lessons and r elated NetTango model (Horn et al., 2020). Over four-weeks, each teacher iteratively developed two lesso ns and a computational model, however, lessons were not implemented due to the COVID-19 pandemic. In Year 2 we iterated on the lessons so Ms. K and Ms. M could implement in their classrooms. We held four weekly, two-hour co-design workshops which were held via Zoom with one member of the research team.  Both teachers implemented their lessons the follow ing school year. Our goal in Year 3  was to iterate on the lessons after the implementat ion. During this year we collaborated with Utah State’s ASPIRE center, who p rovided funding and support to our teachers. The pr oject shifted focus and we worked as a team to consider what we learned from the implementation and aligned lessons with the concepts at the ASPIRE center. We held in-person co-design workshops for two-hours, every week for six weeks with three members of the research team. Both teachers implemented their lessons the following school year. All co-design workshops were video recorded and transcribed for analysis. We calculated the proportion of each person’s talk over the three years, including Ms. K, Ms. M, and the researcher who was leading the co-design work, to show how teachers’ and researchers’ contri butions change. We acknowledge that turns of talk d o not describe the contributions they made; rather we wer e interested if there were changes in their interac tions over time. We then content logged to identify the goals of each year, and constructed narratives to look at  how the goals, turns of talk, and collaboration changed over time.

## Findings

Over the three years we saw that Ms. M's turns of talk increase in proportion to Ms. K's and the lead researchers, while Ms. K’s remained consistent (Figure 1). Ms. K  asked questions, gave feedback, and thought throug h her ideas out loud. Ms. M also shared  ideas and gave feedback but preferred to think on i deas before and after the design workshops. The lead researcher contributed less each year, with Ms. M contribut ing more in Year 3. In Year 1, the research collaboration was brand new. While t he research agenda was discussed, the teachers ofte n requested direct instruction from the researchers a bout what they should design. Both Ms. K and Ms. M felt comfortable with the lesson creation, however, the simulations felt more challenging, requiring more a ssistance from the researcher. Both teachers, but especially Ms. K, communicated feeling overwhelmed and lacking capacity. Ms. M specifically had a shift in her thi nking toward the end of the month and explained the  need to slow down and consider how students were learning and better ways to interact instead of focusing so heavily on curriculum administration. This contribution pointe d the team to focusing more explicitly on getting s tudents to engage in discussion and give meaningful explanations for them to build on student ideas. In Year 2, this became the focus. The team set the goal to dig into how to  elicit ideas from students as they interacted with  the activities and simulations. We also worked on the theory-building practices of getting explanations from stu dents and revising them. This was new to both teachers and pushed them out of their comfort zones. However, both teachers voiced the value of having students share and itera te on their ideas through theory building. In year two, the teachers, as collaborati on partners, began to move away from looking for in structions from the researcher. Between Year 2 and 3, both teachers implemented the ir lessons and simulations. The implementation help ed the teachers and researchers revise their collective go al. In Year 3, the teachers shared challenges in the implementation, most notably was their difficulty getting students to share their ideas. The team decided to iterate on the lessons and create strategies to help elicit  and respond to students' ideas. In this year, w e saw a transition where teachers shared their curriculum and classroom needs weighted equally with the desires of researchers. ## Discussion and conclusion

Over the three years we saw a trajectory of researc h agenda moving out of the researchers' hands and m ore into the teachers' hands. As the teachers became more comfortable sharing their needs and advocating for themselves, we collectively made their needs the goals for the group work. In t he beginning of the partnership the teachers were not used to working with the researchers and were looking for the researchers to instruct them on what to do as they were unsure of their role in the research process (Farrell et al., 2021). This took time to build comfort and capacity for the teachers to want to work as a team. While the teachers contributed to the goal development in the first year, they were much more vocal with their needs in goal development in years two and three (Wentworth et al., 2022). Our future work will further unpack our  shifts in research agenda over time and what eleme nts and aspects of the design process and goal development supported our collaboration.
