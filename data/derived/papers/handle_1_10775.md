# Between Dead-ends and Meatballs: Mathematical Practice as Embodied and Interactionally Accomplished

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper explores the social collaborative nature of mathematical work through a critical examination of the interactions between two professional mathematicians engaged in an afternoon of reading, writing, and mathematical problem solving. Drawing on Goodwin’s (2003) conceptualization of the semiotic body in its environment, I offer an account of mathematics as an embodied practice that is accomplished through social interaction, the recruitment of tools, the creation of artifacts, and a coordinated assemblage of these tools, artifacts, and actors. Focusing on excerpts of interactions and events from an afternoon spent with mathematicians Natalie and Usman, the analysis presented here seeks to unsettle normative understandings of mathematics as a non-material, abstract practice, instead making a third-world feminist case for the embodied, concrete, social-relational nature of mathematical knowledge production.

## Introduction

Traditional understandings of mathematics have marked it as an asocial, intangible, and abstract practice. Recent research in the learning sciences and in mathematic s education have offered departures from this view,  arguing instead that mathematics is a social-cultural practice that draws on the material world and in turn transforms it (De Freitas & Sinclair, 2014; Gholson & Martin, 201 9). The larger study this paper is a part of seeks to m ake further contributions to this literature, specifica lly with the goal of articulating the features of p rofessional mathematical practice in the 21 st century North American university.  Premised on the idea that learning mathematics occurs through shifts in participation in a community of practice (Lave & Wenger, 1991), this paper is interested in illuminating the material conditions, social relationships, and embodied practices out of which mathematical knowledge emerges. To do so, it offers a third-world feminist analysis of an afternoon of collaboration between two mathematicians around a k itchen table as they read, write, and make sense of mathematical ideas.

## Theoretical framework

This paper draws on postcolonial feminist perspectives to make sense of how mathematical work unfolds, with the specific goal of understanding knowledge production in mathematics as a consequence of social-interactional, embodied, and emplaced negotiations. I use Ahmed’s (2006) contention that work and labor—and in this case the sociocultural practice of mathematics—are shaped by the bodies engaged in doing that work. Through Ahmed, a question that frames much of this research is the following: how does the body arrive at the metaphorical table to do mathematical work?  For Ahmed (2006), “phenomenology helps us to explor e how bodies are shaped by histories, which they perform in their comportment,  their posture, and their gestures” (p. 56). Attending to the body in practice, then, makes it possible to surfac e the “sedimented histories” (p. 56) through which the body comes to perform various kinds of work. That history comes to be held in some ways in bodily posture further opens up an empirical methodology through which to understand how, for example, the professional practices within a historical intellectual discipline (such as mathematics) come to be reproduced and transformed over time and through the shifting participation of community members.

Analytical framework For my analytical framework, I draw on Charles Good win’s work on the semiotic body in its environment. Goodwin (2003) characterized certain kinds of situa ted complexes consisting of action, gesture, and ta lk as “symbiotic gestures” (p. 20), suggesting an interwo ven system of distinct elements or parts that opera te through mutual interdependence. For example, in the episode under examination here, there are various moments during which Natalie or Usman use a pen to point to someth ing on a piece of paper, referring to it as “this” in their talk. To make sense of such a scene would require attending not only to the speaker or the gesture itself, but to the way in which aspects of the environment (such as the pa per being pointed at or the pen with which the poin ting is accomplished) are incorporated into the action and speech. In other words, a stray remark like “because this is true” does not carry any significance in isolation;  instead, the environment in which it unfolds offer s important context for the interpretation of the comment itsel f. Conversely, the drawings and inscriptions on the  page that are being gestured at and the pen used for gesturin g do not carry any specific significance in isolati on. Whereas the pen may be seen as primarily serving the purpos es of writing, how it is put to use within a context  is what determines its incorporation as a tool and can only be understood in terms of the talk that accompanies the gesture and the object (the writing on the page) that is be ing pointed to with the pen. Similarly, the drawing  and inscriptions themselves could be seen to represent many different things, b ut what they mean for Natalie and Usman as they work through a question or idea is on ly made apparent by looking at the complex (of the pen, the accompanying talk, the gesture of pointing) as a whole. In Goodwin’s words, “symbiotic gestures are built through the conjunction of quite different kinds of entity instantiated in diverse media: first, talk; second,  gesture; and third, material and graphic structure in the environment” (p. 23). Symbiotic gestures are a useful lens to make sense of the episode analyzed in this chapter for a few reasons. For one, they can help illuminate the ways  in which mathematics is a material practice, one i n which mathematicians use a variety of tools in conj unction with talk to develop a shared understanding  of a concept or theory. Natalie and Usman used pen and paper to draw various pictures as they tried to make sense of the articles they were reading. We find in our analysis  is that these images, which were produced with the hel p of tools and served as a way to “translate” the text in the arti cles into images, provided specific examples of cas es while also “getting in the way” of their understanding of the most general instantiation of a concept. At one poi nt, when Natalie was using a picture Usman had made to make a claim, Usman responded with “that’s just somethin g I drew.” Considering symbiotic gestures offers us one  way to think about the drawing as crucial to the d eveloping understanding that Natalie and Usman are coming to share, both in terms of being something that helps their understanding and in instances where it comes to be dismissed by Usman as “just” something he had drawn. It is not possible to say if this is an intentional demonstration, one made for the benefit of the other person, but in picking up a pen, leaning into the space they are mutually oriented towards, and eventually pulling back from it, Natalie and Usman help us see how mathematical know ledge production is a concrete, material, interactional process that takes place within an interconnected web of actions, gestures, and utterances. At a later point in the episode, Natalie got up to bring everyone—including  Natalie’s husband, who was working from home sitting on the couch, and me —a drink. Natalie had started to talk to me about the soda sh e was offering me, and about how it was “healthier” t han others. As this conversation between me and Nat alie played out in the background,  Usman was staring intently at the screen of the laptop, c ontinuing to read and interpret the paper on the screen  while furiously spinning the fidget spinner in his right hand. And then, almost abruptly, Usman got up from the table and followed Natalie into the kitchen to check on the mea tball sauce. The shift from what appeared to be more explicitly math ematical work to everyday activities (such as cooki ng and bringing everyone a drink) also speaks to the ways in which Natalie and Usman arrive at mathematical labor. The simultaneous doing of mathematical and more social labor—indeed of housework —signifies the everyday character of mathematical practice. The body here is engaged in multiple things at once, and what might get relegated to the background in more traditional tel lings of mathematical knowledge production (such as  the household labor of cooking and caring for each other) is instead foregrounded as part of mathematical activity. It was these seemingly non-mathematical engagements that were able to facilita te the more visibly mathematical labors that Natalie and Usman were engaged in.

## Significance

Together, these early findings point to two important perspectives on mathematical  practice. The first is its embodied and social-relational character, where we are able to see that  mathematical ideas and theory and knowledge are produced—not as traditional research might have you believe in the abstract and  from the depths of mathematicians’ minds—but instead they arise out of relationships, embodied practices, and the recruitment of tools. Secondly, these findings also start to t show the ways in which mathematical life is forged at the confluence of various mathematical and non-mathematical labors.
