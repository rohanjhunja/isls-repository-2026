# Scaling Interleaved Practice: Preliminary Evidence from a Systematic Replication

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Do the positive learning effects spaced interleaving (i.e., mixing problems that require a different solution strategy within an assignment) remain in authentic middle school mathematics classrooms? Our replication study seeks to expand our understanding of interleaving’s potential impact with a larger sample, more diverse educational settings, more realistic time period, and multimodal intervention delivery. We conducted a cluster randomized trial with our sample representing 10 teachers from 6 schools across 3 districts (n = 239 students). Preliminary results from our first cohort (out of three planned) show effects in the predicted direction, with interleaved students performing higher than control students, but the difference is not statistically significant. Though our early-stage study lacks sufficient power to detect small effects, we describe our process of scaling the intervention for this study and contribute to a growing body of research that seeks to understand how to optimally scale learning principles.

Efficacy of interleaved practice Spaced interleaving (or interleaving, for brevity) combines two highly effective learning strategies – discrimination learning and spaced practice – by mixing problems of different types within the sa me assignment (e.g., Pythagorean theorem problems, slope problems, etc.) and spacing problems of the same type acros s assignments over time (e.g., distributing Pythagore an theorem problems over the course of multiple wee ks, months, etc.). Despite a mounting body of evidence demonstrating the benefits of interleaved practice (see Rohrer & Hartwig, 2023 for review), few studies have tested this principle outside of small-scale laboratory settings. One recent randomized controlled trial showed promising  evidence for the impact of interleaving on student s’ math performance in middle school classrooms (Rohrer, De drick, Hartwig, & Cheung, 2020). However, the research sample was relatively homogeneous (all classrooms w ere from one district), potentially limiting the generalizability of the findings.

To test the effects of  interleaved practice in more authentic settings, we  conducted a systematic replication designed both to contrast with and build upon Rohrer et al.’s (2020) original efficacy study.  To expand our understanding of interleaving’s impact we scale  the intervention across the following elements: sample, setting, timescale, and intervention delivery. The sample and setting of Rohrer et al. (2020) were limited to five schools within one district in the Southern U.S and participation was limited to grade 7 students who demonstrated proficiency on the state standardized test. Our replication study will include  schools across multiple districts in the U.S. and will include grade 6 and 7 students with varying math achievement. The timescale of Rohrer et al.’s

(2020) original study consisted of three months of the sch ool year. Our replication study spans the entire sc hool

year. Finally, the intervention delivery was done through paper and pencil in Rohrer et al.’s (2020) original study but is administered through an online platform called ASSISTments in our replication. Our research question asks to what extent does interleaved practice improve student performance on a 4-week delayed researcher-developed mathematical assessment? We use the same type of assessment and period of delay tested in Rohrer et al.’s (2020) study. In this paper, we report on the first part of our study (of three total cohorts). Thus, although we are underpowered to detect effects, we report our methods, preliminary findings, and discuss challenges involved in scaling learning principles in education.

## Methods

Like Rohrer et al. (2020), the study design is a bl ocked, randomized controlled trial, with random ass ignment occurring at the classroom-level. We used a random process that assigned classes, within the teacher, to conditions entirely by chance and every class had an equal chance of being assigned to the treatment versus control condition. We measured impact on student mathematics achievemen t on a proximal, research-developed assessment four weeks after the final intervention materials were delivered.

The study took place in sixth grade classrooms acro ss the United States. The analytic sample contains data from 13 classes (7 interleaved, 6 blocked) rep resenting 10 teachers from 6 schools in 3 districts  (note that this sample represents one cohort of our three plan ned cohorts as the study is ongoing). All 239 stude nts (126 interleaved, 113 blocked) with complete data from n on-attriting classes are included in the analytic samp le. Our study team is currently in the process of collecting demographic data from the participating districts. Students completed 10 review assignments that consi sted of four skills from the common core state standards for 6th grade math: Fraction Division, Decimal Subtraction, Rate Problems, and Finding Per centages. Each worksheet consisted of 6 items. These skills were presented to students as either interleaved or blocked. The pre-assessment consisted of 12 items that covered the 5 th-grade prerequisites which aligned to the 6th-grade math standards included in the review assignments. The assessment had adequate reliability, Cronbach’s alpha =.67. The post-assessment consisted of 12 isomorphic items to those designed for the review assignments. The 12 items consisted of 3 Fraction Division items, 3 Decimal Subtraction items, 3 Rate Problems item s, and 3 Finding Percentages items. The assessment was delivered via an online platform four weeks after the 10th review assignment was assigned and had a Cronbach's alpha of.84.

## Results

We used a two-level hierarchical linear model to account for the nested structure of the design: students within classes, and classes blocked within teachers (Raude nbush & Bryk, 2002). This analysis allowed us to de termine the interleaved treatment’s average impact on the student achievement (as measured by our researcher developed post-assessment) while accounting for the cluster assign ment (between classrooms). Fixed effects covariates include: (at the first level) a measure of prior mathematics achievement (as measured by the researcher-developed pre-assessment), and (at the second level) dummy coded variables for the teacher blocks. We used this mode l to address our research question: Does interleaved practice improve student performan ce on a 4-week delayed researcher-developed mathematical post-assessment?  On average, students in the interleaved (M = 8.40, SD = 3.18) condition scored higher on the 4-week delayed post-assessment than those in blocked (M = 7.77, SD = 3.27) condition, but this difference was not statisticall y significant post-assessment scores, F(1, 9.94) =.61, p =.45, Hedges’ g =.11.

## Discussion

The present study is an on-going replication study to test the efficacy of int erleaved practice in middle school mathematics education. The initial findings from ou r first cohort of participants show effects in the predicted direction, with students who received interleaved practice performing higher, on average, than those who received blocked practice, but the difference is not statist ically significant. The effect size difference is.72, which is predictably smaller than the effect observed by Rohrer et al. (g =.83), as effect sizes tend to be smaller in larger, more heterogeneous education research samples (Cheung & Slavin, 2016). At this point in the project, our sample lacks the statistical power to detect small effects. We are collecting two more cohorts of data, aiming for a total sample of 189 classrooms that are representative of U.S. middle schools.  If, after the full sample is collected, we do not find statistically significant positive effe cts, it will call for a deeper examination of the f actors that were systematically varied from the original efficacy study and influence scaling underspecified learning principles. As part of this replication, several key variables were also s ystematically varied between the original efficacy study and the present study. Notably, the present replication systematically varied the setti ng (national sample compared to single high-performing district), timescale (an entire school year compared to three months), and intervention delivery (online platform compared  to paper and pencil). While there is no theoretica l reason to believe the systematic variations would influence h ow well the key components  of the interleaving intervention (i.e., discrimination and spacing) operate, introducing more noise to the study may obfuscate impacts as a function of barriers and challenges to implementation. For e xample, delivering the intervention on an online pl atform led to implementation challenges because of restricted technology access on school devices, lack of teache r access for reliable IT support, and usability issues reported by the teachers. These are not implementation challenges that have been reported in smaller scale lab studies studying the impact of interleaving. Though this replication study of interleaved practi ce is still ongoing, the initial findings contribute to a growing body of research that seeks to understand the boundary conditions under which scaling learning principles is most effective. Continuing this systematic repli cation study through additional cohorts will be ins trumental in elucidating the subtl eties of effectively implementing interleaved practice at scale. Not all learning principles demonstrated at a smaller scale, or in a homogenous  sample, will produce immediate or detectable resul ts at a larger scale in a more diverse sample and setting. Testing learning principles like interleaved practice  at scale in classroom contexts has the potential to inform classroom practice as well as drive the underlying learning theories.
