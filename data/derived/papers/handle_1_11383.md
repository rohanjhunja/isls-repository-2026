# K-12 AI Education: Exploring Gender Differences in Achievement Motivation, Self-Efficacy, and Performance

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study investigates how seven achievement motivation constructs derived from Eccles’ EVT relate to self-efficacy and performance among 8th-9th grade female and male students in an AI curriculum. Both groups showed significant, medium-sized gains in self-efficacy and performance. However, gender-specific patterns emerged: female students’ performance was weakly but significantly correlated with EVT constructs, while male students’ self-efficacy showed weak-to-medium correlations. Hierarchical regression revealed that self-efficacy could be predicted for males but not for females, despite known links between self-efficacy, performance, and motivation. These findings call for further investigation, especially given the role of self-efficacy in shaping disciplinary identity, career choice, and the persistent gender gap in AI fields.

## Introduction

Globally, women represent only 22% of AI professionals, with representation declining further in leadership roles (Randstad, 2024). Further, female students comprise 30.5% of AP computer science (CS) test-takers in U.S. high schools, but only 7% of 15-year-old females aim for a STEM career after high school compared to 26% of males (Maslej et al., 2024; U.S. Department of Education,  2020). Educational research has examined this gend er gap through the lens of achievement motivation, noting that while elementary students show equal motivation to learn CS regardless of gender, differences in motivationa l beliefs emerge in middle school and widen through  high school (Master et al., 2023; Wang & Degol, 2013; Dw eck, 2002). Eccles’ expectancy-value theory  (EVT) provides a comprehensive framework for understandin g these phenomena, emphasizing the interplay of int rinsic factors (such as task values and self-efficacy) and external influences (such as socio-cultural context) on achievement-related choices (Eccles & Wigfield, 2002, 2023; see Wille et al., 2020). To address the gap in middle and high school AI education, this study examines seven achievement motivation constructs derived from EVT— enjoyment, interest in developing AI, interest in n atural language generation, attainment, utility, learning experience, and prior knowledge —and their relationships with task-specific self-efficacy development and performance changes. Specifically, we investigate w hether there are within-and between-group differences in achievement motivation, self-efficacy, and performance; how these constructs int errelate within each group; and whether achievement motivation predicts changes in self-efficacy and performance.

## Theoretical framework

Achievement motivation and its relationship to lear ning outcomes have been extensively studied for dec ades (Eccles & Wigfield, 2002; 2023). According to EVT, individuals’ achievement-related behaviors are shaped by beliefs about the ability to succeed in a  task (expectancy) and perceptions of the task’s val ue (subjective task value), alongside individual characteristics, and s ocio-cultural factors. In this study, we adopt a modifie d EVT model that replaces traditional expectancy beliefs with Bandura’s self-efficacy (Bandura, 1997) (and excludes cost) to investigate how these factors, along with gender, predict changes in test scores and task-specific selfefficacy within an AI curriculum. By examining these interrelationships through a gender lens, our research builds on prior findings in math education (Eccles & Wigfi eld, 2023) and emerging work in AI education (Lin e t al., 2021; Kim & Kwon, 2024), contributing to a deeper understanding of gender-specific motivational patterns.

## Participants and context

195 students (55.4% female, 44.6% male; 75.4% 8th g rader, 24.6% 9th grader) in 14 classes from three m idWestern U.S. schools participated in the study. In 2023, teachers who received 4 –6 hours of professional development implemented the curriculum in English Literature classes (n = 74) at two schools and CS classes (n = 121) at one school. The sample was predominantly White (63.0%), followed by Black/African American (19.6%), mixed race (6.7%), Asian (2.6%), and Ameri can Indian/Alaska Native (1.6%). Additionally, 13.9 % indicated interest in CS-related careers. Students engaged with a web-based AI curriculum designed for grades 6–12, where they built machine learning models to classify Yelp ice cream store reviews as po sitive or negative. They used StoryQ, a text mining and narrative modeling platform tha t links tables, graphs, and text to enhance model transparency (Figure 1; Finzer et al., 2022). The five 45 –60 minute lessons guided students through exploring and developing text classification models  while also examining AI’s societal implications. ## Data collection and analysis

Data collection through the curriculum’s platform (72% completion rate) comprised two instruments: (1) a survey measuring seven EVT-based achievement motivation constructs (see Table 1) using 5-point Likert scales, and task-specific self-efficacy following Bandura’s conceptualization (fou r items assessing confidence in explaining machine le arning, text classification, developers’ roles, and  providing examples), and (2) a 16-item multiple-choice knowledge test covering mathematical concepts, training data functionality, vocabulary, machine learning limitations, and sentiment analysis. Both in struments were administered pre-and post-intervention, with certain constructs limited to either entry or exit surveys (see Table 1). We calculated achievement motivation constructs usi ng mean scores from 5-point Likert scale items, showing strong internal consistency (Cronbach’s α >.79). Intervention impact was assessed by computing change scores (Mpost-Mpre) for each construct, and the knowledge test was evaluated using point-biserial correlations for item discrimination; five items with coefficients below 0.2 were removed, resulting in a 16-item test. Our analysis proceeded in three phases: first, we conducted pair ed samples and independent t-tests on seven achievement motivation constructs, task-specific self-efficacy, and performance; second, we computed Pear son’s correlations for these constructs, analyzing male and female participants separately; and third, we performed four hierarchical multiple regression analyses to predict self-efficacy and test scores. We verified compliance wi th statistical assumptions. Shapiro-Wilk and Levene’s tests confirmed normality and hom ogeneity of variances for t-tests (all p >.05). Visual inspection of scatterplots confirmed linearity a nd homoscedasticity for correlation analyses. Regression models met all assumptions except for th e male self-efficacy model, which showed deviation from normality (W = 0.978, p =.004), necessitating robust standard errors. All variance inflation factors remained within acceptable ranges (VIF < 2.65), indicating no problematic multicollinearity.

## Results

RQ1. Within-and between-group differences To account for multiple comparisons for the nine constructs, a Bonferroni-adjusted significance level of α =.0056 (.05/9) was used for all t-tests. Both gender groups demonstrated significant improvements in self-efficacy and test scores. Task-specific self-efficacy increased significantly more for females (Mchange =.52, SD =.79, t(107) = 6.94, p <.0000, d =.66), than for males (Mchange =.27, SD =.93, t(86) = 2.65, p =.0048, d = 0.28). Female students also showed a significant increase in test scores (Mchange = 1.09, SD = 2.90, t(107) = 3.91, p <.0001, d = 0.38), as did male students (Mchange = 1.06, SD = 2.66), t(86) = 3.70, p <.0002, d = 0.34. In short, males reported higher self-efficacy overall, but performed slightly lower than  females, revealing that females express less confi dence despite performing better than their male peers. Ma les also reported significantly higher prior knowle dge (M = 2.39, SD = 0.95) than females (M = 1.94, SD = 0.83, t(193) = 3.54, p <.0000, d = 0.77). However, enjoyment only decreased for females (Mchange = -0.27, SD = 1.00, t(107) = 2.80, p =.0029). Other constructs showed minimal changes.

RQ2. Gender-specific correlational patterns As this correlation analysis was exploratory and intended to identify patterns for subsequent regression modeling, we preserved statistical power by using unadjusted p-values rather than Bonferroni corrections. Results revealed distinct gender patterns in how achievement motivation constructs related to le arning outcomes. For female students, increases in test scores showed weak but significant positive correlations with several moti vation constructs: utility (r(106) =.25, p <.01), attainment (r(106) =.20, p <.05), and self-efficacy gains (r(106) =.19, p =.05). In contrast, male students’ test score impr ovements only correlated with further interest in A I literacy (r(85) =.25, p <.05).

The pattern reversed for self-efficacy development. Male students’ self-efficacy gains showed moderate positive correlations with multiple motivation constructs: attainment (r(85) =.43, p <.001), utility (r(85) =.39, p <.001), enjoyment (r(85) =.35, p <.001), and learning experience (r(85) =.28, p <.01). For female students, self-efficacy gains only correlated with changes in enjo yment (r(106) =.21, p <.05). These analyses revealed contrasting gender patterns: female students’ test score improvements showed weak but signi ficant relationships with motivation constructs, while male students’ se lf-efficacy gains demonstrated moderate relationships with these same constructs.

RQ3. Gender-specific predictions Based on the gender differences observed in the cor relational analysis, we hypothesized there might be  a difference in predicting the students’ outcomes. We  conducted a hierarchical multiple regression analy sis predicting self-efficacy and test scores for each group. For the change in test score, both models were non-significant, with females showing R²adj =.05, F(8, 99) = 1.74, p =.1, and males R²adj =.06, F(8, 78) = 1.72, p =.11. While t-tests revealed significant increases in knowledge t est scores, none of the measured achievement motivation variables could predict these learning gains. Gender differences emerged in predicting self-efficacy development. The female model was non-significant (R²adj =.04, F(8, 99) = 1.62, p =.13). In contrast, the initial male model including al l variables was highly significant (F(8, 78) = 5.32, p <.01), explaining 28.6% of the variance (R²adj =.29). Variables were then entered stepwise to examine their individual contri bution. Attainment alone explained 17.3% of the var iance (R²adj =.17, F(1, 85) = 19.04, p <.001). Adding learning experience increased the e xplained variance by 1.5% (R²adj =.19, F(2, 84) = 10.93, p <.001). The third step added enjoyment, increasing explained variance by 2.1% (R²adj =.21, F(3, 83) = 8.57, p <.001). Test scores contributed an additional 0.5% (R²adj =.21, F(4, 82) = 6.86, p <.001). Finally, further interest in AI literacy a dded 7.2% (R²adj =.29, F(5, 81) = 7.90, p <.001). In the final model, all predictors contributed significantly. Attainment (β =.32, p <.01) and learning experience (β =.33, p <.01) emerged as the strongest positive predictors, followed by enjoyment (β =.23, p <.05) and test score (β =.19, p =.05). Further interest in AI literacy showed a significant negative relationship (β = -.35, p <.01). Post-hoc power analyses using G*Power revealed that the last male self-efficacy model demonstrated high statistical power (1 - β =.99, f² = 0.4, n = 87), suggesting sufficient sample size to detect these significant relationships.

## Discussion and implications

First, our findings indicate that while students ga ined knowledge and task-specific self-efficacy, their enjoyment of the subject decreased over time, and in our male regression model, further interest in AI was negatively related to self-efficacy, aligning with Tellhed et al. (2022) who o bserved that increased self-efficacy following programming interventions coincided with decreased interest. This pattern suggests that as students gr ow more confident, their initial curiosity and enjoyment may decline due to perceived task complexity or reduced novelty. Future research should explore whether these declin es are temporary effects of specific instructional approaches or more persistent trends, with longitudinal studies and targeted interventions—such as integrating real-world AI applications and exploring socio-technical topics like ethics and fairness (Peppler & Thompson, 2024)—to sustain student interest and engagement. Moreover, the lack  of a significant relationship between self-reported prior knowledge of AI and ML and other constructs challenges key assumptions of expectancy-value theory (Eccles & Wigfield, 2002), suggesting that students may inacc urately assess their baseline knowledge due to limi ted familiarity or media influences (Nagelhout, 2024). Future studies should employ alternative or complem entary methods, such as objective pre-assessment tools or task-based evaluations, to bridge the gap between percei ved and actual knowledge and inform more effective interventions in AI education. Second, achievement motivation variables predicting  task-specific self-efficacy for male students highlight critical gender differences in self-efficacy development and calibration, underscoring the need for alternative models that better capture female students' experiences in AI education. One explanatio n is that key dimensions—such as sociocultural factors, classroom dynamics, and teacher stereotypes, which are widely recognized as influential in STEM education —were not included in the current model; fem ales may be more influenced by contextual and interpersonal as well as affective factors, affecting their engagement and willingness to learn. Future research should explore how these dimensions interact with achievement motivation and  selfefficacy, parti cularly in emerging fields like AI and ML where stu dents often have limited prior knowledge. Moreover, the role of self-calibration—the accuracy with which students assess their confi dence in relation to their performance (Kallia & Sentance, 2018)—is a promising area for future investigation, as males tend to exhibit higher self-efficacy and better calibration, while literature indicates that adolescent males often overestimate their abilities and females underestimate theirs.  Invest igating these dynamics will allow researchers to develop more comprehensive frameworks that account for the nuanced interplay between achievement motivation, self-efficacy, and gender, informing the design of effective STEM learning environments. Finally, the lack of a significant relationship between achievement motivation constructs and test scores in AI education may be attributed to several factors that complicate the translation of achievement motivation into measurable performance. One explan ation lies in the novelty and complexity of the AI domain. Many students likely begin with little to no prior knowledge, mak ing it difficult for them to apply motivational con structs like utility or attainment to tangible learning outcomes. The high cognitive demands of understanding abstr act AI concepts, such as machine learning processes, can r esult in cognitive overload, limiting the immediate  impact of achievement motivation on performance. This finding aligns with prior research suggesting that in domains where students have limited prior knowledge, achievement motivation may exhibit weaker immediate effects on academic performance (Berro, 2021). Moreover, exter nal factors such as classroom dynamics, peer intera ctions, and teaching quality might also overshadow the effe cts of achievement motivation on test scores, parti cularly in a domain as complex as AI. These findings suggest t hat the relationship between achievement motivation  and performance in emerging fields like AI is mediated by a confluence of domain-specific challenges and external factors. Future research should explore these dynamics to better understand how achievement motivation interacts with these factors to shape learning outcomes in AI education.
