# Designing at the Speed of Trust: Unearthing Implicit Values and Vibes

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Design Based Research (DBR) is a slow, tedious, and overwhelming process. Over the years of their existence, DBR research teams develop into tiny publics where new ways of being and doing develop. While tools such as conjecture maps were developed to support documenting the relationship between theoretical and design-based conjectures, we have found that the conceptual level of these conjectures do not quite capture our idiosyncratic axiologies, or the values, flavor, and vibes that became implicit or taken for granted. In this paper we share reflections from the Choreographing Science project, describing how the choice to end the project by designing something new helped us (1) reveal taken for granted design values and principles that we developed during our 4 years of collaboration, (2) work at a faster, more confident pace, and (3) create something shareable with stakeholders we care about. Shifting design practices to capture cultivated values and vibes As a hallmark of the Learning Sciences, Design Based Research (DBR) (Brown, 1992; Collins, 1992) allows us to advance theories of learning and design together through an iterative, collaborative process of conjecturing, enacting, and analyzing. DBR is a slow, tedious, and often overwhelming process requiring years of engagement. During this time research teams develop into tiny publics (Fine, 2012), where new ways of being and doing can emerge. Bang and colleagues expanded DBR to include attention to relational processes and products as consequential to design (Bang et al., 2016; Bang & Vossoughi, 2016). While tools such as conjecture maps (Sandoval, 2014) were developed to support documenting links between theoretical and design conjectures across iterative cycles, tools for unearthing axiological commitments have remained less clear. In this paper we share a DBR case in which shifting from researcher-led design practices to artistic-led (endogenous) design practices helped us unearth sticky, implicit axiologies we had developed yet taken for granted over our four-year collaboration. As an interdisciplinary team of Learning scientists, Artists, & Scientists, we worked on the Choreographing Science project with a clear sense of our shared values. We set out to disrupt knowledge hierarchies by uplifting embodied, choreographic ways of knowing in order to desettle established ways of doing science that historically dismissed youth perspectives (Vogelstein et al., 2022). We leveraged improvisational choreographic tools for science modeling in the design of summer camps that brought middle schoolers, scientists, & choreographers together. Our traditional social sciences analytic work produced academic papers that highlighted how participants leveraged choreographic tools to make sense of scientific phenomena by doing things like comparing how humans and computational agents interpret and enact movement rules (Wagh, Vogelstein, & Champion, 2024), developing movement rules for scientific entities (Vogelstein, Wagh, & Champion, 2025), and reflecting on the experience of responding to others’ move ment ideas (Vogelstein et al., 2024). While these analytic insights were generative, papers felt limited as our only products. Papers don’t spur movement or make collective choreography. We realized we needed a product that would promote inquiry through movement, and to do so, we switched our mode of design. We let choreographic design methods lead us as we designed a deck of movement cards to support facilitators in getting groups of people thinking by moving together. This process enlivened & aligned our tiny public once more, putting our axiological commitments to practice in the learning spaces we valued most. In this paper we describe how the choice to end our project by switching design methods to design something new helped us (1) reveal consequential, taken for granted design values and principles that we developed during our four-year collaboration, (2) work at a faster, more confident pace, and (3) create something shareable with stakeholders we care about. Our values are reflected in a product that centers uncertainty and begets new questions. Our MOVE about it cards engage our stakeholders as collaborators, replicating our design-based, pedagogical value of engaging our Choreographing Science participants as collaborators and thinking partners. What we made: MOVE about it The Choreographing Science project was a four-year (2021-2025) DBR project run by Learning Scientists, Dancer-Choreographers, & Scientists who believed that bringing choreographic modeling and agent-based modeling together could help create inroads for middle schoolers to contribute to scientists’ inquiry and support 1957 scientists in learning from both embodied and youth perspectives. Over four years, we iteratively designed and implemented three 1-2 week-long, free summer camps in which middle schoolers, scientists, and choreographers engaged in scientific inquiry together using choreographic and agent-based modeling tools. The structure of the camps foregrounded the use of choreographic practices and tools to raise and explore questions about the scientists’ research topics. Our formal analytic work thus far has resulted in six ISLS papers (not including this), and multiple in-process journal articles. In the Winter of 2025, as we began to plan for the ending of the project, we found ourselves in the midst of a federal funding crisis in the US. This crisis left us uncertain whether or not we would be able to finish our project as planned, and left our team with an urgency to create something useful. Our scientist partners had expressed impassioned interest in bringing the activities and tools from our camps into their labs and classrooms, and our friends and colleagues in a multitude of educational spaces were just as excited to get their communities moving alongside these ideas. It was in this action-oriented, communal context that we refocused our efforts toward creating a tangible product with these communities at the forefront. In the end, we printed 200 copies of a deck of movement prompt cards which we named MOVE about it: prompts to get groups thinking together through movement. The MOVE about it deck included 90 cards in total and was composed of 6 instruction cards, 19 ways in cards, 22 ways through cards, 22 ways together cards, & 21 ways out cards. Through a process of choreographic design which included brainstorming, enacting, and grouping, we developed four main categories of cards (like parent codes) and five kinds of each category (like child codes, see Column 1 Table 1). Ways in cards were simple and meant to get people up and moving in approachable ways, ways through were more complicated, ways together explicitly focused on moving in relation to others, and ways out focused on grounded endings (Table 1). The instruction cards gave descriptions for the four categories of movement prompts in the deck (Column 2 Table 1) as well as three activity proposals (multi-card sequencing, body segmenting, & leaders and mirrors). Additionally, we added a new, vital member to our team: dancer, visual artist, and assistant professor of dance, MK Ford. MK created custom illustrations for all 20 sub-categories of cards. In the rest of this paper, we describe the implicit values displayed in our cards, reflections on the process of designing them, and the new audiences and communities these cards allow us to be in conversation with. We conclude by making the argument that this process enabled us to surface implicit axiologies in our design work and create a functional tool for a multitude of formal and informal learning spaces that will live on far beyond the end date of our work together. Any movement (or stillness) can be dance. Dance is not just for trained, lean, not-yet-disabled bodies to perform challenging feats. Interesting and compelling choreography can be danced by any-body and can be sourced from a wide range of movement choices. We take these principles from a lineage of postmodern dance artists that began in the downtown New York City dance scene in the 1960’s (Banes, 1987). Two of their radical commitments remain in our work: choreography can e merge from dancers’ myriad interpretations of movement prompts, and starting with pedestrian movements can be generative. Through our traditional analytic work, we documented our use of movement prompts (or rules) for generating choreography because of their similarity to code that agents follow in ABM. However, we had taken for granted some of the underlying principles and values embedded in our use of movement prompts, which became explicit as we created our cards. Movement prompts for structuring creativity and switching things up Our dance values come from traditions that assert that “good” improvisational dance (Henley, 2025) relies on structure and boundness in order to support expansive embodied explorations. To this end, restrictions are helpful and generative. Asking people to just “dance” will not generate “good” choreography. And by good we mean that the movements are novel in some way or unexpected and surprise us as movers and observers. We value variety and surprise. Prompts should be structured to inspire, switch up, and allow participants the choice of leaving their comfortable movement homeostases (if only for a few seconds at a time). Some of our ways in cards highlight this value. For example, “walk sideways” restricts movements to walking, an everyday way of moving for many people, and instead of enacting it forward, which is very common, the direction is switched to sideways. How one enacts “walk sideways” is still open-ended. You could walk sideways with a mobility aid, at a slow or fast pace, leading with your head or hip, to your right or left, crossing your feet over each other or shuffling. Prompts such as, “circle your elbow,” bring your attention to a body part not often foregrounded in everyday movement, activating creative, unique movement possibilities. The prompts “change your speed,” or, “find a new place in the room,” ask dancers to alter their first impulses, making small changes that open up new possibilities without naming or assuming the presence of body parts. In addition, we also value reminding mover s that silliness and play can generate expansive thinking. Sometimes you just need to “shake to the sky” & see what happens next! Movement prompts for leveraging imagery to try the “impossible” Novelty and surprise were baked into our movement pedagogy. Dance improvisation tools such as imagery and “impossible” tasks were utilized to enact these values. We found that responding to imaginative prompts such as “move like a blade of grass” were more physically challenging than concrete and direct tasks like “walk sideways.” This differentiation led us to create distinct ways in and ways through cards. Some of the more abstract or imaginative movement prompts leveraged movement qualities derived from imagery such as “allow your body to float through the space,” or “travel through the space like a bolt of lightning.” Floating or lightning images were meant to inspire expansive movement qualities and interpretations. Words, however, were not always the best or only way of sparking one’s movement imagination, which brought us to collaborate with MK on adding a visual element. MK’s art, like the grounding image (Table 1, Row 5, Column 3), shows what might be invisible to the eye, but felt in the body. Additionally, their art displays a diverse array of bodies engaging with the movement ideas. The surrealist nature of these provocations (both prompts and images) undergirds the idea of offering “impossible” tasks. You cannot move as fast as a bolt of lightning, nor can you “reach far away through the walls of the room,” or “keep a forcefield between you and someone else.” But we can enact the physical effort and imaginative, communal space of doing so. Not only can we enact these ideas, but it is generative to do so. Stretching our imagination can stretch our movement explorations and choreographic possibilities. Movement prompts for attending to felt sensations as intentional ways to start and end While it became clear that an implicit goal in our work was to get people exploring new ways to move, it also became apparent that beginning and ending these explorations required careful attention. It’s really hard to get a group of people up and moving! We often do not make explicit the vulnerability and bravery it takes to move and dance with others. Even amongst ourselves, a collective of researchers committed to the power of movement for learning, it took a great deal of effort for us to get up from seated, sedentary positions and move. Once we got groups moving, sometimes it was hard to stop. Endings functioned as transitions from a shared movement experience to (most often) a sedentary, verbal discussion. Bringing movers’ attention inward to how their bodies felt while moving acted as a way to ground both the start and end of movement explorations. Some of the ways in cards focused on moving in synchrony with your breath, amplifying movements already happening in your 1959 body: “get bigger as you inhale and smaller as you exhale.” Some of the ways out cards focused on attending to comfort in your body such as, “find a movement that feels like home.” Movements that arise from attending to how your body feels and what it wants to do are good ways to activate movement-based explorations and bring them to a thoughtful close to prepare for whatever comes next. Sometimes choosing what feels “good” or “right” is more important than reaching for novelty and variability in a mover’s interpretations and choice making. Working at the speed of confidence and trust: “This was of me” It took us about six months to design and print our MOVE about it decks. Six months is much shorter than the time it takes to write and revise a journal manuscript (which in the top Learning Sciences journals often takes a few years), but a little longer than the amount of time it takes to write a conference paper like t his one (about a month or two). Our timeline felt fast because of how we moved through the process. We gave ourselves freedom to create without exhaustively describing or defending our rationale or pointing to an empirical moment in our large data corpus to justify each decision we made. Our fast pace was not always easy. It actually made Lauren uncomfortable. She even tried to slow down the group by going back to the data to pinpoint similarities between movement prompts used during our camps and in our developing deck. While the group humored her, they also pushed back against traditional methodological moves that stifled our creative design process. Our collaborator, Curtis Thomas, reminded us that we knew what we were doing and encouraged us to work from a place of confidence and trust: “Who are we trying to convince right now? Because I just believe it. You know, like I have the experience. And to me, I’m like, I don’t necessarily need to go back [to the data] and look because I’m like, oh yeah this was of me.” (Curtis Thomas 5/27/25). For Curtis, returning to the data was a move used to convince others, not ourselves, of the validity of our choices. This shift in design practices felt radical to Lauren who loves and has been trained in the painstakingly slow and labored work of grounding design decisions in empirical evidence. Eventually, we all moved forward with more confidence that our decisions were grounded in what we knew from our designs and implementations, as well as our bodies and experiences as movers. Moving confidently and quickly freed us to consider new questions related to marketing and scope, including purpose & audience, that we previously did not have space for when focused on justifying our decisions to research audiences. Broadening audience Shifting the pace of our design work from the minutia of traditional academic analyses to the more confident, quicker decision-making pace of choreographic design opened us tonew kinds of work. We engage d with ideas related to marketing, such as purpose, scope, and audience, that we had not wrestled with before. We worked hard to develop clear and succinct pitches for the usefulness of our deck, articulating our problem space (“When we’re thinking, we often have to stay still and only move to take a break or get the wiggles out of our system. But we are made to move! What if movement could be part of our thinking?”) and affordances (“When we MOVE about it together, we can: (1) see ourselves build on each other’ s ideas, (2) pose questions, explore relationships, and express ideas as a group, and (2) try out many ideas at the same time”). As we focused our pitch, we also worked to specify our audience. This was challenging because we saw the benefits of moving together relevant everywhere, since most human activity entails working in groups and generating a variety of ideas. We focused on our partner scientists who expressed interest in a deck like this, burnt-out educators who are often too exhausted to disrupt business as usual in their classrooms, dance makers and educators, and after-school space educators, all of whom support learning across “classrooms, workshops, laboratories, and community spaces.” Exposing taken for granted axiological commitments Our MOVE about it cards represent the values we cultivated over four-years together: uplifting communities of movers, expansive creativity, rigorous empathy, and openness for change. We made this deck because we wanted to be useful to communities we care about. Our decision to leverage endogenous choreographic design methods to design something new but related to our DBR project initially felt like a move away from DBR. However, we now see this move as serving key DBR goals by acting as a magnifying glass to expose taken for granted axiologies so fundamental and shared that we did not think to make them explicit. Changing the mode of design changed the kinds of axiologies we were able to see embedded in our work. We see this building on calls in DBR to consider designing for spread and not scale (Taylor et al., 2019), in response to learner ingenuity (Brady & Vogelstein, 2022), and towards axiological innovations (Bang et al., 2016). Shifting our leading epistemological logics felt satisfying in the end. We are excited about gifting educators decks, sharing them with our friends and family, creating art with them (Vogelstein et al., 2026), and researching new adaptations. We designed these cards with the hope that they will live a generative like far beyond the end date of our project. Even amongst ourselves, a collective of researchers committed to the power of movement for learning, it took a great deal of effort for us to get up from seated, sedentary positions and move. Once we got groups moving, sometimes it was hard to stop. Endings functioned as transitions from a shared movement experience to (most often) a sedentary, verbal discussion. Bringing movers’ attention inward to how their bodies felt while moving acted as a way to ground both the start and end of movement explorations. Some of the ways in cards focused on moving in synchrony with your breath, amplifying movements already happening in your 1959 body: “get bigger as you inhale and smaller as you exhale.” Some of the ways out cards focused on attending to comfort in your body such as, “find a movement that feels like home.” Movements that arise from attending to how your body feels and what it wants to do are good ways to activate movement-based explorations and bring them to a thoughtful close to prepare for whatever comes next. Sometimes choosing what feels “good” or “right” is more important than reaching for novelty and variability in a mover’s interpretations and choice making. Working at the speed of confidence and trust: “This was of me” It took us about six months to design and print our MOVE about it decks. Six months is much shorter than the time it takes to write and revise a journal manuscript (which in the top Learning Sciences journals often takes a few years), but a little longer than the amount of time it takes to write a conference paper like t his one (about a month or two). Our timeline felt fast because of how we moved through the process. We gave ourselves freedom to create without exhaustively describing or defending our rationale or pointing to an empirical moment in our large data corpus to justify each decision we made. Our fast pace was not always easy. It actually made Lauren uncomfortable. She even tried to slow down the group by going back to the data to pinpoint similarities between movement prompts used during our camps and in our developing deck. While the group humored her, they also pushed back against traditional methodological moves that stifled our creative design process. Our collaborator, Curtis Thomas, reminded us that we knew what we were doing and encouraged us to work from a place of confidence and trust: “Who are we trying to convince right now? Because I just believe it. You know, like I have the experience. And to me, I’m like, I don’t necessarily need to go back [to the data] and look because I’m like, oh yeah this was of me.” (Curtis Thomas 5/27/25). For Curtis, returning to the data was a move used to convince others, not ourselves, of the validity of our choices. This shift in design practices felt radical to Lauren who loves and has been trained in the painstakingly slow and labored work of grounding design decisions in empirical evidence. Eventually, we all moved forward with more confidence that our decisions were grounded in what we knew from our designs and implementations, as well as our bodies and experiences as movers. Moving confidently and quickly freed us to consider new questions related to marketing and scope, including purpose & audience, that we previously did not have space for when focused on justifying our decisions to research audiences. Broadening audience Shifting the pace of our design work from the minutia of traditional academic analyses to the more confident, quicker decision-making pace of choreographic design opened us tonew kinds of work. We engage d with ideas related to marketing, such as purpose, scope, and audience, that we had not wrestled with before. We worked hard to develop clear and succinct pitches for the usefulness of our deck, articulating our problem space (“When we’re thinking, we often have to stay still and only move to take a break or get the wiggles out of our system. But we are made to move! What if movement could be part of our thinking?”) and affordances (“When we MOVE about it together, we can: (1) see ourselves build on each other’ s ideas, (2) pose questions, explore relationships, and express ideas as a group, and (2) try out many ideas at the same time”). As we focused our pitch, we also worked to specify our audience. This was challenging because we saw the benefits of moving together relevant everywhere, since most human activity entails working in groups and generating a variety of ideas. Shifting design practices to capture cultivated values and vibes As a hallmark of the Learning Sciences, Design Based Research (DBR) (Brown, 1992; Collins, 1992) allows us to advance theories of learning and design together through an iterative, collaborative process of conjecturing, enacting, and analyzing. DBR is a slow, tedious, and often overwhelming process requiring years of engagement. During this time research teams develop into tiny publics (Fine, 2012), where new ways of being and doing can emerge. Bang and colleagues expanded DBR to include attention to relational processes and products as consequential to design (Bang et al., 2016; Bang & Vossoughi, 2016). While tools such as conjecture maps (Sandoval, 2014) were developed to support documenting links between theoretical and design conjectures across iterative cycles, tools for unearthing axiological commitments have remained less clear. In this paper we share a DBR case in which shifting from researcher-led design practices to artistic-led (endogenous) design practices helped us unearth sticky, implicit axiologies we had developed yet taken for granted over our four-year collaboration. As an interdisciplinary team of Learning scientists, Artists, & Scientists, we worked on the Choreographing Science project with a clear sense of our shared values. We set out to disrupt knowledge hierarchies by uplifting embodied, choreographic ways of knowing in order to desettle established ways of doing science that historically dismissed youth perspectives (Vogelstein et al., 2022). We leveraged improvisational choreographic tools for science modeling in the design of summer camps that brought middle schoolers, scientists, & choreographers together. Our traditional social sciences analytic work produced academic papers that highlighted how participants leveraged choreographic tools to make sense of scientific phenomena by doing things like comparing how humans and computational agents interpret and enact movement rules (Wagh, Vogelstein, & Champion, 2024), developing movement rules for scientific entities (Vogelstein, Wagh, & Champion, 2025), and reflecting on the experience of responding to others’ move ment ideas (Vogelstein et al., 2024). While these analytic insights were generative, papers felt limited as our only products. Papers don’t spur movement or make collective choreography. We realized we needed a product that would promote inquiry through movement, and to do so, we switched our mode of design. We let choreographic design methods lead us as we designed a deck of movement cards to support facilitators in getting groups of people thinking by moving together. This process enlivened & aligned our tiny public once more, putting our axiological commitments to practice in the learning spaces we valued most.

In this paper we describe how the choice to end our project by switching design methods to design something new helped us (1) reveal consequential, taken for granted design values and principles that we developed during our four-year collaboration, (2) work at a faster, more confident pace, and (3) create something shareable with stakeholders we care about. Our values are reflected in a product that centers uncertainty and begets new questions. Our MOVE about it cards engage our stakeholders as collaborators, replicating our design-based, pedagogical value of engaging our Choreographing Science participants as collaborators and thinking partners. What we made: MOVE about it The Choreographing Science project was a four-year (2021-2025) DBR project run by Learning Scientists, Dancer-Choreographers, & Scientists who believed that bringing choreographic modeling and agent-based modeling together could help create inroads for middle schoolers to contribute to scientists’ inquiry and support 1957 scientists in learning from both embodied and youth perspectives. Over four years, we iteratively designed and implemented three 1-2 week-long, free summer camps in which middle schoolers, scientists, and choreographers engaged in scientific inquiry together using choreographic and agent-based modeling tools. The structure of the camps foregrounded the use of choreographic practices and tools to raise and explore questions about the scientists’ research topics. Our formal analytic work thus far has resulted in six ISLS papers (not including this), and multiple in-process journal articles. In the Winter of 2025, as we began to plan for the ending of the project, we found ourselves in the midst of a federal funding crisis in the US. This crisis left us uncertain whether or not we would be able to finish our project as planned, and left our team with an urgency to create something useful. Our scientist partners had expressed impassioned interest in bringing the activities and tools from our camps into their labs and classrooms, and our friends and colleagues in a multitude of educational spaces were just as excited to get their communities moving alongside these ideas. It was in this action-oriented, communal context that we refocused our efforts toward creating a tangible product with these communities at the forefront. In the end, we printed 200 copies of a deck of movement prompt cards which we named MOVE about it: prompts to get groups thinking together through movement. The MOVE about it deck included 90 cards in total and was composed of 6 instruction cards, 19 ways in cards, 22 ways through cards, 22 ways together cards, & 21 ways out cards. Through a process of choreographic design which included brainstorming, enacting, and grouping, we developed four main categories of cards (like parent codes) and five kinds of each category (like child codes, see Column 1 Table 1). Ways in cards were simple and meant to get people up and moving in approachable ways, ways through were more complicated, ways together explicitly focused on moving in relation to others, and ways out focused on grounded endings (Table 1). The instruction cards gave descriptions for the four categories of movement prompts in the deck (Column 2 Table 1) as well as three activity proposals (multi-card sequencing, body segmenting, & leaders and mirrors). Additionally, we added a new, vital member to our team: dancer, visual artist, and assistant professor of dance, MK Ford. MK created custom illustrations for all 20 sub-categories of cards. In the rest of this paper,  we describe the implicit values displayed in our cards, reflections on the process of designing them, and the new audiences and communities these cards allow us to be in conversation with. We conclude by making the argument that this process enabled us to surface implicit axiologies in our design work and create a functional tool for a multitude of formal and informal learning spaces that will live on far beyond the end date of our work together. Any movement (or stillness) can be dance. Dance is not just for trained, lean, not-yet-disabled bodies to perform challenging feats. Interesting and compelling choreography can be danced by any-body and can be sourced from a wide range of movement choices. We take these principles from a lineage of postmodern dance artists that began in the downtown New York City dance scene in the 1960’s (Banes, 1987). Two of their radical commitments remain in our work: choreography can e merge from dancers’ myriad interpretations of movement prompts, and starting with pedestrian movements can be generative. Through our traditional analytic work,  we documented our use of movement prompts (or rules) for generating choreography because of their similarity to code that agents follow in ABM. However, we had taken for granted some of the underlying principles and values embedded in our use of movement prompts, which became explicit as we created our cards. Movement prompts for structuring creativity and switching things up Our dance values come from traditions that assert that “good” improvisational dance (Henley, 2025) relies on structure and boundness in order to support expansive embodied explorations. To this end, restrictions are helpful and generative. Asking people to just “dance” will not generate “good” choreography. And by good we mean that the movements are novel in some way or unexpected and surprise us as movers and observers. We value variety and surprise. Prompts should be structured to inspire, switch up, and allow participants the choice of leaving their comfortable movement homeostases (if only for a few seconds at a time). Some of our ways in cards highlight this value. For example, “walk sideways” restricts movements to walking, an everyday way of moving for many people, and instead of enacting it forward, which is very common, the direction is switched to sideways. How one enacts “walk sideways” is still open-ended. You could walk sideways with a mobility aid, at a slow or fast pace, leading with your head or hip, to your right or left, crossing your feet over each other or shuffling. Prompts such as, “circle your elbow,” bring your attention to a body part not often foregrounded in everyday movement, activating creative, unique movement possibilities. The prompts “change your speed,” or, “find a new place in the room,” ask dancers to alter their first impulses, making small changes that open up new possibilities without naming or assuming the presence of body parts. In addition, we also value reminding mover s that silliness and play can generate expansive thinking. Sometimes you just need to “shake to the sky” & see what happens next! Movement prompts for leveraging imagery to try the “impossible” Novelty and surprise were baked into our movement pedagogy. Dance improvisation tools such as imagery and “impossible” tasks were utilized to enact these values. We found that responding to imaginative prompts such as “move like a blade of grass” were more physically challenging than concrete and direct tasks like “walk sideways.” This differentiation led us to create distinct ways in and ways through cards. Some of the more abstract or imaginative movement prompts leveraged movement qualities derived from imagery such as “allow your body to float through the space,” or “travel through the space like a bolt of lightning.” Floating or lightning images were meant to inspire expansive movement qualities and interpretations. Words, however, were not always the best or only way of sparking one’s movement imagination, which brought us to collaborate with MK on adding a visual element. MK’s art, like the grounding image (Table 1, Row 5, Column 3), shows what might be invisible to the eye, but felt in the body. Additionally, their art displays a diverse array of bodies engaging with the movement ideas. The surrealist nature of these provocations (both prompts and images) undergirds the idea of offering “impossible” tasks. You cannot move as fast as a bolt of lightning, nor can you “reach far away through the walls of the room,” or “keep a forcefield between you and someone else.” But we can enact the physical effort and imaginative, communal space of doing so. Not only can we enact these ideas, but it is generative to do so. Stretching our imagination can stretch our movement explorations and choreographic possibilities.
