# What can Computer Science Education Learn from Mathematics Problem Solving Pedagogy?

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Computer Science (CS) offers a promising context to develop transferable Problem Solving (PS) competencies through Computational PS (CPS), yet its instructional foundations are still emerging. As such, we draw on closely related mathematics PS research to advance a conceptual basis for CPS pedagogy and transfer 3 key elements. First, we adopt the concept of “rich” PS activities to CPS, but adapt it through a decision-making lens that views PS as navigating multiple decision paths, which we define by Computational Thinking dimensions. Second, we propose a 4-layer CPS activity design framework organized around design factors that shape a problem’s decision-making space, and integrating classroom discourse — central in mathematics PS education — as a novel layer. Third, we present teacher moves that help preserve and leverage this richness in the design and implementation of CPS activities. Together, these contributions advance a theoretical foundation for CPS pedagogy.

While many fields have explored PS instruction, Computer Science (CS) offers unique opportunities to foster generalizable PS competencies (Li and Oon, 2024; Montuori et al., 2024; Salehi et al., 2020). While CPS research is emerging, it lacks insight into effective instructional practices,  and could therefore benefit from insights established in related fields. Mathematics education  has a long tradition of studying PS, and shares deep epistemological and methodological roots with CS (e.g., Grover and Pea, 2013; Priemer et al., 2020; Wu and Yang, 2022). Yet, despite this overlap, CS education research has rarely drawn on theoretical frameworks from mathematics education (Malmi et al., 2019). This theoretical paper draws on mathematics PS research and transfers key instructional principles to CPS pedagogy by (i) adapting the notion of “rich” PS activities to CPS through a decision-making lens, (ii) proposing a CPS activity design framework that identifies factors influencing a problem’s richness and integrates classroom discourse as a core practice, and (iii) identifying teacher moves that preserve and leverage this richness throughout design and implementation.

## Developing a theoretical framework for effective CPS instruction

Mathematics PS research identifies two main levers for effective PS instruction: designing  and effectively orchestrating “rich” PS activities. While mathematics and CS share PS structures, CPS’s dynamic and  artifactcentered nature with interactive and executable programming environments where learners iteratively test, debug, and refine their reasoning (Robins et al., 2003) calls for adapted frameworks for CPS. A conceptual design framework for CPS activities Conceptualising CPS activity richness through the decision-making process In adapting insights from mathematics to CPS, a central question arises: What constitutes a rich problem in CPS? In mathematics education, a rich problem is cognitively challenging and has multiple meaningfully different solutions (Lester and Cai, 2016). To operationalize these terms in the context of CPS, we draw on research that characterizes PS as a decision-making process (Wieman and Price, 2023).

(1) Meaningfully different solutions can be described as using distinct decision paths (i.e., a sequence of

strategic decisions) which can be described and compared through Computational Thinking practices (Grover and Pea, 2013). For example, a problem might be solved using different concepts (abstraction), be decomposed in different ways, by exploiting different patterns, or solved with solutions with different scopes (generalisation).

(2) Cognitive challenge can be conceptualized in terms of the decisions students engage in that require

intrinsic cognitive load (Kalyuga and Singh, 2016). We introduce the concept of discoverability to ensure that the decisions – and resulting solutions – are within students’ zone of proximal development (Chaiklin et al., 2003). Preserving CPS decision-making richness through the CPS activity design Using the (C)PS as decision-making lens, we propose a four-layer model of CPS activity design where each layer builds on the previous one and either sustains or collapses the problem’s inherent richness: 2761 Layer 1: The Computational Problem  (what is to be solved) establishes the decision space. A rich problem has meaningfully different solutions that are discoverable by the learners, with discoverability depending on the affordances of the programming environment and students’ prior knowledge (Adorni et al., 2025; Bumbacher et al., 2018). The balance between richness and discoverability defines the problem’s potential. Layer 2:  The Task (what is  asked to do and how) specifies how learners engage with the  problem through (i) the task type (e.g., writing, modifying, or comparing codes activate different degrees and types of decisions), (ii) task constraints, whether explicit (e.g., ma x number of lines) or implicit (i.e., embedded in the learning environment, e.g.,  limiting access to specific blocks) or (iii) f eedback mechanisms (e.g., immediate or delayed) which hinder or sustain metacognitive engagement (Brender et al., 2025; Chevalier et al., 2022). Layer 3: Instructional Scaffolding (feedback, hints, instructing, explaining, modeling, questioning) seek to maintain an equilibrium between cognitive challenge and available guidance, and as such, shape what decisions remain. Instructional scaffolding can be conceptual, procedural, strategic, or metacognitive in nature (Jumaat and Tasir, 2014) in order to guide attention and foster productive struggle (Choi et al., 2023; Koedinger and Aleven, 2007), but risks reducing the decision-making space (e.g., when overly directive or frequent) and collapsing the richness defined in previous layers, turning opportunities for reasoning into procedural completion. Layer 4: Classroom D iscourse (how solutions are shared and compared) governs how diversity in individual decision-paths become shared objects of reflection. By juxtaposing strategies and mak ing explicit the reasoning behind them (Evans and Dawson, 2017), students learn to recognize not only the validity but also the affordances and trade-offs of different approaches, thus transforming diversity in decision-making into collective knowledge. If this reflective space is missing or overly evaluative (e.g., by emphasizing correctness over comparisons), the decision-making richness achieved in individual work remains invisible and unexploited, thus reducing CPS to isolated performance rather than shared inquiry. Hence, effective CPS pedagogy requires designing for richness, and classroom discourse to make said richness visible, and generative of collective insight. The framework can be used by teachers and designers to examine how design choices at each layer either preserve or reduce students’ opportunities for meaningful decision-making, as established by the problem itself, and as such, how design decisions either sustain or diminish the epistemic richness of the CPS activity. Teacher Moves for Effective CPS Activity Orchestration Building on mathematics education research, we consider how orchestration practices (Evans and Dawson, 2017; Evans and Swan, 2014; Ingram and Riser, 2019; Stein et al., 2008) can be reinterpreted to support the epistemic goals of CPS. At their core, these practices are subject-and tool-agnostic: they structure classroom interaction to connect, and collectively refine diverse solution strategies. When reframed through the decision-making lens, they can be understood as pedagogical moves that make the richness of decision paths visible and meaningful.

5. Consolidating collective understanding. Teachers synthesize the discussion, highlighting the strategic

As such, it is essential to consider how teachers may acquire such competences. This paper draws on insights from mathematics PS education research to develop three contributions for computational PS (CPS): (i) The conceptualisation of richness as a unifying construct that connects activity design and classroom orchestration, and reframing it through a decision-making lens that emphasizes learning as the navigation of multiple meaningful decision paths. As such, we link how problems are conceived to afford decision-making with how teachers make that diversity visible and productive in discussion. (ii) A four-layer CPS activity design framework for analysing and designing rich CPS activities that is organized around factors that influence a CPS activity’s decision-making space and includes discourse, a typically overlooked practice in CS pedagogy. (iii) A pedagogical guide outlining teacher moves for effective CPS activity orchestration that leverages their richness. The next step is to translate these conceptualisations into usable tools for teachers, developed through participatory co-design to ensure their utility, usability, and acceptability in authentic classroom settings.
