# Teacher–GenAI Collaboration to Co-Design Lesson Plans: Theoretical Framework and Usability Study

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
To actively engage in the process of high-quality lesson planning, teachers require personalized support. This study explores how generative AI (GenAI) can enhance this process via interactive and co-constructive collaboration. A hybrid teacher-GenAI co-design model was developed and tested on an online platform. Results indicate high user satisfaction and improved lesson planning competency. Teachers with stronger AI skills reported greater psychological need fulfillment, though this did not extend to co-design engagement or perceived competency.

## Problem statement

Designing lesson plans is a complex yet essential t ask requiring teachers to design structured, goal-aligned learning experiences  (Bai et al., 2024; Martin et al., 2019). However, i n-service teachers often lack timely, personalized support in lesson planning, which is resource-intensive to provide (Miville et al., 2022). GenAI offers a promising solution, as demonstrated by tools like  LessonPlanner, which assists novice teachers through AIgenerated content (Fan et al., 2024). Yet, these tools often lack theoretical guidance for scaffolding human–GenAI collaboration (Järvelä & Rosé, 2023). This study proposes a hybrid teacher–GenAI co-design model, integrating the collaborative problem-solving framework (Hesse et al., 2015)  and the scientific discovery as a dual search model (Klahr & Dunbar, 1988). The co-design process is framed as collaborative and iterative, involving two key components: the joint problem space and the design space. It begins with teachers providing essential information about instructional content and the specific class context. Teachers critically assess GenAI’s interpretations, refining them as needed to build a shared problem r epresentation. This iterative exchange establishes a shared problem space, characterized by a mutual understand ing of the task and key com ponents. Once this shared understanding is achieved, the design space is activated, allowing GenAI to propose lesson plan elements step by step. Teachers then evaluate and revise these suggestions using their instructional and contextual expertise. Although key collaboration skills have been studied (Hesse et al., 2015), human–GenAI co-design raises new skill requirements.  UNESCO’s AI competency framework stresses the impor tance of understanding AI functions and limitations (Miao & Cukurova, 2024), including recognizing model biases, such as gender bias in STEM content (Hess & Kunz, 2023).  Preliminary empirical findings show that educators may experience cognitive overload due to limited GenAI familiarity  (Woo et al., 2024), while foundational skills can enhance motivation by supporting autonomy, competence, and relatedness  (Cowling et al., 2023). However, empirical insights into how these skills affect the collaboration of lesson planning remain limited. Current study and research questions This study investigates a human –GenAI co-design platform developed by  Peking University, aiming to explore how such collaboration can enhance teachers’ lesson planning skills, foster motivational learning, and clarify the role of foundational AI skills in shaping the co-design process and learning outcomes.  This study examines teachers’ perceptions of a GenAI-supported co-design platform in terms of psychological need sati sfaction, cognitive load, perceived competence gains, intention to use, and overall satisfaction (RQ1). It also investigates whether foundational AI skills predict need satisfaction, cognitive load, and perceived competence improvement (RQ2), as well as co-design behaviors such as time investment and revision behavior (RQ3).

## Methods and results

We developed a GenAI-based lesson planning platform grounded in learning  sciences to support teachers in co-designing lesson plans. The system employs a six-step instructional design model (Dick et al., 2001) and leverages few-shot learning and retrieval-augmented generation to iteratively create, evaluate, and revise lesson plans. In a pilot study with 30 in-service primary teachers, we examined their co-design experiences and how AI skills predicted learning processes and outcomes. The study included a demographic survey, a co-design task involving three student-centered lesson plans, and a post-task user experience survey. Learners’ experiences were measured using Likert-scale items and co-design log data. All Chinese-translated instruments showed acceptable reliability (Cronbach’s α and Omega >.70). Perceived competence, autonomy, and relatedness were assessed with 4, 3, and 3 items (Sailer et al., 2017), and intention to use  the platform and foundational AI skills  (Scheibenzuber et al.,

2024) with 4 and 6 items, respectively. Intrinsic cogniti ve load (Opfermann, 2008), perceived planning

competence, and satisfaction were each measured with a single item. Co-design time and revision behavior were operationalized as z-standardized values based on task duration and frequency of content revisions across sessions.

To address RQ  1, Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were used (due to the small sample size and non-normality) to compare the mean scores of key variables against the neutral midpoin t of 3.0. For RQ2 and RQ3, single linear regressions examined whether AI skills predicted motivation and co-design performance. Regarding RQ1, results show that teachers perceived  the co-design process as satisfying their basic psychological needs —competence (M = 3.98, SD = 0.32), autonomy (M = 4.04, SD = 0.35), and relatedness (M = 3.57, SD = 0.49) —alongside high intentions to use (M = 4.03, SD = 0. 63), improved lesson plan competency (M = 3.87, SD = 0.81), and overall platform satisfaction (M = 3.3, SD = 0.73), all statistically significant (p <.01) compared to the threshold value, supporting the platform's perceived efficacy. Intrinsic cognitive load (M = 3.03, SD = 0.59) showed no significant difference.  For RQ2, regression analyses indicate that foundational AI skills moderately predict perceived competence (B = 0.59, p <.001) and autonomy (B = 0.52, p =.007), but are weakly associated with relatedness and do not significantly predict cognit ive load or perceived improvement in lesson planning. As for RQ3, AI skills did not significantly predict co-design time or revision behavior (all p >.05). ## Discussion

This study proposes a theoretical model for the hybrid teacher-GenAI co-design process (see figure 1) and applies it to develop a lesson-planning platform, examining how teachers' AI skill s relate to their experiences. In conclusion, teachers perceived the GenAI-supported course co-design platform as beneficial, fulfilling, and highly satisfactory. While teachers' foundational AI skills did not predict their revision behavior or time spent in the codesign process  and perceived improvement in lesson planning, they significantly predicted their basic psychological needs during the process.  This can be attributed to the effectiveness of AI-driven interaction in achieving quick outcomes and enhancing user satisfaction, but not in developing  durable skills like lesson planning competency (Hutson & Ceballos, 2023). Moreover, good motivational experiences in problem-solving with advanced tools are not necessarily related to cognitive competence, such as lesson plan competency (Mogavi et al., 2024). Fundamental AI skills appear to be more effective in unprompted LLM environments (Kumar et al., 2024). This may explain why these skills fail to predict perceived improvement in lesson planning competency within this well-prompted platform. Fundamental AI skills primarily emphasize prompting and evaluation, rather than content modification —central to teachers' behaviors during the co-design process —which limits their predictive value for revision-related activities. Future research should investigate how factors like  prior knowledge and collaboration skills shape learner –GenAI interactions, and assess long-term effects through longitudinal designs.
