# Visualizing Collaboration Using Multiple Modalities

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Collaboration is a multidimensional process. Our case study explores ways to visualize these multiple dimensions within a collaborating group, incorporating both verbal and nonverbal interactions while considering the individual and group, as well as temporality. We discuss the affordances and limitations of these visualizations on the insights they provide.

## Introduction and background

Similar to how the parable of the blind men and the  elephant illustrates how different perspectives ca n lead to incomplete and inaccurate interpretations of the wh ole, Hmelo-Silver (2003) posits that examining collaboration as individual components (e.g., only verbal exchang es), without other contexts leads to an incomplete understanding of the collaborative process. Collabo rative learning or computer-supported collaborative learning (CSCL) processes involves multiple dimensions of ve rbal and nonverbal interactions between individuals, their group, and their learning context. Moreover, collaboration has a dynamic temporal dimension that evolv es as participants engage with one another as they engage  in the task. Therefore, multiple approaches and mi xed methods are essential to comprehensively analyze how grou ps interact to co-construct knowledge and learn (Hmelo-Silver, 2003; Puntambekar, 2013). Collaboration res earch has often focused on examining verbal interactions to understand how the group builds knowledge (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2006; Suthers et al., 2010), but recent scholarship highlights the importance of  nonverbal behaviors such as mutual gaze (Schneider  & Pea, 2015), posture (Radu et al., 2020), and gestures (Wang et al., 2017) in collaborative interactions as well. As CSCL researchers, we often utilize visualizations to agg regate these complex interactions, helping to ident ify patterns that enhance our understanding of the collaborative  process (Hmelo-Silver & Jeong, 2021). Ho wever, there is a paucity of research investigating representations that capture the verbal, nonverbal, and temporal nature of collaborative interactions. In this paper, we explo re ways to visualize small group collaborations in ways that aggregate these complex interactions. T he research questions guiding our study were: What visualizations can figurehelp us understand verbal and nonverbal interaction s during collaborative learning? What are the affordances and limitations of each representation?

## Methods

In this case study, we focus on a 5 minute-video (and its transcript) from one small group of three middle school students as they work collaboratively using a share d tool (laptop). This data is part of a larger corp us from the Institute for Student-AI Teaming (iSAT, D’Mello et al., 2024), a multi-institutional effort that seeks to support collaborative learning in secondary (grades 6-12) classrooms by developing AI tools and curriculu m materials. The Collaborative Problem Solving (CPS) framework (Sun et al., 2020) was used to code the verbal excha nges. The CPS framework has three main facets: constructing shared knowledge (construct), negotiation/coordination (negotiate), and maintaining team function (maintain). We also applied the Nonverbal Interactions in Collaborative Learning Environments (NICE) framewor k (Dey & Puntambekar, 2023) to each utterance. The NICE framework captures students' nonverbal behavio rs along three modalities: eye gaze  (joint or individual gaze), hand motions (use of tool and pointing), and body movements (leaning and head nods). We binary coded both verbal (CPS) and nonverbal (NICE) annotations,  where a “1” indicated the observation of a particu lar behavior, and “0” indicated its absence. The categories were not mutually exclusive. We then explored how visualizations could offer a m ulti-dimensional view of collaborative interactions and how it unfolded over time. We adapted prior work by Cukurova et al. (2018), utilizing 100% stacked column graphs to compare individual and group-level differences based on frequencies. We also applied  the CORDFU method (Luckin, 2003) to students’ nonverbal interactions, for a more fine-grained analysis.

## Results and discussion

Examining both verbal (Fig. 1a) and joint nonverbal  interactions (Fig. 1b) reveals that although the group faced challenges in knowledge building, all members were nonverbally engaged (as seen through high joint attention, coupled with gesture and tool usage). This highligh ts the importance of considering both verbal and no nverbal behaviors for understanding collaboration more holi stically. Group interactions are not constant, howe ver, and Figure 1 does not capture dynamic interactions over time. To address this limitation, we segmented the transc ript into 15-second intervals (represented by the x-axis) and created 100% stacked column charts to illustrate the proportion of different modalities over time (Figure 2). Each column is the total contribution by a student (S1, S2, S3) in each interval. We found that proportionally, nearly half the groups’ joint participation was from  their joint visual attention, with consistent tool  use and intermittent pointing. While this representation provides an incremental, high-level overview of participation, we cannot see how individuals varied in their contributions to the group’s collaboration. Figure 2 100% Stacked Column Graph of Verbal and Joint Nonverbal Behaviors Over Time. To differentiate nonverbal behaviors among students while preserving temporal dynamics, we generated a Chronologically Ordered Representation of Discourse and Features Used (CORDFU) diagrams (Luckin, 2003), focusing on students’ nonverbal interactions over each utterance (Figure 3). The CORDFU diagram illuminates unequal nonverbal contributions (e.g., head nods, pointing) as well as the consistent join t visual attention (JVA) and sharing the common tool amongst the group  members. While this diagram offers a  more fine-grained representation of student interactions over time, it is complex, especially if more codes or participants are added. Figure 3 Chronological Representation of Nonverbal Interactions (S1-blue, S2-red, S3-yellow). In returning to the ‘blind men and the elephant’ parable, this paper explores the ways that different facets of collaboration –the individual, group, and their verbal and nonverbal  contributions over time, can be meaningfully represented in K-12 settings. Our findings underscore the importance of examining both verbal and nonverbal for a more comprehensive understanding of the collaborative process (Dey & Puntambekar, 2023). Our work also revealed the challenges of synthesizing  these facets into a single, interpretable  visual. A key insight was that aggregated summaries (e.g., Figure 2) might be sufficient in revealing overall group trends as compared to fine-grained analysis (e.g., Figure 3). We also found that g aze comprised most of the nonverbal interactions, further highlighting the importance of this modality for collaboration. Our next steps are to find ways to better integrate these multiple dimensions to provide more insights into the collaborative process.
