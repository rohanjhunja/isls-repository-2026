# A Model for Shared Epistemic Agency

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Based on seven ethnographic cases of student group projects, this paper describes knowledge creation enacted in assessment tasks in undergraduate units of study, gathered into a model of shared epistemic agency. Key characteristics, including initial framing and ways of creating and using objects, traced between teams indicated patterns of knowledge creation. Some individual students created substantial artefacts based on shared conceptual development. The infrastructure that students created to support their collaboration is integrated within the model. The elements of the model and their connection to types of knowledge creation suggest design principles for similar activities, and avenues for future research.

## Introduction

Skills for complex problem-solving are an identifie d priority for tertiary education, but the processe s by which students practice and acquire those skills are under-researched (Graesser et al., 2018). Designers should consider how to support students in developing epistemic flu ency, the ability to effectively work with knowledg e, and agency in that work: in using tools, resources and processes (Markauskaite & Goodyear, 2017). However, pedagogies that actively support object-centred knowledge creation are in need of further development (Damşa & Muukkonen, 2020). How students exercise epistemic  fluency in ‘mundane’ (action-by-action) ways, a ke y source of understanding, has not been the subject o f much detailed investigation: for example, two emp irical studies of student knowledge creation (Damşa et al., 2010; Muukkonen et al., 2010) took snapshots of projects at stages of their work using interviews and collected objects, rather than observing the projects in full. This study followed day-to-day activities of work w ithin university student group projects, asking the question, ‘How do students exercise epistemic agenc y?’ From the ethnographic, case-based study of grou p projects in university courses, a comparison of cas es produced patterns and key elements of their coll aborative spaces. This resulted in a model (Figure 2) towards  understanding sociomateriality in the exercise of shared epistemic agency: how collaborative processes, objects and material infrastructure are used to create knowledge. It combines epistemic with practical infrastructure to describe the inter-related parts of a group project. Literature The model of shared epistemic agency derived from t he analysis of cases relates together the concepts,  briefly outlined here, of dispositions, knowledge creation, shared epistemic agency, objects and infrastructure invoking distributed cognition.

The early formation of group projects are related t o dispositions, which combine a sensitivity to the occasion, inclination to act and ability to apply skills and knowledge appropriately (Perkins et al., 1993). The way that a student frames the task and subsequently act s is influenced by previous experience as well as p erceived future utility. If the situation is seen as familiar or novel, or could relate directly to a future career goal, for example, it will affect motivation and a decision on whether  old or new ways of working are required. The frami ng of a task is the outcome to the question of “What is going on here?” (Goffman, 1974, p. 8). Framing can happen at the level of the whole project or in terms of personal long-term goals, and it also happens at the moment-to-moment level (Goffman, 1974; Scherr & Hammer, 2009), when students decide to pursue an idea or consider how to set up their communications.

The knowledge creation metaphor (Paavola & Hakkarainen, 2005) underpins t he analysis of cases. In this view, learning is social and centered on the u se and creation of “common objects of activity” (Pa avola & Hakkarainen, 2005, p. 536), which include both mate rial artefacts and concepts. The production of know ledge through objects results in a “public life” (Bereite r & Scardamalia, 2014, p. 35) that makes them avail able for review, interrogation, adaptation and extension by others. In the context of a collaborative project, shared objects are key to productive knowledge creation, placing ideas, discussion topics, resources and record of progress within the gaze and activity of group members.

Shared epistemic agency is where disposition meets knowledge creation in group actions. It is conscious collaborative work with knowledge towards a shared goal in a particular situation (Damşa et al., 2010; Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2006). Similarly to Perkins et al.’s (1993) dispositions above, agency involves reference to past experience, present evaluation and projecti ng towards an imagined future (Emirbayer & Mische, 1998). The situatedness is important: agency may be enacte d differently depending on circumstances. Objects can be both instantiated in artefacts as well as conceptual and are the focus of work in knowledge creation. The two types of objects relevant to this paper are epistemic objects and technical objects. Epistemic or knowledge objects are the produce of knowledge creation: their shape and content are unclear at first but emerge through making sense of, combining and representing ideas in activity (Engeström & Blackler, 2005; Knorr Cetina, 2001). An epistemic object holds contested and negotiated knowledge and can be a thinking tool towards a final product, or the emergent final product itself (Ewen stein & Whyte, 2009; Muukkonen et al., 2010). Techn ical objects (Ewenstein & Whyte, 2009) are used in collaboration but are generally accepted at face value rather than interrogated or changed. Examples might be the peri odic table or a journal article. There may be some interpretation involved, but technical objects are used as referenced resources rather than the focus of work. There is some fluidity between types of objects in particular circumstances (Nicolini et al., 2011). An epistemic object may, once work is completed, be treated as a techni cal object, accepted in its final form, e.g. an ear lier-created diagram may become an illustration within a report.  Instructions for a task may temporarily be interro gated in detail, becoming for a while an epistemic object as students make sense of what is being asked of them. Infrastructure to support collaborative work is an assembly of to ols and processes worked out by the group to support their goals, accommodating to, or adapting, available tools (Damşa et al., 2019). The theory of distributed cognition extends knowledge work into the surrounding environment and social interactions (Hollan et al., 2000): in thinking through problems and their solutions, people use tools to inscribe, hold, discuss and remix ideas within physical and online spaces. The framework for distributed cognition for teamwork (DiCoT, Furniss & Blandford, 2006) outlines areas of focus useful f or thinking about how knowledge work is done by gro ups. These include: an information hub, a shared place for relevant knowledge, communication and resources; situation awareness or visibility of progress, the ability to know how, and how well, a team project has advanced; and a horizon of observation, that is, the ability to see the current actions of team members. An information hub relies on team members adding relevant content to a shared repository. A way of thinking about how this infor mation hub is developed comes from a piece by Perkins (199 4). In writing about an ongoing dialogue between th ree academics, he described the way they, over repeated occasions, tossed ideas onto a heap of shared concepts that were retrieved and mixed, stirred or re-ordered in later discussions. The heap lies “anywhere that information can reside in the immediate environment – in your and others’ working memories, notepads, blackboards, and so on” (Perkins, 1994, p. 90). This term referring to a pu rposeful if rather ad-hoc and semi-structured way o f building and refining a shared set of concepts is an off-beat but evocative metaphor for a basis for knowledge creation.

## Method

This study employs a sociomaterial perspective, which involves  “a stronger focus on people, routines, and situated activity” (Johri, 2011, p. 209). To investigate the activity of student groups in detail, I took an ethnographic casebased approach, videoing in-person meetings and acc essing online spaces used by groups. In transcribin g activities, I took note of dialogue, physical gestures and actions in person and online. I observed how objects were used and created.

## Cases and data collection

Selection criteria for cases included: an undergraduate task that had some level of complexity and ope n-ness in its solution, that is, students had a degree of con trol over what they produced and how they produced it; and collaboration within a small group (3-6 members). Ethics approval was received for the study. Volunteer groups were recruited from two courses: f our groups from education (first-year level) and three groups from engineering (second-year level). The number of group members ranging from three to five, to a total of 27 participants. Recordings added up to 36 hours, covering 29 meetings. In the education task, students were asked to ident ify their own driving question on a relevant topic, create a media artefact in response and present tha t in class. In the engineering task, groups were re quired to identify one element of a larger given project and formulate a plan for executing their part of that project, creating a project plan and a class presentation. There were  several overarching project scenarios to choose fr om. The education project included instructions to set up a shared online workspace and included a mind-mapping exercise at its start. The engineering students were not giv en instructions or tips on how to organize their co llaborative work. The education task ran for approximately four weeks and was worth 30% of the total grade. The engineering task took most of the semester at 10 weeks and its components added up to 85% of the final grade. For clarity of association in this paper, I have named groups using Edu/Eng to indicate the relevant course, a letter to distinguish between groups, and added a  number for  the type of knowledge creation allocated after ICLS2022 Proceedings 800 © ISLS analysis. For example, ‘EngB1’ is an engineering group classified as the first knowledge creation type of ‘divided work.’ Analysis I used discourse analysis of multimodal transcripts of student interactions, analyzing actions, concepts, talk and object use in context, to trace how students made meaning of the task and their subsequent actions. I produced detailed diagrams that traced and connec ted conceptual development, group members and object use and creation over the duration of each project. See Figure 1 for an excerpt of such a diagram. I wrote project narratives based on the dimensions of framing and setup of the project and infrastructure for collaboration and knowledge creation. Putting each case narrative into the same structure enabled comparison across cases, summarized in Table 1. Within that st ructure, I analyzed specific episodes in detail, de scribing interactions using objects and the physical environment.

## Findings

This paper concentrates on comparison of cases and the resultant patterns of collaboration, and here I  present a summary of part of the results from the study. Data  informed a detailed description of the framing and  evident dispositions of the groups, the material infrastructure used for collaboration and their use of objects in knowledge work.

Framing and definition of the problem Through diagrams of each project from start to finish, as well as coding the types of interactions and their use of shared objects, it was clear that the pattern for collaboration for each of the groups was established very early in the project. A higher density and complexity in the lines connecting objects, actors and concepts indicated higher levels of knowledge creation.  I analyzed the set-up of projects in terms of framing and disposition: how the groups understood the task and what it was asking of them.  The groups that recognized from the start a proble m to be investigated and discussed it together tended to follow through with shared conceptual development and objectcentered work. An exception to this was the EduB2 group, which had intended to pool research results but did not follow through. The EngC3 group showed high levels of conceptual development between two of its member s, but the remaining two members working somewhat on the periphery on allocated tasks. In all of the cases, students periodically placed their work in terms of future careers: what the engineering students understood to be a ‘professional’ report or software; the education students relating their project work to experiences in school and how they would use curriculum as teachers. Patterns of knowledge creation I analyzed each case across dimensions of shared conceptual development, collaborative infrastructure and use of objects (see Table 1). Commonalities between cases allowed me to classify them under three types of knowledge creation: 1) divided work; 2) shared conceptual development; and 3) shared conceptual development plus artefacts created by individuals. All groups had some degree of knowledge creation, that is, shared conceptual development centered on objects, but to varied depth and extent.

Two groups, EduC1 and EngB1, in their first meeting s decided to basically divide work and assemble individual sections and continued in that pattern f or the project, so formed the first category of div ided work. These groups had few examples of shared conceptual development, either online or in person. They did n ot establish much in the way of an information hub or artefact repository and there was a lack of clarity as to group and individual progress. They did not know what each member had completed, although EduC1’s online artefact, which was a timeline that they worked on together, showed eventual progress on the final product. One group member assured the others that they had made good progress but only added limited elements late in the project. The EduB2 group intended to be collaborative but fo und it difficult to coordinate work, with a surface approach to their subject. There were no shared res ources and the script for their product, a video, w as worked out without much exploration of the problem and at the time of recording by the group. To that extent, there was some limited shared conceptual development.

Four groups showed sustained conceptual development around shared and created objects, and so higher levels of knowledge creation. EduA2 and EngA2 worke d very much in concert with other group members: in person and online. Their conceptual development mos tly occurred through coordinated discussion and simultaneous work on shared artefacts. They best re presented the second classification of shared conce ptual development.

Individuals’ synthesizing objects The remaining two groups of EduD3 and EngC3 worked together in a similar way to those in the second classification, but also showed an added dimension,  leading to the third category of knowledge creatio n. The added dimension was the creation of substantial obj ects in artefact form by individuals, based on shar ed conceptual development.

The EduD3 group decided to make a video and in their first meeting ranged through a variety of ideas in an expansive discussion. Before the next meeting, one student had edited together a sample video and shared that with group members (a in Figure 1). The video elements represented ideas that the group had discussed and were linked by textual interstitials based on spoken and written words from their meeting. The student in an interview afterwards said that it was to progress the work of the group, which seemed to be slow in coming to a decision on what to do. Later in the same project, another student took images, videos and text shared and discussed by group members, and organized them into an illustrated storyboard with accompanying script (b in Figure 1). Again, this served to put concepts into a practical and organized format that allowed the group to finalize the structure of the video.

Two members of EngC3, Alex and Cameron, worked clos ely together on conceptual development for their project, a plan for building houses in a large village. After discussing materials and conditions for the housing with Cameron, Alex brought a paper sketch of a proposed house design to the next meeting. Discussion centered on the sketch, as Alex explained features and Cameron asked questions and suggested modifications. They each drew on the piece of paper as they considered how t o lay out houses in clusters with common walls. Ale x then brought a digital version of the house sketch to th e next session, which the two students refined by s earching materials and images online. Alex also started a diagram that planned the locations of houses. The initial layout frustrated Alex, as he likened the grid-like struct ure to a prison camp. Cameron then repeated an idea  from an ICLS2022 Proceedings 802 © ISLS earlier meeting related to a circular pattern with a community center, a model of which Alex found in a Google Images search. Alex then worked at home on a new version of the village layout that incorporated both Cameron’s ideas as well as new information Alex found about optimal orientation to the sun. The students who individually created objects in EduD3 and EngC3 did so because they saw a need for them: they were not directed or asked to build them as part of task instructions. The objects were each founded in work that the groups had done – concepts they had discussed and artefacts they had shared – but were shaped by the individual authors, who introduced further perspectives beyond that developed as a group. The objects served to put concepts into a practical form and progress the final product, organizing and expanding upon group ideas. The (semi-structured) heap of shared knowledge In the groups with higher levels of shared conceptual development, creating a common repository of knowledge, a type of ‘semi-structured heap’ of artefacts and c oncepts laid the foundation for knowledge creation. The heap could be comprised of various items: summaries of articles, images, spoken ideas, example videos, social media posts, budget tables, hand-written notes and diagra ms. This provided a common reference and shared con cepts and artefacts from which students could subsequentl y draw and relate tonew ideas and resources. The p roject diagrams used for analysis supported this idea of a  knowledge heap, showing, for example, how a concep t introduced in an earlier week was reprised and mixe d with new ideas in later discussion (see Figure 1 for an example). The building of this set of resources established the materials for the creation of shared epistemic objects, as well as for objects created by individuals. Stud ents in EduC1, EngB1 and EduB2 did not build as muc h of a base compared to the other groups, had little on which to draw in class discussion and were perceptibly hindered from making progress.

Tools and processes supporting distributed cognition Usage of tools in an infrastructure for collaborati on facilitated a persistent shared set of resources, visibility of progress and a window into team members’ activities. One element of infrastructure took the form in En gC3 of the two main collaborators seated next to each other and periodically using screens and paper diagrams to discuss parts of their solution. They had direct sensory inputs into each other’s current activity: overlooking each other’s computer screen; a frustrated sigh from one would prompt the other to look up and ask about the problem. The students in EngA2 used a common Google document  most of the length of the project, from recording initial ideas and links to resources, through to notes and final composition of the report. Progress in the document was immediately visible to all team members. This had been a new method set up for this project: they reported that they would use the same process for future collaborations. EduA2 and EduD3 groups were both active on Facebook Group spaces, sharing links, commentary and images. The features of this tool such as a ‘thumbs up’ and indication that all group members had seen a post also afforded immediate visibility of progress and the activity of members. The EngC3 group used social media more simply as a message board and place to share files.  Most of their knowledge work was done in person, w hich meant that the team members who were not present were necessarily uninformed of progress in the project. ICLS2022 Proceedings 803 © ISLS

## Discussion

The model of shared epistemic agency and activity i nfrastructure (Figure 2) relationally collates the elements I observed in the cases. The main elements are: 1) framing and definition of the problem, 2) the (semi-structured) heap of shared knowledge, 3) tools and processes su pporting distributed cognition, 4) individuals’ synthesizing objects and 5) work on the artefact(s) that are the  final output of the task. All groups achieved some  level of knowledge creation: those that paid attention to and established effective infrastructure across the e lements showed higher shared epistemic agency in knowledge creation. A lack in one element tended to indicate less effective knowledge co-creation, with synthesizing objects optional though important contributions. The epistemic work of groups was bound up in the ma terial ways they worked together, their infrastructure of collaboration, for example, what and how they posted in social media groups, wrote together in a Google doc, shared on paper and on screen in class. Accordingly, knowledge work and infrastructure are intrinsic parts of the same model. In addition, while the fin al product went from initial ideas to practical con struction, projects did not go through distinct phases: there were periods of making sense of the task, researching information and building constituent parts of an output along most of the timeline of each project. The model is therefore not linear, but relational, with each element built upon, and feeding into other elements, over time. The die is cast at the start. Because the tenor of collaboration was set early in each project, the previous experience of collaborative work and related skills would seem to be a large influence on the framing of the task and level of knowledge creation. The context of experience as well as looking towards future use are informed by Perkins et al.’s (1993) dispositions as well as agency defined by Emirbayer & Misch (1998). In their first conversati ons, groups made decisive judgements of the type of  work required as they read instructions for the task.

Student dispositions influenced the way they put the task in context and suggested ways of working. The dynamic of group discussion can change that framing, as in the EngC3 group, where they opted for a mor e collaborative approach, following one member’s stat ed preference for dividing work. The dispositions o f the individuals who created synthesizing objects were k ey to their actions: they saw the situation in a ce rtain way ICLS2022 Proceedings 804 © ISLS (sensitivity), elected to act on it (inclination) a nd brought their abilities to bear on a solution. T he experiences during work on the project potentially feed back to  the personal resources of students and in turn inf luence disposition towards, and constituent abilities for, future tasks. Tools and processes helped improve the conditions for epistemic agency. An effective infrastructure to support distributed cognition that clearly shared i deas and showed progress on the project helped esta blish a foundation for group members to recognize where the y could add value and progress the project. The way communication and collaboration tools were used ten ded to align with the initial framing of the projec t, for example, as either needing in-depth collaborative work or as one that could be divided into separate tasks. Neither wholly the creation of the author nor a group object, the objects created by individuals in EduD3 and EngC3 were interesting phenomena that indicated  a new type of epistemic object. The synthesizing object created by one team member allowed for expressing i ndividual perspectives and goals in the context of shared conceptual development. While not required in group  collaborative work–EduA2 and EngA2 constructed knowledge together effectively without major examples of such objects–it is a useful concept for understanding how individual contributions can translate or transform ideas, separately from but also contingent on group work. The shape of a shared semi-structured heap of knowledge and resources relied on the group recognizing a need for shared conceptual development as well as  establishing tools and processes that supported di stributed cognition. Work on the task’s final product and interim synthesizing objects helped organize, combine and make sense of the resources assembled by the group.

Production of the task artefact(s) in turn was influenced by the ways that the group framed their project, assembled knowledge and organized communications and collaboration. Synthesizing objects, where used, allowed individuals to contribute to the evolving product. Supporting this activity infrastructure and zone fo r shared epistemic agency are the various designed support activities and supplied resources that a course teacher may provide for the task, although in the end group dynamics may have the stronger influence on the shape of collaboration. A lack in one part of the model can affect the level of epistemic agency and more effective implementation of an element could possibly assist in improving the conditions for knowledge co-creation. For examp le, sharing online working documents from the begin ning may have helped increase shared conceptual development for the EngB1 group who, even when situated in the same physical space, tended to work on separate documents with limited discussion.

## Conclusion

The model for epistemic agency in group projects offers a framework for how students work on their group tasks and the elements that they coordinate in that work.  The model and the concept of a synthesizing object  were derived from seven project cases within two undergraduate courses. Their applicability beyond that setting is an avenue for further research.

Framing plays a large part in how a task is defined  and then the type of knowledge creation used in it. Some students will either be already disposed accor dingly or agree through the group dynamic to create knowledge collaboratively. Understanding how teams do this effectively can assist in design for knowle dge creation. The experiences that come before a project are potentially very influential on how students frame a new task: further research might consider the role of p revious experience and expected future utility in f raming of tasks, and how to translate this to design at a program level. The practical aspects of collaboration were set up to serve the task as students framed it, however these  might be influenced by designed supports. Setting time and tasks for problem exploration, mandating a shared o nline workspace and suggesting cloud-based collabor ative authoring of documents are some of the ways that the principles of distributed cognition can be made explicit for students.

The model pays attention to object use, which can i nform design for learning as well as research: who creates or contributes; what previous activities, o bjects and discussion are included or expanded in t he object; how the role of the object changes over time and ho w it contributes to the overall project. Iterativel y tracing modifications that teams and individuals make to ob jects helps researchers see how students make sense  of knowledge in the moment. Understanding the opportunities for contributions and the shapes those contributions potentially take can assist in designing supports and guidance for students. Epistemic agency can be expressed both collaboratively and individually. The construction of a shared ‘heap’ of collective resources forms a basis for effective group knowledge work on epistemic objects and provides opportunities for individuals to modify and expand ideas through synthesizing objects.
