# Teachers’ Dilemmatic Sensemaking as a Source of Knowledge Making: Explaining to Process and Explaining to Explore

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In recent years, there have been calls for teachers to engage in responsive instruction. Simultaneously, teacher education has focused on ‘best’ practices and routines to support classroom responsiveness. However, responsive instruction is definitionally non-routine, and existing professional development often fails to attend to the complexities of responsive instruction, often expressed as dilemmas. Our project collaborates with a professional development organization aimed at supporting teachers in equitable and ambitious mathematics teaching by facilitating video-based formative feedback (VFF) cycles, centered on teachers’ inquiry questions and representations of practice. Looking at VFF cycles of two experienced teachers, we found that both teachers grounded their dilemmatic sensemaking in explanations of tensions they experienced, but subsequent discussions differed. This analysis furthers understanding of teachers’ learning through collaborative talk and points to a need to attend to the dilemmatic nature of teaching to support educators developing responsive practice.

## Introduction

In recent years, there has been a growing recogniti on of the importance of responsive teaching in crea ting equitable, inclusive, and diverse mathematics class rooms (e.g., Bonner & Adams, 2012; Horn & Garner,  2022; Prediger, 2019). This instructional approach emphas izes the need for teachers to possess acute adaptab ility, allowing them to identify and respond to student ne eds and emerging learning opportunities in class (P arsons et al., 2018). Correspondingly, many professional development programs now aim to support teachers’ development of such methods (e.g., Gay, 2002; Philip, 2019). At  the same time, most professional development and t eacher education efforts center practice-based approaches, offering practical principles and resources while overlooking the complex and unpredictable nature of teaching itself, thus limiting attunement to the situated nature of teachers’ learning (Horn, 2019). In other words, standardized guidelines fo r responsive teaching exist in inherent tension with the goal of teachers navigating the complex and ever changing social and interactional dimensions  of classroom teaching (Lefstein & Snell, 2013).

The purpose of this study is to explore how experienced secondary mathematics teachers make sense of responsive teaching practices, expanding their unde rstandings of this work. In collaboration with a Pr ofessional Development Organization (PDO) supporting experienced secondary mathematics teachers, we deve loped a video-based formative feedback (VFF) cycle to invite participating teachers into co-analysis and reflection of their classroom instruction with their school-based colleges and university researcher s. Through engaging in VFF cycles with teachers, we noticed that teachers cons istently brought up dilemmas within their teaching practice. For example, teachers often brought up issues aroun d how to support student thinking and collaboration. Importantly, the issues and questions teachers rais ed were without clear or simple solutions. In our s tudy, such dilemmatic sensemaking opened up possibilities for rich, v aried learning. This study therefore seeks to explo re teachers’ dilemmatic sensemaking as a way to  better understand the theoretical underpinnings of how teachers learn to enact responsive practice.

## Theoretical perspectives

We approach this work from a situative view of teac her learning (Horn & Kane, 2019). According to situ ative perspectives, teacher learning is necessarily compl ex, and their contexts are fundamental to an analys is of their learning. Teachers negotiate new ideas and understandings abo ut teaching with their own conceptual understanding of their practice (Horn & Garner, 2022).  Furthermore, teacher learning occurs through a complex network of learning experiences  (Ehrenfeld, 2022). Situative perspectives allow for  analysis that centers the individual (or teacher) while also attending to the  contextual conditions that shape the individual and activity (or teaching).

Dilemmas as a site for teacher learning As we have previously described, responsive teaching is inherently nonroutine, and teachers must make sense of constant, emerging dilemmas. We conceptualize dilemmas as situations that are value-laden, filled with conflict, and do not have a solution or right course of actio n (Cuban, 1996; Windschitl, 2002). Dilemmas are dee ply contextual – rooted in teachers' local and institutional context s – and personal – connected to their sense of responsibility, obligation, and commitments.  We see these as different  from problems, as problems are talked about as having solutions. Dilemmas are “complex, untidy, and insoluble” (Cuban, 1996, p. 6), and the lack of clear solutions often raise competing tensions that teachers must then navigate. Indeed, generalized or procedural solutions are insufficient in managing recurring di lemmas in everyday te aching practice. Because dilemmas involve such tensions, we conceptualize dilemmas as  fruitful sites for teachers to explore their own i nstruction (Horn & Garner,  2022). Therefore, for the purposes of our study, we  view dilemmas that surfaced in the VFF process as “critical incidents that can be transfor mative sites for learning” (Shapira-Lishchinsky, 2011; CaspariGnann & Sevian, 2022). Most research has focused on exploring different types of dilemmas teachers face. For example, Suurtaam and Koch (2014) operationalized Windschitl’s (2002) dilemmas framework by looking at the conceptual, pedagogical, cultural and political dil emmas that teachers face when trying to enact new a ssessment practices within their classrooms. Similarly,  McKay (2016) explored dilemmas that pre-service teachers experience with creating inclusive classrooms.  We seek to extend this work by arguing that dilemma s are not abstract, universal situations but instead, (1) the y exist within dilemmatic spaces and (2) they offer important resources for teachers.

From dilemmas to dilemmatic spaces Although dilemmas are experienced universally by teachers, the ways that teachers experience and make sense of dilemmas is not generalizable. Scholars often refer to teachers’ unique contexts, the decisions they make, and the way they make sense of these decisions in relation to their practice as dilemmatic spaces (Honig, 1996; Fransson & Grannäs, 2013). Dilemmatic spaces are “social constructions resulting from structural conditions and relational aspects in everyday practices'' in which the emergi ng dilem ma resides (Fransson & Grannäs, 2013, p. 7). Dilemmatic spaces provide opportunities to examine how individuals interact and position themselves within their own contexts. This conceptual framing of dilemmatic  spaces acknowledges negotiations, positionings, and structural dynamics, which become important analytical tools for analyzing and understanding the complexity of how teachers make sense of dilemmas related to thei r instructional practice. These interactions work a cross individuals, their contexts, and broader systems.

The framing of dilemmatic space has been taken up i n a variety of ways within educational research. For example, Lilach (2020) used it to explore the e thical dilemmas that preservice teachers experience  during their practicum. Ding and Wang (2020) looked at the  sources of dilemmas and decision-making with Taiwanese pre-service teacher candidates. We extend this research  by focusing on a different group of teachers and m oving beyond description. We look at experienced teachers and unpack the complex processes through which they learn within and from the dilemmatic space. Through refle ctive conversations in dilemmatic spaces, teachers develop deeper understandings of the tensions they are navi gating and generate ideas about how to respond to t hem. We call this development dilemmatic awareness.  Specifically, dilemmatic awareness is an articul ation of different positionings in relation to structural or situational forces. In interaction, as teachers articulate different dimensions of the dilemmatic space, this awareness of how different dimensions are related to one anot her yields dynamic understandings. When they are newer to the contours  of these tensions, teachers might articulate dimen sions of the dilemmatic space with a more emerging understanding of how they’re positioned in relation  to one another. As the tensions become familiar terrain, teachers m ay have well-articulated, emerged awareness of the various positionings. We do not take an evaluative stance towards differing degrees of dilemmatic awareness. Rather, we present this idea as a way to illustrate the dynami c and interactional nature of teachers’ exploration  of and engagement with dilemmatic spaces. We argue that teachers’ sensemaking within this dilemmatic space is a form of teacher learning. As their dilemmatic awareness emerges or gets refined, teachers produce new understandings and approaches to managing everyday problems of practice. Taking up this interactional conceptualization around teacher learning in dilemmatic spaces aligns with w ork in teacher learning and teacher education that highlig hts the importance of social dimensions and resources to teacher learning (Horn & Kane, 2015; Lefstein et al., 2020).

## Methods

To explore varied ways teachers make sense of the d ilemmatic aspects of their practice, we draw from a  larger, multi-year research-practice partnership between university researchers, teachers, and a well-regarded professional development organization (PDO) in a large urban school district in the Western U nited States. The focus of this partnership is on career-long learning of secondary mathematics teachers in urban schools as they work toward enacting equitable and ambitious mathem atics instruction. We worked closely with 12 experi enced high school mathematics teachers to support their r eflection and inquiry into their practice using a v ideo-based formative feedback (VFF) cycle. The  VFF cycle unfolds as follows: (1) a focal teacher i dentifies a question of interest (e.g., am I intervening with groups equitably?); (2) the day of the lesson, the teacher identifies three focal student groups to record; (3) the research team documents a lesson with the question in mind, using a whole-class camera, GoPro 360o cameras for the three student groups, and a point-of-view camera attached to the teacher; (4) with the teacher’s question in mind, the team reviews the video and creates a visual representation of the teacher’s movement and talk with a tool called the Interactio n Geography Slicer (IGS; Shapiro & Garner, 2022); (5) the teacher, their colleagues, and researchers discuss the teacher’s questions using the video and IGS representations in a subsequent, video-recorded debrief. This analysis focuses on the debr iefs. At the time of this analysis, we completed one year of data collection in which we conducted VFF cycles with 10 total teachers. For each debrief, we captured video and screen recor dings and created transcripts of the video recordin gs. After each VFF cycle, we wrote reflective memos that summarized the data collection events (e.g. classroom and debrief filmi ngs). In particular, these memos summarized debrief conversa tions – what clips we shared with teachers, how teachers responded to the video clips and IGS, and what ques tions, tensions, and ideas were raised in the resul ting conversations. We shared these memos with the entire research team after each VFF cycle to reflect on the debrief and data collection process. Across these debriefs,  we noticed that conversations often raised and cen tered dilemmas. This led us to our initial phase of analysis which we will describe in the next section.

## Phase 1 data analysis: Identifying episodes of dilemmatic sensemaking

In our first phases of analysis, where we aimed to identify moments where teachers expressed a dilemma  within their practice through open coding (Saldaña, 2021).  Initially, we looked for linguistic cues where tea chers expressed uncertainty, challenges, or emotional discomfort about a given topic. For example, we noted moments where teachers would say, “I don’t know,” “I wonder about…”, things being “tricky,” etc. We also noted moments where teachers’ expressed negative emotions such as “feeling uncomfortable.” We created analytic memos (Miles et al., 2020) to summarize episodes in which dilemm as emerged. These memos described what happened bef ore and after the dilemma was introduced and described the dilemma itself. Furthermore, informed by Engeström and Sannino’s (2011) methodological framework of identifying manifestations of dilemmas, our memos attempted to parse out what values were in tension with one anot her. Across the 10 VFFs, we identified over 20 epis odes in which dilemmas emerged in the debr ief conversations. These episodes varied in length – some episodes were relatively short (< 45 sec) whereas other episodes occurred over longer, more sustained conversations (> 15 min).

## Phase 2 data analysis: Comparative case analysis

For the second phase of the analysis, we selected t wo focal episodes for a closer, in depth, discourse  analysis. Case 1 centers a teacher team: Sofia, Lizette, and Julie who taught at Fermat High School, a title I s chool that served a diverse population of students. At the time of filming Sofia and Julie ha d taught for over five years and Lizette for over 10 years. For this VFF cycle, Sofi a served as the focal teacher. We filmed her Algebr a 1 class, which both Lizette and Julie were also teaching tha t year. Sofia was particularly interested in exploring how she was supporting student talk in the classroom video.  Case 2 centers a teacher whom we call Clark. At th e time of his debrief, Clark had been teaching for over 15 ye ars and taught at Banneker High Schoo l, a title I school that served majority Latinx students. We filmed a lesson  from his AP Calculus AB class, and he was interest ed in discussing how his assessment practices were visible in his interactions with students.

Using the framing of professionalizing discourse (Horn & Kane, 2019), we began to look at how meaning was made in practice, specifically how discourse “(1) support[s] teachers in delving beyond taking-as-shared meanings by encouraging and expecting dialogic sens emaking, and (2) [makes] use of the representationa l function of language” (p 20). In particular, we looked  at instances where teachers added on clarifying details about their practice as well as when teachers made use of representations such as rehearsals or replays of their practice (Horn, 2005). From there, we memoed about various featur es of the discourse that we noticed: the temporality of words (i.e., modal or past tense ver bs), the word choice employed (i.e., expressing emo tions or feelings,) and how these features functioned as a r esource for engaging in professionalizing discourse. We memoed extensively about each case and noticed key discursive differences. We selected the focal cases  for this analysis for two reasons. First, in both Clark and Sofia’s team debriefs, there were extended episodes of dilemma sensemaking. Sofia and her colleagues’ episode last ed around 8 minutes. Clark’s episode of dilemmatic sensemaking lasted around 17 minutes. Second, the conversations and sensemaking around the dilemmas unfolded in notably different ways, offering a generative contrast. We conceptual ize these cases as comparative and exploratory (Yin, 2017) to help us theorize teacher sensemaking about responsive practice.

## Findings

In the following sections, we share two cases of di lemmatic sensemaking. Across the two cases, dilemma tic sensemaking emerged topically, and organically. At the same time, dilemmatic sensemaking served two different purposes. In the first case, Sofia along with her colleagues, Lizette and Julie, co-articulated the dilemmatic space which allowed them to also emotionally process thei r shared experiences. In the second case, Clark art iculated dimensions of and interactions within the dilemmatic space and explored future possibilities for different courses of action.

Sofia, Lizette and Julie: Explaining to emotionally process dilemmatic spaces Sofia’s debrief occurred after school in her classr oom with three researchers and her collaborating te achers, Lizette and Julie. The debrief was around 80 minute s long. For her VFF cycle, one of Sofia’s inquiry q uestions revolved around how she supported student talk thro ughout her lesson. To help her think about this que stion, researchers made a geospatial visual representation of her movement overlaid with the different types of talk that she engaged in with students. About 30 minutes into  the debrief, th e researchers shared the representation of practice with Sofia, Lizette and Julie, and the researcher inquired, “Do you think about how you're leaving groups, or is it just in the moment? Do you kind of plan out different questions?” This prompted Sofia to express that she “doesn’t intentionally plan out questions,” but did try to engage other students in the group by directing questions to them because she wanted “them to learn how to wo rk, how to ask for help.” Julie took on this idea t hat Sofia had offered and raised a dilemma that she had been facing in her classroom: I'm struggling with this a lot more post Covid. We keep marking that, that pandemic thing. Where I go up and there's one kid with a question, I'm like, “Oh, they have it. Explain how you did that. And then they do… but they're not coming up with the ideas together. It's like someone with it just helps tutor the team, which feels like a very different thing. Julie recognized that her students worked together,  but they weren’t generating ideas together. Her ob servation was in tension with what she knew and valued as a t eacher working toward responsive practice. Julie’s dilemma of trying to support students in collaboratively working together as opposed to simply working independently and getting answers was situated within a larger contex t. In this case, she positioned and evoked three di mensions of the dilemmatic space:  her students, teaching as a practice, and the COVID-19 pandemic. Furthermore, she positioned herself in relation to all three dimensions: COVID-19 was a global event that people have lived through, students were engaging in work but were currently struggling to collaborate, and herself as a teacher who wanted kids to work together to come up with mathematical ideas (Figure 1A). While Julie offered the initial articulation of these dimensions of the dilemmatic space, she, Lizette, and Sofia continued to further articulate and explore the dilemmatic space together as the conversation u nfolded. However, how these dimensions are currentl y positioned in relation to one another when thinking  about how to support kids in buildin g ideas together is emerging.

Importantly, he  interrogated his stance (that he often communicate d to students) that “AP tests [don’t] matter.” While that stance aligned with his own views of accountability, he asked an important question: “I don’t know if that’s right, or if I’m doing a disservice to parts of this community,” which is predominantly low-income, first generation, Latinx students (Figure 2E). Throughout this sensemaking episode, Clark articulated a dilemma involving his grading s ystem and has an emerged dilemmatic awareness of positioning of different dimensions in the dilemmat ic space. He positioned his students as ones who en gage in disciplinary work without a grade, but also acknowl edged that they are vulnerable to grading as a clas sroom practice outside of his classroom. He positioned himself with expertise as someone who has tried different systems and found that this grading system currently feels like a good option. At the same time, he also posit ioned his students as participants within a broader context, one where students experience accountability system s where grades function differently, and might function as an indicator of potential preparedness for passing the AP exam. He then began to wonder, “maybe if I just was really, really explicit or could communicate that to them” and started to explore alternative possibilities for his grading system – one where his ideas of accountability were made more visible to his students. Clark enlivened multiple possibilities that could exist, such as giving stude nts feedback on their progress for the day and filling that information in the gradebook, but then letting students “pick [their] grade at the end.” He also proposed the idea of giving students the opportunity to “assess themselves” and then he can “send over feedback on Desmos.” Across the multiple, future-oriented ideas that he proposed, his future imaginings were aligned with his ideas of ac countability as making mathematics progress and hav ing a positive experience in his class. In this way, his sensemaking and emerged dilemmatic awareness served  as a resource for him to actively explore future-options for his grading system.

## Discussion

When looking at two cases of how teachers made sense of responsive instructional practice, we found that within both VFF’s, teachers articulated dilemmas and co-constructed dimensions of the dilemmatic space as t hey made sense of, reflected on, and explained their dilemma s. At the same time, the way sensemaking unfolded l ed to qualitatively different forms of learning – emotionally processing their experiences and imagining future courses of action.

Sofia and her colleagues’ dilemma emerged from an I GS representation created by the researchers as a way to support inquiry into Sofia’s question around how she supported student talk. This representation, alongside clarifying questions from the researcher, set up the conditions for Sofia and her colleagues to begin to make sense of enacting responsive teaching after a cataclysmic  event: the COVID-19 pandemic.  On the other hand, Clark’s inquiry question explored how he made his assessment practices visible to his students. While the debrief involved both video clips and geo-spatial representations of his classroom, Clark’s  topical interest of exploring accountability led him to articulate to the researchers what he wanted to talk about. From there, he began to make sense about his own grading system and the tensions  he experienced with conflicting responsibilities i nvolving accountability metrics –both his own and the institutional value placed on grading and AP test scores. This shows that the ways in which dilemmas emerge and are discursively articulated can vary and do not necessarily occur in a one-size fits all or standardized format Sofia and her colleagues had an emergent dilemmatic  awareness. While all three teachers contributed toward articulating various dimensions of the dilem matic space discursively, articulations about how t hese dimensions were interacting (or not) with one anoth er was in the process of being parsed out. However,  an important feature emerged throughout their sensemak ing: these three teachers were not alone in what th ey were experiencing and opened up the possibility for expe riencing the dilemma as “less stressful and not a g round for destructive self-blame among teachers” (Fransson & Grannäs, 2013, p 14). All three teachers were navigating and negotiating various positionings within the dilemma tic space, and how those positionings related and c onnected to one another was emergent during this episode. On  the other hand, Clark articulated positionings in the dilemmatic space that afforded him the opportunity to examine how his own ideas about accountability conflicted with the institutional value placed on merit-based grading and AP test scores. Throughout the sense making episode, Clark articulated how he, his students, and accountability systems were all positioned relati ve to one another. In this case, Clark engaged with us, as re searchers, as a sounding board to talk through thin gs he had already been thinking about, which prompted him to explore alternative courses of action. Because Clar k's dilemmatic awareness was more fully emerged compare d to Sofia and her colleagues, he was able to move into more exploratory sensemaking. Whereas, Sofia and her colleagues’ conversation served as a time to emotionally process together. This is not to say that one degree of dilemmatic awareness is better than another, but to illustrate how dilemmatic sensemaking can lead to different forms of learning.  We characterized Sofia and her colleagues’ dilemmatic sensemaking as explaining to emotionally process.  Through this sensemaking, the teachers engaged in a shared experience of processing feelings toward enacting responsive teaching post COVID-19. This emotional processing led to commiserating and shared understa nding of their current teaching reality. Discursive ly, this came through recalls of past teaching experiences, as well as talking about current feelings within the classroom. We characterized Clark’s dilemmatic sensemaking as explaining to explore. As Clark reflected on and explained his teaching practice, he also began to explore alt ernative possibilities in his grading system. His dilemmatic sensemaking provided Clark with the opportunity to reimagine future possibilities for himself and his students regarding his grading system. Both forms of sensema king brought forward new understandings for th e teachers, and both forms serve an important purpose in supporting teachers’ and their learning. This paper makes three contributions. First, by ill ustrating the situational specificity of teachers’ dilemmas in responsive instruction, we highlight the limits of universal prescriptions for instruction. Second, this analysis furthers understanding of th e learning resources in teachers’ collaborative tal k by identifying learning processes grounded in dilemmatic sensemaking. Final ly, by highlighting dilemmatic sensemaking as a res ource for teacher learning, this work points to a need to attend to the dilemmatic nature of teaching to enable educators in developing responsive practice. A dilemmatic fra ming of teaching presents an onto-epistemic shift in what teaching is and what it means to know how to do it,  one that embraces the uncertainty inherent to resp onsive instruction.
