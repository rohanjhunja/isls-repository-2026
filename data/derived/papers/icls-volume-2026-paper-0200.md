# Investigating Mid Semester Experiences of Primary Source Projects in Mathematics through Learning Experience Network Analysis

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study investigates undergraduate experiences with Primary Source Projects (PSPs) at the midpoint of a History of Mathematics course. We examined reflections from the third and fifth PSPs with learning experience network analysis (LENA) to understand how student strategies evolve after the initial adjustment period. The analysis revealed five clusters capturing key struggles and successes. Students reported continued difficulty interpreting historical notation but valued opportunities to contextualize ideas and compare them with modern methods. Collaboration consistently supported learning, with teamwork and discussion enhancing comprehension of complex arguments. These mid-semester findings illustrate how student experiences develop over time. They show collaboration shifting from a helpful supplement to a critical tool for managing conceptual complexity and point to design considerations for history-based mathematics instruction.

Over the last decade, the TRansforming Instruction in Undergraduate Mathematics via Primary Historical Sources (TRIUMPHS) project has developed peer reviewed Primary Source Projects (PSPs) for undergraduate mathematics and made them widely available, as described by Barnett et al. (2014) and by the National Science Foundation (2015). These classroom materials share certain features and are designed such that multiple PSPs can be used within courses. The experiences of students engaging in PSPs can be expected to be different in the beginning of a course when this type of learning activity is novel than later in a course when students have internalized basic routines related to PSPs and begin to plan, monitor, and develop socially shared regulation (Järvelä et al., 2018). By the middle of a semester in an undergraduate course, for instance, the PSP functions as a shared artifact that groups use to plan, monitor, and adapt strategies together (Cress & Kimmerle, 2018). The distinctive feature of PSPs lies in how they immerse students in the original processes of mathematical discovery. Rather than receiving results in finalized form, learners are asked to work through problems as they were originally encountered, interpreting unusual symbols, reasoning within shifting conceptual frameworks, and attempting arguments that differ from modern textbook routines (Jankvist, 2014). This approach positions mathematics as an evolving human pursuit. It highlights the field’s cultural embeddedness and connections to broader intellectual traditions (Danish & Gresalfi, 2018; Massa-Esteve, 2025). Learning sciences research explores how student experiences, motivation, and engagement develop over time within designed environments. After an initial adjustment, the experience of students midway through a course can be quite different than their experi ences in the beginning. Learners at this stage may move from an initial situational interest toward a more developed individual and socioculturally situated interest which alters how they approach challenges (Renninger et al., 2018). This period provides a  valuable perspective on how students begin to reconcile their prior approaches with new sociocultural expectations (Danish & Gresalfi, 2018). This study contributes to such process-oriented work by analyzing how PSPs shape student perceptions of mathematics after novelty has passed (Clark et al., 2022). In this spirit, the present study analyzes student reflections from the middle of a semester-long course taught exclusively with PSPs, focusing on how learners constructed knowledge and navigated difficulties after the initial adjustment period but prior to the final synthesis of the course. This research represents the initial phase of a design-based research effort (Barab, 2022) that examines how students experience and respond to historically grounded, problem-based instruction in mathematics. To capture learning processes from the student perspective, we used learning experience network analysis (LENA; Donaldson et al., 2024). LENA combines qualitative coding with social network analysis, allowing us to map and interpret relationships among ideas expressed in student reflections written during the middle of the course. 1482 Data were collected during the Fall 2024 semester in a History of Mathematics course at an R1 university in the southeastern United States. The course enrolled 18 students—17 undergraduates and one graduate student — distributed across mathematics (7), computer science (6), and engineering (5). The curriculum traced the development of mathematical ideas over time, beginning with number theory (Pythagorean triples, Euclidean algorithm), moving through early algebra, trigonometry from ancient Greece through the  Renaissance, and culminating with early calculus (17th–19th centuries). The course was taught entirely through nine PSPs, of which students completed seven fully and two partially.

This study focuses on reflections associated with PSPs 3 (Completing the Square: From the Roots of Algebra starting with al- Khwārizmī) and PSP 5 (The Trigonometric Functions Through Their Origins: Hipparchus’ Table of Chords), which occurred in the middle of the semester. By this point, students had moved beyond their initial adjustment to PSP-based learning and were beginning to refine strategies for working with historical texts and problem sets. A total of 34 reflections were collected and coded using a collaboratively developed codebook. The final codebook included 42 codes, which were applied across 353 text segments. Two researchers independently coded all reflections, and weekly discussions with a third researcher ensured consensus, supporting inter-rater reliability and consistency in code interpretation. After coding, we generated a correlation matrix of co-occurring codes across all reflections. These results were visualized in a network map, with node size representing betweenness centrality to highlight which concepts functioned as central connectors in  student thinking at this stage of the course. To identify cohesive thematic clusters, we conducted a Girvan–Newman cluster analysis (Girvan & Newman, 2002). This approach enabled us to detect subgroupings of ideas and interpret patterns in how students were making sense of mathematics midway through their PSP-based learning experience.

The LENA process produced a network map (Figure 1), identifying correlations at the p < 0.05 level. Analysis with the Girvan–Newman approach revealed five well-defined clusters (Q = 0.513), each illustrating a particular combination of student obstacles, achievements, and theoretical insights tied to the learning experience. In the pink down triangles cluster, students emphasized that clear instructions and consistent expectations were central to their learning. Within both the Hipparchus and al -Khwārizmī PSPs, the step-by-step structure provided a stable framework that allowed students to focus on making sense of the mathematics rather than being overwhelmed by ambiguity. With this clarity in place, students described gaining new awareness, making meaningful connections across tasks, and recognizing the broader relevance of the material. These outcomes align with transformative learning theory, particularly in how learners reported shifts in perspective and deeper approaches to problem-solving. A possible design move is to provide checkpoint callout boxes after each step or mini-task, prompting students to briefly record what action they just completed and why it works. This structure can be reinforced with micro-summaries, where students write one or two sentences at the end of each section to capture the key idea. Together, these checkpoints and summaries create regular moments of clarity, helping students monitor their understanding and connect new insights across tasks. In the light blue squares cluster, students’ main struggles involved the challenge of historical notation and terminology. For example, in the Hipparchus PSP, students had to interpret sexagesimal numeration and arc– chord relationships, while in the al- Khwārizmī PSP they worked to translate rhetorical descriptions of “roots, squares, and simple numbers” into modern symbolic algebra. Many found it difficult to reconcile these historical approaches with the modern methods they were more familiar with, especially when symbols or conventions did not align neatly. Despite these difficulties, students expressed appreciation for the historical context and valued the opportunity to compare past and present perspectives. These outcomes align with cognitive constructi vist theory, which emphasizes how grappling with multiple representations deepens learning. A possible design move is to use a translation t-chart, placing historical terms or notations in one column and their modern equivalents in another, and to extend this by arranging parallel lines of historical procedures and modern symbolic methods sideby-side. This combined structure supports students as they decode unfamiliar language, align it with familiar concepts, and directly see how historical reasoning maps onto the methods they know today. The green diamonds cluster highlights the strong role of social learning in the PSPs. Students in this group benefited from teamwork, discussions, and collaborative problem-solving, which consistently deepened their understanding. In both the Hipparchus and al -Khwārizmī projects, opportunities to compare interpretations—whether of chord tables or rhetorical algebraic procedures —were especially effective when discussed in groups. Students also noted that visual representations, such as polygon constructions or geometric demonstrations of completing the square, supported their reasoning and gave multiple entry points for dialogue. These experiences align closely with social constructivist principles, which emphasize the importance of scaffolding from peers and instructors, engaging with multiple perspectives, and building knowledge collectively. 1483 No additional design moves are needed beyond ensuring collaborative discussion remains consistent, since students already reported strong benefits from teamwork and shared interpretation. At the same time, students noted challenges such as difficulty applying certain concepts and gaps in background knowledge. These experiences resonate with cognitive constructivist learning theory, which emphasizes the importance of activating prior knowledge, consolidating new information, and reinforcing concepts through repeated connections. A possible design move is to begin lessons with 2-Minute knowledge activation warm-ups that refresh prerequisite skills such as factoring, polygon properties, or base-60 numeration, and to pair these with resources for background knowledge such as quick-reference glossaries or guided search prompts. Together, these supports activate prior knowledge, lower barriers tonew content, and allow students to independently revisit foundational concepts when gaps arise.

The purple up triangles cluster reflects students who approached learning through structured, step-bystep methods and who valued the opportunity to compare alternative approaches. They also noted moments of realizing the broader impact of mathematical exp erts. Their main difficulty arose in interpreting visual representations—for example, working with geometric diagrams in the trigonometry PSP or understanding the quadrate construction in the algebra PSP. These findings connect to metacognitive theory, particularly the practice of monitoring one’s own strategies and reflecting on how learning unfolds. A possible design move is to implement an N-N-R routine (notice–name–relate), guiding students to first list visible components of a diagram, then assign meaning to each element, and finally describe the relationships that tie the parts together. This scaffold helps students interpret visuals systematically and extract conceptual meaning. The analysis of PSP 3 and PSP 5 reveals several themes that both parallel and extend those found later in the semester. Students consistently struggled with historical engagement, particularly reconciling al -Khwārizmī’s rhetorical algebra or Hipparchus’ sexagesimal notation with modern approaches (light blue squares). While these challenges sometimes developed into broader conceptual difficulties as the material grew more abstract, collaboration emerged as a positive counterbalance (green diamonds). Initial ly seen as an enrichment tool, collaboration evolved into a critical coping strategy for managing increasingly complex content. Persistent difficulty with applying concepts (yellow circles) echoed later struggles with comprehension and transfer, suggesting that application remains a central challenge of the PSP model. Finally, although clarity of instructions was a strength earlier in the course (pink down triangles), later reflections highlighted greater ambiguity and the need for teacher guidance, underscoring the shift in student experience as tasks demanded deeper abstraction and independent reasoning.

## Discussion & Implications

These findings suggest several design considerations. Scaffolding the interpretation of historical notation should be continuous and revisited throughout the course, rather than treated as a one-time hurdle. For example, repeated opportunities to practice translating al- Khwārizmī’s “roots and squares” into modern symbolic algebra or converting Hipparchus’ sexagesimal numeration into degrees would reduce the frustration students described when first encountering unfamiliar terms. Opportunities for collaboration should also be intentionally structured and expanded as the material grows more complex, allowing students to rely on both peer and teacher scaffolding when interpreting challenging representations. Low-stakes application activities can bridge the gap between historical understanding and practical use, such as linking Hipparchus’ chord values to modern sine functions or framing al -Khwārizmī’s “dirhams” problems in contemporary applied contexts. Finally, instructors should anticipate the transition from clarity to ambiguity, preparing students for more conceptually difficult topics by gradually fostering reflection, perspective-taking, and comfort with open-ended problem solving. This study is limited by the small sample size and the context of a single course implementation. Even so, applying learning experience network analysis across different points in the semester provides valuable insight into how student experiences with PSPs evolve. By identifying both enduring struggles and shifting needs, this work highlights ways to refine instruction and help students engage more deeply with mathematics through its historical development. More broadly, these findings underscore the value  of historically based approaches in supporting not only mathematical understanding but also the development of reflective, collaborative, and adaptive learning practices.
