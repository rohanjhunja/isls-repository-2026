# How do Key Network Actors Shape Discourse in Twitter Hashtag Streams? A Two-Year Comparative Analysis of an Agricultural Educators' Conference

**Conference:** ISLS 2023

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This research examines the network structure and discourse types of Twitter hashtag streams related to a global agricultural educators’ conference. Our focus was to examine patterns of network interaction and discourse types over two years to understand knowledge sharing and diffusion within the space. We conducted social network analyses and discourse coding of approximately 1390 tweets. Our findings showed that a set of common active, key participants emerged over both years and that knowledge sharing was heavily influenced by these key participants. Most original tweets were related to the conference and almost half of the tweets for each year were retweets. Participants fell into distinct participation categories based on intensity and frequency of tweeting. Network and discourse analyses together indicate that the hashtag streams functioned as affinity spaces around an interest in agricultural learning.

## Introduction

Social media have opened possibilities for individuals to learn in a variety of  informal contexts (Bell et al., 2009). Twitter is a popular tool to support educators’ informal learning, just-in-time professional development, and as a backchannel to connect participants during academic and professional conferences (Greenhow et al., 2019). We examined Twitter engagements in an annual agricultural educators’ conference, Global Learning in Agriculture (GLAG), which provides opportunities for educators at various levels and contexts  to engage in professional learning and connect with other educators around shared ideas. Conference particip ants could be considered a community of practice (COP) (Lave & Wenger, 1991) due to their commitment to agricultural education. The integration of networked technologies and social media into the conference broadens par ticipation opportunities via the use of the GLAG hashtag (#GLAG) allowing the framing of these  interaction as affinity spaces (Gee,

2005) or as hybrid learning communities (James & Busher, 2013) that employ multi-modal methods for engaging

in practice and learning. Prior research on Twitter use in academic conf erences has focused on exploring how it supports professional learning and participation (Greenhow et al., 2019), information shar ing (Wilkinson et al., 2015), and the uses of Twitter by conference participants (Luzón & Albero-Posac, 2020). Other work has explored what Twitter reveals about the communities, connections, and flow of informa tion within conference backchannels (Williamson, 2021). While most studies have looked at either discourse  or structure of the community, we bring together the focus of prior work by examining the structure of interactions and the types of tweets within the Twitter space over two consecutive years. We draw on sociocultural frameworks of learning to understand how knowledge sharing is mediated via community structure and discourse. For  example, how donetwork structures and discourse allow us to uncover individuals’ peripheral o r central participation (Lave & Wenger, 1991) in the space and what kinds of knowledge are shared (e.g., Gee, 2005)?

## Research questions and methods

The primary research question guiding this study was: What insights donetwork structur es and key discourses taken together provide about who shares knowledge and what type of knowledge is shared within this space? We collected tweets indexed with the #GLAG20 and #GLAG21 hashtags during each conferenc e timeframe using a social media aggregator, Keyhole. 1389 tweets were collected over the entire time frame, with 667 tweets for

#GLAG20 and 722 tweets for #GLAG21. Data were downloaded as csv files and c leaned prior to analysis. We

conducted social network analyses using Gephi, which provides visualizations as well metrics related to the structure of the network and actor activity. To examine types of discourse, we used an open coding process  built on prior work (Ebner et al., 2010). After multiple rounds of refining codes and the ir definitions, we identified a set of seven codes including: Online Presence (OP) where tweets expr essed the author’s digital identity and emotion; Conference Logistics and Schedule (CLS); Sharing Personal Opinions or Ideas (SOI); Sharing Resources (SR); Community Building (CB) where tweets reference the community and joint action of some type including posing questions and soliciting responses; Session Commentary (SC) where tweets comment provided an opinion or summary of conference sessions; and, Miscellaneous (MI), where tweets did not fit prior categories CSCL 2023 Proceedings  © ISLS 214 or were incomplete text fragments. Two raters independently coded all or iginal tweets. Differences between coders in assigning final categorization were resolved via discussion. Retwee ts were not given a separate code because the actor’s retweet intent was undeterminable. Only one code was assigned for each original tweet.

## Findings

We first present network structures and participants over the two years, fol lowed by the types of discourse over the two years, followed by considerations of both data streams in framing the space and knowledge sharing.

## Network structures and participants over the two years

Our final data set contained 1389 tweets and 394 unique actors over the two years. We generated sociograms and degree metrics for all individual actors. Table 1 shows the degree metrics for the top five actors over the two years and uncovers the emergence of a group of key actors that were highly active and central within the networks. Key actors across both years are glob, Fost, and GlMe (note: we have only used the 4 first characters of the twitter ids to avoid direct identification and maintain privacy of the actors). These th ree key actors together account for the greatest number of messages (as identified by degree values) and have togethe r sent (out-degree) and received (in-degree) the most tweets. During GLAG20 they are also highest for betweennes s centrality meaning they are highly influential in how information flows through the network and act as connectors be tween different actors. In GLAG21, however, we see other actors who have lower degree values but higher betweenness centrality values such as JenW (degree 20; betweenness 3194, which is higher than that of GlMe). ## Combined results of network and discourse analyses

We examined the structure of the network and the types of discourse to better  understand knowledge sharing within the space. We looked at the discourse types most often used by key actors over both years and as visible in Figure 1, the top three key actors, glob, Fost, and GlMe, engaged in mostly consistent types of interaction over the two years. For example, glob had the highest number of tweets related to conference logistics and schedules (CLS) and retweeted the most of any of the actors in both years. Similarly, Fost had the second highest number of retweets of all actors but few original tweets. Despite a high betweenness centrality value,  GlMe posted a fraction of tweets and retweets as compared to glob and Fost. Other trends in actor posting were observed. For example, RCMc posted both in 2020 and 2021 and was the fifth most central actor in both years. T he dominant discourse type for RCMc’s posts was Online Presence (OP) and was the account with the highest number of posts in this category. Another actor, hsin who was active in 2021 presents an interesting contrast to RCMc. hsin posted a high number of tweets and used a variety of discourse types, with at least one post falling into each of the seven categories. ## Discussion

Our research goal was to examine both structure and type of interaction in Twitter hashtag streams for the GLAG conference over the years 2020 and 2021 with the intent of understanding knowledge sharing. O ur findings indicate that information flow within the network is influenced by key actors suc h as glob, Fost, and GlMe. The role of active actors and their role in sustaining participation and information sharing has been documented on the web as the long tail of participation (Anderson, 2004). Our work offers a direct view into the role that key actors play in sustaining and shaping participation within smaller, online networks suggesting tha t the type of information that is disseminated within the network is highly influenced by tweets sent and received by key actors. We identified different roles that users take on in this network. When we focused on the top 10 influential actors, we identified distinct participation categories based on frequency, number of posts, and duration of involvement. The first category includes actors who identify as conference organizers, the second category includes participants who are highly engaged over the duration of conference, whom we classified as highly engaged long-term or short-term participants. The last category of participants with low activity  and engagement were classified as tangential participants. Based on earlier work exploring online engagement profiles, we note similarities between organizers and loyal and hardworking engagement profiles (Aristeidou et al., 2017)  characterized by steady, long term, and high rates of participation. The highly engaged participants in our study participate with a blend of loyal and persistent engagement including  a tendency to return to the community over ti me engaging in consistent posting activity (Ponciano & Brasileiro, 2014). Our last category of tangential users is most akin to visitors (Aristeidou et al., 2017) who appear once or twice and have low engagement in the activity. Another research goal was to understand participant use of Twitter. Most tweets related to confer ence logistics and scheduling (CLS) matching trends in other conferences (Bert et al., 2016). Retweets we re the largest proportion of overall tweets and usually related to the conference; the second most frequently retweete d posts were related to online presence, defined as positioning one’s digital identity or expressing emotion. This activity i s consistent with research on other conference related backchannels, which suggest that individuals are likely to promote their own research or work (Ebner et al., 2010; Greenhow et al., 2019) but confirms that  tweets with emotion-related content are more likely to be retweeted (So et al., 2016). Our analyses also indicate  that the hashtag stream functions as an affinity space that welcomes participation by anyone regardless of experie nce or status and offers open mechanisms for distributing and sharing knowledge and resources (Gee, 2005) relate d to global teaching and learning in agriculture.

CSCL 2023 Proceedings  © ISLS 216 Future directions Multiple opportunities exist to deepen and broaden our exploration of the use of Twitter by participants. We plan to explore how continued participation by key members shapes the space. Bommarito (2014) calls for research on the sense of “belongingess” tha t may exist in affinity spaces to expand the explanatory power of the concept  of affinity spaces. This sense of belongingness was visible in the Online Presence (OP) category, where participants often revealed emotion or engaged in social chit-chat. Thematic analyses of how topics, hashtags, resources, and other artifacts affects information flow and participant connections within the space would also provide an insight into how participants use different linguistic and communicative functions within their interaction (e.g., Luzón & Albero-Posac, 2020). Finally, Twitter allows information to be viewed by a broade r audience, not all of whom may interact immediately with the content. Following and tracing these “impres sions” or views by other Twitter users may also help to expand the notion of who participates, and how, in this affinity space.
