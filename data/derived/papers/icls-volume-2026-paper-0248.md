# Queering Biological Science and STEM Curricular Resources

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
STEM education has historically marginalized queer identities, shaping inaccurate curricula and fostering unwelcoming classroom environments. To address this, my research uses design-based research to investigate the design of queer-inclusive STEM curricu lar resources. I partnered with in-service secondary STEM teachers amidst a professional development course to co-create curricular resources. My research is grounded in queer theory, using a queer literacy framework to collaboratively develop inclusive design principles. My findings revealed a strong desire by teacher participants for more authentic and fluid representations of gender and sexuality in STEM curricula, particularly beyond the biological science discipline. My study emphasizes the importance of teacher collaboration research and underscores the need for more resources to foster equity for LGBTQIA+ students in STEM classrooms.

This study investigates teachers' sensemaking on the development of queer-inclusive STEM curriculum through the use of inclusive frameworks. The educational disciplines of STEM (Science, Technology, Engineering, and Mathematics) have historically misrepres ented queerness through erasure and inaccuracy, despite the overwhelming existence of queerness (Letts & Fifield, 2019). Traditional STEM curricula are typically reductive, erasing the existence of queerness in both society and nature. Not only do students  learn inaccuracies, e.g., (sex determination is solely based on XX or XY chromosomes), but they also learn through the hidden curriculum, who is and is not welcome in STEM spaces. LGBTQIA+ (queer) students have statistically poor perspectives on their STE M education experiences, possibly attributed to the inaccurate representation of queerness in STEM curricula (Kosciw, 2022). Disrupting this by addressing the limitations set by a prescribed STEM curriculum is imperative in improving students' perceptions of their STEM education. Supporting teachers in making more inclusive changes to their curricula helps make teachers' classrooms more inclusive and, by extension, their students feel more included. To do this, I investigate the few existing resources in collaboration with a professional development organization.

## Theoretical background and literature review

The theoretical framework of this study is based on queer theory, particularly work by critical theorists such as Judith Butler’s (2013) belief in the fluid nature of gender and sexuality. Queer theory critiques normativity, interrogating the socially constructed status quo of sexuality and gender, and the intersection of those identities among cultural factors (Fifield & Letts, 2014). Through the act of “queering”, I critiqued literature for a better utilize a queer-affirming lens in the interrogation of curricula perpetuating heterosexism. In conducting my literature review, I chose to 1) challenge normality assumptions, 2) employ critical subjectivity, and 3) embrace a non-linear process. From my literature review, I analyzed 31 related articles, identifying important themes, highlighting existing information, and the gaps that still need to be addressed. These themes include 1) the importance o f identity, 2) cis-heteronormativity and queer erasure, 3) gender and biological essentialism, 4) disrupting cis-heteronormativity, and 5) gender and sexual diversity (GSD) frameworks. My literature review secondary education curricula (Mattheis et al., 2021).

The final theme from the literature review findings uncovered the possibility of GSD inclusive frameworks and recommendations, giving educators tools for disrupting cis-heteronormativity (Long et al., 2021) in their curriculum, as reflected in Figure 1. GSD frameworks substantiate the possibility of improving LGBTQIA+ students' perceptions of their STEM education. By “removing the stigmatizing and isolating language that reinforces a binary view of human biology as the normal condition, [teachers] can support LGBTQIA+ students feeling a greater sense of belonging, academic success and retention in STEM” (Goldina et al., 2023, p. 43). This means addressing the hidden curriculum within STEM education, the “taken-for-granted practices of learning settings, whic h indicate what is valued and normal” (Gobby, 2017, p. 22). While queer theory helped me frame the purpose of this study, design-based research (DBR) informed the structure of the study’s research protocol and data analysis methodology. DBR is a learning sciences methodology that engages in design to develop knowle dge that improves educational practices (Sandoval, 2004). It is an “emerging approach to relating research and practice that is collaborative, iterative, and grounded in systematic inquiry,” which mirrors the engineering procedures instrumental in STEM education research (Fishman et al., 2013, p. 140). Research protocol was structured around the following DBR phases: 1) Identify participants and, in collaboration, the design principles, 2) co-develop an intervention (curricular resource) based on previous interventions and the established design principles, 3) teacher participants implement and redesign the intervention in an iterative cycle, and 4) analyze the final design of the intervention. For my study, I partnered with a professional development organi zation (PDO) in a large urbandistrict. The PDO supports cohorts of secondary STEM teachers to refine their classroom practice through participation in professional development (PD) courses. I developed and facilitated a PD course (three, two-hour-long sessions) consisting of teacher participant discussion and curricular workshopping, guided by the Gender Inclusive Framework (Long et al., 2021). The PD course was centered on the following design principles pulled from the Gender Inclusive Framework. Design principles

1. Authenticity: Align lessons with teachers' own developing understanding of gender and sex diversity.

2. Continuity: Students are consistently taught a more complex truth vs. oversimplification with

“exceptions”.

3. Affirmation: Lessons celebrate queer scientists and naturally occurring diversity of gender and

sexuality.

4. Anti-oppression: Lessons highlight and challenge oppression in current and historical science practices.

5. Student Agency: Students make choices about their learning and give input and feedback about lessons

Each PD course culminated in teacher participants designing their own curricular resources (e.g., lesson plans, unit projects, etc.) based on the design principles. Following the final PD course, I invited teachers to a follow-up semi-structured interview where I asked them to reflect on the design process. This included reflective questions on how the design principles influenced them, and the fidelity with which the principles were 1723 implemented. The collaborative and iterative nature of the PD courses helped capture teachers’ sensemaking, instructional decision-making, and experiences designing inclusive curriculum. Data sources A total of 13 teacher participants self-selected to partake in the PD courses, agreeing to share their experiences in the design of an inclusive curriculum. Data collection included the recording of research events, the PD course, and one-on-one follow-up interviews, to produce transcripts for data analysis. Analytical methods included interaction and discourse techniques to identify themes from collected data (Creswell, 2025). Deductive open coding was done by analyzing teacher participants' conversations within transcripts from recorded research events. These codes occurred throughout conversations with the teacher participants, as reflected in Figure 2, with code 4 (understand gender and sexuality as flexible/fluid) as the most frequently referenced code throughout the transcripts. The quote below encapsulates one of the teacher participants' complex understandings of the fluid nature of gender and sexuality. “And I feel like that's such a hard concept to explain to kids [gender], but when you have this visual, they're like, oh, okay, if this is one part of the galaxy, I can be one thing and also be another thing at the same time.” (Sophie: 21:59) In this example, the teacher is referencing an image from an existing curricular resource [gender galaxy visualization], highlighting the importance of a resource that can showcase the fluidity of gender and sexuality to their students.

In addition, Long and colleagues’ framework helped establish teacher conversations within the PD course and follow-up interviews. During both research events, I asked teacher participants about the relevance of the five principles (authenticity, continuity, affirmation, anti-oppression, and student agency) to the design of their curricular resources and future queer-inclusive STEM curricula. After analyzing the transcripts, it appears that teachers most frequently referenced authenticity, continuity, and a ffirmation. Teacher participants naturally gravitated towards the Authenticity principle, saying that they were “focused on” this principle as an entry point to inclusive teaching practices. The surfacing findings from these frameworks helped me better understand the design implications of this study. In particular, my findings emphasize the desire by teacher participants to address both 1) gender and sexuality fluidity, and 2) to teach a more authentic interpretation of STEM. These themes were reflected in the final curricular resources designed by teacher participants. Additionally emergent themes from the feedback received from teachers' follow-up interviews included

1) teachers expressing excitement at having the potential for more more queer inclusive STEM curricular

1724 resources at their disposal, and, 2) teachers specifically highlighting the need for more resources for non-biological science disciplines (specifically, mathematics, chemistry and physics), where the implications for queering content are not as inherent. This information affirmed my research objective of investigating teachers' sensemaking on the development of queer-inclusive STEM curriculum. Constraints due to the set schedule of the PD courses limited the redesign potential for the teacher participants' final curricular resources. I recommend that further research be conducted to better understand the process of designing future queer-inclusive STEM curricula. The implications of my research are twofold. Students' perceptions of STEM education have direct ties to students' well-being in the classroom, influencing their safety from anti-queer violence and bullying. In addition, students’ perceptions of STEM education influence their likelihood to pursue a STEM career. More inclusive curricular resources could help teachers make more inclusive classrooms, which could help LGBTQIA+ students feel more accepted and interested in STEM education moving forward. With limited existing resources for queering STEM curricula (as highlighted by the literature review findings), learning how to design future queer-inclusive STEM curricular resources is an important next step in resource development. Disrupting the misrepresentation and erasure of queer identities in STEM education by improving student belonging is imperative (Penuel et al., 2023). It encourages more students to enter the field of STEM, but also provides students with a more accurate representation of each discipline (Wright & Delgado, 2023).
