# Designing for Dialogue: Exhibit Design Ignites Patterns of Parent-Child Causal Reasoning

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Children learn cause-and-effect relations through family conversations and play. Two behaviors key to this development are exploring the environment and constructing explanations about an experience with other people. This research leverages sociomateriality to link principles of exhibit design to patterns in family conversations that may be productive for causal learning. This work characterizes dynamic transitions between parent-child exploring and explaining as oscillations and identifies specific exhibit design principles that facilitate oscillatory patterns of interaction.

## Introduction

Children learn about cause-and-effect relationships by exploring the environment and constructing explanations with other people as part of everyday life (Callana n et al., 2017, 2020; Legare, 2017). When parents p rovide an explanation, their child's exploratory behavior is supported and can create an explore-explain pattern of interaction; these behaviors interact in complex wa ys we have yet to fully understand (Callanan et al., 2017; Crowley et al., 2001; Willard et al., 2019). Callanan et al. (2020)  observed that parents can use causal talk at crucial moments during exhibit exploration that propels their children into another cycle of exploration behaviors, which may facilitate children's causal thinking. However, the extent to which this pattern occurs in other contexts remains to be seen.

Science centers offer a valuable context for examin ing the social nature of learning about causality, as they prioritize play, hands-on interaction, and family dialogue in their missio n to foster family engagement with science (Gutwill & Allen, 2010). This paper leverages sociomateriality as a theoreti cal frame for investigating children’s causal learning in playful, informal env ironments. Sørensen (2009) characterized learning as a socio-material assemblage, where learning is social, and both learners and teachers are entangled with learning tools and resources during the practice of teaching and l earning. Learning about science, or causal relations, become s a social and material endeavor as the two are interwoven: materials can shape  human perception and activity in the same way that a human candirect or shape a material thing (Latour, 2005; Tietjen et al., 2021). Oscillation as metaphor for characterizing patterns of family causal reasoning To better understand these dynamic interactions, I propose an oscillation metaphor. In physics, oscillation involves a cyclical back-and-forth movement between two states. Similarly, this study aims to characterize parentchild causal reasoning behavioral patterns as oscillations–how parents and children make dynamic transitions between exploring and explaining depending upon the environment or activity they are  engaged with and the materials they have access to. Central to this inqu iry is how specific exhibit design features may spa rk or sustain exploration and explanation oscillation patterns in a way that creates space for learning to occur. I characterize exploring as the physical actions —motoric action, gestural, or verbal —that children use to act on the physical or social world to generate information from the environment or other people. E xploring may include non-verbal behavior, verbal utterances, or some combina tion of both. Explaining behaviors are the strategies for how children elicit and generate ver bal information about a set of causal mechanisms, c ausal relations, or underlying causal principles that gov ern those relations (Legare et al., 2017; Willard e t al., 2019). Explanatory behaviors identified as crucial to caus al learning include making causal connections, pred ictions, personal connections, science principle connections, or labeling the causal mechanism. Sociomaterialit y allows for a comprehensive lo ok at the shared meaning-making in museum spaces by attending to the assembl ages created between families and spaces.

## Method

The Science Center of Iowa houses six permanent hands-on exhibits and one temporary visiting exhibit. Eac h exhibit varies in the amount of physical interactiv ity and level of technology integration, all with l arge spaces to move about. Visitors are not confined to a single trajectory th rough the Center and are able to freely dictate the speed and direction of their experience. Two major design decisions were made at the experimental exhi bit (Seismic Shaker) to facilitate explore-explain patterns of behavior based upon foundational principles of humancentered design (Norman, 2013). A visual design change, iconic stickers, were added to the Seismic Shaker exhibit to boost exploration in children. Conversation cards were provided to families in an effort to boost explanation in parents.

Participants were 9 first-time family visitor groups with one or more children between the ages of 4 and 9 years of age. One parent and any eligible child were considered participants. Families with multiple siblings within the age range were considered for participat ion, but analysis focused on parent-child interactions. Data collection took place on days when the science center was open to the public. Each family was consented upon entry to the science  center and fitted with a harness GoPro camera for one parent and one or two children that met inclusion criteria. Families were encouraged to visit the science center as they normally would and for as long as they wished. Participants were asked to specifically visit the  Seismic Shaker table (a  target exhibit) and were provided with a conversation card. After the visit, parents compl eted a demographic questionnaire and children completed a narrative reflection task (adapted from Acosta et al., 2021).

## Data analysis

Data analysis used audio and video from parent and child harness GoPro cameras and multiple stationary cameras placed around the target exhibit. Demographic and r eflective task data were  used to supplement qualitative analyses. Parent and child v ideo streams were stacked horizontally and merged t o create a single video file, allowing for a more accurate view of the participan t’s bodily orientation in relation to one another a nd to the physical environment as they play (Marin and Bang, 2018). Starting with an adapted set of a priori codes (Callanan et al., 2020), project-developed codes were developed to systematically se arch the data set for patterns of exploration and explanation, and then “zoom-in” on those episodes to hypothesize about what sparked that pattern of interaction. Episodes begin when two family members first intera ct with one exhibit and end when only one family member remains or when both people leave the  exhibit because sustained cycles of interaction wi ll no longer be possible. The analysis focused on turn-taking and bounded at episodes of interest rather t han the entire interaction.

## Results

Data analysis is near completion and the results will be  presented at the conference in full. Emerging analy ses suggest that younger children need more directive a nd well-timed scaffolds from parents or the environment to persist through frustration and interpret their experience when the exhibit does not behave as expected. Consistent with prior work in this area (see Gutwill & Allen, 2010; Jant et al., 2014; Willard et al., 2019), par ental prompts are relatively simple ways to effectively shape the  ways parents interact with their children and mate rial spaces, but this work highlights that it may be critical fo r parental supports to emphasize exploring the envi ronment and explaining observations in order to create a sustai ned cyclical interaction pattern. A common finding in both highly controlled (Gopnik et al., 2001; Schulz & Bonawitz, 2007) and informal settings (Callanan et al., 2020) is that children are more likely to engage with and learn about causal mechanisms if they can explore and manipulate the objects within their environment (Caporaso et al., 2022). Emerging results suggest that when goals for  play are set and achieved collaboratively between parent  and child, the likelihood of dyads oscillating fro m exploring to explaining will increase. A sociomaterial perspe ctive would posit that children participate in shar ed meaningmaking while engaging with parents and the physical exhibit environment and candevelop causal thinking within these social interactions and practices (Callanan et al., 2020). An oscillation metaphor leveraging sociomateriality has the potential to change the way we view dynamic  patterns of family causal reasoning while giving e qual attention to the ways exhibit design mediates shared meaning-making and children’s causal learning.
