# Learning in the Wild: PAL's New Tools and Methods for Research in Early Childhood Mathematics

**Conference:** ISLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
PAL (Personal Assistant for Learning) is an adaptive, smart learning system that operationalizes sociocultural theory, Knowledge Space Theory, and learning sciences principles to support and study early mathematics learning in real-world contexts. PAL guides parents and teachers to facilitate hands-on, socially mediated interactions aligned with each child’s Zone of Proximal Development while generating frequent, fine-grained evidence about children’s mathematical learning. The system integrates a research-grounded knowledge model, an explore–exploit adaptive engine, and a digital assessment system to provide personalized guidance and scalable, ecologically valid measurement. Early pilots show substantial gains in children’s learning and strong associations between adult engagement and child learning. Designed as a learning accelerator and a research platform, PAL provides the Learning Sciences community with a tool for examining learning trajectories, scaffolding dynamics, and conceptual and skill development as they naturally unfold across a child’s learning ecosystem— while producing evidence needed to connect research insights to practice at scale.

Early childhood mathematics is more conceptually complex than it appears on the surface (Geary et al., 2019). Foundational ideas, such as one-to-one correspondence, comparison, subitizing, spatial relations, and patterning, develop along systematic learning trajectories and form the cognitive architecture for later mathematical reasoning (Clements & Sarama, 2020). Yet in practice, these learning trajectories remain largely invisible in curriculum materials, instructional routines, and everyday adult–child interactions, leaving families, teachers, and even researchers without clear markers of how mathematical understanding develops across early concepts and skills (Clements & Sarama, 2024; Schmidt, 2025). Early math is frequently perceived as intuitive or simple, leading to chronic underinvestment in research infrastructure, professional learning, and high-quality assessment tools (Bittner & Bull, 2022; Gallegos, 2025). This misalignment is reinforced by a broader ecosystem-level “expertise gap.” Practitioners have limited professional knowledge for teaching early mathematics (Ban et al., 2024), and the field lacks validated, operationalized learning progressions, granular assessments, and research-grounded instructional tools (Clements & Sarama, 2025). These gaps also appear in emerging educational technologies: most AI systems inherit the same misconceptions and oversimplifications embedded in the materials they were trained on (Betts, 2025), leaving them unable to provide effective, developmentally appropriate recommendations for young children. As a result, the adults who support young children (i.e., teachers, parents, caregivers), often lack access to understandable domain-specific guidance aligned with how mathematical concepts develop for our youngest learners.

Although the Learning Sciences community has developed rich theoretical traditions—such as sociocultural perspectives on adult–child interaction (e.g., “More Knowledgeable Others”; Vygotsky, 1986), conceptual structure theories such as Knowledge Space Theory (Doignon & Falmagne, 1985), and ecological views of learning environments (e.g., Bloom, 1984)—the field still lacks the infrastructure needed to operationalize and study these perspectives at scale (Motz et al., 2025). There are no widely available, fine-grained knowledge models of early mathematics; few instrumented home-learning environment tools capable of capturing naturalistic learning behaviors; no scalable methods for tracing children’s learning sequences and progressions; and no mechanism for linking instructional guidance with concept-level assessments in real-time. These gaps limit the Learning Sciences community’s ability to test hypotheses about learning trajectories, conceptual dependencies, scaffolding, and the dynamics of early cognition—not just in math, but in other subject areas as well.

PAL is an adaptive “smart” learning system developed to address this missing infrastructure. Grounded in sociocultural theory, Knowledge Space Theory, and Bloom’s Objects of Change, PAL provides personalized, dynamically-adapting support to adults in guiding children’s hands-on, real-world math learning while generating research-quality evidence about early learning (Betts, Ryon, & Laski, 2026). Today, PAL integrates a validated, c oncept and skill-level knowledge model of early mathematics, interactive assessment items aligned to granular knowledge units, and an adaptive recommendation engine that personalizes activity guidance based on simple adult feedback. Delivered through SMS and a lightweight web interface, PAL embeds developmentally grounded learning moments into daily routines and creates a data-rich environment that captures evidence of growth in children’s mathematical knowledge and skills, as well as adult scaffolding practices and engagement across the learning ecosystem (e.g., home, school, community). In doing so, PAL functions both as a learning accelerator that (1) maximizes learning by keeping children within their Zone of Proximal Development and (2) as a research platform that enables the Learning Sciences community to study early mathematical cognition and learning sequences in authentic settings and at scale.

The theoretical grounding of PAL PAL’s design and methodology is grounded in sociocultural learning theory, Knowledge Space Theory (KST), and Bloom’s Four Objects of Change. Sociocultural learning theory informs PAL’s focus on strengthening adults as effective “more knowledgeable others” who can guide children through meaningful, hands-on activity; the Zone of Proximal Development (ZPD) provides the foundation for PAL’s adaptive algorithms, which personalizes recommendations to place each child within an optimal zone for growth (Vygotsky, 1986; Zaretskii, 2009). Knowledge Space Theory (Doignon & Falmagne, 1985) models knowledge as a structured set of possible states defined by prerequisite relations among concepts. For PAL, this framework enables computational inference of which mathematical ideas are reachable, which are dependent on earlier concepts, and which constitute the child’s “next learnable” steps. Additionally, Bloom’s (1984) Objects of Change emphasize improving the learner, the adult, the materials, and the environment in concert—an approach reflected in PAL’s ecosystem design.

## Overview of PAL

PAL’s design is powered by a comprehensive knowledge model developed through expert synthesis of researchbased early math learning trajectories, developmental progressions, and various state, national, and international standards frameworks for early mathematics learning, and continues to evolve based on data and insights from pilot studies. Comprising hundreds of granular early mathematics Knowledge Units (KUs), the model encodes conceptual understanding, prerequisite relationships, and recommended scaffolding strategies derived from decades of developmental and cognitive research and built directly into the core knowledge infrastructure. This enables PAL to generate a probabilistic estimate of a child's current knowledge state, identify concepts they are most ready to learn next, and recommend adult-mediated activities that maintain an optimal level of challenge and provide observable evidence of meaningful learning (Foster & Piacentini, 2025; Mislevy, Almond, & Lukas, 2003)..

PAL’s adaptive engine uses an explore–exploit strategy to support learning within the child’s ZPD in real-world contexts. The system periodically “explores” adjacent KUs to probe emerging learning readiness and receptivity to challenge, while “exploiting” identified strengths by deepening understanding in known areas. Adaptation is informed by two key forms of evidence: (1) lightweight adult feedback (e.g., “did independently,” “needed help,” “struggled”), which provides immediate and regular insight into children’s performance in realworld activities; and (2) performance on periodic, brief formative assessment items generated through PAL’s embedded assessment mechanisms. This adaptive cycle reflects learning engineering principles that integrate evidence, inference, and iterative refinement into real-world learning contexts. To support scalable, developmentally appropriate measurement, items in PAL’s KU-aligned digital assessment system approximate hands-on early math tasks and enable concept-level, task-based evaluation that mirrors children’s natural math learning activity. These brief, targeted digital assessments (2-3 minutes in duration) offer low-friction, ecologically valid evidence of children’s learning and provide a mechanism for validating indirect evidence provided by adults in the ecosystem, verifying conceptual dependencies, identifying misconceptions, and monitoring growth across learning trajectories. These assessments enable concept-level personalization that traditional early childhood assessments (which are typically broad, infrequent, and laborintensive), are unable to offer at scale (Banerjee & Luckner, 2013). Beyond its conceptual and adaptive architecture, PAL is intentionally designed for accessibility and equity. Guidance is delivered through simple SMS messages linked to a lightweight web interface. Activities require no special materials and are intended to be integrated into everyday routines, making them feasible for caring adults (parents, caregivers, teachers) to facilitate across diverse settings. This low-bandwidth, adultsupporting design enables use in contexts where device-based or 1:1 instructional technologies may be impractical, developmentally inappropriate, or inconsistent with community preferences regarding screen use. Taken together, these components position PAL as a learning-acceleration system that integrates concept structure modeling, adaptive guidance, and aligned assessment to support and enhance capacity for high-quality a dult–child interaction, generating structured evidence about children’s learning trajectories in naturalistic settings.

Evidence of PAL’s effectiveness Early pilots of PAL demonstrate promising effects on children’s early math development, suggesting that a learning-sciences-grounded, adult-facing adaptive system can meaningfully accelerate learning in real-world contexts (Betts, Ryon, & Laski, 2026). In a recent implementation with a cohort of Voluntary Prekindergarten (VPK) classrooms (N = 19 children), families in the PAL condition engaged in short, hands-on math activities guided by PAL’s adaptive recommendations over a 3.5-month period. Pre-and post-assessments were conducted using the Test of Early Mathematics Ability, Third Edition (TEMA-3), administered by trained assessors independent of the PAL system. Though the sample was small, the results were promising. Children whose families used PAL showed substantial gains, with 100% improving their TEMA-3 scores compared with 57% in a business-as-usual comparison group. The effect size exceeded 1.0, indicating a significant improvement in early mathematical skills. Importantly, the degree of parent engagement—operationalized as completion of PAL activities and submission of feedback—was positively correlated with children’s learning gains (Spearman r =.62), underscoring the role of adult mediation in accelerating children’s conceptual growth. Building on these early findings, PAL is being implemented in larger, multi-site pilots (n > 500) that will provide broader evidence across diverse educational contexts. One pilot is underway in a large, public school district in Glenolden, Pennsylvania, with children in PreK, kindergarten, and first grade. A parallel implementation is running with a small consortium of private schools in Santa Monica, California, serving preschool, PreK, kindergarten, and first-grade students. Across these sites, children, families, and teachers are engaging with PAL-supported math activities, creating opportunities to examine usage patterns, adult engagement, and learning outcomes in both public and independent school settings. PAL as research infrastructure PAL currently serves as an extensible research infrastructure for studying early cognitive development in mathematics, learning progressions, and adult–child scaffolding in both naturalistic and classroom settings. The Learning Sciences community has long recognized the importance of early math and informal learning environments; however, it has lacked shared, scalable tools to observe learning as it unfolds outside laboratory or controlled settings (Hornburg et al., 2021; Motz et al., 2025). Rather than examining isolated snapshots of instruction or development, researchers can now investigate early math learning as a dynamic, real-world process, capturing how children’s understanding emerges, shifts, and evolves across everyday interactions. PAL provides a granular representation of early mathematical understanding through its knowledge model, comprised of hundreds of developmentally sequenced Knowledge Units (KUs). Because each KU encodes conceptual and skill dependencies, misconceptions, and scaffolding strategies, the model supports investigation of learning trajectories at a level of precision often not available in early childhood research. As a directed conceptual graph, the KU structure also enables empirical testing of prerequisite relations, reconstruction of learning sequences, and identification of points where learners accelerate or struggle across contexts. PAL’s explore–exploit adaptive logic further supports research on ZPD-aligned learning mechanisms, continuously probing readiness on adjacent KUs (“explore”) while reinforcing emerging competencies (“exploit”). Behavioral evidence from adult feedback provides insight into how support is applied and how ZPD positioning evolves in real-world contexts.

Complementing these conceptual and behavioral layers, PAL also instruments the early learning ecosystem, capturing lightweight telemetry that spans Bloom’s Objects of Change—learner, adult, materials, and environment—and the dynamic relations among them. The system logs how adults engage with nudges and recommendations; how they select, adapt, or sequence activities; how multiple caregivers or teachers coordinate around a child’s learning; and how contextual rhythms shape opportunities for engagement. Simultaneously, PAL captures children’s performance across hands-on tasks and digital assessments, enabling researchers to link moment-to-moment behavior with longer-term conceptual development. Such multi-actor, multi-context evidence is rarely accessible in early childhood research, where observations are episodic, resource-intensive, and typically limited to laboratory or classroom settings. By making these cross-level interactions visible at scale, PAL opens new possibilities for examining how instruction, environment, and adult beliefs jointly influence young children’s mathematical development in authentic settings. Moreover, PAL’s integrated, data-rich design enables researchers to envision new lines of inquiry, while offering a shared platform that invites collaborative extension and refinement across the Learning Sciences community.

D emonstration format The demonstration will provide a guided walkthrough of PAL, highlighting how its theoretical foundations, adaptive engine, and assessment architecture operate in practice. The session is structured to provide attendees with direct visibility into how PAL works and its application as a research tool.

1. Knowledge model overview

The demonstration will begin with a brief visualization of PAL’s research-grounded Knowledge Model, showing how concepts and prerequisite relations are represented as a structured conceptual graph. This segment illustrates how PAL operationalizes foundational Learning Sciences frameworks to model the systematic nature of early mathematical understanding.

2. Adaptive recommendation logic

The presenter will demonstrate how PAL uses both direct and indirect data to assess a child’s likely knowledge state and generate personalized activity recommendations positioned within that child’s predicted Zone of Proximal Development. This live walkthrough will demonstrate how the system maps learning trajectories across content, domains, and activities using algorithmic methods and rationales.

3. Digital assessment items

The presenter will demonstrate examples of PAL’s KU-aligned digital assessment items, highlighting how each item maps onto specific, individual knowledge units on the knowledge model and provides direct, fine-grained, ecologically valid evidence of conceptual understanding. This segment highlights the potential of the assessment system to support scalable research on early mathematics learning in diverse settings.

4. Real-world activity design

Several sample PAL activities will be presented to illustrate how complex early math concepts are translated into concise, developmentally appropriate, hands-on interactions that naturally fit within home and classroom routines. This segment clarifies how PAL supports adults as effectively more knowledgeable others.

5. Researcher view and data opportunities

The final segment will highlight PAL’s value as research infrastructure. Examples of anonymized learner progression data, patterns of adult scaffolding, readiness signals, and trajectory evidence will be displayed to show how PAL can support studies of learning progressions, learning in informal contexts, and the dynamics of ZPD support at scale.

Throughout the demonstration, the presenter will show how PAL integrates developmental theory, knowledge modeling, adaptive inference, and embedded assessment to produce both high-quality learning interactions and research-grade data. Attendees will leave with a clear understanding of how PAL operates and its applications in researching early cognition and learning trajectories in naturalistic settings. PAL illustrates how developmental research, Knowledge Space Theory, and learning sciences principles can be operationalized into an adaptive system that accelerates early mathematics learning while generating forms of evidence that are largely inaccessible at scale for both research and ongoing system refinement. By strengthening adults as effective partners in children’s education, PAL embeds dynamically adapting, ZPD-aligned support into everyday activity and creates an instrumented home-learning environment capable of capturing learning as it naturally unfolds. As both an intelligent learning system and a research platform, PAL enables investigations of learning trajectories, scaffolding dynamics, and conceptual and skill development in authentic, real-world contexts.

Importantly, PAL makes visible the broader learning ecosystem itself—providing scalable ways to examine Bloom’s Objects of Change as a coordinated system rather than isolated components. This cross-level visibility enables researchers to link conceptual development to variations in adult practice, environmental rhythms, and material use, providing a more comprehensive understanding of how early learning emerges and evolves. In doing so, PAL offers the Learning Sciences community new research infrastructure for connecting theory and practice, deepening foundational understanding of early learning, and empowering families, teachers, and young children. We envision PAL as shared infrastructure for the field—designed to be used, interrogated, and extended by researchers seeking to advance the next generation of early learning science.
