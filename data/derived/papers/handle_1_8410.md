# Investigating Experts’ and Middle School Learners’ Uncertainty Management During Collaborative Engineering Design Tasks

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Young learners are increasingly engaging in engineering design activities in their classrooms. However, limited research exists regarding uncertainty management practices in engineering design, what experts do, and how to scaffold the process for novice learners. To address this gap, we investigated uncertainty management practices in engineering design of experts and beginner designers at the middle school level. We interviewed five design experts and identified six prominent uncertainty management practices in engineering design. We also analyzed data from five groups of middle school learners working on ill-structured design problems to understand their uncertainty management processes and identified learner difficulties in productively engaging in uncertainty management practices.

## Introduction and Background

Managing uncertainties is an integral part of solving engineering design problems. While there has been a growing emphasis on teaching young students about design thinking and productively engaging them in engineering design practices (Crismond & Adams, 2012), the focus lacks  on how to help them tackle the inherent uncertaint y. Engineering design problems require learners to tackle uncertainties at every stage of the design process, choose from multiple alternatives, and deal with incomplete information and unclear objectives while collaborating with multiple team members (Dym, Little, Orwin, & Spjut,  2009; Ullman, 2001). Prior research acknowledges t hat learning how to deal with uncertainties productively is crucial and young learners can and should enga ge in the process at the school level itself to further develop their reasoning and sense-making (Dasgupta, 2019; Dym et al., 2009; M. E. Jordan & McDaniel, 2014). However, prio r work sheds limited light on how learners' subject ive experiences of uncertainty affect their actions and  design approach, how it is different or similar to  expert designers, and how we can help learners manage uncertainties productively. A mongst the limited literature available on this top ic, seminal contributions by Jordan et al. (2014) highlight the important role of peer responses for collaboratively tackling uncertainty faced by middl e school learners during a robotics engineering activity. Th e authors concluded that learners respond to uncert ainties in both supportive and unsupportive ways, shaping the trajectory of their uncertainty management processes. Other researchers focusing on K-12 learners have highlighted how uncertainty affects learners' engagement in science practices (e.g., Metz, 2016; Radinsky, 2008), scientific argumentation (e.g., Chen, 2020), and how can we engage learners in "productive uncertainty" (Manz, 2018) in the context of science learning. We take these relevant and valuable research into consideration as we investigate the following research questions: (a) What are uncertainty management practices in the context of engineering design as defined and practiced by experts? (b) Wha t challenges do middle school learners encounter while productively engaging in engineering design practices? Cognitive and learning scientists have favored contrasting expert and novice processes for understanding disciplinary practices in a domain and identifying scaffolds required for supporting novices' processe s (e.g., Crismond & Adams, 2012). We interviewed five design experts to identify and describe uncertainty management practices in engineering design. The experts in our  study have eight to twelve years of experience in solving engineering design problems in different capacities like-participant in large projects, mentor, and project manager. These experts are at the "Informed Designer" level (Crismond & Adams, 2012). Expertise at this level maps well to "a generic model of design expertise that corres ponds to ways of perceiving, interpreting, structur ing, and solving complex problems" and is considered appropriate for contrasting and interpreting learners' beh aviors at the "beginner designer" level (Crismond & Adams, 2012). We also referred to  literature on design expertise to inform and validate our findings. Then, we analyzed  middle school learners' behaviors and interactions  during their attempts to resolve uncertainties to help us unpack learner processes and problem areas that hin der their productive engagement in uncertainty management practices.

## Participants and Setting

We interviewed five professionals (four males and one female) specializing in the fields of engineerin g, architecture and design to answer our first research question i.e. what are uncertainty management practices in the ICLS2022 Proceedings 107 © ISLS context of solving engineering design problems? All  the professionals have experience of eight to twel ve years working on various engineering design projects. The  projects range from devising solutions for bio-med ical problems to developing tools for assessing environment friendly and sustainable designs. Two of the male experts (Expert ND and Expert KG) are currently working as design entrepreneur. Expert SA (female) is an assis tant professor at a technical institute in India. Expert  AK (male) is a Senior Project Manager at a Medical  Device Development Unit at a technical institute in India and Expert AR (male) is a Senior Interaction Design er and Design Expert at a multinational company.

The interviews were semi-structured in nature and conducted by the first author of the paper, in onlin e mode using the Google Meet platform. Each interview  lasted for sixty to ninety minutes and the medium of communication was English. The experts were informed about the broad goals of the research and provided with the sample questions that would guide the interview. The questions probed them to talk about different situations and scenarios where they experienced uncertainty, reflect on how they dealt with them, the role of team members in the uncertainty management process, and how different uncertainty situations and management strategies relate to the engineering design process. All the experts also have teaching and mentoring experience, therefore we also asked them about their views on teaching and learni ng of uncertainty management and kind of support th at learners might need in this regard.

To identify what difficulties and challenges learne rs face in productively engaging in uncertainty management, we analyzed data of five groups of sixt h and seventh grade learners. Data came from two st udies that were conducted with the broader aim of understanding middle school learners’ uncertainty manageme nt processes in collaborative settings. The first study was a four-hour study conducted in a one-day workshop mode with five seventh grade learners from multiple scho ols in an Indian metropolitan city. Learners had to  design a cleaning robot using the LEGO MindstormsTM kit that could accomplish certain tasks efficiently while optimizing the design cost (Kaur & Dasgupta, 2019; Kaur, Patel, & Dasgupta, 2019). We grouped learners into two t eams randomly for working on the design problem. First team (Group A) consisted of two students (one boy and one girl) and the other team (Group B) consisted of three students (two girls and one boy). The second study was a four-day study (approximately one hour each day) conducted in a middle school classroom setting in a large public school in an Indian metropolitan city. A total of 44 students participated who were randomly divided into 12 teams (groups of 3 or  4) by the researchers. We analyzed data from three  teams who were the most vocal (hence had clear audio). Gr oup A consisted of four participants (three males a nd one female), Group B also consisted of four participants (two males and two females), and Group C consisted of three participants (two females and one male). We gave students a design task of building a balloon powered toy by maximizing the distance traveled in a straight line (Kaur & Dasgupta, 2021). We collected both audio and video recordings of the teams for the entire course of th e activity in both the studies. The authors of the paper were present in the class as observers.  Teaching assistants (T.A.s) from the same department (two T.A.’s for first study and three T.A.’s for the second study) were also pr esent. They handled the audio and video data collec tion and also mentored groups from time to time.

## Data analysis

We analyzed interview data from the five experts us ing thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2012) to ide ntify themes related to uncertainty management practices of experts. We also reviewed literature on design e xpertise to support this analysis process. Thematic analysis  is an appropriate method for our goal since we wan ted to explore the range of ways experts experience and de al with uncertainties. Following the methods of ind uctive thematic analysis, we first familiarized ourselves with the data by listening to the audio recordings of interviews and transcribing them. Then we generated initial codes from three interviews from our data set. Refer to Table 1 for an example of how initial codes emerged from transcript. The next step was to review the literature on design expertise to find if more codes emerge related to experts’ uncertainty management. Then we collated the related codes into categories and themes (See Table 2 for a n example), which was followed by reviewing the the mes against the remaining two interviews to ensure consistency. Finally, we refined our themes by repeated discussions where we examined their descriptions.

(List is not exhaustive) Taking feedback from relevant sources Themes emerging from analysis of expert data were then used as a guiding framework to identify learner difficulties and challenges in productively engaging in uncertainty management practices during the collaborative engineering design task. For the learners’ data, we first used a coding scheme by Jordan et al. (2014) to identify all the episodes where uncertainties were expressed  by the team members. This coding scheme uses indic ators like hedges (e.g., ‘maybe,’ ‘sort of,’ ‘perhaps’), probabilistic statements (e.g., ‘likely,’ ‘probably’), and non-verbal cues (e.g., use of gaps like ‘mmm…’) to identify expressions of uncertainty. These indicators marked the start of an uncertainty episode for the team. The episode ended when the conversation or the physical activity of the team members no longer focused on the specific uncertainty the group was dealing with. A total of ninety-four episodes were analyzed from the two learners’ data sets comb ined. We analyzed these episodes following inductiv e and interpretive approach and using methods of interaction analysis (Jordan & Henderson, 1995). Our unit of analysis was “individual in a group” (Hogan & Fisherkeller, 2005) and we paid attention to both verbal and non-verbal learner behaviors during their interaction. By analyzing learner interactions against the uncertainty management practices identified from the experts’ data, we identified difficulties and challenges that the learners encountered during the uncertainty management practices.

## Findings

We identified six prevalent uncertainty management practices that experts engage in during the engineering design process. The following section will define these six practices (P1 to P6) and simultaneously discuss middle school learners' uncertainty management behaviors concerning these practices. Acknowledge and proactively respond to uncertainty (P1) The experts who participated in our study partially agreed that ignoring uncertainty is a common practice in their design practice. They reported that ignoring is not  a ‘good’ way of dealing with certain types of unce rtainty. According to the experts, some uncertainties cannot be dealt with immediately because of reasons like dependence on other tasks or situations that cannot be control led in the present. Experts suggest that instead of  completely ignoring uncertainty in such situations, one should proactively discuss them and plan immediate and future steps that would help in addressing them. For example, ex pert ND mentioned that his strategy of dealing with  such uncertainties is a combination of "proactive" and "ignorance." He says, "proactive is the first immediate future, first two stages or something and the rest, I am ignorant about it because it is too early to think about it." Experts also mentioned that strategies like "categorizing" and "prioritizing" uncertainty helps in planning how to manage them. Experts prioritize uncertainties based on asp ects like-their relevance to the current design p hase, dependence on other uncertainties or tasks, and which uncertainties are critical for making progress. In contrast, beginner learners usually ignore uncer tainties expressed by their team-members at various instances. Learners from our study dismissed uncertainty expressed by others by either explicitly declaring it as irrelevant, delaying it without any discussion or b y choosing not to respond. For example, consider the excerpt below from Team A’s episode in Study 1. Aditi expressed the uncertainty regarding whether they should design ICLS2022 Proceedings 109 © ISLS their robot to clean the eraser dust (one of the trash materials) as she considered it to be a difficult task. However, the other team member (Sneha) completely ignored this uncertainty by calling it “unimportant.” Sneha: Our robot will clean everything.

Aditi: Should we use Eraser dust? Won't it be difficult to clean it? It kind of…sticks to the floor. Sneha: This is not important right now; we will be able to do it. One of the factors contributing to learners ignorin g uncertainty was the way they position themselves and other team members. For example, in study 2, gr oup B (See Table 3), Anu and Anmol ignored Krish wh en he raised his concern about Anu’s decision (line 9). Instead of responding to his concern, Anmol remin ded him of the task he had asked him to do and instructed h im to concentrate on that (lines 9, 10). Even Krish  did not confront Anmol; instead, he updated him about his w ork and asked what he should donext (lines 11, 12). Both Anmol’s and Krish’s behavior indicates that they po sitioned each other in a supervisor and assistant k ind of relationship. This kind of relationship between Anmol and Krish persistently existed in other uncertainty episodes also. Anmol’s behavior of ignoring Krish’s uncertai nty and Krish’s behavior of not confronting Anmol a nd following his instructions seemed to stem from their positioning dynamics. Summary. Expert designers acknowledge and proactively plan mitigation steps to address uncertainties at appropriate design stages. However, learners may not acknowledge every uncertainty and usually respond by delaying them without a plan or completely ignoring them. Maintaining uncertainty (P2) It is the tendency to increase uncertainty by going into the depth of knowledge required to resolve it. The following excerpt from Expert KG’s interview where he talks a bout expanding on ideas in the ideation phase of de sign interview speaks to this practice: “So I had to think three-four ways of cleaning the membrane. For all of them, I had sketched down ways in which I could do it and these sketches, what I did was, I had taken to the expert from the ‘X’ institute….but I let that uncertainty be in the ideation phase so that it does not affect my ideation….So if I kill this stupid idea right now, I will not get those two brilliant ideas later and I would have never learned what all right or wrong cold happen with them.” There are episodes where beginner learners engage in deep discussions for understanding and resolving uncertainty. These situations provide learners opportunity to share and discuss alternative perspectives, negotiate ideas, and collaboratively build knowledge to make progress in the design process. However, in most cases such productive discussions do not sustain. One of the r easons is the tendency to prematurely converge on ideas and make quick decisions instead of expanding uncertainty further. The other team-member, Krish, also confronted her (line  9) but both Anu and Anmol ignored his concern.  In  this episode, Anu’s actions of ignoring the disagreement  expressed by her team-mates (lines 5 & 9) and prov iding superficial rationales for her decision indicated t hat she expected to manage uncertainty by making a quick decision, without engaging in any sense-making discussion. Taking feedback from relevant sources (P3) When uncertainty is experienced, one common practice is identifying and approaching the appropriate source of knowledge. This can help reduce the uncertainty by clarifying doubts and confusions and introducing mo re uncertainties that would further guide the knowledg e seeking path, or act as an anchor for deciding ne xt steps. Experts emphasized that this practice is particular ly essential during the initial stage of design whe n they are defining problem as it helps in “planning” what to donext “better”. For instance, Expert AK said - “You get it clarified with your industry, your clinical partners, your technology partners, whoever it is, the stakeholders who are there, that’s where your prior art becomes really important in clearing the uncertainties. May be reading up literature on that, may be reading existing patents  regarding the same. So all of this helps in cleari ng up these uncertainties, the unknowns which are there in the project. So once the ground up work is done, we pla n accordingly, at least some part of the unknowns become known to us and then planning becomes better.” A crucial aspect of clarifying uncertainty is asses sing and evaluating the gathered information in the context of problem that is being solved. For example, Expert ND says, “After doing certain experiments, we saw some observations. So if I were to interpret those observations, I would interpret it in a certain way but if I give the same experiment results to an academic person, they would see it in a very different way. I have to consider both perspectives and see how they serve the purpose of resolving my uncertainties.” On the other hand, some learners usually ask for direct solutions and answers from authority figures to resolve uncertainty instead of asking relevant questions that could guide their uncertainty management processes. Some learners do attempt to engage in collaborative  discussions and reasoning with fellow team-members, however in most cases, there is problem of unequal participation. Learners usually approach only specific teammembers to discuss their uncertainties, as was the case with Anmol from group B, study 2 (see Table 3). Anmol only approached Anu for discussing which nozzle str aw to use. This was clearly evident as Anmol was tu rning towards her and maintaining eye contact only with h er during the discussion of the uncertainty (lines 6, 7, 13). These behaviors usually produce knowledge that lacks sound reasoning and is biased due to limited perspectives. Learners also faced difficulty in framing their unc ertainty into relevant questions that could trigger constructive discussion or feedback and usually do not persist on asking questions or expressing uncer tainty to elicit responses. They even do not assess the infor mation received from other sources, and usually acc ept it especially if it comes from a person considered as a higher source of knowledge authority. Summary. Similar to experts, learners do engage in taking f eedback. However, they struggle in articulating their uncertainty and lack critical assessment of gathered information.

Using engineering design specific tools and techniques (P4) There is evidence that experts use conceptual and t echnical tools and techniques for resolving uncerta inties. Experts from our interviews used tools such as "usability goal setting"  (Joshi, 2009), “sketches and drawings”, and "standard project management tools" which help resolve uncertainties at different design phases. The beginner designers do not have awareness about the existing engineering design tools and techniques. Although, they do intuitively engage in tactics like brainstorming and making drawings and sketches for different purposes during uncertainty management. However, le arners lack knowledge about how to use these tools ICLS2022 Proceedings 111 © ISLS effectively.  For example, sketches made by learners in our study lacked details like labeling of parts and mention of specifications like dimensions, size, and materials. Leaners miss out on important details when they use these sketches during design phases like creating prototypes, causing problems later. Summary. Experts effectively use standardized design tools and techniques to manage uncertainties, while beginners lack apriori knowledge about these tools and their application. Keep track of micro and macro progress (P5) All the experts in the interviews had a view that c ontinuous progress in the uncertainty management and the overall design process is an important indicator of productive uncertainty management. For example, Expert ND when asked about how they judge if their students are managing uncertainty productively during a design project, says, “the biggest, the first marker is-Are they moving forward? If I am looking at them every half an hour or 2 hours, when I come back, if they are still solving the same problem, that means there is something wro ng, they are not moving forward..... so basically it is the progress of whatever they are doing. Are they stuck at the same point? if yes, why are they stuck? Why are they not able to resolve that uncertainty? That is the question.” Experts monitor the micro progress made on sub-tasks within  a bigger problem and consider it essential for tac kling uncertainty. They frequently reflect on the state of uncertainty and accordingly prioritize them to decide how and when to address them. They simultaneously keep track of the overall progress (or macro progress) of the design life cycle which helps them regulate tactics and strategies for resolving uncertainties. For example, in the excerpt below, Expert AK emphasize the need to “track progress” by ways like setting “milestones” for managing uncertainty and regularly checking if the team is p rogressing towards them. Similarly, Expert AR talks  about tactic of using “weekly reports” to “capture all the uncertainties, all the dependencies” and use it an anchor for discussion with the teammates and for tracking progress. In our learner data, we came across only a few inst ances where learners reflect on their state of uncertainty and regulate their uncertainty management strategies and this was not a regular practice for any of the groups. The teams reflected on the state of their progress only towards the end when they were left with limited time to complete the task and when they were unable to resolve a critical uncertainty. For example, consider the excerpt from Team B, study 1. After failing to figure out where and how to place the EV3 brick for a long time, Team B participants reflect back on ideas that did not work for them earlier. Maintaining positive attitude (P6) Experts emphasize the importance of maintaining a positive attitude when dealing with uncertainties. For example, while reflecting on his earlier experiences of mana ging uncertainty, Expert ND says:  "You are working on a project, and you want it to succeed, so all the uncertainties have to be resolved. Now you cando it with a positive attitude or a negative attitude. Negative attitude will not lead you anywhere, so positive attitude is must to resolve all those uncertainties. And I think I learned this  from the funding application process long back whe re a lot of experts are sitting in a room, and they are just throwing questions at you. Is it going to be safe? How is it going to work? What percentage? And all of that.” Similarly, while talking about her teaching experie nce with undergraduate students, Expert SA says that learners generally start panicking if they cannot resolve uncertainties. In such cases, her approach is to intently listen to their concerns and encourage them to look at the uncertainties as "opportunity for learning” instead of feeling “demotivated.” ICLS2022 Proceedings 112 © ISLS We observed that learners in our study also get demotivated when they felt stuck in a situation or experience multiple uncertainties simultaneously. For example,  in one of the episodes from Study 2, Team C participants used phrases like "let us go die" (figuratively speaking) and "we will be disqualified" when all their efforts failed to figure out why their toy vehicle was not moving. Their tone was anxious and indicated disappointment. Also, after this episode of repeated failures, the team members changed their uncertainty management approach. Earlier, they were engaged in systematic experimentation and  logical reasoning to troubleshoot the problem. How ever, afterward, they blindly took decisions with the foc us to somehow complete the task instead of making s ense of their actions.

Summary. Experts emphasize on keeping a positive attitude for effective uncertainty management. Learners often panic in difficult situations and hence needs support for staying positive to ensure progress.

## Discussion

Findings from the analysis of experts' interviews advance our understanding of how uncertainties are managed in authentic scenarios of solving ill-structured engin eering design problems. Comparative analysis of lea rners' uncertainty management behaviors with respect to experts' uncertainty management help identify potential learner difficulties and challenges while managing uncertainties. These challenges could throw light on the key problem areas where learners need support.

Our findings revealed that learners' response to th e uncertainty of their team-member(s) is not very supportive (refer P1). This is aligned with the fin dings of Jordan et al. (2014) regarding the support ive and unsupportive role of peers during a robotics engineering activity. We found that in our study, the learners ridicule, ignore or delay uncertainties expressed by their team members. One of the reasons leading to these behaviors was found to be learners' positioning of themselves and  their team members during the uncertainty manageme nt process (P1). Therefore, to ensure that learners ac knowledge uncertainty expressed by their team membe rs and learn from their experiences to resolve it, they  should consider themselves and other team members as equa l collaborators and equally participate in discussions and decision-making. These findings thus indicate towards the possible need to help learners build team rapport and enable equal participation so that they acknowledge each other’s uncertainty and collectively engage in its management process. Prior research suggests “ignoring uncertainty” as one of the uncertainty management strategies of designers (Lipshitz & Strauss, 1997). However, experts suggest that instead of completely dismissing or ignoring uncertainty, one must acknowledge it and proactivel y plan mitigation steps to address the uncertainty (P1). Furthermore, experts frequently expand uncertainty and then logically converge to a decision (P2). Thi s aligns with prior research that suggests that instead of converging to decisions early, problem-solvers need to indulge in divergent thinking, especially during the initial phases of design, where they break down uncertainty further, ask more questions, explore various ideas and seek as m uch information. This helps in clarifying uncertain ty, expanding knowledge repertoire, and ensuring that decisions are logical and sound (Dym et al., 2009). Thus, based on these findings, we suggest that learners need su pport to build tolerance to uncertainty  so that they feel comfortable with its existence, acknowledge it, and also allow it to persist till they have adequate knowledge to make informed decisions.

Another finding was that learners often ask questions that demand direct answers or information when seeking feedback and usually accept the information  without assessing or evaluating them (P3). In cont rast, experts seek information from multiple reliable sou rces before taking a decision (Dym et al., 2009). E xperts engage in practices like critical thinking, analytic discussions, argumentation and systematic experimentation for assessing information, evaluating it and applying i t in the relevant context in order to make design d ecisions (Crismond & Adams, 2012). This suggest that learner s will likely require support for articulating uncertaint y better and posing relevant questions that guide their uncertainty management process. Learners may also benefit from support for making judgments and decisions about the credibility, relevance, and application of information received from various knowledge authorities. These supports could be in the form of tools along with instructions regarding their applicability to various uncertain situations. Tools serve as a medium for asking questions and finding answers as the design process unfolds (Dym et al., 2009) and are frequently used by experts (P 4). Such scaffolds are part of an expert’s repertoire and often help them keep track of micro and macro progress (P5). Uncertainties can be overwhelming and lead to a state of 'paralysis' (Ullman, 2001). To overcome these situations, maintaining a positive attitude and persevering in the face of adversity become imperative (P6). Hence learners may benefit from environments that foster positive attitudes and behaviors.

## Limitations, Implications, and Conclusion

A limitation of this study is the relatively small sample size of experts and learner groups to make claims about the generalizability of the findings. Although the large number of episodes analyzed from the data of five ICLS2022 Proceedings 113 © ISLS groups provides sufficient breadth for analysis, in cluding more heterogeneous groups would ensure dive rsity amongst participants. Similarly, interviewing more experts and conducting an ethnographic study of exp erts resolving uncertainties in their workplace setting could help in validating and refining the findings. This paper looks at engineering design practices from the lens of uncertainty management and highlights six practices that are essential for managing uncer tainty effectively. These findings provide new insi ghts for informing teaching and learning of engineering desi gn at the K-12 level. Uncertainty is an inherent pa rt of the engineering design process and findings from this study can inform the design of learning environments to support productive engagement in disciplinary practices of engineering design. For example, our study highlighted how experiencing certain uncertainties at specific desi gn stages triggers negative emotions like feeling a nxious and demotivated, and how it affects learners' uncertainty management processes. These findings can be leveraged by the Learning Sciences research community for creating robust scaffolds for supporting uncertainty management in classrooms. Future research could further elaborate on key prob lem issues that hinders learners’ engagement in engineering design practices and identify releva nt pedagogical and technological solutions to addre ss them. Simultaneously, we need to keep advancing our knowledge regarding underlying processes that affect learners’ uncertainty management.
