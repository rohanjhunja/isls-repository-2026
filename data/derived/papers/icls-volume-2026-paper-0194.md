# Learning with Eco-Hope: How Young Environmentalists Sustain Possibility through Doing, Belonging, and Becoming

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
We explored how young environmentalists sustain constructive eco-hope amid growing eco-anxiety. While depictions of environmental crisis shape how youth imagine their futures, eco-anxiety, which is characterized by distress and uncertaint y, can also serve as a motivator for learning and engagement. Drawing on Ojala’s framework of constructive hope and sociocultural perspectives on emotion and social movement learning, this study examines how eighteen young environmentalists, teenagers involved in environmental and climate initiatives, navigate the emotional tensions between hopelessness and possibility. Using Ecoportraiture, we analyze how constructive hope is enacted through practices of doing, belonging, and becoming. Findings show that eco-hope emerges through collective action, relational networks, and ecological identity work, which transform eco-anxiety into motivation and agency. Rather than a barrier, eco-anxiety can be a generative force for learning when acknowledged and supported through shared practices that foster meaning, community, and future-oriented imagination.

We must rely on information about extreme greenhouse episodes in the Earth’s distant past to light our way forward into this Sixth Circle of Hell”. (Mark Lynas in Six Degrees, p. 241) Such depictions of a world on the brink do more than inform; they shape how children and youth perceive their futures and experience the emotional burden of environmental crisis. Children’s increasing awareness of environmental degradation and climate chan ge-related disasters is leading to heightened feelings of worry, despair, helplessness, fear, and sadness (Lger-Goodes et al., 2022). Eco-anxiety is often used as an encompassing concept to describe these significant psychological and emotional response s to climate change, characterized by anticipatory distress and uncertainty about the future (Coffey et al., 2021; Gifford & Gifford, 2016). While it reflects a genuine awareness of ecological crises, its relationship to learning is complex. Heightened anxiety can overwhelm and paralyze, yet it can also act as a motivator for seeking knowledge, developing coping strate gies, and engaging in collective responses. Educators are increasingly challenged by how to integrate environmental challenges into learning contexts: raising awareness of climate problems risks provoking eco-anxiety (LegerGoodes et al., 2022; Edwards et al., 2024). Consequently, educators often emphasize hopeful narratives and practical engagement over problem-centred instruction. At the same time, research indicates that eco-anxiety can be more than just detrimental: when acknowledged and supported, it can foster engagement with proenvironmental behaviour  (Chawla, 2020; Galway et al., 2021).  Eco-anxiety is especially consequential for learning because it is future-oriented and uncertainty-laden. Unlike anger, which often directs action outward, or guilt, which centers on responsibility, eco-anxiety destabilizes expectations about personal and collective futures. This future-oriented uncertainty directly affects motivation, identity formation, and engagement. Although prior research documents eco-anxiety and its psychological impacts, fewer studies examine how eco-anxiety can be pedagogically and socially transformed into constructive participation and identity-building through collective environmental action. This study addresses that gap by exploring how young people who are already active in environmental and climate movements navigate the tension between anxiety and hope. Specifically, we answer the question: How do young environmentalists build and sustain eco-hope in the face of eco-anxiety?

## Theoretical framework

We engage with Oj ala’s framework of constructive hope (2012, 2016) to understand how emotions invoked by environmental degradation and climate change influence people’s willingness to act and engage with social movements. Hope describes the intersection of having a goal, fe eling that there are ways to reach it, and the agentic motivation to do so (Snyder et al., 2001). Hope is, therefore, decidedly future-oriented: you need to be able to imagine a future in which you can reach those goals. Especially for younger generations, imagining a future amid the accelerating rates of environmental degradation and depletion, climate change, natural disasters, and biodiversity loss is challenging and a root cause for their increased anxiety.  Constructive hope involves acknowledging these challenging eco-emotions, such as fear and anxiety, while promoting coping strategies that connect these feelings to imagining different futures and taking action to reach them. Rather than suppressing eco-anxiety or denying the reality of today’s environmental challenges, constructive hope reframes these emotions 1452 as part of a developmental process that supports agency, motivation, and long-term engagement. Ekl öf and Klöckner (2025) demonstrate how interventions that validate eco-emotions while emphasizing collective action can help young people to transform eco-anxiety into constructive eco-hope and renewed motivation to act.  We focus specifically on eco-anxiety because, unlike many other climate-related emotions, it is  directly and bidirectionally linked to climate action and activism, functioning both as a motivat or for engagement and as a state that can be reshaped through action  (Clayton & Parnes, 2025). Following this, we see eco-anxiety as a potential entry point for deeper engagement. Learning processes that combine emotional acknowledgment, opportunities for meaningful action, and experiences of belonging create conditions for eco-hope to emerge. In this sense, learning requires holding the tension between despair and possibility, anxiety and hope. From a sociocultural perspective, learning is not merely the acquisition of knowledge, but a relational process situated within activity systems, communities, and cultural practices (Rogoff, 2003; Lave, 2009). Emotions are integral to this process. Vea (2020)  describes them not as fixed internal states, but as emotional configurations: situated relationships between feeling, sense-making, and practice that emerge within social activity. From this perspective, emotions are shaped by norms, discourse and power relations, and in turn influence how people learn and act collectively. Eco-anxiety, then, can be understood as more than an individual psychological response: it is a socially negotiated experience through which young people learn to interpret, engage with, and reshape environmental challenges. Environmental activism provides a cri tical context for understanding how learning emerges through collective action. As Curnow and Jurow (2021) argue, social movements are productive sites of learning where participants generate new forms of knowledge, tactics, and identities through coming together and engaging in boundary-pushing practices. In climate movements, learning is not confined to institutional spaces but rather occurs in the streets, through organizing collectives, and in relational networks that promote and sustain action. These processes illustrate how eco-anxiety can be transformed into collective hope and agency, as activists learn not only about environmental systems but are also invited to collectively imagine and advocate for sustainable ecological futures. Taken together, these perspectives highlight eco-anxiety as both a deeply emotional and a deeply social phenomenon. Constructive hope provides a framework for understanding how difficult emotions can be reworked into possibility; sociocultural theory posit ions these emotions within shared practices of meaning-making; and social movement learning shows how collective activity enables new forms of agency and belonging. This study is part of a larger project using Ecoportraiture. This choice was informed by posthumanist theory and relational epistemologies. Following Jackson and Mazzei’s (2013) notion of thinking with theory, our framework illuminated how intergenerational connections, collective action, and everyday practices shape young people’s developing relationships with nature, environmental stewardship, and their experiences of eco-emotions such as hope, care, and anxiety. These perspectives foreground learning as an emergent, relational process that unfolds through entanglements among human and more-than-human actors, positioning eco-anxiety and other ecoemotions not as barriers but as possible generative and motivational forces that shape engagement, reflection, and collective action.  E coportraiture (Blenkinsop et al., 2022) extends Portraiture (Lawrence-Lightfoot & Davis,

2002) by combining empirical and aesthetic inquiry to represent participants'  lived experiences in relational and

multispecies contexts. This o pen-ended, iterative methodology blends analytical rigour with narrative form, enabling co-construction of knowledge through dialogue, reflection, and creative engagement. The researcher’s role involves making deliberate choices about themes while remaining  true to participants’ voices and experiences.

## Methodology & Study Context

Participants were eighteen teenagers (ages 13 –18) in southwestern Canada who were actively and voluntarily involved in out-of-school environmental and climate initiatives (e.g., youth climate groups, restoration projects, community sustainability organizat ions). Participation in these initiatives was volunteer-based and not part of the formal school curriculum.  Data included recorded semi-structured interviews, go-along interviews conducted during environmental activities  (Carpiano, 2009), field notes, artifacts from creative and visual activities (e.g., future-imagining drawings, personal meaning mapping), and audio-recorded group discussions. imaginaries (e.g., “You were 14 and decided to take on this big environmental project, what motivated you to do this?”). All interviews and discussions were transcribed and imported into NVivo for coding and thematic and Clarke’s (2006) six-phase approach, we immersed ourselves in the data through repeated reading, inductively coding by identifying patterns of ecological connection and emotional experience, and developed these into themes through collaborative discussion and refinement. Our integrative lens of constructive hope, sociocultural 1453 perspectives on emotions, and social movement learning guided our analytical attention, shaping how we identified and interpreted themes related to the participants’ activist experience, eco-anxiety, and ecological engagement. Analytically, we attended not only to emotional expressions but to how emotions were linked to participation, action, relationships, and identity talk. This allowed us to trace how eco-anxiety and eco-hope were configured within social practices rather than treated as isolated internal states.  We moved recursively between phases, revisiting and revising themes as new insights emerged, with particular attention to how participants experienced feelings of eco-anxiety and eco-hope. Through crystallization (Ellingson, 2009), we integrated multiple ways of knowing and representation, producing layered accounts that foreground both emotional texture and ecological relationalities. Ecoportraiture supported layered representation by combining narrative accounts, participant voice, researcher interpretation, and contextual detail across modalities, enabling emotional, relational, and ecological dimensions of experience to be analyzed together. Although the larger project presents findings as full Ecoportraits, this paper focuses on selected participant quotations to illustrate key patterns relevant to eco-anxiety and hope. The results are therefore not presented as complete portraits, but they were interpreted, selected, and contextualized through an ecoportraiture lens, attending to the relationa l, affective and multispecies dimensions of participants’ meaning making. This approach maintains the interpretive integrity of Ecoportraiture while adapting its representational forms to the space limitations. This study examined how young environmentalists sustain eco-hope while living with eco-anxiety. Rather than treating eco-anxiety as a singular or static emotional state, our analysis identified multiple, grounded sources of eco-anxiety across participants’ narratives. These included (1) belonging-related anxiety about not fitting dominant activist scripts or spaces (e.g., discomfort and overwhelm in protests or large crowds), (2) efficacyrelated anxiety emerging when participant s learned that individual action alone has limited systemic impact: “I thought… it doesn’t matter what I do”  (Sena), (3) burnout-type overwhelm linked to sustained engagement, recognition pressures, and uncertainty about future pathways: “And I felt in that moment, I felt kind of lost because I hadn’t received, like, any recognition for all of the work that I’d done” (Lavender), and (4)  loss-and futureoriented affective anxiety, expressed through sadness, grief, and anticipatory despair in response to ecological degradation. Participants described fear that “nothing can be done anymore” (Alyssa), and intergenerational loss comparisons such as: “My great-grandfather got to swim in the river… It really shocks me that water is now probably toxic… It is terrib le… really sad” (Urso). These accounts reflect eco-anxiety grounded not in participation pressure or activist identity, but in perceived ecological loss and temporal comparison across generations. These forms of eco-anxiety were tied to specific learning moments, participation contexts, and developmental transitions, rather than expressed as generalized emotional distress.  Across cases, participants narrated temporal pathways in which eco-anxiety was re-worked through engagement in practice rather than alleviated. A recurring sequence involved early moral “doing” (small, concrete pro-environmental actions), followed by a crisis of individual efficacy, and then a reframing of action as a pathway rather than a solution.  In these accounts, eco-anxiety did not disappear; instead, its role shifted from a stopping point to a starting condition for participation. We therefore interpret eco-hope not as the opposite of eco-anxiety, but as an emergent orientation produced through participation over time. The three analytic categories, doing, belonging, and becoming, describe the mediating practices through which anxiety and hope co-existed, with their relative predominance shifting as participants engaged in sustained action.

Hope through doing Action emerged as a primary pathway to eco-hope through participation in shared environmental practices. For many young environmentalists, hands-on engagement in activism, restoration projects, or awareness initiatives helped counter feelings of paralysis and overwhelm: “Instead of seeing the negativity and the problem, see it as an action… that’s the best way you can look at these things.” (Verbena). Participants consistently described not the disappearance of anxiety, but a functional shift —from immobilizing to mobilizing —through structured opportunities to act. Several accounts illustrated how this shift followed moments of disillusionment about impact. When individual efficacy felt undermined, participants re-oriented practice rather than withdrawing. As Sena explained, recognizing the limits of individual carbon reduction initially led to discouragement, but later reframed individual action as “a pathway and a means to empowering people to call on powerful systems and create change” (Sena). Here, agency was mediated by practice: engaging in meaningful, situated actions reshaped emotional orientation and restored a sense of influence. As Emily noted, “If you look at the problem as something you can work on, it feels less hopeless… otherwise you just get stuck in the negativity.” (Emily). Importantly, action was described not only in terms of outcomes but also symbolic and affective effects. Restoration projects, in particular, were experienced as hope-affirming and generative of ripple effects: “This 1454 transformation from clean to polluted to restoration inspires a lot of hope… actually going out and doing something is really useful.” (Sena). In this sense, doing functioned as an emotional and learning practice through which anxiety was re-channelled into agency.

Hope through belonging Belonging emerged as a second mediating process through  which eco-hope was sustained. Participants emphasized that hope was not maintained individually, but through participation in relational networks of care and shared purpose, including families, peers, and youth environmental communities. These spaces often appeared after or alongside moments of discouragement, functioning as stabilizing participation contexts. As one participant explained, “The important thing is that you do your own part, and that will inspire people around you to also do their own part.” (Sena).  Through belonging, eco-anxiety was normalized and shared rather than internalized. Participants described how surrounding themselves with others who shared environmental values helped sustain motivation: “Those people have influenced me a lot in how I view  the world and how I choose to approach sustainability.” (Sena). These relational settings functioned as communities of practice in which emotional validation, shared narratives, and joint activity supported continued engagement even when uncertainty or doubt persisted. In line with constructive hope, collective contexts enabled participants to hold anxiety while maintaining trust in ongoing action.

Hope through becoming The third pathway to sustaining eco-hope was grounded in embodied encounters with nature and intergenerational continuity. Across participants, direct experiences in natural places and shared outdoor practices with family were repeatedly described as stabilizing motivation and future commitment. Young people linked experiences of beauty and joy in nature to reasons for environmental action: “I’ve lived a beautiful life on this planet… I want the future to be able to see it as well.” (Alysa). Here, hope oper ates not as abstract optimism but as identity-in-practice as an ongoing process of becoming in relation to ecological communities.  Participants described eco-hope as something learned and enacted through evolving self-understandings shaped by relational experiences with both people and more-than-human worlds. These experiences mediated how eco-anxiety was interpreted: worry and overwhelm were not erased but re-oriented toward situated forms of contribution. Positive associations with family, friends, and time outdoors were central to this process: “Being exposed to nature just shows you what you’re trying to protect and gives you a reason why you want to fight for it.” (Jake).  Alysa described one such formative experience: “A trip in Thailand once… my grandmother and my sister and I… started picking [garbage] up… It created a bond… camping trips in nature with my family created really good memories… it helped foster really good connection. From a sociocultural perspective, these accounts show emotions not only as conditions of learning but also outcomes of participation in relational practice. Eco-hope emerges here through identity formation and meaning-making across generations and settings, extending constructive hope beyond cognitive reframing to embodied, affective, and relational learning. Together with doing and belonging, becoming shows how eco-anxiety is not simply replaced but worked with over time, supporting durable commitment under conditions of uncertainty.

These findings highlight eco-hope as a situated and sociocultural learning process that emerges  through young people’s engagement with eco-anxiety. In this study, hope was not expressed as abstract optimism or emotional avoidance but as practice, with participants actively cultivating what they did, who they connected with, and how they came to see themselves in relation to nature and the future. Doing, belonging, and becoming represent the interrelated practices by which eco-anxiety is reconfigured into constructive eco-hope. Through doing, participants engaged in environmental actions that allowed them to transform fear into agency. Belonging sustained hope through relational networks of peers, families, and mentors who validated difficult emotions and modelled persistence in individual and collective struggles. Becoming reflected the longer-term learning process of integrating environmental care into one’s identity and worldview, supported by intergenerational stories, collective outdoor experiences, and relational encounters with the more-than-human world. Together, these participation. Rather than treating eco-anxiety as a pathological barrier, our findings affirm that it can serve as an entry point for new forms of learning when embedded in supportive practices of doing, belonging, and becoming. For educators and learning scientists, this suggests that fostering eco-hope requires creating contexts where young people can act meaningfully, connect relationally, and develop enduring ecological identities, thus enabling them to imagine and enact sustainable ecological futures.
