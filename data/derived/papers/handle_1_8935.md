# Racism With Antiracists: Examining Sensemaking in a School-University Partnership for Antiracism

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In this paper we explore dialogue between members of an RPP who shared a goal of developing an antiracist learning community. We use one area of disagreement that emerged throughout the partnership to depict how antiracist discourses can align with racist narratives. In our analysis we identified three ways in which RPP partners justified focusing on anti-Black biases over a broader definition of racism, including the need: to uproot internalized anti-Black biases; choose how to use limited resources towards antiracist goals; and create conditions that mitigate Black studentsÕ behaviors. Each of these justifications reinforced racist ideologies by separating racialized groups, putting them in competition for attention rather than promoting solidarity and collaborative change, centering white people and their thoughts, and promoting a racist/non-racist binary. We conclude with implications for supporting the design of antiracist, transformative learning opportunities for educators.

Eduardo Bonilla-Silva (2020) describes Òracism without racistsÓÑor, how white supremacist structures, systems, and practices could persist in a society in which m ost people disavow racism and would never consent t o characterizations of themselves as racist. While Òcolor-blind racismÓ certainly persists (ibid), the current moment of blatant white supremacist violence (e.g., the recent wave of anti-Asian hate crimes, the January 6 insurrection, etc.) and highly visible resistance (e.g., Black Li ves Matter) has spurred many people, including educ ators, to adopt the label Òantiracist,Ó explicitly rejecting racism and race-evasive approaches to change. Antir acist movements are not necessarily free of racism themselves, however. In this paper, we present our analyses of an RPP that actively sought to develop an Òantiracist learning community,Ó exploring how we and our partners made sense of antiracist goals through particular narratives and discourses. As we show, racism may be not only present but actually reinforced in discourse that takes up an explicitly antiracist stance. Thus, in this paper, we explore the question: How can ostensibly antiracist discourses support racist ideologies?

## Theoretical Background

Race is a social construct, created to differentiat e racial groups and to justify white supremacy as a  system of racial domination (Solórzano & Yosso, 2001). This system involves racist structures, practices, and outcomes; it also involves sociohistorical narratives that posit ion (American) white ways of thinking, learning, fe eling, and being as normal and good while others as obviously and self evidently less than (e.g., Ferguson, 2000; Menchaca & Valencia, 1990; Shah, 2019). Further, racism and antiracism are complex and intersectional. In the w ords of Lorde (1984), “there is no such thing as a single-issue struggle, because we do not live single-issue lives” (p. 138). Systems of oppression based on race, ethnicity, gender, sexual orientation, language, class, disability, and other socially constructed categories function simultaneo usly and in concert to produce inequality in school s as elsewhere (Grant & Zweir, 2012). Thus, to be truly antiracist is to fight all forms of hierarchy and r ecognize all humans as “levelly human” (the Combahee River Colle ctive, as quoted by Kendi, 2019, p. 187). Coordinat ing this understanding of racism with literature characterizing multicultural, antiracist, and socially just education, we conceptualize antiracist learning communities in schools as contexts that value and respond to the full humanity of each student, particularly each student of color (e.g., Kendi, 2019). Our partnerships with schools have led us to conceptualize radical belonging as a way to center the social and academic needs o f students of color in a democratically constituted antiracist learning community (Louie, Berland, Pacheco, Roeker, Grant, under review).

## Literature Review

As we partner with a school to develop an antiracis t learning communityÑa community that fosters the radical belonging of each of its students, particularly students of colorÑwe understand that “in the attempt to correct so many generations of bad faith and cruelty, when it is operating not only in the classroom but in society, [we] will meet the most fantastic, the most brutal, and the m ost determined resistance” (Baldwin, 1963). It also manifests in the unconscious disruption of antiracist projects, even by those who claim antiracist or equity-oriented projects as their own. For example, Louie (2020) found that teachers actively working to create environments that would support academic belonging did so in ways that reproduced ability hierarchies. In this and other examples (e.g., Ladson-Billings, 2014), “determined resistance” came in the form of unconscious ideologies that disrupted teachers’ con scious intentions. Despite those intentions, teachers did not have the political and ideological clarity (Bartolomé, 1994) to enact antiracist goals, nor were they supported to develop such clarity. In this study, we explore how systemic racism continued to pop-up and “resist” the process  of working towards an antiracist learning communit y throughout the discourse of our partnership.

## Methods and Context

This study stems from a partnership between our uni versity and Eastwood Elementary, located in a mid-s ized Midwestern city (the names of the school and participants not on the university team are pseudonyms). Eastwood teachers and administrators invited us (pre-pandemi c) to support them on their commitment to become an antiracist learning community. Most of Eastwood’s s tudents are students of color. The staff is 90% white. We were invited to partner with Eastwood because, despite their efforts towards addressing race-based harm in their school, racism persisted throughout the school: Eng lish monolingual students were bullying peers who s poke other languages, Asian American students were being mocked by classmates squinting and stretching their eyes, students with disabilities were being called the R-word, and a white student repeatedly called Black educators the N-word. Moreover, as a Black teacher at the school explained, the school lacked a shared understanding of racism and commitment to antiracism.

Over the course of a year and a half, we met month ly with the school-based leadership team, spent time in classrooms, and facilitated two summer retreats. For the present analysis, we draw on field notes and recordings from the second summer retreat (in 2020) and field notes from debriefing/planning meetings between the University team and Eastwood leadership. The Eastwood principal encouraged all teachers and staff to attend the retreat and invited parents; 21 teachers and staff and four parents participated. The 2020 retreat ran for 5 days. Our analysis focuses on a topic that emerged repeatedly throughout the retreat, and which stood out to us because of the strong emotions it evoked (for the paper authors as well as other participants) and the ways in which it repeatedly disrupted discussions of other topics. Specifically, our analysis focuses on our discussions about whether our develo ping antiracist learning community at Eastwood shou ld specifically and exclusively target Black students and families or take a broader lens to the work. Pa rticularly active in these discussions were the principal (Amy, a white woman), the vice principal (Keith, a Black man), the school social worker (Neal, a white man), the distr ict restorative justice coach (Josh, a Black man), the teacher who initiated our partnership (Malik, a Black man),  and the mother of an Eastwood student (Che, a Hmon g woman). These discussions occurred during debriefing/planning meetings with the Eastwood leadership team as well as during the retreat itself.

Our analysis of the discussions had two phases. In the first phase, we sought to understand the justifications offered by participants for either a) focusing on anti-Blackness or b) defining racism more broadly. To that end, we employed a process of in-vivo codin g, constant comparison of coded episodes, and team discussion to identify themes in the justifications  for the different perspectives. We present these t hemes in the findings section. Our second phase was to interpret the themes in terms of how they disrupted or reproduced racist and white supremacist narratives and/or created opportunities for antiracist learning. We offer these interpretations in our discussion section.

We locate ourselves in our analysis as both researc hers and co-partners with the Eastwood educators. Not only were all members of this partnership learn ing together, but each of our contributions to the discussion shaped the shared (or unshared) meanings that were being co-constructed. Our team is racially diverse (including Black, Chicana, Chinese American, and white individuals) and brings different areas of expertise (e.g., learning sciences, and math, science, teacher, and multicultural education) to the work.

## Discussion

By publicly acknowledging race-based harm and calling for work to combat it, participants positioned themselves discursively as critically conscious antiracists. However, we argue that the narratives embedded in some of these concerns ultimately reinforce white supremacy. In particular, we see three ways in which the explicit justifications for exclusively focusing on Black students and anti-Black sentiment implicitly align with the maintena nce of white supremacy. We believe our Eastwood partners h ad good intentions, but racism persisted in and thr ough them—a phenomenon that becomes clear when contextua lizing and historicizing their justifications withi n the systems of oppression upon which the United States is built. ● Erasing Latina/o/x and Hmong students and families: elevating the experiences of Black students and family members to the exclusion of all others devalued Latina/o/x and Hmong students and their families, dismissing their school experiences as unimportant and ultimately rendering them invisible. ● Obscuring Black studentsÕ full humanity:  emphasizing behavior reduces  (anti)racism to externally visible outcomes that can be measured, observed, and compared, rather than nuanced experiences that can be shared, understood, and improved. That is, by making Black studentsÕ behavior hyper-visible, participants made studentsÕ capacities and needs as learners, knowers, and thinkers invisible. ● Undermining efforts to build interracial solidarity: by making it seem as though the school had to make a choice between either addressing harm to Black students and families or addressing policies and practices steeped in white supremacy more broadly, antiracism was framed as a limited resource for which marginalized individuals must compete.

● Centering white people: while engaging white individuals in confronting their internalized racial biases and white privilege is valuable, emphasizing the in ternalized racism of white educators at Eastwood served to center the thoughts of white people without attending to the experiences of students of colorÑ the very individuals and experiences that motivated our collaboration. Further, focusing on individuals’ internal biases does not move to transformation of practice, so it does not have a clear impact on the experiences of the students of color.

In summary, while each individual in our partnershi p was there to disrupt racism in Eastwood, we see w ays in which the discussions in which we engaged unintentionally reinforced white supremacy. Members of both the university and Eastwood portions of our partnership experienced one anotherÕs positions as racistÑas harming students of colorÑand this racist/non-racist binary that implicitly emerged in our discussions eroded collective trust in the partnership. Indeed, toward the end of the debrief about the first day of the retreat, Keith said: ÒIÕve been disheartened by the conversation today. Last summer we were making a lot of progress. I donÕt think right now there is respect for our idea s É It felt to me like the conversation wasnÕt a di alogue, [Eastwood] was attacked.Ó And, this was not resolve d. At the end of the retreat, the partnership did n ot have a shared vision for our joint antiracist work. Instea d, the contradictory views of the focus of our part nership remained throughout the summer retreat, repeatedly coming up and disrupting other discussions. Learning theories (pick your flavor, they agree on this point!) tells us that deep conceptual change takes time, and willingness to be wrong. We had neither and we now wonder about strategies for resolving such fundamental disagreements hidden by superficially similar discourse but based on very different ideological underpinnings. One possible strategy for moving forward could have  been to collaboratively unpack the different justifications for the exclusive focus on Black stu dents and to co-construct a new vision that brought  the implications of the different positions into alignm ent. Another possibility could have been using East wood educatorsÕ interest in focusing on Black students to motivate a careful exploration of the cultural wealth (Yosso,

2005) that different Black students and their commu nities have. The goal of this work would have been to

acknowledge the diversity within the label ÒBlack s tudents,Ó and using that nuance to motivate antirac ist pedagogical and curricular practices that make space for the diverse ideas, experiences, and cultural practices of Latina/o/x and Hmong Eastwood students as well. Regardless of the specific strategy we took, we would need to focus on building the Òpoliticized trustÓ (Vakil et al., 2016, p. 199) that could have enabled us to identify ways in which we were using words differently and the implications of those differences. This analysis returns us to a question Carl Grant (a member of the University team) repeatedly asked throughout our partnership: ÒCan you create an antiracist school in a racist school system?Ó. While this study does not answer this question, it suggests that time and  politicized trust are necessary for taking the fir st steps of envisioning what that antiracist school community could even look like.
