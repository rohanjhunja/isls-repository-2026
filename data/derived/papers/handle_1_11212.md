# Encountering Freedom in the Wild: A comparative Case Study of a Formal Classroom and Nature-based Informal Learning Environment Concerning the Role of Knowledge Authority and Subjectification

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study explores the dynamics of a formal classroom and nature-based informal learning environment, focusing on their impact on student subjectification and knowledge authority. This comparative case study, involving 27 fifth-grade students and their teacher, examined shifts in teaching approaches and student autonomy across these settings through analysis of observational data, recordings, interviews, and surveys. A didactic approach centralizes knowledge authority in the classroom, while nature-based programs adopt a more dialogical model that promotes exploration. Findings reveal that the environment supports a shift in teacher and student roles, enhancing student agency and subjectification. This research contributes to the discourse on generative learning environments (GLE) and highlights practices that balance teacher authority with student empowerment. It also underscores the need for further research on the connections between informal learning contexts and student subjectification.

## Problem statement

Envisioning a classroom today often evokes images o f neatly arranged desks, a chalkboard, and the ever-present U.S. flag—a snapshot reminiscent of the first author’s own schooling experiences in the rural Midwestern United States. Despite teaching in diverse learning environments  for over a decade, this persistent memory symboliz es an entrenched education system that often prioritiz es rote memorization over authentic learning (Giroux, 2010; Hooks, 1994; Robinson, 2001). Such a system, centered on disseminating a singular “truth,” frequently suppresses creativity, enforces conformity, and perpetuates what Freire described as a transactional model of education—one in which students are positioned as passive recipie nts of knowledge (e.g. objects) rather than active constructors of meaning (e.g. subjects) (Freire, 1993, as cited in Biesta, 2017). Despite recent curricular reforms advocating for student-centered approaches, formal classroom learning environments (LEs), characterized by structured curricula and standardized assessment methods (Grunzke, 2019), can inadvertently limit students’ opportunities to explore ideas freely, (Hand & Cavagnetto, 2023; Low ell et al., 2024). Conversely, informal learning environments, specifically nature-based settings, offer alternative spaces for student-driven inquiry, providing more opportunities  to construct knowledge in authentic contexts (Link, 2023; Mann et al., 2022). Although extensive research has investigated knowle dge authority  in formal classroom environments (Chen et al., 2017; Fiorella & Mayer, 2016; Lammert et al., 2022; Pace & Hemmings, 2007; Suh et al., 2022), fewer studies have explored these dynamics specifically within informal, nature-based environments. To explore these two environments this  study investigated how one  fifth grade  teacher and her 27 students navigated  the shift between their formal classroom and a three-day nature-based learning experience. Findings provide insights into how nature-based environments disrupted traditional roles, aff ording greater freedom for teachers and students to engage in dialogic discourse compared to the formal classroom setting. This research suggests that nature-based settings can cultivate more generative and dialogical student-centered learning environments.

## Theoretical framework

This study is grounded in two frameworks—Gert Biesta's (2017) philosophical exploration of freedom and the theoretical principles of Generative Learning Theor y(Osborne & Wittrock, 1985). Biesta's work, particularly in "The Rediscovery of Teaching" (2017), challenges tr aditional educational models and argues that educat ion should go beyond qualifying students for the workfo rce and socializing them into existing norms; it sh ould also support their subjectification —the process of becoming autonomous, responsible agents. This study applies Biesta's ideas to explore how the two different LEs influence students' opportunities to engage as subjects. Generative Learning Theory (GLT), proposed by Osbor ne and Wittrock (1985), complements Biesta's framework by emphasizing the active role of learners in constructing meaning. GLT posits that generative learning occurs when students actively engage in the process of integrating new information with their existing knowledge. We used GLT to examine how the formal or informal L E shapes students' opportunities to construct knowl edge and exercise their subjec t-ness. This study focuses on the role of dialogue, l anguage, and teacher-student interactions in fostering or hindering subjectifica tion. The research aims to provide a nuanced unders tanding of how LEs maximize or hinder students' opportunities to engage in their ideas by integrating these two frameworks. Furthermore, while scholars have utilized GLT to ex plore knowledge authority and the impact of languag e and dialogic interactions in the classroom (Chen et al., 2017; Fiorella & Mayer, 2016; Suh et al., 2022), researchers are only just beginning to explore how the concept of equality of intelligence and subjectification relates to GLT in the classroom LE (Hand & Cavagnetto, 2023). Exploring these concepts in LEs outside the formal LE remains underexplored. This warrants further investigation into how knowledge authority and language, as they relate to subjectification, manifest in informal NBL environm ents and could provide valuable insights  for educators and researchers alike.

This study focused on dialogue as a way of exploring and understanding subjectification in each LE. Vygotsky (1987), as cited by Matusov et al. (2013), posited that the essence of humanity is dialogue, w hich in education involves interactive communication betwee n teachers and students, where ideas are exchanged, challenged, and refined (Lemke, 1990). Dialogue pro vides a window into the unseen conscious experience s of students, offering insights into how they process i nformation and construct meaning. By prioritizing d ialogue as the primary form of data, this study captures the interactive nature of learning and subjectification, observing how students articulate understanding, respond to criti cal probing, and negotiate meaning in real time. Th e teacher's role is crucial in framing the LE to facilitate this process, aligning with Biesta's educational philosophy and GLT to maximize the development of autonomous, engaged, and critically thinking individuals.

## Social and material context

Ms. Ash Ms. Ash is a warm and welcoming 5th-grade teacher with 23 years of experience in the K-12 school system. Her commitment to student learning is evident through c onversations and observations, and she is dedicated  to her students. Her structured classroom routine created a calm start to each morning. She spent time greeting as many students as possible and began each day with a morning circle, discussing emotional management and other socioemotional learning strategies. These daily greetings served to check in with students. As part of this study, Ms. Ash completed an epistemic orientation survey (EOS, Suh et al., 2022) that explored her epistemic beliefs and the Connectedness to Nature Scale  (CNS, Mayer & Frantz, 2004). Due to some incomplete survey responses, it was not possible to fully score Ms. Ash’s responses, however her survey data was utilized to provide context of how Ms. Ash situates  herself. The surveys revealed three key findings about her connection to nature, her attitude toward teaching in nature, and her beliefs about learning:

• Connection to Nature.  Ms. Ash enjoys nature and feels strongly connected to it. She appreciates the

intelligence of other living organisms, feels part of the web of life, and considers herself embedded in the natural world.

• Attitude Towards Teaching in Nature.  Ms. Ash is excited about teaching in nature and bel ieves it

positively impacts student learning. She understand s effective strategies for teaching environmental concepts and is confident in finding ways to integr ate nature into her teaching. However, some observations indicated a slight nervousness and lack of confidence in navigating teaching in nature.

• Beliefs About Student Learning. Ms. Ash believes that teachers significantly impact student learning. She

agreed that teacher effort leads to positive change s in student achievement in understanding environmental concepts. She also believes that when  students improve, it is due to the e xtra attention from the teacher, indicating a teacher-driven approach to student learning. Furthermore, M s. Ash’s believes in the social construction of knowledge. S he does not believe her knowledge came solely from self-construction and interactions with people but also ackn owledges that learning happens through negotiation.

These findings somewhat align with Ms. Ash's respon ses in the EOS, where she agreed that students learn best when they have ownership of their learni ng and that it is acceptable for students to develo p incorrect answers if they can justify them. She also emphasiz ed the importance of students negotiating meaning b etween their prior knowledge and new information. However, a  slight contradiction emerged regarding control ove r learning. While she expressed that teachers should not dominate classroom knowledge, her responses in the attitudes survey indicated that she views teachers as primarily responsible for student learning. This suggests that Ms. Ash's epistemic orientation for learning is som ewhat teacher-driven, placing her toward the less generative side of the orientation spectrum through the lens o f GLT. This perspective aligns with many classroom teachers who see themselves as drivers of the learning process. It is important to note that her beliefs are not reflective of good or bad teaching but provide valuable insight i nto understanding learning environments. Although s elfreported surveys have limitations, they offer a gli mpse into Ms. Ash's views on learning  and provide context to the learning environments that Ms. Ash creates.

The formal learning environment: The classroom The formal classroom environment takes place in a large urban school in the Midwestern United States. The main focal point of the classroom features large windows  along the back wall, which allow ample natural lig ht to illuminate the space. These windows a re surrounded by various plants, contributing to th e room's integration of natural elements. The shelves beneath the windows d isplay a collection of items, including a photo of primatologist Jane Goodall and a Jane Goodall Barbi e doll. The classroom is decorated primarily in earthy tones of blues and greens, which align with Ms. Ash’s connectedness to nature. While Ms. Ash selected most of the room's decoratio ns, the hallway leading to the classroom displays students' projects and work. This contrast indicates that student work is displayed prominently, albeit outside the main classroom space. Each morning, classical music plays as students enter the classroom. The board lists tasks for students to complete while waiting for morning announcements, typically including selecting their lunch, finishing previous assignments, or reading.

Beyond the classroom's physical structures, the learning environment consi sts of a strict scope and sequence of curricula. Ms. Ash is expected to teach the same concepts and material, at the same time and pace as her two fellow 5 th grade teachers, as well as keep pace with all other  elementary schools across the large school district to help support a large transient populati on of students. Through the observations and conver sations with Ms. Ash, this limited the amount of flexibility she  had in what she taught and the pace at which she t aught. This issue highlights the tension many teachers face in the formal classroom environment. Informal nature-based learning environment: School of the Wild School of the Wild (SOTW)  offers a NBL experience during the traditional acad emic year, either in the fall or spring. Students transition from their usual classr oom settings to local state or county parks near their school for at least four days. In small groups of 8-15 peers, they engage in one of five unique areas of study each day under the guidance of local naturalists, conservationists, and their classroom teachers. The curriculum ensu res that the environment is integral to the activities, making the concepts relevant to the students and their local context. The curriculum is interdisciplinary and incorporates sc ience, language arts, social studies, art, math, ph ysical education, and outdoor recreation (UI WILD-The University of Iowa, 2022). The data presented here is part of  a larger study, following three teachers from this school and their students as they participated in SOTW. However, our focus here is on just one of those thr ee teachers, Ms. Ash. Participants in this study attended SOTW for three days at a local state park 30 minutes from the school. On Day One, students explored the landscape's geological features and wetlands. Their classroom teacher taught this day. During Day Two, another fifth-grade teacher taught students about archeology and how the land can tell us about our past. On Day Three  (this group’s final day), students learned about birds and identification p rocesses. This day was taught by another fifth-grade teacher and a SOTW staff member. The original planning structure was supposed to be four days. However, the school lost a day due to a school violence threat, which caused the student group structure to be rearranged at the last minute  and only allowed for a three-day experience and larger group sizes, which is not usual.

Positionality The first author  has 12 years of  classroom teaching experience and a background in environmental educat ion, which influenced the study design and the interpret ation of findings. Their professional experience in  structured classroom settings provided insights into traditional pedagogical practices, while their acad emic experience in environmental education informed their interest in exploring nature-based learning environments. These dual perspectives shaped the research questions and meth odology, bringing both a critical understanding of formal education and an appreciation for the interdiscipli nary potential of nature-based learning. Throughout the study, the author engaged in reflexive practices to ensure  the participants’ voices and experiences were fore grounded and to mitigate potential biases stemming from their professional and academic background.

## Method

We conducted a comparative case-study analysis of the formal classroom LE and the informal NBL environment (Stake, 2005). Data were collected through classroom and nature-based observations, audio  recordings in the classroom and video recordings with wearable cameras in nature, focus group interviews, and surveys, providing a comprehensive view of the experiences and interac tions of the participants within each environment. Ethical considerations were rigorously addressed, with informed consent obtained from guardians, adult participants, and student assent. By contrasting these environments, the study sought to uncover how the teacher positioned herself and her students in both environments, particularly  regarding knowledge authority. This involved exploring how her pedagogy differs between the formal classroom and the nature-based program and how these similarities and differences impacted their subjectification.

Examining these distinct roles, we asked: (1) How does the teacher shift their approach to teachi ng in the informal nature-based environment compared to the formal classroom LE? (2) How do students respond to and engage with encounters of freedom in the formal  classroom LE versus the informal nature-based classroom LE? (3) What role does the social and material context of SOTW play in differently support ing student subjectness? Analysis All audio and video recordings from the classroom and nature-based observations were transcribed verbatim. The transcripts were segmented by turns, as defined by Johnstone (2008). Each segment started when a speaker began talking and ended when either the speaker was inter rupted or stopped talking. Transcripts were coded a  priori using a Generative Learning Environment Matrix (GL E-Matrix) (Saldaña, 2021). This matrix included codes related to Probing (Bailin & Battersby, 2021), Language and Representation  (Fiorella & Mayer, 2016), Knowledge Authority (Suh et al., 2022), and Connections between learners (Lemke, 1990). Probing looks at ways questioning and prompts occur. Language is looking at the type of language used to see who is authorin g the language. Knowledge Authority codes indicate the source of knowledge used. Finally, the Connections category looks at the connections students or teachers make  between each other and the big ideas in the classro om. While the entire codebook cannot be represented in its en tirety, Table 1 highlights the main high-level category codes. These top coding categories are presented with the transcripts in Table 2 to show how this codebook was used. RQ 2: How do students respond to and engage with encounters of freedom in the formal classroom and the nature-based environment?

The analysis reveals differences in how student sub ject-ness manifested in both environments. In the formal classroom, where the teacher maintained a more trad itional role as the primary authority of knowledge,  students were observed to seem more passive, often waiting for direction and validation from the teacher before engaging with the material. The structured nature of the lessons and the emphasis on correct answers limited opportunities for students to exercise or explore their subject-ness. Table 3 shows how, in the classroom LE, very few students were active in the dialogue, leaving a missed opportunity for the teacher to gain insight into what they knew. The informal, nature-based environment offers a contrast to the more str uctured classroom settings, providing students with greater opportunities for o pen dialogue and exploration. In this setting, stud ents encountered numerous moments where they took up t heir "subject-ness," actively engaging as curious learners. This was evident during a hike, where students' nat ural curiosity led to deeper inquiry. As they explo red their surroundings, they asked more questions, driven by their interests. A particularly illustrative moment occurred when the students stumbled upon an unusual tree with a giant mushroom growing on it. In this episode, Ms. Ash le d them through the discussion without offering her own knowledge of the tree. The students engaged in expl oratory dialogue, making observations about their surroundings, such as identifying tree fungi and di scussing the potential habitats within a hollow tre e. The conversation touched on topics like wild edibles, w here SOTW staff emphasized the importance of certainty before consuming anything from nature. Throughout the episode, Ms. Ash encouraged curiosity by prompting the students to consider what might live in the hollow tree and how such formations come to be, suggesting  future research opportunities. Ms. Ash’s probing questions prompted students to hypothesize and engage in collaborative problem-solving, showing a moment where they took up their subject-ness. This encounter sparked conversation among the group. At this time, Ms. Ash shifted her knowledge authority to the students. Rather than pr oviding immediate answers, she encouraged them to investigate and discuss their observations. The alignment with student curiosity and Ms. Ash giving up her knowledge autho rity provided an opportunity for stude nts to take up their subject-ness.

Within SOTW, students embraced their subject-ness, actively taking initiative and expressing cur iosity. Nature hikes, beach explorations, and other activit ies provided them with choices, empowering them to make observations and pose questions of their own. Ms. Ash’s use of open-ended questions, such as “What do you think this is?” or “How does this happen?” further encour aged students to think critically, share ideas, and collaboratively build understanding. These inquiry-based interactions allowed students  to move from passive recipients of knowledge to engaged participants, em bodying their role as active subjects within the le arning process.

RQ 3: What role does the social and material context of SOTW differently support student subject-ness?

The social and material aspects of learning environ ments can influence how students engage and participate. In many structured classroom settings,  the environment is often arranged to reflect autho rity and control, with desks typically organized in rows and  communication flowing primarily from teacher to student. Participation might follow a structured pattern, su ch as raising hands to speak, which could subtly em phasize correct answers and the teacher's role as the centr al knowledge authority. In contrast,  the nature-based setting at SOTW appeared to offer a more open and flexible space. W ithout the presence of desks or formal seating arrangements, students seemed to move and interact more freely, with the social dynamics shifting towa rd collaboration and exploration. Knowledge in this se tting was potentially co-constructed through dialogue and shared discovery, suggesting that these less rigid social and material features could help foster a different kind of student engagement.

These contrasting social and material features provide important context for understanding how students perceive the "costs" of participation in each environment. By examining students' experiences and behaviors, we can explore how these settings may influ ence their willingness to engage in dialogue, share  ideas, and exercise their agency in the learning process. In structured  classroom environments, participating in dialogue often involves a high perceived cost for students. They m ay fear social critique or feel apprehensive about the effort required to engage meaningfully in teacher-led discussions (Cavagnetto et al., 2020). For participation to be sustained, the perceived benefits must outweigh the se costs. Focus group interviews revealed that stud ents frequently felt uncomfortable sharing ideas in the classroom. One student noted feeling “nervous or embarrassed to be “wrong,” while another student expressed, “In class, it’s like the teacher is alwa ys just busy,” suggesting a perceived lack of individual attention and support.

The nature-based setting at SOTW appeared lowered these perceived costs, fostering a more inf ormal atmosphere that encouraged students to participate without fear of judgment. Students felt “taken more  seriously in nature” and that “the teacher actually cares,” leading them to feel more open to engaging without waiting to be called upon. One student mentioned that in nature, they could ask peers questions directly without the  formality of raising their hand. This shift in behavior suggests that students felt empowered to exercise their “subject-ness” in the nature-based setting, engaging freely with both the teacher and their peers. The reduction in perceived social and cognitive cos ts was further evident in specific observational episodes. In Ms. Ash's classroom, students often mirrored her actions—such as copying verbatim what she wrote on the board—indicating a reluctance to deviate from the teacher’s example due to the structured, correct-answerfocused environment. In contrast, SOTW highlighted a shift in authority, with students confidently ini tiating dialogue, asking questions, and making independent observations without the fear of immediate correcti on or social critique. This informal setting encouraged s tudents to engage in dialogue-driven learning, suggesting that lowering perceived costs through a nature-based environment can significantly enhance student participation and agency.

## Discussion

This study suggests that informal, nature-based learning environments offer unique opportunit ies for fostering student subjectification. By comparing these settin gs with traditional classrooms, the research provid es insights into how knowledge authority and dialogic pedagogy can shape learning experiences that allow students to engage more actively with their subject-ness. The findings contribute to the ongoing discus sion of generative learning environments (GLEs) by exploring how material and social contexts influence the roles of teachers and students. Ms. Ash’s pedagogical shift from a teacher-centered model in the formal classroom to a dialogi c approach in the nature-based environment supports Biesta’s (2017) framewor k of subjectification. Biesta posits that education should enable students to become aut onomous, critically thinking individuals who engage meaningfully with the world. While this study does not entirely address these concepts, it does illustrate how the informal NBL encouraged students to engage more in dialogue and explore their curiosity. By promoting a dialogic rather than monologic teaching approach, SOTW reduced barriers to student participation and enhanced their subject-ness. This supports the findings involving student participation and the cost-benefit ratio in the formal classroom and contributes to the findings in an informal context (Cavagnetto et al., 2020). Future studies could examine the interplay between knowledge authority, subjectification, and dialogue across various informal contexts. Mann et al. (2022) highlighted the need for comparative analyses bet ween traditional and informal learning settings, and this study begins to address that gap. Future research could explore how other informal environments, such as museums or  community spaces, similarly support student autono my and engagement. Additionally, longitudinal research  could assess the long-term i mpact of informal learning experiences on students’ academic and personal deve lopment, offering deeper insights into how such environments contribute to sustained learning outcomes.
