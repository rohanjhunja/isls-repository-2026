These The purpose of science education is to grow an understanding of how the natural world works and its role in society using scientific knowledge, practices, and skills (Harlen, 2010, 2015). One goal of science education is to develop a scientifically literate society where all learners can apply scientific concepts to make sense of phenomena, solve problems, and think innovatively (Harlow & Bianchini, 2020). The purpose of teaching science in elementary and middle school (preK-eighth grade) is to meet this goal and help students develop a deeper understanding of the world.

Unfortunately, many students struggle to learn science. Science learning challenges students for many reasons. Some students display learning differences (LD), lack motivation, are distracted, have inadequate skills or preparation, or a combination of these factors. An LD is defined as “a disorder in one or more of the basic psychological processes involved in understanding or using language, spoken or written, that may manifest itself in the imperfect ability to listen, think, speak, read, write, spell, or  to do mathematical calculations” (34 Code of Federal Regulations §300.7(c)(10)).

Science can be challenging to LD students because of the diverse demands it places on cognitive performance (Brigham et al., 2011). Motivation can play a significant role in their science learning, as it can influence achievement, the development of skills, ability, and the desire to prepare (Bonney et al., 2005). Social environments and the people within them influence the motivation of individuals by altering their internal motivation and growth (Ryan & Deci, 2000). The literature documents that students with LDs can have less motivation in academically-related learning than students without an LD (Seyed et al., 2017). A LD's relation to motivation and learning is complicated but can be explained. LDs can cause individuals to think differently from individuals without LDs (Stage & Milne, 1996). Some differences cause students with LDs to struggle with fitting in with their peers (Baum & Owen, 1988). In academic learning environments, when students with LDs are first motivated to fit in, learning is sacrificed, because only remaining motivation and cognitive resources are used to learn. This can be understood through the lens of working memory, which has a limited capacity (Simmering & Perone, 2013). When a student with an LD is in an academic environment with their peers, they are using some of their working memory to connect with other students (Oberauer et al., 2016). This means that there is less working memory left to learn academics.  Utilizing the knowledge in pieces theory (KiP), if LD students are not constructing knowledge at the same rate as their classmates, they will fall behind (diSessa, 1988).

To aid LD students in learning science successfully, one promising alternative to traditional classroom settings is outdoor, nature-based learning (NBL) (Ayotte-Beaudet et al., 2017). Physical settings shape the way knowledge is encountered, embodied, and constructed. Nature-based settings are intrinsically pedagogical, facilitating not only environmental education but also sensory, emotional, and social engagement (Emmoth, 2025). Some of the positive outcomes of NBL include a deeper understanding of the world around us, critical thinking, self-confidence, and problem-solving  (Kochanowski & Carr, 2014; Truong et al., 2016), while also encouraging curiosity, creativity, and a love of learning (Emmoth, 2025; Longbottom & Butler, 1999). Based on prior research, NBL is thought to be an inclusive approach that supports learning and motivation for LD students. However, 810 further investigation is needed to understand how specific features of NBL environments might support or impede motivation and learning for students with LDs.

## Theoretical framework

This study uses Self-Determination Theory (SDT) as its theoretical framework for motivation (Ryan & Deci, 2000). SDT is an approach to human motivation that identifies the need for competence (Harter, 1978; White, 1963), relatedness (Baumeister & Leary, 2017; Reis, 2013), and autonomy (DeCharms, 1968; Deci, 1975)  for facilitating motivation, growth, and integration, as well as for constructive social development and personal wellbeing (Ryan et al., 1997). SDT’s arena is the investigation of people’s growth tendencies and psychological needs that are the basis for their self-motivation and personality integration, as well as for the conditions that foster those positive processes (Ryan & Deci, 2000). While we acknowledge that there may be cultural and contextual differences in how individuals weigh these motivational factors and what each factor means to them, we believe that SDT provides valuable insights into factors that intrinsically motivate lea rners. This study uses a KiP theoretical framework (diSessa et al., 2016; diSessa, 1988)   to characterize the knowledge resources of students. KiP is an epistemological perspective that models science learning as reconstructions of prior experience and knowledge’s context-sensitive roles in emerging competence (Davis et al., 2013). A KiP perspective views ideas as composed of small cognitive units, which are neither correct nor incorrect in and of themselves, but valuable in context (diSessa et al., 2016). KiP is consistent with the larger theory of constructivism. Constructivists believe that students do not come to learn as blank slates, which means their prior knowledge plays a crucial role as they construct new understandings (Elby, 2000). For this analysis, we are using KiP, because NBL offers unknown knowledge resources for students with LDs, and we want to understand those knowledge resources.  Within the KiP theoretical framework, disequilibrium plays a crucial role in motivating learning, because students seek to resolve the discomfort of disequilibrium by reorganizing existing knowledge pieces into a more stable system. We see this account of motivation as both aligned with SDT’s identified need for competence and as providing a mechanism through which a sense of competence might be achieved. Nature is filled with rich phenomena as opposed to discourse. Therefore, using the KiP cognitive structure makes sense as the study’s starting point. This structure employs phenomenological primitives (p-prims), which are tied to interpretations of experiences in the real world (phenomena) and serve as the minimal elements of memory. P-prims are cued by context and with other p-prims, which means that understanding of phenomena is actively constructed in context from a set of p-prims cued at the time, rathe r than existing as stable mental models (diSessa, 1993). Within KiP, the information observed or discussed changes the knowledge that students reveal (Elby, 2000; Sherin, 2001; Sherin et al., 2012), indicating that context matters. This aligns with McDermott

(1993) work, which conceptualizes different contexts as being more or less supportive of students with LDs,

thereby making students appear more or less differently abled in their interactions within these contexts (Chaiklin & Lave, 1993).

Similar work by Keifert and Stevens (2019) using interaction analysis (IA) to understand science inquiry in context emphasizes the role of adults (parents or teachers) in taking up or shutting down children’s motivation and attempts at inquiry. Since IA provides a prism for reconceptualizing learning from the learner's perspective, it will make motivation components more visible (Keifert & Stevens, 2019). This is aligned with a KiP perspective, which suggests that teachers adapt lessons for LD students in NBL in the moment to meet students’ needs to construct knowledge (Harlow & Bianchini, 2020). KiP is well-suited to help us understand and support students with LDs, because KiP positions all learners as holding different varieties of knowledge resources. In addition, KiP approaches model how these partially formed knowledge bits, which might otherwise go unnoticed or undervalued during instruction, serve as building blocks during science learning interactions. Based on this prior research, our analysis focuses on addressing the following research questions: (1) How do students with LDs experience NBL? (2) How do specific features of NBL environments support or impede motivation and learning for students with LDs?

## Method

To answer these questions, we examine a case study of one LD student, Sequioa, participating in a NBL environment, called School of the Wild (SOTW). Sequioa is developmentally delayed. She has been described as socially and intellectually less mature than her peers. Specifically, her writing skills are below grade level, and she learns at a slower rate than her peers.  SOTW is a statewide program in the Midwestern United States that operates during the academic year for K-12 students. It is designed to conne ct students with their local environment and engage them in science-based exploration and problem-solving. During the program, students spend 4-5 days visiting one or two parks. Students’ experiences vary depending on their local natural environment 811 as well as educators’ goals and understanding of their professional learning, including suggested activities and themes that are consistent across sites. In groups of 12-16 students, different habitats and activities are explored each day with local conser vationists, naturalists, and/or their classroom teachers. The habitats explored may include prairies, wetlands, and woodlands. Specific areas of study include wildlife, ornithology (the study of birds), ecosystems, natural history, archaeology, and outdoor  skills. Activities include things such as fishing, shelter building, tagging and releasing birds, birdwatching, creating art projects using natural materials (temporal art), throwing the atlatl, bingo, and photographing nature. During Fall 2024, the students (ages 11-12) at Sequoia’s large, urban middle school participated in SOTW for the first time. Sequoia is a female, African American student who was identified to the research team as having an LD. Our observations of Sequoia at SOTW revealed that she was willing, motivated, and interested in learning in this context, and, as we will demonstrate in our analysis, actively engaged in knowledge construction, connecting new information from NBL to her existing knowledge pieces. Thi s is important, as it contrasts with how she was characterized to our team, based on her in-school participation, with one school staff member discouraging us from putting a camera on her, implying that her LD would prevent her from engaging in NBL in ways that would be interesting to our research.

As part of a larger design-based implementation research project on SOTW, we collected video, field notes, audio recordings, focus group interviews, and pre-and post-surveys. We used these data sources to capture the experiences of students, teachers, and  naturalists from their respective perspectives. Sequoia’s specific case was chosen for analysis to address the gap in the literature regarding the motivation and learning of students with LDs during NBL. Our analysis here focuses on video of Sequioa’s interactions during SOTW, captured by chestmounted cameras worn by Sequioa, her teacher, and three of her peers. Building on our theoretical framework, we employed a combination of knowledge analysis (KA) and interaction technique for characterizing students’ developing understandings of natural systems from moment to moment (diSessa et al., 2016; diSessa, 1988). It examines the content and form of knowledge to understand the learning process (diSessa et al., 2015). For this study, KA incorporating KiP was used to analyze the knowledge Sequoia displayed and constructed during NBL interactions. IA is the study of in vivo learning interactions, focusing on how people think and learn in situated contexts, in interaction with other people, tools, and representations through embodied action and talk (Jordan & Henderson, 1995). Here, we specifically employed IA of knowledge in use to understand how the context of SOTW and interactions with adults, peers, and nature in that context did or did not support Sequioa’s motivation and her display and construction of knowledge (Hall & Stevens, 2015). Consistent with IA methods, after reviewing all the videos from Sequioa’s week at SOTW, we selected three episodes of interaction for close analysis and presentation that represented typical examples of her interactions at SOTW. The first episode took place during her group’s day exploring the prairie habitat, where she and her peers engaged in a student-centered question-and-answer (Q&A) session with Ms. Palm. Before exploring the prairie habitat, students received a structured lesson from Ms. Palm. They were taught about the prairie. They touched coyote fur and learned about a monarch butterfly tagging program. The Q&A session was a teaching approach that involved answering students’ questions (Macmillan & Garrison, 1983). Questioning is an integral part of meaningful learning and scientific inquiry, which is structurally embedded in the thinking operation of critical and creative thinking, as well as problem-solving (Cuccio‐Schirripa & Steiner, 2000). Sequoia became very excited, animated, and motivated, engaging in numerous direct verbal interactions, which we used to analyze her learning. Then, the students were allowed to explore and learn about the prairie habitat independently. Following the individual prairie exploration, Ms. Palm instructed students to write a journal entry about what they had learned, which they would later share with their classmates. We can see in the episode in Table 1 that interactionally, Ms. Palm initially made space for Sequioa’s knowledge sharing and supported her in constructing knowledge during the question session. For example, when Sequioa and her classmate compared the coyote pelt to a wolf and a dog, Ms. Palm responded, “Ya, coyotes, dogs, wolves, and foxes, they’re all cousins” (line 4) and then elaborated on the scientific relationships between these animals. This sparked Sequioa’s further interest and motivation, because following Ms. Palm’s explanation that “Bears and bison and elk, these animals cannot live here naturally anymore” (line 13), Sequioa asked “Why?” (line 14). This is meaningful in relation to KA because when we say something is “natural” that often implies noneed for further explanation, and thus this data point suggests a possible knowledge resource or element. Similarly, 812 when Ms. Palm explained how humans are destroying prairie habitats (line 18), Sequioa asked, “Can they fight back?” (line 21), where “they” seemed to refer to coyotes. These interactions suggest that Sequioa’s interest developed in response to interaction with others and materials (IA). Specifically, these experiences conflicted with intuitions formed from Sequioa’s prior experiences (KA). P-prims account for comfort with predictions, observations, and explanations. The intuition of fighting back is deeply rooted in human experience and intuitive understanding. KiP highlights how “fighting back” holds a conceptual schema akin to other p-prims (e.g., Overcoming), which serve as resources for learning and teaching (diSessa, 2018). Simultaneously, SDT guides us to notice, interactionally, how Sequioa was seeking out and receiving recognition of her competence here by sharing her knowledge and curiosities and having her questions taken up by Ms. Palm. Through this same process, she wa s also achieving relatedness with Ms. Palm, which may have supported her motivation to learn (Ryan & Deci, 2000). We can also see the personal connections (relatedness) she feels towards animals, including pets, a recurring theme throughout her discussions at SOTW. Motivated to continue her inquiry, Sequoia’s questions became increasingly detailed as she attempted to connect her knowledge to the Q&A session and build further connection with Ms. Palm and visible competence. Sequoia asked about coyotes fighting back against humans, “Can they fight back?” (line 20). She also asked Ms. Palm about cats (line 28), fish (line 31), and dogs (line 33). During this interaction, it seems Sequoia wondered which animals would succeed or fail in a fight against a coyote, drawing on the animals she had encountered. This interaction sustains around conflict, a key construct in KA, drawing on a general schema of competing influences. However, the questions end by asking about a dog winning in a fight with a coyote, which is more specific and could have been occurring because her knowledge pieces were being reassembled (diSessa, 1993). In KiP, knowledge is often reassembled to rebuild explanations, resolve inconsistencies, and form a more coherent understanding. KiP argues that learning is not a process of replacing misconceptions with correct theories, but rather it is a gradual restructuring of many fine-grained, commonsense, or intuitive notions that persist throughout the learning process and play a crucial role in scientific expertise (diSessa, 1993; Sherin, 2006). We saw evidence of this where Sequioa asked, “Is there a chance for a dog could win in a fight with a coyote?” (line 33), and Ms. Palm answered “Yes, umm. I would say there is a chance a dog could win in a fight with a coyote” (line 34) and elaborated to say, “but it depends on the size of the coyote and if the coyote has a pack” (line 36) and “That is what I just said. It depends on the size of the dog, size of the coyote” (Line 40). In response, Sequoia was intrinsically motivated to ask, “What if there were 20 dogs and one coyote?” (line 44) to build her knowledge and thus enhance her competence. In this situation, Sequioa was restructuring the fine-grained notions that a dog candefeat a coyote in a fight, and when a dog can win (using a pack). Before learning about coyotes, Sequoia likely knew that some animals could defeat a dog in a fight. This interaction with Ms. Palm shows Sequioa’s competence and relatedness, which aligns with SDT (Ryan & Deci, 2000). At first, Sequioa is also afforded an opportunity for autonomy, as she is allowed to ask questions and has her questions taken up by Ms. Palm. However, in lines 45-46, Ms. Palm stated, “Guys, we can play this game all afternoon, but we are going to keep going.” This shuts down Sequoia’s autonomous knowledge construction in the coyote interaction, in favor of Ms. Palm continuing to teach about other topics.
