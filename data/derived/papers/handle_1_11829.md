# Practical Measures for Teacher Noticing: Developing an Interpretive Practice

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study examines a researcher-practitioner collaboration focused on developing equitable noticing practices through iterative engagement with practical measures. Drawing on an expansive learning framework, we explore how a secondary mathematics teacher, Lorenzo, engages in a four-stage process to adopt an interpretive practice. Our findings reveal that Lorenzo’s involvement in co-designing, administering, and analyzing practical measures validates his interpretations of students’ academic and relational success and fosters a deeper understanding of how equitable noticing can support student learning and belonging. These findings illuminate how practical measures and their iterative use in Plan-Do-Study-Act (PDSA) cycles create opportunities for teachers to hone their equitable noticing within a collaborative professional learning context. This work contributes to teacher education by identifying interpretive practice as a promising focus for fostering equity-oriented teaching.

## Introduction

Mathematics education continues to reinforce inequi ties affecting underrepresented youth from racial a nd linguistic communities (Mosqueda, 2010; NCES, 2019). Research draws on the construct of teacher notici ng to understand how inequities are perpetuated during in struction (Stovall et al., 2024). An emerging findi ng is that teachers’ dispositions and commitments to disrupt i nequities shape teachers’ classroom noticing to ado pt assetbased, anti-deficit lenses to notice students and their contrib utions (Louie et al., 2021). While this work has shed light on how teachers’ noticing can create affirming classrooms, little research has investigated how teachers learn to notice to advance equity in their classrooms. Research doc uments the power of classroom video and student artifacts to provoke expansive noticing (Crespo et al., 2021). We aim to build on this work to conside r how practical measures, or quick iterative cycles of collecting and measuring data (Jackson et al., in press; Sjöblom et al., 2023), can support teachers’ expanding their noticing by gaining insight into students’ learning experiences. Developing one’s noticing from student data is even more important for the intersection of relational and academic dimensions of mathematics classrooms for minoritize d youth, given that these students’ experiences are  more likely to be noticed from a deficit perspective (Lo uie et al., 2021). To develop a teacher's capacity to interpret students from a strength-based perspective, we use improvement science methodology to examine its affordances in supporting teachers’ equitable noticing.

Teacher noticing is a promising avenue for advancin g equity in math education. Research describes noticing as a form of self-awareness necessary for disrupting inequities in the moment, as opposed to in reflection (Mason, 2011; Patterson-Williams et al., 2020). Existing frameworks can focus teachers’ inner  witness to issues of equity and inclusion, such as Winn’ (2018) four part-framework that includes attention to race, justice, history, and language (Winn, 2018). Other work notes the imp ortance of narrating and renarrating with tools and frameworks to expand one’s awareness (Philip, 2019). Frameworks and practices can provoke multidimensi onal noticing by increasing awareness of alternative interpretations, such as alternate social and racial experiences (Mendoza et al., 2021). By engaging in conversations from alternative perspectives, teachers have the opportunity to interrogate the objects of their noticing and ho w they understand students’ classroom participation  and adapt their own practices to be more inclusive and equity-oriented. Research examines various contexts used to support expansive noticing such as video analysis, curriculum design, and analysis of student work (Sa ntagata et al., 2021; van Es & Sherin, 2008), with practical measures being among the most recent (Jackson et al., in press; Sjöblom et al., 2023). This study aims to build on this work to ask if and how the use of practical measures can expand teachers’ noticing for equity aims. Practical measures have many of the same affordances of video  analysis – they allow for capturing students’ experiences, create a record that facilitates iterative learning, and are anchored in teachers’ own authentic contexts. The process of administering practical measures and analyzing s tudents’ feedback engages teachers in a cyclical pr ocess that is well suited for their learning to notice. First,  designing the practical measure requires teachers to identify and make visible their dispositions (Bryk et al., 2015). Analyzing data that utilizes students’ own words removes some of the inter pretive process that other interventions, such as v ideo analysis, might allow for. Teachers then are positioned to make adjustments to their teaching practices based on the data they collected and analyzed. Teachers can then engage in another cycle of designing, admi nistering, and adjusting instructional and noticing  practices based on practical measures.

To be clear, using the term noticing practice empha sizes noticing as not merely an individual cognitiv e skill but a practice cultivated through sustained e ngagement and deep involvement in a community of pr actice. This lens allows us to consider how teachers’ noticing for equity is shaped by their interactions with frameworks, tools, and feedback from students and colleagues. W e aim to build on this conception of a noticing pra ctice to better understand how teacher noticing can be expanded to support equitable teaching. Thus, to better understand teacher noticing for equity, we ask the following question: How does the use of practical measures in a plan-dostudy-act process support a teacher to develop an interpretive practice in the context of their noticing for equity?

## Literature review and theoretical perspectives

We draw on three bodies of work to understand how teachers’ noticing for equity might be supported through the use of practical measures. First, we discuss teacher noticing frameworks and its various components. Second, we articulate the need for teachers to learn to notice  more expansively and the difficulties in doing so. Third, we propose that plan-do-study-act (PDSA) cycles of using practical measures is a gene rative context for learning to notice. Lastly, we state our theoretical perspective, expansive learning, as a theoretical perspective to study teacher learning to notice for equity through the process of doing practical measures. Theorizing teacher noticing Noticing has been widely characterized as consistin g of two broad constructs-attending and interpreting. Attending captures where teachers focus their atten tion (van Es & Sherin, 2002), such as strategies students use to solve a problem  or how students collaborate with each other. Simult aneously, teachers also interpret their observations by asking questions and demonstrating curiosity about an event or  interaction (van Es & Sherin, 2002, 2022). For instance, a teacher may notice a student hesitating to c ontribute to a discussion  and might interpret that as the student being unsure of their  answer or needing encouragement. This interpretation depends on the teacher’s knowledge of the student, subject matter, classroom context, as well as the student’s history in mathematics education (Louie et al., 2021). Other n oticing components include: deciding to respond, sh aping interactions, and framing (Louie et al., 2021; van Es & Sherin, 2021), which suggests noticing is inte gral to classroom interactions, with teachers privileging some ideas and aspects of a classroom and not other s, and making judgements and inferences to inform their de cisions. This shift positions interpreting not just  as an analytical process but as a deliberate and situated  practice that shapes how teachers respond to and e ngage with their students. Here, we focus on the interpretive component and how it might be understood as a practice, aligning with the view that teaching itself is a practice involving intentional, iterative actions. Research finds that teachers’ noticing is shaped by  teachers’ dispositions, dominant social narratives, and students’ histories with mathematics. Dispositi ons are the consistent tendencies through which ind ividuals perceive and interact with situations, sh aped by their positioning within broader social com munities and discourses (Hand, 2012). Dispositions are dynamic, evolving as teachers navigate the sociopolitical co ntexts of their work, reflecting their commitments, values, a nd responses to the environments they engage with, thus suggesting their importance to understanding teache rs’ noticing of students and classroom interactions. Teacher noticing is also shaped by pervasive dominant narratives in mathematics education related to race, ability and classroom management (Louie et al,. 2021). These narratives shape what teachers notice and int eract with students’ personal and cultural histories. Thus, a third factor that shapes noticing is the interplay between teachers’ and students’ personal and social histories (Agarwa l & Sengupta-Irving, 2020; van Es et al., 2022). This work identifies that teachers' histories shape the ways they attend to classroom dynamics, interpret student actions, and frame their responses, often reflecting deeply held  beliefs and assumptions about teaching, learning, and equity. Similarly, students bring their own histories, cult ural knowledge, and lived experiences, which intera ct with teachers’ noticing practices in ways that can either reinforce or disrupt traditional power dynamics. Understanding these intersecting histories emphasizes the importance of fostering reflective practices that help tea chers become more aware of how their perspectives and students’ experiences influence what they notice and how they respond. Research further highlights how equitable teacher noticing is associated with instructional practices that promote fairness and inclusivity in the classroom (Mendez & van Es, 2024). For example, teachers who n otice equitably actively work to broaden access to mathem atical discourse, provide varied entry points to ta sks, and value diverse ways of thinking and problem-solving (Agarwal & Sengupta-Irving, 2020). Such noticing candismantle traditional hierarchies of competence by recognizing and amplifying all  students’ contributions. Similarly, Kang (2019) found that teachers who attend to multiple dimensions of classroom interactions—such as students’ emotional engagement, relational dynamics, and participation structures —create more affirming and supportive learning environments. These practices foster a sense of belonging among students while encouraging their academic and social development. Together, this research suggests that equitable noticing practices can lead towards teaching practices that better meet students’ needs.

## Leveraging practical measures and PDSA cycles to support equitable noticing

Developing equitable noticing requires teachers to engage with actionable, contextualized data that dr ives their reflections and practices. Efforts to support teach ers' learning to notice often center on structured opportunities for reflection and analysis  of teaching, helping educators make sense of classr oom interactions and student thinking. Learning to notice is defined as making specific connections between observed phenomena and broader principles of teaching and learning and growing mor e specific in one’s interpretations (van Es & Sheri n, 2008). Researchers have centered the role of reflection to cultivate teachers’ noticing. This work highlights the notion of an "inner witness", which serves to support teacher s’ in-the-moment noticing by increasing a wareness of marginalized and privileged positionalities (Patter son Williams et al. 2020). Currently, there is limi ted research on different contexts in which learning to notice takes place (König et al., 2022). Practical measures emerged prominently within impro vement science, a framework that emphasizes iterative, data-driven problem-solving in education (Bryk et al., 2015) and have b een used in research on teacher learning based on the notion that teachers learn be st when they engage with data that directly relates  to their classroom contexts (Jackson et al., in press). Practical measures refer to tools, instruments, or methods developed to capture and assess specific aspects of a phenomenon in a way that are actionable and can be directly applied to a real-world context (Hinnant-Crawford, 2020). These measures are typically simpl e, reliable, and capable of being used effectively in diverse, real-time classroom settings (Yeager, 2013). Some features of practical measures make them suitable for cultivating teachers’ equita ble noticing. First, teachers are positioned to cre ate questions that reflect their commitments. Second, there is a focus on actionable data that is specific to the teachers’ context. Third, pract ical measures have an emphasis on disaggregated dat a, allowing for reflections and discussions focused on targeted students or groups (Bryk et al., 2015). Practical measures have been used in plan-do-study-act (PDSA) cycles in math classrooms to support teachers’ collaborative design processes, and self-directed inquiries to support their learning by allowing teachers to gain insight into students’ experienc es (Rakes et al., 2023). Teachers who engage in PDS A cycles make connections to previous experiences to build capaci ty, specifically noting that this process was able to “validate what they had already been doing” (Tichnor-Wagner et al., 2017, pg. 478). By periodically drawing on feedback from students, teachers candevelop an understandin g of their progress and areas for instructional imp rovement (Hattie & Timperley, 2007). Recent work utilized a similar approach of Educational Design Research (ED R) cycles, to support math teachers’ noticing (Sjöblom et al., 2023) and found that teachers developed an awareness of their own roles as facilitators for students’ group discussions and the need for explicit instruction. This increased awareness advances teachers’ interpretations of their students’ learning by enabling them to recognize the nuanced ways students engage with context and interact with peers. Importantly, engaging in PDSA cycles positions noticing as a sociocultural process shaped by sustained participation in a community of practice. Defining and verbalizing dispositions toward equity within professional development contexts helps teachers articulate their goals and justify their use of planning and instructional tools aimed at equitable outcomes (Philip, 2019). Codesig ning practical measures similarly engages teachers in narrating their dispositions, analyzing student dat a, and reimagining pra ctices through iterative cycles of data-driven action. Parallel work in mathematics educati on, such as lesson studies and video clubs, also de monstrate the value of inquiry-based processes that involve analyzing, discussing,  and reflecting on contextualize d data to deepen teachers’ understanding of student thinking (van Es & Sherin, 2010). While these methods for expanding teachers’ noticing are all promising, we aim to propose an alternative approach that gives teachers access to students’ pe rspectives to expand their classroom noticing. Maso n (2011) and Dominguez (2019) remind us of the in teractional and reciprocal nature of noticing, such  that it takes place between teachers and students. Thus, gaining access to students’ experiences may provide teachers an alternative window into whether and how their classroom noticin g supports their academic success and emerging identities in the classroom. This is particularly important fo r teachers working with students from non-dominant communities, as students’ mathematical responses and ways of participating are connected to their lived experiences (Dominguez, 2019). Given the centrality of students and their experiences in teachers’ noticing-we explore if and how receiving feedback might help expand teachers’ noticing.

## Methods and data context

This pilot study took place in an ongoing collabora tive research project involving middle and secondar y mathematics teachers and educational researchers, w ho are committed to advancing equity through notici ng practices. It emerged from the teacher’ partici pants who asked how they could get insight into stu dents’ experiences, as a way to confirm or refute their efforts to create more equitable mathematics instruction. This pilot study involved a collaboration between one teacher participant, Lorenzo, and one researcher (the first  author). Collaborative actions incl uded working together to generate a practical measu re by reviewing, discussing, and iterating on items from existing measures while con sidering Lorenzo’s commitments to equitable teachin g. Through this process, they refined the measure to a lign with his instructional goals and the kinds of insights Lorenzo sought from students. Once the measure was developed, the two met regularly to talk through it s implementation and examine what he was noticing in the data, using these discussions to refine both hi s interpretations and subsequent instructional decisions. Lorenzo’s participation in the collaborative resear ch project brought to his attention the importance of noticing, which served as the first stimuli. It also highlighted a common conflict of motives that motivated teachers to ask: how could they claim that their students were experiencing the classroom as equitable drawing only from their own interpretations, without hearing from students’ perspectives? Data sources Data include a combination of five design meetings,  five teacher interviews (which draw on recorded cl assroom observations and student interviews), analysis of a rtifacts (such as meeting notes and codesigned surv ey questions), and a final reflective interview in whi ch we discuss the entire process of learning from s tudents’ experiences. Throughout the design and data intervi ew meetings, the first author continuously asked Lo renzo to narrate and reflect on his noticing and design choi ces. Through regular meetings in which we offered g uidance on data collection, Lorenzo shapes his own learning trajectory by focusing on his pedagogical commitments. Analytical approach: Using an expansive learning framework We draw on expansive learning theory (Engeström, 20 01) to analyze the patterns and practices that cont ributed to a teacher’s learning to notice for equity. Expan sive learning results when members of an organizati on engage in collaborative improvement-a process that begins with identifying historically-driven contradictions that have the potential to hinder or enable transformation wi thin the activity system (Engeström, 2001). In this  study, contradictions to reflective learning for mathemati cs teachers em erged from the school districts’ policy and practices through: an objective to increase student s’ self-reflection (Phillips, 2020), highlighting specific “standards for student mathematical practice” (Cali fornia Department of Education, 2023), and district-wide layoffs to school staff during the year of data col lection. These contradictions became explicit durin g the collaborative design of the practical measure. Left  unaddressed, contradictions can reinforce inequiti es, making their identification essential for expanding equitable practices. Through the PDSA process, Lorenzo’s engagement with practical measures provided a lens to examine and respond to these contradictions. Expansive learning also recognizes the collaborative nature of transformation, emphasizing that learning is a collective process of reimagining and reconstructing activity systems (Engeström, 2001). Collaborative design involves cycles of negotiation, co-construction, and reflection, where participants identify contradictions, develop new tools or practices, and refine their practice through experimentation (Tessier, 2020). In this study, Lorenzo's engagement with practical measures unfolded through  ongoing dialogue, joint analysis, and shared decis ionmaking with the first author. As part of this colla boration, Lorenzo and the first author codesigned a  practical measure informed by research and Lorenzo’s commitments and examined how using the practical measure shaped his interpretive practice, fostering a more expansive and systematic approach to equitable mathematics instruction. An expansive learning framework includes multiple c onstructs that guide the analysis of an iterative codesign process: double stimulation, conflict of m otives, practical experimentation, germ cell, theoretically mastered concrete, and indicators of generativity (see Table 1). Double stimulation forms the foundati on of the framework, involving two types of stimuli used to help a learner address a problem. The first stimulus represents the problem itself, while the second stimulus acts as a mediating tool that enables the learner to reframe or resolve the issue (Engeström, 2001). In this study, double stimulation aligns with the iterative design and ad ministration of practical measures (Hinnant-Crawford, 2020). In between the primary and seconda ry stimuli is the conflict of motives, a critical phase where learners grapple wi th competing considerations. This concept resonates  with improvement science tools (e.g. fishbone diagrams),  which help clarify causal relationships and guide action planning (Peterson & Carlile, 2021). In both expans ive learning and the iterative design process, prac tical experimentation emerges as a shared practice, emphasizing cycles of testing and refining solutions. The germ cell construct highlights the identification of a simple, foundational idea or practice with the potential for exp ansive development through reflection and action cycles and represents the seed of transformative change in the activity system. Finally, the theoretically mastered concrete  refers to the advanced understanding and transforma tive learning outcomes as visible through the application of theoretical knowledge in practical situations. With this framework in mind, the first author itera tively analyzed by open coding, refining codes, and theming data to generate an understanding of how Lo renzo attended to, interpreted how his students rep orted experiencing his classroom to reconceptualize the role of data-driven practices in his noticing. ## Findings

Through the four stages of expansive learning, Lorenzo develops an interpretive practice that deepens and hones his equitable noticing. We use the term interpretiv e practice to refer to one of the core dimensions o f teacher noticing (i.e. interpretation) while highlighting t he sociocultural nature of this dimension as a prac tice that one develops through deeper involvement in a community of practice. Specifically, through designing, administering, and analyzing practical measures, Lorenzo learns to  draw on students’ lived experiences when interpret ing the effectiveness of his instructional practices that aim to support students’ academic and relational success. Identifying contradictions: Recognizing the difficulties of making interpretations Before our collaboration began, Lorenzo developed h is own measure, which he called a reflection, based on a district-wide initiative (Phillips, 2020). This initial meas ure asked students which core mathematical practice s they had used in class (California Department of Ed ucation, 2023). However, Lorenzo found it difficult  to confidently interpret hi s students’ experiences. During the second intervie w meeting, the first author shared his interpretation that Lorenzo’s honors class seemingl y did not struggle w ith the mathematics content. Lorenzo responded by acknowledging the difficulty in interpreting students’ ease or difficulty with the content: I have the same challenge as well in determining where they’re at based on the [students’] group work. You’ll get the superstars often doing a lot o f the thinking. It’s an interesting challenge trying to interpret what they write up here and who  really knows that? Is it all three of you? Is it two of you? Is it just one of you, are all three  of you lost and you borrowed the ideas from [another group]?

This excerpt reveals Lorenzo's recognition of the i nherent ambiguity in his interpretations. While gro up work is intended to surface students’ thinking, it often ob scures individual contributions, leaving Lorenzo qu estioning how much understanding is distributed within the gr oup and how much is concentrated in a single “super star” student. Lorenzo immediately discussed that he addr essed this problem by “talking to individual groups  and I target the specific students that have struggled tr aditionally, and I ask them questions.” His response to this noticing illustrates his attempt to mitigate this p edagogical challenge by focusing his attention on s tudents he deems have struggled “traditionally”. His attention is driven by his school’s historical and political context, as he recognizes some students enter his classroom with prior academic disadvantages. However, Lorenzo’s reflections suggest this strategy’s limitations result from him  remaining unsure about the extent to which his int erpretations capture the n uances of his students’ learning. This ongoing tens ion of supporting students’ reflections interacts with his own desire to understand how students are experiencing his classroom and highlights the need for Lorenzo to establish an interpretive practice based on early data. Modeling solutions collaboratively: Shifting towards data-driven interpretations While Lorenzo identified that his interpretations were potentially misaligned with students’ actual experiences in the first stage, the second stage begins to address  this problem through an epistemological shift arou nd data use. Lorenzo and the first author modeled solutions collaboratively by focusing on students' developing math identities as the central topic for the co-developed practical measure. In the last design meeting, Lorenzo acknowledged the importance of reflecting on his students’ experiences to guide his next steps. When asked if he had noticed certain trends in students’ experiences prior to administering practical measures, Lorenzo responded: “I think I knew. But I don't think it was nagging me as much as having the reflections there and seeing it in writing, like, ‘oh no, I need to do something about this”. This shift from having  relied on a gut feeling to making data-driven interpretations marked an epistemological change in how Lorenzo con ceptualized the connection between his noticing and practical measures.

Lorenzo further commented on the power of data to i nform his interpretations when, during a data interview, the first author and Lorenzo discussed s tudents’ responses to “ how much did you share your math thinking with others today? ” Dismayed by students’ mixed responses to this que stion, Lorenzo initially rationalized that the particular lesson during whic h he conducted this practical measure was not well-suited for students to share their math thinking. However, Lor enzo soon change d his interpretation, “Th is gives me some feedback because I want [students] sharing their th inking almost every day.” This highlights how data prompted Lorenzo to reevaluate his interpretation of student s’ experiences and reinforced his goal of fostering  regular opportunities for students to share their mathematical thinking in cl ass. He then elaborates on his aspiration to create constant opportunities for his students to share their math thinking: I want it to be normal and fluid. I don’t want it t o be dependent on what kind of activity we’re doing. If they’re working on the Chromebooks, I wan t them to be working together. If they’re working on the whiteboards, I want them to be worki ng together. Practice sheets, same thing. So that’s good feedback for me that [students] only feel they’re getting to share their ideas when we’re up on the whiteboards.

Lorenzo’s thinking illustrates an interpretive shif t from attributing students’ lack of dialogue to th e particular lesson of the day to recognizing a broader instruct ional pattern which he could control. By reframing the data as feedback for him to learn from, Lorenzo highlighted  his desire for students’ mathematical dialogue to be a consistent and embedded aspect of student learning,  rather than occasional and task-dependent. Lorenzo also reflected on a proposed change in his instruction, “I need to do something different on the Chromebook s”. His reflection underscores the importance of practical measures in surfacing patterns in students’ experie nces that might otherwise go unnoticed. Recognizing these pat terns can help teachers determine when instructiona l adjustments are needed to better align their practices with students’ lived experiences. Implementing new practices: Incorporating student feedback earlier Lorenzo acknowledged the difficulty in making interpretations about how his students experienced the classroom and began shifting in his interpretations based on early iterations of the practical measure. Lorenzo noted how administering practical measures earlier in the school year could guide him towards making instructional adjustments that were validated by students’ experiences, which he articulated in our third data interview: Students walk in with all kinds of situations and backgrounds. It’s hard for me to address every inequity, or to balance all the needs in my classro om, but I think that I cando better at pinpointing them and identifying them. And this ref lection process has helped me to develop a system to do that. Lorenzo's discussion of practical measures as a sys tem that he can implement in his classroom is akin to implementing an instructional practice, in that it requires him to interpret and act on student feedback in real-time. This interpretive process highlights the dynamic in teraction between his intentions as a teacher and t he lived experiences of his students, reinforcing the notion that noticing is not just an observational activity but an ongoing, adaptive interpretive practice. Lorenzo continues by articulating a new practice: And I can pick these [issues] out earlier. We didn’t start [administering and analyzing surveys] until March. [...] I wasn’t sure what kind of respo nses I was going to be getting. But they’re pretty good. They’re honest with me about all the t hings. And if I can trust the students, to be honest, I cando this way earlier in the year and p ick these things out earlier and adjust my instruction accordingly. (Data Interview 3) Building on this reflective insight, Lorenzo recogn izes that students’ honesty in their feedback allow s him to establish a strong feedback loop earlier in the school year, enabling him to identify and address issues proactively. By interpreting student fee dback through practical measures, Lorenzo tailors h is teaching to address students’ challenges and meet their diverse needs. This practice makes his classroom more adaptive and inclusive, where students’ unique backgrounds shape instructional decisions. Incorporating student feedback earlier allows for i terative improvement and responsiveness to student needs, allowing him to build on the systemic contradictions in his activity system related to his district’s push for academic achievement. This stage could serve as an essential link, as it allows for ongoing adjustments before the formal reflective phase, making the feedback proces s continuous rather than retrospective. This stage could also set the groundwork for effective reflection and consolidation by ensuring that any insights developed are already partially tested and refined through direct student feedback. Reflecting and consolidating: Developing an interpretive practice Finally, in this fourth stage of expansive learning, Lorenzo developed an interpretive practice. From this sociocultural perspective of noticing, his interpre ting shifts towards an intentional and iterative pr actice. Developing an interpretive practice is not merely a bout making sense of student behaviors or responses  in the moment; it is about continuously engaging with data, reflecting on assumptions, and refining instructi onal decisions to better align with students' needs and identities. Lorenzo's development of an interpretive practice i s evident through his shifts in noticing and his commitment to the data-driven approach discussed in this study. Initially, he recognized that while he had always been committed to supporting students, he lacked a concrete method to assess the effectivenes s of his teaching practices, as discussed in the identifying contradi ctions stage of expansive learning. Engaging in the  design and use of practical measures, Lorenzo better aligned h is classroom instruction with his philosophical commitments, noting that this partnership “gave me the tools [...] to pick apart what I’m actually doing, what my habits are, and do they align with my philosophies”. Lorenzo's jour ney illustrates how an interpretive practice deepen s his understanding of students’ experiences and fosters a critical self-awareness that bridges the gap between intention and action, ultimately paving the way for more equitable and responsive teaching. As Lorenzo collected and interpreted data from stud ents, his noticing grew in alignment with students’ lived experiences, as we presented during the secon d stage of expansive learning. As Lorenzo shared, h earing from students "is a more honest experience [than wa tching video] because it’s what they are witnessing  and... experiencing in the classroom" without his filter as the teacher. This shift allowed him to refine how he conducted practical measures by considering both social-emotional and academic dimen sions of his classroom. By integrating student voices into his reflections and  using them to adjust his teaching strategies, Lore nzo strengthened his ability to interpret classroom dyn amics with greater consistency and confidence, ulti mately developing an interpretive practice.

## Discussion

Teacher noticing shows promise for improving teachi ng practices for equity and justice, yet the role o f practical measures remains an underutilized tool for supporting teachers to develop their noticing skills. From an expansive learning perspective, this gap can be understood as  a contradiction within the activity system of teac her professional learning —while teachers aim to foster equitable instruction,  they often rely on limited tools for interpreting student experiences. Our findings sugg est that struct ured frameworks like PDSA cycles can help teachers notice for equity by shifting their epistemological stance toward needing to connect their interpretations with that of students’ experiences—a process we refer to as interpretive practice. Interpreting, in the context of teacher noticing fo r equity, is more than making individual judgments.  It reflects a commitment to engage in transformative p ractices that disrupt deficit-based perspectives (Louie et al., 2021). Through PDSA cycles, teacher s can continuously refine their interpretive practi ces by systematically collecting and analyzing information, reflecting on their assumptions, and making data-informed decisions about their next steps. This iterative process mirrors th e expansive learning  cycle, in which contradictions create opportunities for transformation, leading teachers to reconceptualize their noticing practices and exp and their agency in shaping equitable learning environments.

Teachers candevelop an interpretive practice by us ing evidence-based tools, engaging in discussions with colleagues, and revisiting classroom scenarios  to critically evaluate their assumptions and inter pretations (Cain, 2011). The iterative nature of PDSA cycles supports this by providing structured opportunities for teachers to test their hypotheses, make adjustments based on student responses, and then reflect on how these adjustments impact student learning and engagement. Ov er time, this process enable s teachers to move beyond adaptive expertise—where they simply respond to challenges —to transformative agency, where they actively reima gine and reconstruct their approach to noticing for equity.

Positioning interpretation as a practice emphasizes the dynamic and evolving nature of teacher noticing, where teachers continuously refine their ability to  attend to, interpret, and respond to student think ing (Scheiner, 2021; van Es & Sherin, 2021). The PDSA framework ma kes this process more explicit by offering a struct ured method for teachers to track and evaluate their pro gress over time. It also aligns with a sociocultura l perspective on education (Vygotsky, 1978), which views teaching  and learning as  socially situated and shaped by cultural-historical contexts. Through expansive learning, teachers do not simply refine existing practices but fundamentally reconceptualize their noticing as an evolving, equi ty-centered process that grows through ongoing engagem ent with student perspectives.

## Limitations and future directions

This case study examines the codesign process between one teacher and researcher, which limits generalizability of the findings. While the findings show the potent ial of practical measures to expand teachers’ inter pretative lenses for equitable noticing, we envision opportun ities for teachers to collaborate with other teache rs and could also generate worthwhile findings. These practitioner collaborations could similarly allow teachers to interrogate their commitments in the process of codesign of a measure, while also providing different conceptions of equitable noticing and instruction in practice. Future resear ch could explore how larger group collaboration sha pes the development of interpretive practices and impacts outcomes for equity-focused noticing. Second, the findings raise the challenge of sustain ing partnerships against competing district demands and the limited resources available for teacher col laboration. Structural barriers such as time constr aints, insufficient funding, and conflicting policy priorities can make it difficult for teachers and researchers to maintain consistent engagement in PDSA cycles. Additionally,  teacher workload and potential burnout pose signif icant risks to the longevity of such initiatives, particu larly when collaboration is seen as an added respon sibility. High teacher or leadership turnover further complicates continuity, often requiring partnerships to repeate dly rebuild trust and alignment. These challenges underscore th e need for systemic support and alignment betwe en research goals and district priorities to foster sustainable, equity-focused collaboration.
