# Empowering Students in Learning Engineering and Design: Ethical and Transformative Pedagogy for a Socially Conscious Future

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This symposium includes five papers focused on ethical and transformative pedagogical approaches to empower engineering students to become socially conscious and responsible agents of change in disrupting social injustices. In exploring the social relations and contextual dimensions of learning, ethical and transformative pedagogical approaches delve into community-based learning, liberatory pedagogy, critical consciousness, social responsibility, and equitable education. Our work describes the physical, sociocultural, and relational contexts that allow and empower students to bring their whole selves into engineering learning experiences. The papers describe work in K-16 contexts, illustrating features in the learning environments that allow students to leverage and display their social, linguistic, cultural, and cognitive resources to develop innovative and creative solutions to meaningful engineering problems. We also consider new social relationships in disciplinary practices and the role teachers, faculty and researchers can play in developing novel social configurations to co-create new social possibilities through engineering and engineering design education.

## context of a high school engineering classroom

Christopher G. Wright

## Introduction and conceptual framework

This research seeks to contribute to the engineering education community’s understanding of the development of engineering identities, as well as features within learning settings that foster the formation and tra nsformation of these identities. Specifically, this work highlighted a segment of the US popul ation that Brower (2015) referred to as “STEM’s invisible minority:” African American males. The fields of engineering and engineering education have been consistently challenged with the persiste nt and complex question of “why are there so few Af rican American men?” This study looked to contribute to this problem by focus ing on the development of engineering competencies and identities.  Building on frameworks of situated learning, identi ty theory, and resource pedagogies, a practice-linked identities framework (Nasir & Cooks, 2009; N asir & Hand, 2008) guided this research. Practice-linked identities “are the identities that people come to take on, construct, and embrace that are linked to participation in particular social and cu ltural practices” (Nasir & Hand, 2008, p. 147). Deploying this framework required unpacking or uncovering the aspe cts of these social and cultural practices that aff ord differential opportunities for engagement (Warren & Rosebery, 2011).

## Data sources and methods

This study utilized a case study methodology (Stake, 1995; Yin, 2012) to examine the development of engineering competencies and identities within a STEM high scho ol that serves predominantly Black and Brown studen ts. A total of six African American adolescents were included in the study. The data collected included semi-structured interviews and field note data. The author used a thematic cod ing approach to identify salient themes across the participants, however, identified a single student to highlight due to the importance of the narrative that emerged.

## Findings

The analysis of interview data revealed that Royal’ s positive identification with being considered a “ good engineering student” was placed-based. Royal’s interpretation of being able to engage in and excel at engineering practices varied across the context s of home, out-of-school programs, and school. At home and in the out-ofschool program, Royal could engage in practices such as defining problems, engaging in argument using evidence, as well as obtaining, evaluating, and communicating information. In contrast, when posed with the notion of being seen as a good engineering student in school  contexts, Royal responded, “not in this place.” Royal’s, as  well as other students in the class, abilities to authentic ally engage in engineering practices such as collaboration and argumentation were constrained by classroom rules and policies.

## Implications and contributions

Findings from this study contribute to arguing for greater emphasis on the design of equitable learnin g spaces. The analysis of Royal’s case pushed the research team to consider a dditional frameworks, such as critical geography, where interrogation of how students make place within the intersections of space, power, and identity are essential elements in understanding learning and identity. Paper 2: Re-imagining the US-Mexico border through educational justice: The influence of community resources and media in engineering design Greses Pérez, Ymbar Polanco Pino, G.R. Marvez and Clara Mabour

## Introduction and conceptual framework

The goal of engineering is to develop solutions and technologies for the benefit of all of us, a growing diverse society. To rise to this challenge, we need engineers that not only reflect the communities they serve but who are also explicitly educated to incorporate the perspectives of people in engineering knowledge  and practices. However, it is not yet well understood how exactly this learning process of developing connections bet ween communities and engineering works. This study focus es on this gap between communities and the discipli ne by paying particular attention to  power and culture in engineering learning (Secules et al., 2023). Specifically, we focus on the role of language practices and cultural understandings  on how students define socially-oriented engineering design problems, with attention to media and audience. Drawing on frameworks from situated cognition (Brow n et al., 1989; Greeno et al., 1996; Pea, 1993), and justice in science and engineering (Calabrese B arton & Tan, 2019, 2020; Costanza-Chock, 2020; Morales‐ Doyle, 2017), this quasi-experimental study investigates the role of community resources (language practices and cultural understandings) in engineering desig n. Our work  explores what factors do Latina/o/x/é engineering students consider when formulating social-oriented, ill-defined, engineering design challenges, such as those associated with climate change, AI technologies in healthcare or the US-Mexico border.

## Data sources and methods

Through a mixed methods parallel convergent design (Creswell & Clark, 2017), this study investigates t he influence of community resources, language practice s and cultural understandings,  in design considerations during problem-scoping. Problem-scoping is the “practice of defining the problem to identify and understand the criteria, constraints, and principles that govern t he problem solution ” (Brockway, 2017, p. 11). Participants included 26 Latina/o/x/é undergraduate students maj oring in engineering, who grew up in the U.S. in Sp anishspeaking communities. The data included responses to an engineering task about the factors that students consider important in designin g the US-Mexico border, semi-structured interviews and survey responses. Student s participated in either of two conditions: (1) Spang lish with an Afro-Latina engineer or (2) English with a White man. The media presented also mimicked these langua ge conditions. The quantitative and qualitative dat a was analyzed using an analysis of variance as well as d omain and taxonomic analysis  (See Figure 1 for example of initial codes generated in the analysis of the data), respectively (Saldaña, 2016). The primary goal wa s to understand the nature of factors generated by the students in the two conditions.

## Findings

The findings suggest that students considered the e nvironmental, social, technical, political and dipl omatic complexities of the border task. However, participants in the Spanglish condition were more likely to draw on the experiences of their communities to define the engineering problem. Students in the Spanglish condition made nuanced connections between Latinx/é communities and engineering as compared to other participants. This work also suggests the potential role of audience (facil itator), language and media reflective of communiti es in providing opportunities for students to access cognitive resources when defining engineering problems, to engage in alternative thinking, and to consider a wider range of factors. Paper 3: Centering community relationships: Renegotiating roles and responsibilities in an undergraduate engineering design course Monica Cardella, Alexandra Coso Strong, Stephen Secules and Trina Fletche

## Introduction and conceptual framework

Community-Based Learning (Mooney & Edwards, 2001) is gaining increasing attention in engineering education as an approach where students work on projects with  community partners. One concern in community-based engineering learning is the relationships that stud ents have with the community partners (e.g., Delaine & Thompson, 2021); do students see community partners as having  relevant knowledge and expertise, or just as needing the students’ help? Do the students see the community partner as a “user” or as a whole person to develop a relationship with? We consider design and learning to both be social activities (e.g. van Amstel et al., 2017; Lave & Wenger, 1991; Vygotsky, 1962), and that “good” design is human-centered. We also believe that both education and design should be informed by asset-based approaches (e.g. Martin & Wendell, 2022). We build on prior work that has recognized that undergraduates’ experiences with Community-Based Learning vary: students might see the work as purely service; see it as technology-focused design that includes community partners in perfunctory ways; or recognize the need to engage with community partner s through sustained relationships (Zoltowski, Oakes, & Cardella, 2012).

## Data sources and methods

Our data comes from an undergraduate engineering design course focused on human-centered design and systems thinking taught at a large Hispanic-Serving Institution. Students began their design work by identifying a “people group” that they wanted to learn from and with, whe re our intention was: (a) for students to begin the ir design work with a focus on people rather than a project or a solution, and (b) for students to have agency in identifying a people group that they wanted to focus on. We sha re a case of a student group who chose to focus on “nonprofit organizers” as their people group. Data incl ude course materials (e.g. PowerPoint slides), teac hing reflections (i.e., notes captured after each class session), student project work, and student written reflections.

## Findings

When project groups were first formed during the fourth class se ssion, this group planned to focus on “maintenance workers” as their “people group.” Howe ver, by the end of the class period they shifted th eir focus to “non-profit organizers.” Through later conversations with this team, we realized that this shift allowed members of the group to center community members that they had long-standing relationships with, where they were able to use their course assignment work to continue to work with leaders from local non-profit organizations in ways that were personally meaningful to the students and respectful towards the community partners.

## Implications and contributions

While a recent focus in community-based pedagogies has been on developing experiences  where community partners’ expertise are recognized and valued, this  case reminds us of the need to continue to conside r how we recognize and value assets students bring to underg raduate engineering design experiences. T ypically, undergraduate design projects are identified by engineering facul ty, perhaps working in collaboration with community partners, without student involvement. Ho wever, in the case of the “non-profit organizers” team we are reminded that students are already actively eng aged in our communities; they may not need faculty to broker projects and relationships for them but instead be given space to build on  existing relationships with community partners.

Paper 4: Building meaningful education with engineering to foster care and relationality Brian Gravel, Eli Tucker-Raymond, María Olivares, Cara Hovhenessian, Chris Fitzpatrick and Amon Millner

## Introduction and conceptual framework

Meaningful educational participation is ethically g rounded in care, humanizing relationships, and dign ity (Espinoza et al., 2020). In this paper, we describe engineering as pivotal for creating justice-oriented and equitable learning environments, as it we aves together peoples, materials, and relationships. Increasing awareness of the problems with engineering’s technocentricity, utili tarianism, and neoliberalism (Gunkel & Tolbert, 201 7) motivates new perspectives for reorganizing enginee ring (Benavides et al., 2023). We present an asset-based learning design that blends design, making, and rel ationality in supporting learners to center care and equity through engineering learning.

We integrate descriptions of engineering practice w ith artistic practice (Calabrese Barton et al, 2021), making (Gravel et al., 2021), and explicit consideration of care and equ ity (Loterro-Purdue & Settlage, 2021) as the basis for an educational design. We view learni ng as a cultural and relational process (Nasir & Ha nd, 2006; Nasir et al., 2006), and focus on novel configurati ons of teachers, students, and researchers as co-makers of new social possibilities through engineering. We descri be computational making (Gravel et al., 2021) as a manifestation of engineering, where addressing comm unity needs motivates attention to materials and relationships, and elevates learner resources in caring for and learning within communities (Mejia et al., 2018).

## Data sources and methods

Our data come from a design-research project focused on professional learning f or teachers, in which we invited teachers to (re)negotiate their relationships to di sciplines, tools/materials, and others through proc esses of computational making (Gravel, Olivares & Tucker-Raymond, 2022). We share a case study of a racially and linguistically diverse group of teachers and studen ts engineering a project that addressed issues of e quity and healthy living in city parks.

## Findings

The group coalesced in designing for an issue in yo ung people’s lives: they want to spend time in the city parks, exercising with friends and building relationships, yet they do not have equitable access to sports equipment. They designed “Parq-u-Play,” a locker-style installation that allows visitors to borrow s ports equipment in parks for free. Using a micro:bit to prototype the design, the user is texted a code to open the box automatically. When they borrow the piece of equipment, an embedded social n etwork alerts their friends to come join them to play  in the park. The networking was a means of accountability,  to ensure the equipment did not go missing, but al so an opportunity to build relationships. Through this work, new relational structures were built among the students and teachers, and new relationships to STEM disciplinary practice were forged through computational making, which closely resembles engineering described through jus tice-oriented frameworks (Costanza-Chock, 2020). We elaborate on this case, using vignettes that center conversations aro und care, equity, and relationality within the engineering design work the teams of learners engaged.

## Implications and contributions

Engineering understood as a process of care and relationship building –to materials, with peoples, to ethical entanglements designed for improved life and humani ty–makes its role central to the purposes of meaningfu l education. We argue engineering/computational makin g can reorganize educational spaces grounded in care and relationships. When we build things together, we can build caring relationships grounded in equity and dignity. Paper 5: Engineering students’ epistemologies in design problem solving: exploring the gap between professed and enacted epistemologies Trevion Henderson and Joshua Cohen

## Introduction and conceptual framework

Students’ personal epistemologies —their understanding of knowledge and knowing —play an important role in ill-structured problem solving, such as engineering des ign (Jonassen, 2000). Students’ personal epistemolo gies are particularly important in team-based design education, where students must communi cate, evaluate, and negotiate engineering ideas to make engineering jud gments (Dym et al., 2005; Francis et al., 2022). However, while students’ personal epistemologies have long b een studied as “beliefs'' about knowledge and knowi ng, research consistently finds that what students say they believe about engineering knowledge in the abstract, such as in surveys, differs from what one might deduce a bout their epistemologies from their behaviors (Lou ca et al., 2004; Elby & Hammer, 2010). Our research was guided  by two questions: (a) What are the characteristics  of engineering students’ professed and enacted epistem ologies? (b) What are the contextual dynamics that explain differences between students’ professed and enacted  epistemologies.  We draw on Elby and Hammer’s (2010) account of epistemologies as context-sensitive, fine-grained knowledge resources that students possess a nd activate across contexts. This account of epistemol ogies turns attention to the elements of context th at serve as cues for students to activate various epistemologic al resources. However, while Elby and Hammer point to pedagogical elements, such as the form of instructi on or content of learning activities, we consider s ocial power dynamics—the degree to which referent others can exert influence that shape students’ enacted epistemologies.

## Data sources and methods

Our data come from focus groups with engineering students that took place in two stages. In Stage 1, we captured students professed epistemologies using handwritten  surveys, after which we asked students to describe  their rationales for their survey responses. In Stage 2, we captured students’ enacted epistemologies by asking them to respond to engineering vignettes by evaluating the rationales presented by five engineers facing a cri tical design decision.

## Findings

Our analysis suggests that while students consistently expressed that scientific objectivity and technical rationality are important aspects of engineering thinking and k nowing, there are contexts in which one’s prior exp eriences, cultural knowledge, and political beliefs can and s hould shape engineering decision making. However, w hile evaluating engineering vignettes, students consiste ntly objected to the use of one’s cultural beliefs and prior experience in engineering decision making, elevatin g technical knowledge and technical rationality. Moreover, the gap between students’ professed and enacted epi stemologies appears to be shaped by patterns of inf luence between students—some students changed their positions in conversation with others during the focus group.

## Implications and contributions

This research suggests that social power dynamics b etween students is one dynamic underlying the gap b etween students’ professed and enacted epistemologies, cau sing some students to draw on different epistemolog ical resources to conform to dominant narratives about s ystems of knowing in engineering. We argue that understanding how social power dynamics can serve a s a cue that activates students’ epistemological re sources for design problem solving, particularly as it relates to conforming to dominant narratives in engineering can have implications for broadening participation in engine ering education. Opening engineering to diverse epistemologies is one way to support students’ learning in teamwork pedagogies.
