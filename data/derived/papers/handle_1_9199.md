# A Knowledge Building–Modeling Approach to Understanding Sustainable Development Goals

**Conference:** ISLS 2023

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study examines how elementary students engaged in knowledge building discourse about United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) as interconnected systems. Students participated in online discourse and constructed models to represent collective understanding. Results show that students consider a system as connected components that work together; collaborative discourse helped them identify elements and processes within and across SDGs that were incorporated into their models. Students engaged in systems thinking to some extent, as reflected in identifying components and relationships; discussion of cycles and loops was rare. Ongoing work explores effects of scaffolds to facilitate engagement with more advanced systems concepts and use of models to drive deeper inquiry.

## Background

Addressing sustainability issues such as biodiversity and climate change requi res understanding their socioecological complexities. An emergent topic in education is systems thinking to help students reason beyond causes and effects and adopt a more holistic approach to structures, patterns, and interdependencies that underlie complex issues (Meadows, 2008). Research shows that young students tend to provide simplistic account s for complex systems (Hmelo-Silver & Pfeffer, 2004). Chi et al. (1994) found that students who provided elaborate explanations of complex phenomena developed more sophisticated understandings of system component s and relationships. Pedagogies for engaging students with sustainability issues should therefore e nable students to theorize, synthesize, and create more ideas and representations of complex proble ms, and help them reflect on the usefulness, limitations, and plausibility of their ideas (Jacobsen & Wilensky, 2006). Exploring systems concepts should happen within educational environments that foster dis cussions, collaboration, and reflection (Jacobson and Wilensky, 2006). Knowledge Building is an educational approach which engages students in collaborative problem solving focused on improving ideas a nd advancing collective understanding (Scardamalia, 2004). Knowledge building communities work with ideas in design mode – theorizing, designing, synthesizing, and identifying new areas to be explored (Bereit er & Scardamalia, 2014). Such practices attempt to bridge the gap between classroom activity focused on assigned tasks and knowledge work as it happens in the real world. This shift requires that students engage in sustained creative work with ideas, with all ideas treated as improvable. This aligns with sustainability initiative s which are not assessed as right or wrong, but in terms of leverage points that minimize trade-offs (Gray et al., 2019) and move toward increasingly advanced states as opposed to pre-determined states of perfection. Computational modeling candepict phenomena that are difficult or impossible to manipulate in the real world due to time, cost, or other constraints. Several studies examined student ’s understandings of complex systems as they engage with agent-based modeling such as NetLogo (Wilensky, 1999) o r systems dynamics modeling like Stella (Richmond, 1985). The current study investigates how Knowledge Buildin g and modeling can be used together to advance systems thinking. Students work as a knowledge building community to explore underlying causes and connections between SDGs. They then construct models to represent their ideas; these, in turn, serve as objects of discourse to generate new ideas and questions. This study focuses on preliminary efforts to engage students in discourse around SDGs and to facilitate the construction of models to capture and visualize their understanding. We investigate three questions (1) How do students understand the concept of a ‘system’?

(2) How do they engage in systems thinking as they participate in knowledge building discourse about SDGs? (3)

How did knowledge building discourse facilitate the construction of models?

## Method

Participants include 14 grade six students in a private school in Ontario, C anada, exploring a social studies unit titled “global issues.” For eight, 40-minute sessions, students discussed the concept of systems and generated ideas and models. Students engaged in discourse on Knowledge Forum (KF), a to ol designed to facilitate knowledge work (Scardamalia, 2004). Students also created system models using SageM odeler (Damelin et al., 2017), a tool to create models by adding image representations of system elements  and creating links to show relationships (see figure 1).

CSCL 2023 Proceedings  © ISLS 226 Students began the inquiry by discussing their understanding of systems in a KF vi ew titled “What is a system?” with pictures of a traffic system, solar system, photosynthesis, a nd a pile of clothes added to stimulate students’ thinking. Next, students posted ideas on what SDG goals involve and how they are connected in a view titled “UNSDGs: A better world is possible” (see figure 2a). Students then used SageModeler to construct models that represent their ideas. In class, students worked in pairs to explore interrelationships between different goals– but each student produced their own model. Students shared their models on KF for others to explore. Knowledge Forum data were analyzed as follows (a) logs were used to me asure students’ participation (reading/posting) in online discourse; (b) contributions to the view “What is a System?” were analyzed qualitatively to determine students’ intuitive unders tanding of a system in terms of structure, behavior, and function (Hmelo-Silver & Pfeffer, 2004); (c) contributions to the view “UNSDGs: A be tter world is possible” were analyzed qualitatively to determine engagement with systems thinking pr actices (Assaraf & Orion, 2005). Models were analyzed quantitatively to determine correlation with KF par ticipation and further examined to determine transfer between KF discourse and models.

## Results and discussion

Intuitive understanding of a system Twelve students contributed to “What is a system” view. The average number of notes written was 3.5 (SD=1.8); 38.1% of the notes were read. Notes consisted of intuitive understanding (“I think a system is the way all natural things survive by working together”), examples (“plants grow using a system containing light, water, and seeds”), or social interactions (“Gee, thanks”). Almost 21% of notes were classified as social interaction, which could be due to this being the students’ first interaction with KF. Almost half of the notes contained examples derived from pictures on the view (e.g. solar system) or systems that students studied as part of the curriculum (e.g. digestive system). One student suggested a system not in the view or curriculum, that a high school counts as a “a system of teachers, student and even schedules and classrooms working together to create an educational environment.” Around 70% of the intuitive understanding notes defined a system in terms of its structure (“A system is a group of multiple things that work together as one ”). 19% highlighted that a system need s to have a function (“A system is made up of multiple things that are interconnected or physically  connected to do something efficient”). Only one student suggested that a system involves some form of repetitive c ycle “a system is a loop of something that happens over and over again as the circle of life”. There was no mention of behavior in student notes. This is consistent with the literature which suggests that novices tend to  think of systems in terms of structures, and system behaviors are often too difficult to articulate or represent (Hmelo-Silver & Pfeffer, 2004). Students’ understanding of SDGs and their interrelationships All students contributed to the “UNSDGs: A better world is possible” view. The average number of notes written was 5.1 (SD = 2.7). 24% of the notes were read. Notes were analyzed qualitatively to determine engagement with system thinking practices. Three categories drawn from Assaraf & Orion (2009) were identified. A) Identifying relationships A system consists of components, relationships, and interactions that lead to its behavior (Hmelo-Silver & Pfeffer, 2004). The most basic level of systems thinking involves identifying relationships betwe en two components (Assaraf & Orion, 2009). Considering each goal as a component, students were able to several relationships across the goals. For example, student S1 connected gender equality to three othe r goals. S2 built-on this idea by suggesting a connection to zero hunger.

S1:  …the UN is working to have women treated as equals to men, as well as lessen sexual harassment. It connects to 10; reduced inequalities, because that is also a goal related to making sure everyone is equally respected. It also connects to 16; Peace, J ustice, and strong institutions because they are all working toward everyone living in a happy, healthy, safe world. - ] S2:  zero hunger could be part of this because males sometimes can get a  better job. Men also can get a better education witch lead to better jobs. if you have a good job then you can get a decent amount of money. With this money you can buy food and people will not be hungry. - ] Students in their simple language were able to articulate relationships across goals. The collaborative KF space allowed students to build on each other’s’ notes, extending meanings and relationships. For students to CSCL 2023 Proceedings  © ISLS 227 develop deeper ideas and identify larger number of connections, they need to engage in more sustained inquiry to explore the inner behaviors and synergies of SDG interactions. B) Identifying socio-ecological components Addressing sustainability goals involves reasoning about social and ecological di mensions (Assaraf & Orion, 2009; Gray et al., 2019). Students demonstrated some ability to think of how the dimens ions intersect. For example, S3 described how human behavior was connected to climate change, and S4 built-on by connecting human behavior to processes such as deforestation, global warming, and animal extinction. S3:  If the government orders to cut down all the trees or bans electric cars, that can cause climate change. Another example is if the person in power declared war, the bombs and tanks produce lots of air pollution which causes climate change. S4:  …war can lead to tanks and bombs, which lead to flattening of the earth/deforestation and pollution. Pollution and deforestation can lead to climate change, which leads to global warming and the death and extinction of animals. These contributions suggest that through dialogue, new ideas connecting human action to  different ecological processes can emerge, which can help students develop a more holistic understanding of SDGs. C) Identifying patterns and cycles The ability to identify dynamic processes of systems such as feedback loops is considered an advanced systems thinking skill (Assaraf & Orion, 2009). Students expressed these concepts  to a limited extent. For example, one student suggested that taking actions towards global warming should be continuous process because of its dynamic nature – “even if we solved it, we couldn't stop trying and think it was the end, because then it would be an endless cycle repeating over and over again”. Another student pointed to reinforcing feedback when discussing poverty: “homelessness, overpopulation, no education, no job, no money, and poverty again.”  This indicates that young students can use simple and intuitive language to express advanced systems thinking concepts. Models to advance discourse All students created their own models. The average number of elements was 9.7 (SD=2.8) and the average number of relationships was 12.4 (SD=6.6). Pearson’s correlation shows no association betwee n the number of elements/relationships and the number of notes created. However, there was a significant correlation between the number of elements/relationships and notes read (r=0.66, p<0.05 and r=0.63, p<0.05, respectively). This suggests that the more students read notes, the more they can identify elements and relationships within and across UNSDGs.

Examining student models supports this finding. For example, the model in figure 1 was produced by student S5 to examine how life on land (goal 11) connects to sustainable citie s and communities (goal 15). ## Implications and significance

This paper presents findings from a study of grade 6 students’ systems thinking as th ey engage in knowledge building discourse and modeling activities in the context of SDGs. Findings sugges t that collaborative discourse can help students develop broader and deeper perspectives about system com ponents and connections. Future research will explore the design of scaffolds to facilitate more advanced systems thinking, where models become objects of inquiry to help sustain discourse.

Research shows that engaging students with computational modeling can help them under stand the dynamic behavior of systems (Wilensky & Resnick, 1999). Our on-going research aims to expand on this research space by exploring that how students can use models to represent ideas and questions that arise during knowledge building discourse, where new knowledge emerges from social interactions. The expec tation is more advanced construction of knowledge as this combined approach can help bridge the gap between the orizing and representation of ideas, while bringing multiple perspectives to the forefront (Sengupta et al., 2018).
