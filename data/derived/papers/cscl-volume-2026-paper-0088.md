# Programming Self-Efficacy and Outcomes, and Programming-oriented Prompting: A Comparison of Two Human–AI Collaboration Configurations

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study investigates the effects of GenAI-assisted programming learning on fifth graders’ computational thinking, programming self-efficacy and outcomes, and programming-oriented prompting. A total of 99 fifth graders from four classes participated and were randomly assigned to two GenAI-assisted programming instruction groups with different human– AI collaboration configurations: single student-AI collaboration (SSAC), in which each student worked individually with a computer with ChatGPT, and dyad student-AI collaboration (DSAC), in which two students worked in pairs, collaboratively using a shared computer with ChatGPT. The instruction was implemented over 6 weeks (1 class per week). This study revealed no significant difference in computational thinking between the two groups of students after GenAI-assisted programming instruction (p>0.05). In contrast, the students in the DSAC group showed significantly higher programming self-efficacy and better programming outcomes (p<0.05). Moreover, significant differences in the distribution of programming-oriented prompting between these two groups of students were also found (p<0.05).

In recent years, programming education for younger students has been highlighted (Lai et al., 2021). Conventionally, programming consists of three major iterative phases, including design, implementation, and debugging. In the design phase, learners specify the needs, requirements and constraints of an application, online system or platform, breaking it down into smaller, more manageable subtasks. During the implementation phase, the requirements are translated into executable code to build a working prototype. In the debugging phase, the prototype is evaluated and, through processes such as algorithmic improvements and the identification of syntactic errors, ultimately optimized  (Abouelenein et al., 2025). In this context, computational thinking, encompassing four key components, including decomposition, pattern recognition, abstraction and algorithm design, is widely recognized as crucial for programming (Ogegbo & Ramnarain, 2022). With visual and block-based coding languages and tools, such as Scratch, students have increasing opportunities to engage in programming at an earlier age in elementary schools (e.g., Xu et al., 2023). These virtual programming environments reduce the syntactic complexity of programming, allowing beginners to focus more on the design stage  (Wong, 2023). Previous research has also confirmed that visual programming environments can enhance students’ positive attitudes and their willingness to engage in solving complex problems (Montuori et al., 2024). Although visual programming environments are indeed a promising entry point, many elementary school students still have difficulty learning abstract programming concepts and computational thinking (Grover, 2021). In particular, these visual programming environments are still insufficient for younger students to develop computational thinking meaningfully. In recent years, with the emergence of generative AI (GenAI), its potential in AI-assisted programming has been highlighted (Liu et al., 2026). Because of its accessibility, students can program in natural language, making programming more intuitive. In particular, as advocated by researchers, GenAI, such as ChatGPT and other large language models (LLMs), has much potential in developing elementary students’ computational thinking (CT) (Yilmaz & Karaoglan Yilmaz, 2023). However, empirical research examining this perspective remains limited. Moreover, as advocated in previous studies, in early programming education, student self-efficacy towards programming may play a more critical role than learning outcomes or performa nce because confidence can significantly affect continuous programming learning (e.g., Xia et al., 2022). That is, GenAI-assisted programming experiences are likely to enhance young students’ self-efficacy toward programming from the outset. Also, the effects of AI-assisted programming on young students’ programming self-efficacy (PE) are underexplored.

539 To address the aforementioned important issues, the current study examined how GenAI-assisted programming learning affects younger students’ programming learning. In particular, two human –AI collaboration configurations, single student-AI collaboration (SSAC) and dyad student-AI collaboration (DSAC), were developed and integrated into GenAI-assisted programming learning activities in this study. Then, the effects of GenAI programming learning activities, under the two human–AI collaboration configurations, on elementary school students’ computational thinking (CT), programming self-efficacy (PE), and programming outcomes were examined separately and compared across groups.

The research questions of this study are as follows: (1) Do GenAI-assisted collaborative programming activities significantly affect elementary students’ CT and PE? (2) After experiencing GenAI-assisted programming activities, do the elementary students with different human –AI collaboration configurations have any significant difference in their CT, PE and programming outcomes? (3) What are the elementary students’ programming-oriented prompting revealed in GenAI-assisted programming? Do elementary students  with different human –AI collaboration configurations show significant differences in their programming-oriented prompting?

## Method

This study was conducted with a quasi-experimental design, with nonequivalent groups and a pretest –posttest design. A total of 99 fifth graders from four classes at a public elementary school in Taiwan participated in this study. These classes were randomly assigned to two instructional intervention (i.e., GenAI-assisted programming activities) groups with different human–AI collaboration configurations: single student-AI collaboration (SSAC), comprising two classes, and dyad student-AI collaboration (DSAC), comprising two classes. The primary goal of GenAI-assisted programming activities in this study was to develop a web-based game through collaboration with GenAI. The activities were implemented over 6 weeks, with 1 class (40 minutes) per week (240 minutes in total). In the first half of the course, each student used a computer to build fundamental programming skills in collaboration with GAI.  In the latter half of the course, students were assigned to two different human –AI collaboration configuration groups, SSAC and DSAC. In the SSAC group, each student worked individually with a computer with a GenAI application (i.e., ChatGPT) to develop a web-based game, whereas in the DSAC group, two students worked in pairs, collaboratively using a shared computer with a GenAI application (i.e., ChatGPT) to develop a web-based game. To evaluate students’ CT skills, PE, and the quality of their programming outcomes (i.e., the web-based game), three instruments were used in this study. The Computational Thinking Scale, adapted from Korkmaz et al. (2017), was used to assess the students’ CT. The scale comprised five subfactors: creativity, algorithmic thinking, cooperativity, critical thinking, and problem-solving, with 7, 6, 4, 5, and 6 items, respectively, for a total of 28 items. T he overall reliability of the scale was 0.84, revealing high reliability for assessing students’ computational thinking. The Programming Self-Efficacy Scale, adapted from Kukul et al. (2017), was used in this study to evaluate students’ programming self-efficacy. It is a unidimensional scale comprising 31 items. The overall reliability of the scale was calculated to be 0.95. Moreover, the Game Design Rubric was used to evaluate the students’ programming outcome in this study. It was adapted from a previous study (M.I et al., 2021), which comprises four dimensions: originality, fluency, flexibility and elaboration (α =.88). In this study, the students’ programming outcomes (i.e. their web-based game) were scored from 1 to 3 points for each dimension. In addition, students’ prompts used for automatic code generation with ChatGPT were analyzed using a three-layer coding scheme. Typically, programming involves three major phases: design, implementation and debugging. Accordingly, this study identified three types of programming-oriented prompting with GenAI: design-oriented prompting, focusing on creating or modifying game features; coding-oriented prompting, referring to direct code-generation requests with minimal elaboration; debugging-oriented prompting, concerning the identification and correction of program errors. Moreover, both the design-oriented and debugging-oriented promptings were further categorized into two subcategories: UI (interface) -related prompting and UX (game element)-related prompting.

## Major findings

Effects of AI-assisted programming activities In this study, the effects of GenAI-assisted programming activities using the single student –AI collaboration (SSAC) and dyadic student–AI collaboration (DSAC) configurations on fifth-graders’ CT and PE were examined. As shown in Table 1, the single student-AI collaboration (SSAC) group showed no significant differences in students’ CT and PE between the pretest and the post-test. However, different results were found in the dyad student-AI collaboration (DSAC) configuration group. In particular, in the DSAC group, a significant 540 improvement in students’ PE was revealed between the pre-test and the post-test (p <.05), whereas the CT was not significant. It indicated that only GenAI-assisted programming with the dyad student-AI collaboration (DSAC) configuration could enhance students’ confidence in programming. One possible explanation is that collaboration with a peer allows students to seek support and exchange ideas, which in turn fosters a greater sense of competence during the activities. However, no significant improvement in CT was observed in either group following the GenAI-assisted programming activities. It suggests that further instruction in computational thinking, such as explicit CT skills instruction or guided, deliberate reflection, is needed and should be integrated into GenAI-assisted programming learning.

## Discussion & Implications

In conclusion, two human-AI collaboration configurations, single student-AI collaboration (SSAC) and dyad student-AI collaboration (DSAC), were developed and integrated into GenAI-assisted programming activities in this study. It was revealed that, compare d to SSAC, DSAC could significantly improve elementary students’ confidence in programming, which may play a critical role in their continuing programming learning. Similar thinking did not improve significantly in either group, suggesting that further efforts are needed to develop effective scaffolding to support the development of students' computational thinking in GenAI-assisted programming environments.

The application of Generative AI (GenAI) in STEM education has been growing because of its potential to transform teaching and learning. As GenAI tools begin to appear in classrooms, teachers’ perspectives play a key role in shaping how these technologies are adopted in practice. It is crucial to examine how teachers perceive, accept, and engage with these technologies to ensure that GenAI supports, rather than replaces, teachers’ agency and autonomy (Collie & Martin, 2024). Existing studies have examined STEM teachers’ perceptions from diverse angles, including epistemic cognition (Garofalo & Farenga, 2025), technology adoption frameworks (Zhai & Krajcik, 2024), targeted interventions (Kim, 2024), and sociotechnical perspectives (Cheah & Kim, 2026). Among multiple perspectives, the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) framework has been widely used to explore teachers’ perceptions and attitudes toward generative AI. As one of the technology adoption models, TAM explains an individual’s technology adoption based on the core dimensions of perceived usefulness (PU), perceived ease of use (PEU), and attitude towards using (ATT) that influence the behavioural intention (BI) on the technology (Davis et al., 1989). In educational research, it is an influential m odel adapted and validated across various contexts and technologies to explore the acceptance of educational tools from teachers’ perspectives (Salahshour Rad et al., 2018; Zhai, 2025). With the increasing integration of GenAI into classrooms, a growing body of research has employed and extended the TAM frameworks to explore teachers’ perspectives in response to the contextual complexities of these emergent technologies (Hsiao & Tang, 2025; Kong et al., 2024; Nevárez Montes & Elizondo-Garcia, 2025). For instance, Kong et al. (2024) proposed an extended TAM to investigate K-12 teachers’ acceptance of GenAI. It is based on the integrated TAM, which incorporates additional dimensions of self-efficacy (SE) and subjective norm (SN) (Davis et al., 1989; Venkatesh & Davis, 2000), to better understand how teachers’ adoption of GenAI tools is influenced both internally and externally. The current study adopts this extended version of the TAM as an interpretive framework to investigate STEM teachers’ perceptions, opinions, and discourse towards GenAI.

While prior work has employed surveys and semi-structured interviews to collect teachers’ perceptions of GenAI tools, social media platforms such as X and Reddit provide additional complementary sources that capture educators’ opinions about GenAI in education (Smith-Mutegi et al., 2025). These open, data-rich forums provide informal and anonymous spaces that encourage greater self-disclosure, especially for intimate or negative content (Ma et al., 2016). Due to the lack of data-based research about teacher s’ diverse perspectives on AI in STEM education on social media, the current study aims to analyze STEM teachers’ discussions about GenAI in Reddit subgroups to examine how educators collectively construct their perspectives on GenAI. Specifically, the study aims to explore the Research Question: What themes characterize STEM teachers’ discussions of generative AI in online communities? To address this question, we apply BERTopic for topic modeling and analyze the discourse with the adjusted TAM framework to identify key themes and patterns.

## Method

544 Reddit, a social media platform that researchers have utilized to examine specific themes within educational communities (e.g., Wu et al., 2024), is selected as the data collection site. Data collection was conducted using the Python Reddit API Wrapper (PRAW), capturing the data from October 2023 to October 2025. We authenticated through a research account to query the subreddits about posts and comments, which are: r/Teachers (2.2M visitors), r/education (218k), r/edtech (31k), and r/Professors (167k). For each subreddit, we retrieved the up to 500 most recent and high-upvote posts, capturing the generative AI-related threads among the STEM teacher community. With the search strategy, we got 213 posts with 2057 corresponding comments. We combined the posts and the comments into a dataset as our corpus. After pre-processing, we applied the BERTopic algorithm, which integrates vectorization, dimensionality reduction, and clustering to generate topics. BERTopic is a topic modeling method that employs transformers and c-TF-IDF to create interpretable topics (Grootendorst, 2022) and is suitable for short and informal texts on social media (Egger & Yu, 2022). We also finetuned the model by applying UMAP to reduce dimensionality while preserving local semantic neighbors, followed by HDBSCAN to identify clusters suitable for short and sparse online discourse. The final model yielded 29 topics, each defined by representative keywords and example posts/comments. After manual checking and synthesizing, we report the most dominant 9 topics.

Since topic modeling provides only an overarching summary of STEM teachers’ discourse in the threads, we employed a two-stage manual review process. The qualitative coding process strengthens the validity of the machine-generated topics and better interprets how teachers’ perceptions of GenAI are shaped not only by individual beliefs but also by peer discourse and emerging norms. Using the adjusted TAM framework (Kong et al., 2024), two authors independently coded 20% of the dataset, achieving substantial i nter-rater reliability (Cohen’s Kappa =.71). Following this, the first author coded the remaining dataset to ensure consistency. In total, 1248 discourse from the top 9 topics were coded.

Attitudes (ATT) Teachers expressed more negative attitudes toward the use of AI in STEM education, with one of the most dominant concerns being students’ ethical use of GenAI. As one participant reflected: Honestly, I’d be pretty happy if students could take an AI output, recognize whether it meets the criteria and technical requirements, fix the elements that are off. I mean, dang, they aren’t even willing to do that with revisions on their own work. (ID 34) This example illustrates that teachers are worried about students’ overreliance on AI at the expense of creativity and problem-solving skills. While teachers expected authentic work, AI misuse has made originality harder to detect. As another teacher noted: It’s super hard to detect AI-generated code; there’s no signature or anything like that similar to image guardrails. Stylistically it could also be super inconsistent. (ID 64) These concerns reflect a broader tension: the difficulty of detecting human-generated vs AI-generated content. To cope with this challenge, some teachers described proactive strategies, such as creating highly personalized rubrics, emphasizing process over product, handwritten notes on printed assignments, and increasing in-class presentations. Despite these concerns, a subset of teachers conveyed more positive attitudes, emphasizing the potential of AI if used responsibly. These teachers underscored the importance of preparing students for a future where AI is ubiquitous, while ensuring students still master foundational competencies. Perceived usefulness (PU) Teachers reported mixed experiences regarding the usefulness of generative AI in teaching. On the positive side, teachers acknowledged AI’s potential to enhance their own instructional productivity, especially for time-consuming tasks, such as lesson planning, assignment generation, and preparing practice problems. SAVE ALL LESSON PLANS…every single thing you do! Some might suck and some might work but all are good ideas…until proven otherwise… and then… you just have to update them. I teach robotics and engineering…same process. (ID 758) However, many described significant challenges when attempting to integrate AI into classroom practice. These challenges often reflected the limitations of the technology itself, such as “hallucinations,” imprecise answers, or difficulties handling mathematical problems and formulas. As one teacher noted: It would have been great if it worked. I have been playing with AI for a while. I could not get AI to be precise enough. At best, it performs as a really bad, half-wit TA. (ID 33) These comments highlight that teachers’ judgments of usefulness are closely tied to reliability: when the tool produces errors, its perceived value in instructional contexts drops sharply. Teachers recognized the value of AI in streamlining routine tasks, while also cautioning that its outputs require adaptation, critical oversight, and professional judgment.

Self-efficacy (SE) In terms of teachers’ confidence in using AI, a dominant subtheme concerned the need to develop teachers’ AI literacy. Several excerpts underscored the idea that teachers must first build their own competence before they can guide students in constructive use. For example, one participant emphasized, AI is not ruining education. Teachers need to learn how to use AI in the classroom. Teach your children how to use it in constructive ways. But first, you have to learn the same. (R39) 546 Teachers with more positive attitudes toward generative AI frequently paired this optimism with explicit mentions of competency development. At times, teachers framed AI as a supportive student “peer”, but emphasized that effective use depended on prompt engineering skills and responsible practices. Subjective norms (SN) Teachers’ intentions to use AI were also shaped by contextual and social pressures. One participant reflected: Personal thoughts on general AI use in education aside, I think one of the problems with ‘the push’ to integrate it into classes and such is that a lot of the people loudly saying ‘everyone should be using it, it’s here and it’s the future whether we like it or not, etc.’ offer no actual ideas for how to use it. (R42) This comment illustrates how peer pressure and broad societal discourse create pressure for adoption without sufficient guidance or concrete strategies. School policies also emerged as a key factor under the subjective norm category. Some teachers reported  avoiding AI use because of institutional bans, while others expressed frustration at the lack of clear policy guidance from their universities/schools. This tension highlights how top-down directives, or their absence, shape teachers’ willingness to experiment with AI. Finally, the teacher– student relationship appeared as a subtle but meaningful influence on adoption. As one participant explained: I get that there is a difference in using AI to do your homework and using AI to grade, but if I were a student and my teacher used AI when I wasn’t allowed to, I would be at least a little bit upset. (R49) Most STEM teachers in this study expressed negative or skeptical attitudes toward the use of GenAI largely due to concerns about student ethics, the difficulty of detecting authenticity, and the lack of AI literacy. These findings align with prior research  on challenges surrounding algorithmic bias, academic integrity, and the limitations of detecting AI-generated content (Zhai, 2025). However, they diverge from earlier accounts, suggesting that initial adopters of GPT tools tended to report more enthusiasm  and optimism (Haque et al., 2022). This shift suggests that, as GenAI becomes more widely used in educational settings, teachers’ perspectives are evolving toward a more cautious and critical stance.

Beyond documenting these attitudes, our findings reveal a key pattern that is less visible in prior work: teachers’ acceptance of GenAI is role-dependent. STEM teachers in this study expressed greater willingness to use GenAI for their own professional tasks, such as lesson planning and content generation, while remaining more skeptical of its use by students. Rather than representing a uniform stance toward AI, teachers’ perspectives vary across contexts of use, highlighting the need to conceptualize technology acceptance as situated and relational. These findings also point to broader structural challenges shaping teachers’ adoption of GenAI. Echoing Kumar and Sharma (2025) and Smith-Mutegi et al. (2025), teachers described tensions between expectations to adopt AI and the lack of clear guidance, policy, or institutional support. This highlights the role of subjective norms (SN) in shaping adoption, where external pressures may encourage use without providing sufficient pedagogical direction. Addressing this gap requires coordinated efforts across professional development, curriculum design, and policy frameworks.

This study contributes to learning sciences by examining large-scale, naturally occurring discourse data in online communities to understand how STEM teachers collectively make sense of GenAI use. Our findings clarify that teachers’ acceptance of GenAI dif fers depending on the performance of tools, the AI literacy skills, and whether it’s used for their own professional work or for students’ learning. The findings suggest that effective integration of GenAI in STEM education requires moving beyond a generalized notion of “AI adoption” toward more differentiated approaches that explicitly address the practical strategies of AI use. Taken together, these insights highlight both the opportunities and tensions that GenAI introduces into STEM education. Teachers recognize AI’s potential to reduce workload and enhance instructional efficiency, but their skepticism reflects unresolved challenges around trust, ethics, and pedagogy.
