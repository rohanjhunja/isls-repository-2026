# Argumentation With Summary Tables in Geoscience Learning

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In this study, we examine the interactions and discourse of a middle school classroom as they use a Summary Table to scaffold the generation of scientific arguments during the implementation of an online plate tectonics curriculum. Creating time and space for scientific discourse provided students with sense-making opportunities around complex science ideas to support their explanations of the phenomenon. The Summary Table scaffolded the development of increasingly sophisticated explanations by structuring students' observations to connect with the driving question of the unit. Specifically, the Summary Table supported small and whole group discussions positioning students to argue and partake in open critique of their claims and evidence in increasingly public ways as they develop sophisticated science explanations.

## Introduction

The fundamental vision of Ambitious Science Teachin g (AST) is to support students from all backgrounds  in understanding core science ideas, participating in authentic science practices and problems, and devel oping students’ ability to learn alone (Windschitl, Thomp son, & Braaten, 2018). To achieve this vision, the AST framework incorporates four sets of teaching practi ces for teachers to invest in as they attempt to ma ke pedagogical shifts in their teaching. One central set is designed for supporting students’ evolving thinking, often grounded in public discourse events. Here, the AST framework introduces the Summary Table as a tool to support students' ongoing scientific sensemaking (Windschitl et al., 2018). Summary Tables provide students with a way to structure and organize their thoughts based on c lassroom discourse regarding an anchoring phenomeno n (Windschitl et al., 2018). Students can engage in conversation where they compare ideas in reflective critiques to refine and build new science ideas as they work to complete the Summary Table. We view this collective critique as a form of argum entation, a subset of discussion with a persuasive nature and necessity of reasonable critics as participants (Shemwell & Furtak, 2010). Students naturally engage in arguments in everyday life, but the AST framewor k scaffolds students’ development of scientific arg uments inclusive of talk moves around claims, evidence, rebuttals, and counterclaims (Windschitl et al., 2018). We argue that the framing of the Summary Table includes crit ical discourse of these talk components that can be instrumental in developing students’ explanations and arguments of scientific phenomena. It is with th ese ideas in mind we ask: In what ways does the use of Summary Tables influence student argumentation?

## Context

Our study took place in a middle school classroom in the northeastern US, where veteran teacher, Keith (all names used are pseudonyms), taught 82 seventh grade (12-and 13-year-old) students across six classes. Keith  was experienced in AST and argumentation, having had ongoing professional learning opportunities in both areas over the past decade through his local college. The summ er prior to the study, he participated in a week-lo ng professional learning workshop to support the use o f a digital plate tectonics curriculum part of the Geological Models for Explorations Of the Dynamic Earth (GEODE) project. The workshop, designed and facilitated by the authors, centered the Summary Table as a tool for t eachers and students to use in developing an explan ation of the driving question, “What will Earth look like in  500 million years?” Keith used the Summary Tables in his classroom in iterative phases of small group and teacher-led whole-class discussion. The whole class discussions included negotiating students’ ideas into an agreed-upon statement for the class Summary Table which students copied into their science notebooks.

Data and Analysis Specifically, this study analyzes the classroom discourse associated with the use of a Summary Table during the implementation of GEODE. We captured audio and video recordings of whole-class interactions, audio recordings of small group table talk, video and screen capture of pairs of students using the curriculum, and artifacts produced ICLS2022 Proceedings 1025 © ISLS by students, including their Summary Tables and sum mative scientific arguments (CERs) in their science notebooks throughout 13 90-minute class periods.

To understand how students’ interaction with the Su mmary Table influenced the students’ final explanation, we analyzed data from one class period (N = 24 students) in four phases. In the first phase, we created event maps (Kelly & Chen, 1999) of the curriculum’s  implementation to identify the sections of instruc tion in which the class discourse centered around their Sum mary Table, resulting in three 90-minute class peri ods for further analysis. In the second phase, we expanded the event maps; we transcribed discussions to create units of analysis for discrete discourse events in which students discussed a specific scientific idea related to the Summary Table task. We transcribed both whole class and small group talk derived from the conversations of thr ee focus groups of four students who had consented to partic ipate. In the third phase, using grounded theory (C harmaz, 2014), we identified salient themes of ideas that o ccurred during these discourse events, such as publ ic argumentation and refinement of ideas. Finally, we traced the emergence of these ideas in the discourse through small and whole group talk, students’ Summary Tables, and final arguments located within CER Journal entries.

## Findings

In this section, we follow the narrative evolution of students working to develop explanations and arguments using the Summary Table. Students collectively refine an observation for the Summary Table, increasing in sophistication during each iteration. Next, the tea cher uses talk moves to re-focuses a pair’s idea wh ich led to open critique and argumentation in small groups before being brought up during the whole-class discussion. The findings here focus on four students and their teacher, Keith, negotiating an explanation for why some mountains have volcanoes and others do not, one step in expla ining the phenomenon of plate tectonics. By followi ng the evolution of this idea through its use in the Summary Table and associated discourse, we see students' explanations increase in sophistication. The structure of the Summary Table document kept track of the different stages of ideas as the students progressed through different activi ties, instead of requiring them to memorize key obs ervations and claims. We argue that multiple iterations of critique, aided by the structure and documentation of the Summary Table (Figure 1), support the development of students’ later arguments. In a previous class period, students had oscillate d between small and whole group discussions to arrive at a class-wide claim of “Everywhere that a volcano forms, you will find earthquakes, but you can find earthquakes alone.” Captured in the students’ Summary Tables at  the end of the lesson, this statement is built upo n in the following discussions as part of the patterns and evidence that supports claims about plate boundaries. Here, we illustrate the further evolution of this idea throu gh a case of one small group (Eden, Emily, Cal, and  Anna) navigating the relationship between earthquakes and volcanoes through the Plate Tectonics curriculum. During the Summary Table discussions, students’ public thinking was subjected to critique and guidance from other actors in the classroom (peers and teach ers) as they argued communal explanations. As the c lass accepted the observation between volcanoes and earthquakes into the Summary Table, Keith guided students to search for more observations to add to it, building on the relationship between active volcanoes and earthquakes. In their small groups, students split into dyads to  explore and discuss. Anna and Eden began looking f or connections between the three locations they had ex plored through the curricular tools (Andes Mountain s, Himalayas, and Aleutian Islands), eventually noting  that the volcanoes and earthquakes in the Andes ar e near water (Pacific Ocean) as seen with a data visualization tool. They called Keith over to their table to share the idea. He focused the pair on the types of plates near the Andes and stated there is an ocean plate and a land (continental) plate. Keith then asked about the types of plates n ear the Aleutians and Himalayas, to which the pair responded with “ocean and ocean” and “land and land” respectively. Although scientific vocabulary around plate tectonics used in this discourse event may be considered unsophisticated, they were building ideas that would likely increase in sophistication (McDonald et al., 2018).

The two pairs at the table were brought back toget her as Anna interrupted the other dyad (Emily and Cal) at her table, “Emily! You know how in the Aleutians you have ocean and ocean plates? But in the Himalayas, you have land and land. That's why there's not volcanoe s [in the Himalayas]!” The foursome then briefly di scussed the location of the Aleutian Islands before Cal said, “Wait a second, why does it [the type of plate] matter? ‘Cause shouldn’t it-.” Anna interrupted him and argued, “No, because remember land and land don't cause [volc anoes] because remember when we did the plate (a reference to a simulation in the module), where [we] had two [plates] come together? There were just mountains, there wer e no volcanoes.” Cal then stated that he remembered  now and accepted Anna’s idea. Anna and Eden’s discovery enabled the group to elicit Cal’s ideas about plat e types, as well as provide Anna with the opportunity to connect her claims to evidence from the module. When Keith brought the class together to discuss wh at to place in the Summary Table from their discuss ions, Anna was the first student to publicly offer an idea, exuberantly waving her hand in the air. She claimed, ICLS2022 Proceedings 1026 © ISLS Okay, so what we noticed is that most of the volcan oes are near water. But then we did a land and a land coming together, it didn't [form volcanoes].

In response to this, Keith pressed the whole class to develop a grounded claim for the Summary Table based on Anna’s idea, specifically thinking about each of the observed plate interactions. Eden said, “Well for the Andes it's land and water plates but for the Aleutians, i t’s water and water.” Keith began to document these  ideas as generalized claims within the Summary Table. As he continued to revoice Anna’s ideas, Keith moved away from the use of phrases like “water ones” using the more scientific term, oceanic plates. He documented these refined ideas into the Summary Table’s overall claim for the activity (see Figure 1), which served as a connection to the driving question of the unit. Rather thandiscussin g their reasoning as a class, each student used the  class claim from the Summary Table as a claim within their CER Journal (see Figure 2), giving them a chance to mak e an individual argument for the activity while collecting additional evidence from the module. Within small group talk, we saw an ins tance of hesitation when Cal did not agree with Ann a’s idea, to which Anna argued her position citing direct evidence from the module (Shemwell & Furtak, 2010). As Anna ICLS2022 Proceedings 1027 © ISLS argued her position, Cal was able to gain insight i nto Anna’s conceptions and reasoning, seeing a diff erent perspective to establish the communal idea. Anna’s ideas then were critiqued in her interaction with Eden, their sharing with Keith, and her debate with Cal, before sharing with the whole group. We also highlight that Keith served a critical role  in focusing and revoicing the students’ developing ideas as he used AST teacher talk moves to focus the students’ talk to his intended curricular goal of completing the Summary Table (Windschitl et al., 2018). First, we see him situate students to engage in peer-to-peer talk in dyads. When he visits the small groups, he presses students to expand their ideas in pattern finding t o bring the notion of types of plates to their explanation. Finally, in the whole group discussion, Keith uses revoicing to bring students’ everyday language together with scientifi c language, such as connecting “water ones” with “o ceanic plate.” Together, Keith’s use of talk moves and the Summary Table supports students’ development of scientific explanations for the phenomenon.

## Conclusion

Our findings illustrate that creating time and space for scientific discourse provided students with sense-making opportunities around complex science ideas to support their explanations of the phenomenon. The Summary Table scaffolded development of increasingly sophisticated explanations by providing a structure for students to discuss and connect ideas with the driving question of the unit (Windschitl et al., 2018). Specifically, the Summary Table supported small and whole group discussions positioning students to both construct and critique ideas in diverse public ways (Ford, 2008). Ideas which were made pub lic (small or whole group) were able to be debated to improve upon the idea before it became part of students’ explanations at the end of the activity (Duschl & Osborne, 2002). Furthermore, the process of publicizing thou ghts provided students with access to the conceptio ns and reasoning of the teacher and students in the class (Windschitl et al., 2018). This allows them to expe rience unfamiliar perspectives of complex ideas and develop communal explanations of scientific phenomena. However, talking in small groups does not eliminate the non-normative explanations from arguments, as students may not have the knowledge and skills to recognize the inco nsistencies in their peers’ arguments. Using the fi ndings of Berland and Lee (2012) we see this as less concerni ng, as students have multiple opportunities for con struction and critique of ideas in the iterative classroom discussions supported by the organizational of the Summary Table. This study shows the importance of critical talk be tween students and teachers framed by the Summary T able. We argue that the Summary Table scaffolded classroo m discourse around complex phenomena in ways that supported the development of increasingly sophistic ated scientific explanations.
