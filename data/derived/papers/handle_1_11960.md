# Towards Designing for Entangled Agency Between Human and Non-Human Actors in Collaborative Learning: Futures of Generative AI?

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This poster contributes to the theoretical discussion on the potential futures of emerging human–non-human (AI) collaboration within the context of CSCL. It highlights a paradigm shift away from human-centric approaches in learning design toward frameworks that acknowledge the entangled agency of both human and non-human actors—such as conversational agents—with the aim of fostering authentic collaboration, characterized by shared objectives, processes, and outcomes for both actors involved.

This poster offers a theoretical contribution to the evolving discourse on human–non-human (generative AI) collaboration within the context of Computer-Supported Collaborative Learning (CSCL). While much of the research has focused on how generative AI tools—such as ChatGPT—can enhance students' performance in learning contexts (e.g., Cukurova, 2024), the understanding of how humans and AI agents might achieve authentic collaboration—defined by shared objectives, processes, and outcomes for both actors involved (e.g., Dillenbourg, 1999)—remains in its infancy (e.g., Cress & Kimmerle, 2023; Lodge et al., 2023; Sharples, 2023; Zhou & Schofield, 2024). This poster seeks to add to this gap. To this end, the work draws on a post-humanist, critical perspective of decentering the human (e.g., Nicenboim et al., 2023), as developed in the field of human-computer interaction (HCI) with its long-standing tradition in usability studies. The poster provides preliminary insights into how this perspective can be meaningfully translated into the CSCL context. In doing so, it moves beyond viewing humans as the sole measure of value and agency in human–AI collaboration, especially in the design of learning practices, which remains central to the field of CSCL (Baker & Reimann, 2023).

## perspectives in learning design

The concept of decentering the human challenges traditional understandings of the relationship between human and non-human actors by recognizing their interconnectedness and entangled agency within shared environments. This perspective aligns well with the current understanding of productive collaborative interactions in learning settings, where the contextualized and balanced interconnection of diverse actors is considered a key (e.g., Barron, 2003).

In both the humanities and social sciences, decentering is commonly understood as a critical perspective and methodological tool used to challenge dominant viewpoints, theories, and narratives (Nicenboim et al., 2023). In terms of design, the shift involves moving beyond conventional concerns of human-centered design focused on users and products towards an emphasis on relations and ecologies (Yoo et al., 2023, see also Nicenboim et al., 2023). It embraces the recognition of multiple “voices” and advocates for the inclusion of non-human agency in the design processes (e.g., Giaccardi, 2020; Nicenboim et al., 2023). While decentering the human repositions the focus away from traditional user-centered methods, it does not negate human perspectives or values. Rather, it seeks to situate human actions within a broader context—opening design to include non-human actors and their “lifeworlds” (Smith, 2019). However, realizing this shift remains highly challenging, particularly due to the significant gap between theoretical frameworks and practical implementations (Nicenboim et al., 2023). Adapting this approach into CSCL situations presents several challenges. The primary objective when designing for learning in this context is to facilitate and enhance the quality of interactions between human and non-human agents to support meaningful collaboration. A key challenge lies in the difficulty humans may encounter to fully decentering themselves, due to the inherent nature of the partnership (Nicenboim et al., 2023). This partnership requires users not only to ask questions and receive information from an AI agent, but also to engage in a dialogue in which both partners exchange information and stimulate each other’s contributions and reflections (Cress & Kimmerle, 2023).

Accordingly, to develop a deeper understanding of this dynamic, learners should be first systematically and extensively observed in their real-world interactions with generative AI tools. As Cress and Kimmerle (2023) emphasize, it is essential to understand what the interplay between humans and AI agents actually looks like and how it can be meaningfully described. This raises critical questions about how their complementary capabilities can be effectively harnessed (Dellermann et al., 2019). For this purpose, it is crucial to first examine the human and non-human contributions to the shared process (Cress & Kimmerle, 2023). As they aptly ask: “Is the polyphony of different voices valid when some of these voices are not even human? Is it even communication if a language-producing system makes contributions but is not a cognitive system?” (p. 610). Finally, this paradigm shift in HCI has introduced specific concepts, methods, and practices aimed at decentering the human, such as conducting ethnography with non-human actors (e.g., Murray-Rust et al., 2019). In the context of CSCL, such exploratory approaches could support the analysis of how individuals engage with conversational agents like ChatGPT, as well as their perceptions of these technologies’ capabilities and limitations (Cress & Kimmerle, 2023). For instance, real-time observations of learner-AI sessions that document changes in roles, dependencies, and the learner’s shifting engagement with the AI would offer valuable insights into the dynamics of human–non-human interactions.

## Discussion & Implications

To conclude, while generative AI tools are still in the early stages of development, they signal the futures of AI conversational agents and their potential impacts on facilitating collaborative interactions and learning (Cress & Kimmerle, 2023). Current debates around generative AI reflect a spectrum of views—ranging from calls to ban such tools to proposals for their full integrating into education (e.g., Bastani et al., 2024; Kasneci et al., 2023; Stadler et al., 2024). Yet critical questions remain: If generative AI agents communicate in ways that resemble human interaction, can they be considered agents capable of co-constructing knowledge with humans? As Cress and Kimmerle (2023) argue, the CSCL community has a pivotal role in conceptualizing and investigating this emerging field. Important questions include: What implications do these developments have for learning? How can AI agents most effectively interact with humans to collaboratively enhance learning outcomes? This poster contributes to these ongoing conversations by proposing theoretical foundations and offering directions for ethnographically oriented empirical research that acknowledges the entangled agency of human and non-human actors in CSCL.
