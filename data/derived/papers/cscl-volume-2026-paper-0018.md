# How Do Pre-Service Teachers Integrate Multiple Peer Feedback Messages? Identifying Different Integration Patterns

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Computer-supported peer feedback is a valuable approach to enhancing student learning in CSCL environments. Yet, little is known about how learners process multiple peer feedback messages. In an exploratory study with N = 100 pre-service teachers who analyzed classroom cases, provid ed feedback, and integrat ed feedback from two peers, we aimed to identify different feedback integration patterns (RQ1) and investigated how these are predicted by objective feedback features and perceived feedback quality (RQ2). A hierarchical cluster analysis yielded three integration patterns: a confirmation pattern, a conflict pattern, and a selective integration pattern. Multinomial logistic regressions revealed that objective feedback features—specifically accuracy, relevance, and implementability —predicted these i ntegration patterns, whereas perceived feedback quality did not. These findings highlight that integrating multiple peer feedback messages is a complex, feature-dependent process. In addition, t hey offer implications for designing adaptive, computer-supported peer feedback systems in CSCL settings. Aims of the study Peer feedback, conceptualized as a collaborative learning process, has been extensively examined within the area of Computer-Supported Collaborative Learning (CSCL) and has consistently been demonstrated to facilitate student learning (Özbek et al., 2024; Zong et al., 2021). Although current research provides strong evidence that providing feedback constitutes a powerful mechanism for learning, how learners actually use the feedback they receive from their peers effectively remains rather unclear. This is espec ially the case when learners receive feedback from more than one peer, that is when they need to integrate multiple peer feedback messages (Lundstrom & Baker, 2009; Nicol et al., 2014). Nevertheless, systematic theoretical frameworks and empirical investigations elucidating how learners integrate multiple peer feedback messages remain scarce. Drawing on research concerning learning from multiple documents (List & Alexander, 2019), we posit that the optimal integration of multiple peer feedback messages entails four steps: identification, separate representation, simultaneous relation, and relational designation. After relationships between the multiple peer feedback messages have been identified, students need to connect these multiple peer feedback messages through synthesis and reconciliation (List & Alexander, 2019). Yet, it remains unclear (a) to what extent and in what ways learners engage in these integration processes when integrating multiple peer feedback messages, and (b) how both objective feedback features (relevance, accuracy, implementability, structural quality) and the perceived quality of the received feedback (adequacy, subjective quality) influence this integration process. The present study seeks to provide empirical evidence to address these questions. Peer feedback in the context of CSCL The peer feedback process basically consists of four phases (Kollar & Fischer, 2010). In the task performance phase, students work on a task and produce an initial task solution (e.g., a written case analysis). These initial task solutions represent the product on which peers then formulate feedback in the feedback provision phase. This is followed by the feedback reception phase, during which students process the feedback they have received and compare it to their initial task solutions. The feedback process ends with the revision phase, in which students revise their initial task solution based on the feedback they have received. In the context of CSCL, peer feedback is regarded as an important instructional method, as learners collaborate and digital technologies can support the peer feedback process (Zong et al., 2021). From a sociocultural perspective (Vygotsky et al., 1978), students in the peer feedback process may provide support to each other’s Zone of Proximal Development (ZPD), enabling them to operate in their ZPD and thus advance their actual development. This is achieved by helping each other through asking questions and offering suggestions for possible revisions. From a socio-cognitive perspective, the reason for the effectiveness of peer feedback lies in the fact that important cognitive processes take place during both feedback provision and feedback reception (Nicol & McCallum, 2022; van Popta et al., 2017). 80 Compared to analogue forms of peer feedback, computer-supported peer feedback offers several advantages, such as greater flexibility in application (both synchronous and asynchronous) and the ability to randomly, anonymously, and automatically assign feedback pairs (Chen, 2016; Cho & Schunn, 2007; Panadero & Alqassab, 2019). Furthermore, computer-supported peer feedback processes can easily be structured by incorporating instructional support into each phase (e.g., through the provision of assessment criteria, rubrics, or training; Alqassab et al., 2018; Dmoshinskaia et al., 2021) and easily monitored by the teacher (Farahani et al., 2019). Several empirical studies have confirmed these advantages in CSCL contexts, showing that computer-supported peer feedback processes can positively impact student performance (Özbek et al., 2024; Zong et al., 2021), particularly when supported by instruction (Hornstein, Keller et al., 2025). Integrating multiple peer feedback messages Although the feedback reception phase in the peer feedback process is conceptually complex and has received relatively limited empirical attention, it offers considerable potential for promoting student learning (Nicol et al., 2014). When learners engage with feedback from their peers, they need to compare their initial task solutions with the feedback messages received. This comparison exposes them to alternative perspectives, encourages critical reflection on their prior understanding, and potentially supports the refinement of their initial task solution (Nicol & McCallum, 2022). Such learning benefits may even be larger when students receive feedback messages from multiple peers rather than from a single peer, as integrating multiple peer feedback messages is likely to involve even deeper cognitive processing (Cho & Schunn, 2007). However, despite their importance, the nature of these integration processes remains insufficiently explored. Empirical investigations into how students process and learn from multiple peer feedback messages are still scarce. In one study, Li et al. (2010) provided each participant with feedback from two peers, explicitly noting that feedback quality might vary and that conflicting suggestions could occur. Students were therefore required to compare both feedback messages and decide which comments to incorporate into their revisions. Interestingly, the study found no significant relationship between the quality of the feedback participants received and subsequent revision quality. By contrast, Cho and Schunn (2007) reported that students who received feedback from multiple peers produced essays of higher quality than those who received feedback from only one peer. More recently, van Meenen et al. (2023) examined how students process discrepant peer feedback using eye-tracking data and simulated feedback comments. Their results revealed pronounced individual differences: while some learners recognized inconsistencies but disregarded them, others actively attempted to reconcile these discrepancies. These findings indicate that students do not automatically engage effectively with multiple peer feedback messages; instead, they display diverse integration patterns, ranging from largely ignoring the feedback to actively resolving conflicting information. However, despite these initial findings, it is still unclear exactly how (high-quality) integration of multiple peer feedback messages looks like. To advance our understanding of how learners integrate feedback from multiple peers, we used the Integrated Framework of Multiple Text Use (List & Alexander, 2019). This framework describes how individuals engage with, process, and construct meaning from multiple text s and comprises three stages: preparation, execution, and production. In the preparation stage, learners orient themselves toward the task by considering external task features, personal perceptions, and individual factors such as prior knowledge, which shape their cognitive engagement. During the execution stage, students employ metacognitive monitoring and cognitive strategies—including comprehension and cross-textual linking—to connect and integrate information across texts until they determine whether their cognitive goals are met. Finally, in the production stage, learners translate the cognitive and affective outcomes of text processing into external products, such as written work, which may or may not fully reflect their internal conceptual, emotional, or belief-based changes. Within this model, processing activities—particularly those related to information integration—play a central role. A key process identified by List and Alexander (2019) is cross-textual linking, which involves establishing meaningful connections between multiple texts to ensure mindful integration (Berndt et al., 2018). This process unfolds in four sequential steps. First, during identification, learners search for potential relationships between texts. Second, they create separate representations of the r elevant information from each text independently. Third, through simultaneous relation, they draw explicit connections between the content of the different texts. Finally, during relational designation, learners classify the identified relationship, determining whether it reflects similarities, additions, or contradictions. After identifying relationships between sources, learners engage in synthesis, defined as the integration of information from multiple texts into a coherent and comprehensive understanding. During synthesis, the information across multiple texts may align, complement, or contradict each other. When discrepancies arise, learners perform reconciliation, a sub-process of synthesis, which may take several forms: they may attempt to resolve conflicts between multiple texts, favor one text over another, or disregard the conflict entirely. Successful 81 synthesis marks the completion of the integration process (List & Alexander, 2019). Therefore, this model implies that learners may exhibit varying approaches to integrat e multiple texts. Applied to the processing of multiple peer feedback messages, this may result in distinct patterns of peer feedback integration. These integration patterns are important for the design of CSCL environments, because without understanding the m, current CSCL tools might inadvertently overload students or encourage superficial processing when assigning multiple peers. Although they did not test their model empirically, their work illustrates that theoretical insights from research on learning with multiple documents can provide a valuable foundation for understanding how students combine feedback from different peers. Building on this premise, the present study aims to empirically examine the processes by which students integrate multiple peer feedback messages and to investigate how the characteristics of received feedback influence this integration. Based on prior research, we posit that the features of feedback messages students receive from peers can shape how they integrate them. Thereby, both objective feedback features and the perceived quality of the feedback are likely to play a role. Objective feedback features include the content and structural quality of the feedback (Nelson & Schunn, 2008; Wu & Schunn, 2020). Content quality refers to the accuracy and relevance of the feedback—that is, whether it correctly addresses the task. Structural quality, in contrast, concerns the presence of specific feedback element s, such as reminders of task requirements (feed up), evaluations of student performance (feed back), or suggestions for improvement (feed forward; Hattie & Timperley, 2007). Effective structural feedback is also expected to be clear, illustrative, and targ eted at the aspects of the task that require attention, and it may include explanations or examples that clarify why certain aspects are correct or incorrect (Gielen & De Wever, 2015). Wu and Schunn (2020) found that explanations in feedback increase the likelihood of its implementation, and that this effect may be even stronger when learners receive multiple peer feedback messages. By contrast, Nelson and Schunn (2008) observed that summaries and concrete solution suggestions were more likely to prompt implementation than explanations. Despite these mixed findings, evidence suggests that objective feedback features play a critical role in guiding how students integrate feedback. In addition to objective feedback features, the recipient’s perception of the quality of the feedback they received may influence the integration process (Strijbos et al., 2021). Perceived feedback quality encompasses both the adequacy of feedback (e.g., fairness, usefulness, and acceptance; Strijbos et al., 2021), and subjective evaluations of specific feedback elements (e.g., feed up, feed back, feed forward; Hattie & Timperley, 2007). Strijbos et al. (2021) demonstrated that students’ perceptions of fairness, usefulness, and acceptance predict their motivation to improve and their affective responses. Conversely, Huisman et al. (2018) found no effect of perceived feedback quality on peer feedback outcomes. Although findings are somewhat inconsistent, perceived feedback quality appears to influence how students engage with feedback. Overall, previous research suggests that both objective feedback features and perceived feedback quality can affect subsequent processes on the part of the feedback recipient. However, most prior studies have focused on feedback implementation, whereas research on how learners integrate multiple peer feedback messages prior to implementation remains limited. We propose that both objective feedback features and perceived feedback quality are likely to influence feedback integration (Richter & Maier, 2017). For example, if one feedback message contains inaccurate information, while another is correct (content quality), the recipient needs to reconcile the conflict during integration, whereas integrating two accurate messages may require less effort. Similarly, if a student perceives one peer feedback message as useful and another as not, the latter may be ignored. Empirical studies testing these assumptions are currently lacking. ## Research questions

The present study investigates how pre-service teachers integrate feedback they received from multiple peers and examines how objective feedback features  (accuracy, relevance, implementability, structural feedback quality) and perceived feedback quality (adequacy, subjective quality) shape this integration process. Specifically, the study addresses the following questions:

(1) Candifferent integration patterns be distinguished empirically?

(2) Do objective feedback features and perceived peer feedback quality predict integration?

82 Sample and design The study included N = 100 pre-service teachers from a German university, with a mean age of M  = 22.88 years (SD = 5.16), who were on average in their fourth semester (M = 4.47, SD = 1.01), indicating that participants were approximately halfway through their teacher education program. Consistent with typical demographics of German pre-service teacher cohorts, 77% of the sample were female. The study was embedded within three regular courses, all of which covered identical content about educational psychology. Students engaged in a CSCL environment as part of these courses. This environment presented authentic case scenarios in the form of written descriptions of problematic classroom situations, corresponding analytical tasks, and relevant educational evidence. Participation in the peer feedback exercise was required for course credit; however, pre-service teachers could opt out of the use of their data for research purposes without any academic consequences, in which case their data were removed from the dataset. The sample analyzed in this study is a subset of a larger dataset that has been published elsewhere (Hornstein, Greisel & Kollar, 2025). Digital peer feedback tool The peer feedback  process was conducted using the  computer-supported peer feedback  tool “getFeedback!”, which enables instructors to provide targeted guidance for each phase of the peer feedback process, including the task performance, feedback provision, feedback reception, and revision stages. Within this tool, all feedback exchanges are anonymous, and the tool randomly assigns students to provide and receive feedback from different peers (Fey et al., 2023).

## Task and procedure

The peer feedback process took place over four weeks, with participants  completing all tasks remotely through the “getFeedback!” tool. In Week 1 (task performance phase), participants were presented with a written description of a hypothetical problematic teaching scenario and instructed to analyze it according to a five-step scheme for evidence-informed reasoning: problem identification, problem description, problem explanation, goal setting, and selection of actions (Greisel et al., 2022). To support their analysis, participants were provided with brief summaries of two theories from educational psychology: cognitive load theory (Sweller, 1999) and the ICAP model (Chi & Wylie, 2014). In Week 2 (feedback provision phase), each participant ’s analysis was randomly assigned to two peers, who were tasked with providing feedback on the assigned analyses. During Week 3 (feedback reception and revision phase), participants received their own analyses along with the two peer feedback messages and were instructed to integrate the feedback and revise their initial work accordingly. Finally, in Week 4, participants analyzed a second authentic teaching scenario, again using cognitive load theory and the ICAP model, but this time without peer feedback, to assess their domain-specific reasoning performance. Instruments Feedback Integration. To capture participants’ integration processes, participants were asked to highlight the comments in the feedback they received from peers using different colors: green for similarities, yellow for additions, and red for contradictions between the two feedback messages. For this, students received a step-bystep instruction based on the Integrated Framework of Multiple Text Use (List & Alexander, 2019), demonstrated through a sample solution. First, participants read both feedback messages. Second, they marked similarities, additions, and contradictions using the color-coding scheme. Third, they revised their analyses based on these markings. Finally, in the fourth step, participants checked their markings again within their revised analyses. Objective Feedback Features. Each feedback message was analyzed in terms of content quality (accuracy and relevance), structural quality, and implementability. Content and structural quality were coded using a theory-driven framework (Hattie & Timperley, 2007; Nelson & Schunn, 2008; Yu & Schunn, 2023). Content quality  was assessed using the dimensions of accuracy and relevance (Nelson & Schunn, 2008). A feedback message was coded as accurate if its content was correct relative to the original case analysis,  and relevant if it addressed all major deficiencies in the analysis. Structural quality  was operationalized using eight criteria, such as comparisons between the initial solution and task requirements, feedback length, and level of detail. An exploratory factor analysis (based on the Kaiser-Guttman criterion, scree plot, and MAP test) indicated that all eight criteria loaded on a single factor. Factor scores derived from a confirmatory factor analysis were used to compute a composite structural quality score. Each feedback message was analyzed as a whole across all problem analyses. To validate the coding, two independent coders rated 10  % of the data, achieving sufficient to excellent interrater reliability (Gwet’s AC1  = 0.66–0.94). Discrepancies were resolved through discussion, and the remaining 90% of messages were divided among the coders. Feedback was also coded for implementability; 83 two coders independently rated 10% of the messages, achieving good agreement (Gwet’s AC1 = 0.83), after which the remaining data were equally divided and coded.

Perceived Feedback Quality. Perceived feedback quality was measured in terms of adequacy and subjective feedback quality. A dequacy (Peer 1: ω =.80; Peer 2: ω  =.82) was assessed using Strijbos et al.’s

(2021) Feedback Perception Scale on a nine-point Likert scale, covering three dimensions with three items each:

fairness (e.g., “I would be satisfied with this feedback”), usefulness (e.g., “I would consider this feedback useful”), and acceptance (e.g., “I would accept this feedback”). Subjective feedback quality (Peer 1: ω  =.71; Peer 2: ω  =.82) was measured using 18 items derived from Hattie and Timperley’s (2007) feedback dimensions, rated on a five-point Likert scale. This included Feed Up (e.g., “The feedback specifies the goal of the task”), Feed Back (e.g., “The feedback informs me whether I have met the requirements”), and Feed Forward (e.g., “The feedback indicates how I could expand my elaboration”).

Statistical analyses To address RQ1, we examined the number of student markings in each color category—similarities, additions, and contradictions —and conducted a hierarchical cluster analysis using the Ward method with Euclideandistances to identify distinct peer feedback  integration patterns. To further characterize differences between the identified patterns, we performed separate ANOVAs for the frequencies of similarities, additions, and contradictions, followed by post-hoc Tukey HSD tests. For RQ2, we conducted multino mial logistic regression analyses, using the objective feedback features (accuracy, relevance, implementability, structural feedback quality) and the two perceived feedback quality measures (adequacy, subjective feedback quality) as predictors, and the integration patterns as outcome variable. All analyses were performed in R [4.3.2], with the alpha level set to 5%.

To examine how objective feedback features (accuracy, relevance, implementability, structural feedback quality) and perceived feedback quality (adequacy, subjective feedback quality) relate to participants’ integration patterns (RQ2), we conducted a multinomial logistic regression, using the integration pattern as the criterion  variable and the feedback features as predictors. Computer-supported peer feedback is widely employed as a learning strategy in CSCL environments (e.g., Özbek et al., 2024; Zong et al., 2021). Nevertheless, questions remain regarding how and to what extent pre-service teachers integrate multiple peer feedback  messages (RQ1), and the influence of objective feedback features and perceived feedback quality on this integration (RQ2). In the following, we provide a summary and interpretation of our findings in relation to these two research questions. RQ1: Candifferent integration patterns be distinguished empirically? confirmation pattern, the conflict pattern, and the selective integration pattern. The confirmation pattern, which occurred most frequently, was characterized by numerous markings of similarities and additions between peer feedback messages. In contrast, the conflict pattern involved a higher frequency of contradictions identified by participants, while the selective integration pattern was marked by c omparatively few overall annotations. These patterns closely mirror the synthesis and reconciliation processes outlined by List and Alexander (2019). Specifically, learners following the confirmation pattern seem to construct a synthesis by emphasizing convergent and complementary elements while overlooking inconsistencies. Those adopting the conflict pattern, however, appear to focus on resolving contradictions between feedback messages. Learners displaying the selective integration pattern seem to engage with only a limited number of feedback elements, selectively synthesizing specific aspects of the input.

Recognizing these distinct integration patterns offers promising opportunities for developing adaptive scaffolds within CSCL environments that use peer feedback. For instance, learners who demonstrate a confirmation pattern might benefit from prompts encouraging them to critically examine discrepancies between the peer feedback messages. Such guidance could promote deeper engagement with feedback, potentially enhancing both feedback implementation and learning outcomes (Richter & Maier, 2017). Conversely, l earners following a conflict pattern could be supported with prompts emphasizing commonalities and complementarities between feedback and task solutions to mitigate possible frustration stemming from perceived criticism (Panadero et al., 2023).

Learners exhibiting a selective integration pattern may require special consideration. On the one hand, they might profit from more explicit scaffolding targeting all three integration processes, each of which can foster learning (Barzilai et al., 2018). On the other hand, it is also plausible that these learners already engage in mindful integration (Berndt et al., 2018), intentionally selecting only the feedback they deem most useful. In the latter case, additional prompting might not be necessary. Distin guishing between these possibilities would require further investigation, potentially employing qualitative approaches such as think-aloud protocols or eye-tracking (see van Meenen et al., 2023, for initial work in this area). Moreover, generative AI tools  could prove valuable for detecting integration patterns and providing real-time adaptive support during feedback reception (Bauer et al., 2025). Future studies should explore the potential of these tools to enhance learners’ engagement with peer feedback processes.

RQ2: Do objective feedback features and perceived peer feedback quality predict integration?

We also examined how the feedback participants received predicted the different integration patterns  we identified. In doing so, we distinguished between objective feedback features —namely accuracy, relevance, implementability, and structural feedback quality—and perceived feedback quality, which included adequacy and subjective feedback quality. Our analyses revealed significant associations between objective feedback features and the observed integration patterns. Specifically, the accuracy, relevance, and implementability of the feedback 86 emerged as predictors of how participants  engaged in integrating peer feedback. Participants who displayed the selective integration pattern tended to receive feedback that was highly accurate but less relevant and less implementable compared to those showing the other two patterns. One possible explanation for this finding comes from a motivational perspective: when feedback lacks relevance or cannot be readily implemented, learners might be less inclined to highlight or annotate it, perceiving it as unlikely to improve their original work during revision (Strijbos et al., 2021). Interestingly, however, the selective integration pattern was also associated with receiving more accurate feedback. Compared to the confirmation pattern and conflict pattern, this could suggest that learners  engaging in selective integration might be performing a form of mindful integration (Berndt et al., 2018). More accurate feedback may demand less cognitive effort to process, leading learners to agree with it and only mark these specific segments. In such cases, when the feedback aligns closely with learners’ own understanding, they might accept it implicitly and proceed with selective annotations.

Surprisingly, we found no significant relationship between perceived feedback quality and integration patterns. This was unexpected  to some extent, as prior studies suggest that learners’ agreement with and perceptions of feedback quality play a substantial role in whether they implement feedback (Strijbos et al., 2021). However, it is also consistent with existing research showing that the perceived feedback quality does not influence its implementation  (Huisman et al., 20 18). A potential explanation could  be that when objective feedback quality is consistently high, its influence may overshadow learners’ subjective perceptions, rendering perceived quality less relevant to how feedback is integrated (Nelson & Schunn, 2008; Gielen & De Wever, 2015). Overall, these findings emphasize the importance of maintaining high objective feedback quality in peer feedback contexts. When the accuracy, relevance, and implementability of feedback are ensured, learners may be more likely to engage productively in the integration process. Future research should further explore these mechanisms to clarify how different dimensions of feedback quality jointly shape feedback processing and integration behavior.

## Limitations

Of course, this study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the integration patterns identified in our study  may be closely tied to the specific learning content and context used. To make more generalizable claims about how learners integrate multiple peer feedback  messages, additional research is needed across different subject domains and learning environments to test the robustness and transferability of these patterns. Second, although participants were explicitly instructed to visualize their integration processes by using color-coded markings, it remains uncertain how diligently and consistently participants followed these instructions. To gain deeper insights, future research should aim to capture the underlying cognitive mechanisms that guide learners in identifying similarities, additions, and contradictions when processing feedback from multiple peers. Third, there are likely further influencing factors that were not addressed in this study but may play a crucial role in how learners integrate peer feedback. These could include variables such as learners’ comprehension of the feedback, their motivation, or individual characteristics that shape how they process and apply feedback information. Future work should therefore seek to identify and model such individual-level predictors to provide a more comprehensive understanding of feedback integration processes. Despite these limitations, the findings of this study provide empirical evidence that distinct patterns of peer feedback integration can be systematically identified in CSCL settings. Moreover, the results show that the likelihood of specific integration patterns is influenced by certain objective feedback features. This understanding offers valuable implications for the design of targeted, pattern-specific interventions to support learners in effectively integrating multiple peer feedback messages. Such interventions could be enhanced through generative AI tools or learning analytics systems capable of providing real-time, adaptive feedback support (Bauer et al., 2025). Exploring these possibilities in future research could significantly advance the practical application of peer feedback processes in digital learning environments.
