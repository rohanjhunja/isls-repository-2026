# Can Money Buy Better Judgment? High Incentives Backfire on Misinformation Discernment, But AI May Fix It

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Misinformation susceptibility is often attributed to motivational lapses (failure to engage in analytical reasoning) or information gaps (lack of necessary knowledge). While prior research has explored these factors separately, little is known about their interaction. This study examines how monetary incentives (to increase motivation) and AI-generated credibility analyses (to provide informational support) influence misinformation discernment. In a 2×3 factorial experiment (N=300), we find that high monetary incentives reduce discernment by increasing skepticism and false negatives. However, when paired with AI analyses, this effect is reversed, improving performance beyond baseline. AI access alone had no significant impact. Qualitative findings suggest trust in AI depends on self-reliance, skepticism, and alignment with personal judgment. These results highlight the need for interventions that balance motivation and cognitive support to enhance misinformation discernment and calls for more research to understand the cognitive and metacognitive processes during misinformation discernment.

## Introduction

Why do people fall for misinformation? Two camps of  thinking offer compelling explanations: motivational lapses and information gaps. The motivational lapse perspective, referred to as “lazy thinking” by Pennycook and Rand (2018), suggests that people fail to engage in analytical  reasoning not due to inability, but because news consumption is often a low-stakes, habitual activity where engagement and emotional resonance take precedence over accuracy (Oeldorf-Hirsch, 2018). Even individuals capable of discerning misinformation may neglect careful reasoning if they lack motivation. On the other hand, the information gap perspective argues that misinformation susceptibility stems from a lack of necessary knowledge or cognitive tools for accurate judgment. This framework underpins interventions such as media literacy prog rams, AI-driven fact-checking, and misinformation inoculation—each designed to supply users with relevant information to enhance discernment (Paris et al., 2022; Roozenbeek et al., 2022; Lin et al., 2022).

While these perspectives are typically studied  separately, their interaction remains underexplored (Pennycook et al., 2023). An intervention that provides motivation for discernment may still fail if individuals lack the knowledge to make accurate judgments, whil e knowledge-based interventions may be ineffective if people remain disinterested in accuracy of informat ion. To bridge this gap, we conducted a 2×3 factorial experiment (N=300) manipulating two key factors: monetary incentives, designed to encourage greater cognitive effort, and access to AI-generated credibility analyses, intended to scaffold informational support. Drawing from both behavioral science and learning sciences, our study examines whether extrinsic motivation (monetary stakes) and cognitive scaffolding (AI analysis) interact to enhance misinformation discernment or, conversely, introduce unintended cognitive biases. Our findings reveal th at high incentives alone impaired performance by in creasing skepticism and false negatives, but when paired with AI support, discernment improved b eyond baseline. These results highlight the complexity of designing effec tive misinformation interventions, emphasizing the need to balance motivation and knowledge-based strategies to improve critical thinking in digital environments. Theoretical frameworks Misinformation susceptibility has been linked to motivational laps es and information gaps. The former suggests that low-stakes, habitual news consumption leads people to p rioritize engagement over accuracy, even when capable of discerning misinformation (Pennycook et al., 2019). Some studies find that monetary incenti ves enhance analytical reasoning and misinformation discernment (Ronzani et al., 202 4), while others suggest they increase effort but not in cognitive reflection performance  (Enke et al., 2023). The information gap perspective suggests that misinformation  susceptibility stems from a lack of knowledge. With recent advancement in Large Language Models, AI-generated news analyses have become more viable (Quelle & Bovet, 2024; Costello et al. 2024). However, trust in AI-generated information is not guaranteed (Choung et al., 2023). If users d o not trust an AI system, they may not benefit from it, even if it provides valuable insights for identifying misinformation. While both motivational and knowledge-based interventions have been studied independently, their combined effects remain underexplored. Theoreticall y, monetary incentives could prompt more effortful reasoning, but it could also lead to overthinking, which may reduce their ability to discern misinformation or trust AI-analysis. Conversely, AI support may provide useful cogniti ve scaffolding, but only if users are sufficiently motivated to engage with it. This study examines ho w these factors interact in misinformation discernm ent, addressing the gap between extrinsic motivation and knowledge-based interventions. By testing monetary rewards and AI access both separately and in combination, we provide insights into whether these interventions reinforce or counteract each other, offering implications for  the design of misinformation interventions that ac count for both cognitive effort and trust in AI as a knowledge source.

## Methods

Experimental design A 2×3 factorial experiment  was conducted  to examine how monetary incentives and AI-generated credibility analyses influence misinformation discernment. 300 demographically representative participants were re cruited via Prolific (IRB24-0926). They were randomly assigned to one of six co nditions, varying by monetary stakes (normal vs. high) and AI-generated analysis (no AI, neutral AI, direct AI). In the normal stakes condition, participants receiv ed a fixed $1 USD payment, while in the high stakes condition, top scorers had a chance to win $250 USD. AI conditions varied in the level of analysis pro vided. In the no AI condition, participants saw headlines wit hout AI feedback. In the neutral AI condition, AI-generated responses provided an assessment of journalistic qu ality but did not offer a direct credibility judgme nt. In the direct AI condition, AI explicitly labeled each hea dline as credible or n ot. To ensure ecological validity, we d id not encourage nor discourage the participant from clicking and revealing the AI analysis. This design allowed us to examine not only whether AI helps but how the fr aming of AI-generated analysis affects discernment. AI analyses were pre-generated using GPT-4 and embedded in Qualtrics. When participants click the button under each headline, one of 10 pre-generated AI analysis will be revealed randomly, as shown in Figure 1.

## Measures

Misinformation discernment was assessed using the M isinformation Susceptibility Test (MIST-20) (Maertens et al., 2023), which consists of 10 real and 10 fake h eadlines. Participants classified each headline as real or fake, producing five key scores:  overall veracity discernment  (total scores), real news detection  (correct real news discernment), fake news detection  (correct fake news discernment), distrust (false negatives), and naïveté (false positives).

Pre-test measures included news consumption habits and prior experience with AI. Post-test questions assessed whether participants used AI-generated analyses, their reasons for engagement or  avoidance, and their trust in AI assessments.

## Discussions, implications, and future directions

This study demonstrates that high monetary incentiv es can hinder misinformation discernment by  reducing real news detection and increasing skepticism, as shown in the increase of false negatives. While increased motivations are often assumed to improve analytical reasoning, our results show that excessive or misplaced motivation can make individuals overly skeptical, leading them to distrust all news rather than improving accuracy. This aligns with research showing that misinformation intervent ions can reduce misperceptions while unintentionall y increasing skepticism toward factual information (H oes et al., 2024).  Further analysis should examine which questions were answered correctly without incentive s but misjudged under external motivation.  Some news headlines may function as cognitive traps—cases where the intuitive response is accurate, but engaging in effortful reasoning can prompt overthinking and ultimately impair judgment. To unlock these insights, future work should examine the cognitive and metacognitive processes involved. It may be helpful to view news consumption through the lens of self-regulated learning—focusing on how individuals monitor, evaluate, and adjust their reasoning under different motivational states (Greene and Azevedo, 2007). At the same time, AI analysis played an important r ole in counteracting this effect. When participants had access to AI, the negative impact of high incen tives on discernment was ameliorated. This suggests that AI may have helped participants in the high-incentive condition rectify their cognitive biases and return to more intuitive or rational judgments. However, AI was not universally adopted, and many p articipants ignored its recommendations due to skepticism or a preference f or their own judgment. This reflects a broader challenge — even accurate AI-generated analysis is only useful if people are willing to engage with it. Effective misinformation interventions will need to consider not just what information is provided, but how users interact with and trust AI systems.

From a learning sciences perspective, this study sheds light on how external scaffolds, such as AI analysis, interact with motivation to affect information processing and decision-making. Although news consumption differs from traditional learning environments, dis cerning misinformation relies on core learning proc esses, including sensemaking, metacognitive awareness, and  self-regulation. Our fin dings suggest that effective interventions must integrate both motivational and cognitive supports to enhance dis cernment. This underscores the valuable contributions of learning sciences in addressing challenges around news consumption. Several limitations should be noted. Monetary incen tives, while effective for manipulating stakes, may not fully replicate real-world motivations. The lack of real-time AI interaction may have influenced trust and engagement, and the use of Prolific respondents int roduces potential sampling biases. Additionally, ou r misinformation measure (MIST-20) lacked contextual cues such as news branding, limiting ecological validity. Future research should explore alternative motivation-altering interventions, such as reputation-based or social incentives. Studying real-time AI interactions—where users engage dynamically with AI —could provide a more realistic understanding of AI adoption in misinformation discernment. Finally, integrating multimodal and qualitative data would deepen insights into trust, skepticism, and long-term engagement with misinformation interventions.
