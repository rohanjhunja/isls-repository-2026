# Graphs Are Arguments: Modeling Choices, Belonging, and Critical Statistical Literacy in Climate Data Visualizations

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper explores how graphs operate as arguments through modeling choices including time window, scale, framing, aggregation, and audience. Drawing on contrasting climate visualizations built from the same data, it shows how representational choices sha pe interpretation and support different claims. It also argues that students were able to critique these choices because the classroom was organized as a Beloved Community, where belonging functioned as participation infrastructure for critical statistical literacy. Overview Public data visualizations are often treated as neutral displays of information, yet they are constructed through choices that shape what becomes visible, what counts as evidence, and what interpretations appear plausible. This paper argues that modeling choices such as time window, scale, framing, aggregation, and audience are central to how graphs make arguments (D’Ignazio & Klein, 2020; Weiland, 2017). It further argues that students were able to critique those choices because the classroom had been organized as a Beloved Community in which belonging functioned as participation infrastructure (Love, 2019). Using contrasting climate visualizations built from the same data but displayed across different time windows as a central example, this paper examines how representational decisions shape interpretation and how students moved from reading graphs to critiquing them. The analysis draws on classroom activity in which students engaged public data visualizations as objects of interpretation, comparison, and redesign. In particular, students examined contrasting climate graphs built from the same underlying data but displayed with different time windows, and they discussed how design choices shaped the claims each graph appeared to support. The analysis focuses on students’ discourse as they interpreted, questioned, and revised visualizations, with attention to the representational choices they named and the forms of critique they developed (Sfard, 2008). A central modeling contrast in this analysis is time window. When the same climate data are shown across a long historical span, the pattern can appear gradual and may invite familiar responses such as “Earth has changed before.” When the time window narrows to the recent past, the same underlying data can instead foreground the rate of increase and support a different interpretation, such as “Climate change is accelerating.” The point is not that one graph is true and the other false. Rather, representational choices shape salience. They influence what viewers notice, what kinds of arguments seem reasonable, and what conclusions become easier to draw. In this sense, graphs do not simply report reality. They organize attention and render some stories more visible than others (D’Ignazio & Klein, 2020). Time window is especially powerful because it often appears innocuous. Yet it works alongside other modeling choices such as scale, framing, aggregation, and attention to audience. Scale influences whether change looks dramatic or muted. Framing shapes how a graph is introduced, labeled, and interpreted in relation to a broader issue. Aggregation can either highlight patterns across groups or flatten important differences within them. Audience matters because graphs are built for someone, and design decisions anticipate particular readers, questions, and consequences. Taken together, these choices show that data visualization is not merely technical presentation. It is rhetorical and political work carried out through mathematical representation (Gutiérrez, 2013; Weiland, 2017). Within this framing, students’ discourse shifted from reading graphs as visual objects to critiquing graphs as designed arguments. Early responses focused on visible features of the display. Students noticed what a graph looked like, identified lines and labels, and described overall patterns. Over time, however, their talk became more analytic and more critical. They began to ask how the graph had been constructed, why certain choices had been made, and what those choices accomplished rhetorically. This movement from reading graphs to critiquing graphs matters because public visualizations often circulate as if they were transparent windows onto the world (D’Ignazio & Klein, 2020). In fact, they are composed artifacts shaped by inclusion, omission, emphasis, and intended audience. These shifts can also be understood as changes in classroom discourse, where students increasingly participated in forms of accountable and sociomathematical argumentation (O’Connor & Michaels, 1996; Yackel & Cobb, 1996). Early in discussion, students described graphs primarily in terms of visible features such as lines, labels, and overall shape. Later, they began to ask what the selected time span emphasized, why that span had been 2566 chosen, and how different viewers might interpret the same data differently. This shift marked a movement from noticing what a graph showed to interrogating how the graph had been built to make a claim. Two interrelated clusters of practice are especially important in this analysis. The first is argument-with-evidence. Students justified scale choices, explained uncertainty, and documented data provenance. In the climate example, this meant treating time window as part of the mathematical argument rather than as a cosmetic formatting decision. Students increasingly recognized that a graph’s domain, labels, and contextual features shape interpretation. They also came to see that uncertainty bands, source notes, and framing devices do mathematical work because they guide what counts as evidence and what can reasonably be inferred (Philip et al., 2016; Weiland, 2017). In this way, representation became a form of warrant rather thandecoration. The second cluster of practice involved audience and ethics reasoning. Students questioned categories, disaggregated groups, and critiqued representation choices in relation to who would read the graph and what consequences might follow from the way it was built. These moves extended beyond technical correctness alone. A graph can be statistically defensible and still be ethically misleading if its categories flatten important differences, if its framing obscures the stakes of the issue, or if its design invites viewers toward a simplistic conclusion. Students therefore learned to ask not only whether a display was mathematically acceptable, but also what story it told, what story it hid, and whose experiences were made visible or invisible. This shift positioned critique as both an intellectual and a civic practice (Philip et al., 2016; Weiland, 2024). These critical practices did not emerge in a vacuum. Belonging functioned as participation infrastructure, organized through connection, norms, and shared purpose. In this framing, belonging is not a soft supplement to rigorous mathematical work. It is part of the classroom architecture that enables students to speak publicly, challenge one another’s interpretations, and revise claims without disproportionate interpersonal risk. When students experience connection to one another, participate in negotiated norms, and share a collective purpose, critique can become a communal responsibility rather than a threat to status or safety. Belonging, in other words, supports the conditions under which critical reasoning becomes possible (Goodenow, 1993; Nasir et al., 2014; Walton & Cohen, 2011). This view is consistent with learning sciences work that treats participation, relationship-building, and social change as co-constitutive dimensions of learning (Bang & Vossoughi, 2016). This claim is especially important in work with public data visualizations. To question framing, audience, omission, or aggregation is to question decisions that are often treated as settled, objective, or authoritative. Students are more likely to engage in that questioning when the classroom makes room for uncertainty, disagreement, and accountability to others. In this sense, belonging does not sit beside critical statistical literacy as a separate concern. It helps constitute the social conditions through which such literacy candevelop. If educators want students to engage critically with graphs in civic life, instructional design must address not only technical tasks but also the relational and normative conditions that make critique possible (Goodenow, 1993; Walton & Cohen, 2011). This work contributes to research on critical statistical literacy by showing that critique of data visualization is not only a matter of technical competence, but also of the social and relational conditions that make participation possible (Weiland, 2017). It also contributes to learning sciences discussions of classroom discourse by arguing that belonging operates as infrastructure for critique. When students experience connection, shared norms, and collective purpose, they are better positioned to question authoritative-looking representations, make interpretive claims in public, and revise those claims in response to others (Bang & Vossoughi, 2016; O’Connor & Michaels, 1996; Yackel & Cobb, 1996). A key design principle emerging from this work is to show multiple representations of the same data. Representational contrast makes hidden assumptions visible because students can compare what changes in a graph and what changes in interpretation. In the climate example, holding the data constant while changing the time window exposed how design choices shape the story a graph appears to tell. More broadly, such comparison can support critical statistical literacy by inviting students to ask: What story does this graph tell? What story is hidden? Which modeling choices shaped this interpretation? These questions matter beyond a single classroom or a single climate example. They position students to participate more critically in a world saturated with charts, dashboards, and politically consequential data displays (Gutiérrez, 2013; Weiland, 2017, 2024). Taken together, this paper advances a compact but significant claim. Graphs shape arguments. Students can learn to identify and critique that shaping. Belonging is one of the conditions that makes such critique possible in classroom life. When learners com pare representations, attend to audience and ethics, and work within a classroom organized for belonging, they can move from consuming visualizations to interrogating them.
