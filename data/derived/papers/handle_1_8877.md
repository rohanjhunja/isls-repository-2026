# Illuminating Supports for Representations of Science Through Examining Modes of Reproduction

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Knowing how to move towards scientific literacy for all requires an understanding of how science is represented within classrooms. Patterns in science representation range across a continuum from science as isolated truths to an integration of knowledge, practice, and conceptualization that creates holistic science representations. This action research case study examines science representations across this continuum within a science course for elementary education majors at a minority serving institution and how these practices of representation are supported across multiple levels of the educational system. Findings presented summarize overall course patterns and supports and give details of a pattern that leads to suggestions for reform efforts towards the goal of scientific literacy for all. The need to look beyond singlelevel changes is evidenced by how supports acted at a multitude of levels and how supports that sustained holistic representations of science interplayed with those that supported non-holistic representations.

## Introduction

As science education works towards the goal of scientific literacy for all, it is important to examine how science is represented in classrooms, but simply characterizing science representations within a classroom is insufficient. There is a need to understand how and why science classroom practices beneficial to student learning are or are not implemented (Gardner et al., 2021). It is only by understanding how content is represented within a web of systematic supports that lasting change can be enac ted (Cobb et al., 2009). This requires knowing how those representations are supported both within the classroom and across the complex, multi-leveled education system that situates the classroom.

In this study, I examine science representations w ithin a science content course for future elementar y educators studying at a minority serving institution and preparing to teach in urban schools. I ask two questions:

(1) How did practices of science representation manifest in this course, and (2) how are patterns in these practices

supported in a way that sustains them across the course? To do this, I use methods based on the MoRe Institutional Framework to engage in a multi-leveled analysis of the supports that sustain both ideal and non-ideal practices (Anderson & Colyvas, 2021; Zeller, Under Review).

Parts of Science Lederman and Lederman (2020) discuss that there are  three parts of science that are integral to develo ping scientific literacy: scientific knowledge (SK), sci entific inquiry (SI), and the nature of scientific knowledge (NOSK). SK is the facts and knowledge that have res ulted from science (e.g. Newton’s Laws, the Big Ban g Theory, and the density of water). SI is the practi ces of scientists, the process by which SK is creat ed (e.g. experimentation, data analysis, and analyzing claims). NOSK is characteristics that are inherent to SK and set SK apart from opinions and unsubstantiated claims (e.g. empirically based, tied to human creativity and culture, and changing/tentative). In order for someone to be sci entifically literate, they need to have an understa nding of all three parts of science.

In this study, I operationalize “holistic scien ce representation” to mean a collection of representations that showcase all three parts of science. This holi stic representation is one end of a continuum, the other end of which has science as isolated facts that are taken as total truth. Along this continuum are various gr adations of non-holistic representations that combine parts of science, such as SK and SI coupled together but not  showing NOSK within classroom materials and activities.

It is important to note that looking at science re presentations in the classroom is only an initial s tep towards creating learning environments that develop scientific literacy. Specifically, research shows that students do not learn NOSK through simple exposure and that learning NOSK requires explicit instruction (Lederman & Abell, 2014; Marchlewicz & Wink, 2011). Looking at representations, however, lays the groundwork by looking at how NOSK is present within classrooms before spe cific efforts have been made to implement explicit instruction on NOSK.

ICLS2022 Proceedings 83 © ISLS MoRe Institutional Framework In order to expand my view beyond simply what is happening in the classroom, I utilized the MoRe Institutional Framework (Anderson & Colyvas, 2021). This framewor k is rooted in institutional theory, starting with Jepperson’s (1991) definition of institutions as continually reproduced “social patterns” that are sustained by “selfactivating social processes” (pp. 144-145). Anderson and Colyvas (2021) discuss these self-activating processes as modes of reproduction and how they manifest in observable ways at multiple levels of complex systems. They present categories of modes of reproduction that the literature shows are prominent in education, including policy, framing, stakeholder interests, learner identities, roles, assessment, routines, and professional norm s. Modes of reproduction are the key to understanding why a pattern of practice continually occurs within a classroom. What makes the MoRe Institutional Framework powerf ul for investigating classroom practice is looking beyond a single, idealized pattern of practice by examining modes of reproduction supporting both ideal and nonideal practices. These practices are often conflicting. Practices do not often manifest in binaries, one practice or the other. Rather, it is better to conceptualize th e range of these practices as a continuum that crea tes space to investigate complexity. Looking at how institutiona lized patterns of practice manifest along a continu um illuminates the interplay between opposing practices. It is possible that one practice may even act to support the very practice it is trying to replace (Anderson, 2017).

## Methods

This work is part of a larger study conducted within the methodologies of case study and action research (Zeller, Under Review). Case study effectively allowed me to  look in depth into the patterns of practice within  a single classroom and develop rich, detailed descriptions (Yin, 2006). Utilizing action research, through having the author as part of the instructional team, afforded access and insider understanding of situated processes and evidence that are not available within observational studies (Roth, 2001). Case study and action research methodologies require specific tools for data analysis. In this study, th e MoRe Institutional Framework provides those methodological tools, including categories and coding strategies that were used to create the case descriptions. Within this paper, I pay attention to the particular ways that the MoRe Institutional Framework facilitates description of practices of science representation and how these practices were supported.

## Context and participants

The course examined in this study was a science content course for elementary education majors during the Fall 2020 semester. The course ran a single lecture sect ion of 72 students and three lab sub-sections synch ronously online. The course is a required class for the major in elementary education, which focuses on preparing students to teach in urban schools, at a large, R1 universit y in the Midwestern United States. The university i s classified as a minority serving institution. The course covered physics, space, and earth sciences topics and is one of two required science content courses.

The course team consisted of two co-instructors and  two teaching assistants (TAs) and is traditionally taught by one professor from the physics department and one from the earth sciences department. In the semester of the study, the earth sciences instructor was the professor who had taught the class in years past, and the author, a graduate student in learning sciences with six years’ experience teaching high school physics, filled the role of physics instructor of the course. For clarity within this paper, I refer to myself in the first person as a researcher and in third person as an instructor. A graduate student from the physics department was the TA for one of the lab sections while a graduate student from earth sciences was the TA for the other two sections. The course is unique within the university as it i s taught by staff from two separate departments of the College of Liberal Arts and Sciences and is a requi red course for undergraduate students within the Co llege of Education. Each co-instructor earns a full course teaching credit for the course, regardless of co-instructing. It is extremely unusual for a course to be co-taught like this, especially across departments at the university. The TAs for the course are regularly hand-picked, before other appointments are made in the departments, to select graduate students who are interested in teaching and fit the  role of working with education majors. One artifac t of these special conditions is that the instructors have more freedom and flexibility, which leads to the instructors regularly using the course as a place for innovation.

## Data collection and analysis

Data analyzed for this study included course materials as well as reflections and interviews from the course team members. The course materials included the slide de cks for both lecture and lab, activity documents and poll questions used during lecture, lab assignment docum ents (prelab assignments, instructions, activities,  and conclusion questions), and summative portfolio asse ssments. Interviews were semi-structured and focuse d on ICLS2022 Proceedings 84 © ISLS individuals’ experiences within the semester. The course team reflections were completed before each interview and used to inform some of the interview conversation. I conducted three rounds of reflection/interview with each other team member. A separate research associate conducted two rounds of reflection/interview with me, as the physics instructor. The data was initially grouped into two strands: practices data and supports data. Utilizing the structure of the MoRe Institutional Framework to il luminate the complex web of supports and patterns i n the classroom, I empirically investigated both patterns of practice and modes of reproduction (Zeller, Under Review). The analysis of the data for practices focused on patterns of science representation in the course. Patterns of science representation variation by discipline Here, I give a detailed account of how science representation varied by discipline within the course structures of lecture, lab, and assessment.

The earth and space science units included more NOSK than physics units. In lecture, physics units mostly presented science as just SK and SI while the earth and space units represented science more holistically through the additional inclusion of NOSK. All units included representations of SK and SI, but two of the three physics units lacked representations of NOSK in lecture. Only the electricity physics unit represented NOSK significantly, by discussing electricity withi n the socially-embedded context of how electricity is embedded within everyday life and the theory-laden way historical decisions (e.g. the direction of conventional current flow) affect how we interact with and study  electricity. The earth and space units, in contras t, all represented NOSK. Earth and space units consistently made ties to the history of science and the human aspect of theory development. This represented science as inc luding human creativity and being theory-laden. The  space and geology units also strongly represented science  as changing/tentative and empirical because they d iscussed historical shifts in theory: heliocentric to geocentric and continental drift to plate tectonics. Overall, the earth and space unit lectures provided more holistic science representations by including more representations o f NOSK along with the SK and SI that was well represented across all units, while physics had fewer NOSK representations. For the labs, all units, across earth, space, and physics, represented NOSK. This was directly tied to the routine lab structure. Labs that specifically repre sented NOSK the most were the labs on plate tectoni cs (in the geology unit) and climate. The plate tectonics lab repeatedly represented science as changing/tentative, theoryladen, and empirical through the discussion of the shift from continental drift theory to plate tectonic theory. The climate lab represented science as empirical through the analysis of longitudinal data sets to identify changes in ICLS2022 Proceedings 87 © ISLS atmospheric carbon and global temperatures. Overall, the labs more consistently represented NOSK across units than lecture, but the earth and space units still had more representations than the physics units. For the summative unit assessments, SK and SI were generally well represented but NOSK was minimally represented across units in both disciplines. The earth and space assessments still had a slightly higher representation of NOSK through socially embedded questions and empirical data sets provided for interpretation. The first two physics assessments included unspecif ic connections to everyday life and analysis of a p rovided data set, providing weaker, but still present NOSK representations. The last physics assessment, howev er, completely lacked representations of NOSK and inclu ded the least SK and SI of any unit assessment. Thi s assessment covered the shortest unit and focused more on reflection on learning over the whole semester than the content of the final unit. Even with the general la ck of NOSK representation across all summative assessments, the earth and space assessments still generally rep resented it more than the physics assessments, matc hing the pattern seen across lecture and lab.

Supports for science representation variation by discipline To understand why the difference in science representations between disciplines persisted across the semester, I developed an account of the supports that reproduce d this pattern. More holistic science representatio n in earth and space units than in physics units was supported  by modes of reproduction at the individual, course, and scientific-field levels. At the individual level, the framing and roles assumed by members of the instructional team shaped the focus of various units. Disciplinary professional norms drove different practices within each unit, and course-level policy in the form of learning goals further connected course practices to professional norms. The general lack of NOSK in physics was connected t o a strong focus on SK and SI. This focus was supported at the individual level by how the physic s instructor’s training in Modeling Instruction (We lls et al.,

1995) for high school physics permeated their teaching of this course. This pedagogy focuses on students learning

through developing and using scientific models and shaped the physics instructor’s framing of student learning goals. The physics instructor reflected on how Modeling Instruction shaped their instructional choices: I really felt strongly that, um, the tools of modeling that I've learned from Modeling Instruction and using visual models to help build conceptual un derstanding of the concepts is more important than ‘plug and chug and get an answer’… S o I tried really hard to focus on these models, using graphs and force diagrams and bar charts and different semi quantitative models, to talk about the concepts of motion force and energy (Physics Instructor, Interview 1). This meant that the physics units, especially the m otion unit, were more focused on the integration SK  and SI, which is evidenced by the strong SK and SI represen tation across the first unit. Although Modeling Ins truction does not specifically exclude NOSK, it caused the physics instructor to focus more specifically on inclusion of SI. This focus also likely contributed to the absence of socio-cultural connections because of the difficulty students had in mastering the graphical and mathematical models of motion, leaving less space for NOSK representation through rich, socially embedded context. The way th e physics instructor implemented Modeling Instructi on supported rich representations SK and SI, to the neglect of NOSK. Supports for the inclusion of NOSK in earth and space units came from the individual level through the earth sciences instructor seeing their role as a ge neral education instructor as helping students buil d a general appreciation and understanding of science. They talked about this in an interview: A lot of [the goal of the course] for me is just pa ssing on interests in science and excitement about science and being able to share that with their students… I think [this course] is trying to cultivate interest and feeling more confident in th eir science skills (Earth Science Instructor, Interview 1).

This understanding of their role helped reproduce the inclusion of NOSK representation through connecting with students’ interests with highly contextualized examples in the earth and space units, including the as sessments. This supported socially embedded science representations across earth and space units. The earth sciences TA’s individual-level framing of science as focused on understanding the mechanistic explanations for phenomena supported inclusion of NOSK. This TA helped design the earth and space labs and therefore had more impact on those units than the p hysics units. They said that for lab redesign and i nstruction they, “wanted to communicate these mechanisms and such, and that being a big point, but the practice of actually trying to get them to understand and check-in with them as we go” (Earth Science Teaching Assistant, Interview 2). The earth sciences TA also had a background in planetary science and was a guest lecturer during t he space ICLS2022 Proceedings 88 © ISLS unit. Therefore, their framing especially reproduced the NOSK representation of science as empirical in the space unit’s discussion of helio-vs. geocentrism through the focus on evidence that supports each theory. The specific content of this course, which created the base structure for inclusion of SK, SI, and/or NOSK, was reproduced by the semi-formal policy of course learning goals. These goals, however, are tied to higher-level traditions of what is taught in introductory earth science and physics courses. Although the specific content of this course is also influenced by state-level, K-12 science teaching standards, these too are descended from the fieldlevel traditions of Physics and Earth Sciences. The professional norms of each discipline reproduced variation in how each unit represented science. These norms include both field-knowledge development and traditions of what is included in introductory courses.

Within the earth and space units of the course, professional norms reproduced representations of NOSK through the examination of theory change and the ex amination of large, longitudinal data sets. The per vasive focus on theory shifts in the earth and space units was supported by professional norms at the scientific-field level, reproducing representations of science as changing/tentative. Classic introductory earth and space topics, include models of the solar system and the earth’s crust. These two topics are imbued with NOSK representation because of the major historical shifts in theory related to them. This is especially true for the earth’s crus t since the shift from continual drift theory to plate tectonics theo ry happened quite recently. Science was also repres ented as empirical through the traditions of using large, longitudinal data sets to analyze climate change. The nature of the content traditional to earth sciences courses supported pervasive representation of NOSK. Introductory physics courses traditionally focus on motion (including kinematics, dynamics, and energy). Within the scope of an introductory course, the the ory for motion has not recently changed. Additional ly, this content is often taught in an algorithmic, fact-based fashion, which focuses on computing the correct answer to a question. These traditional approaches are SK forward, neglecting the SI and NOSK opportunities. The physics instructor’s framing through the lens of Modeling I nstruction contributed to more representation of SI, but as previously discussed, not NOSK. Although many reforms (e.g. Modeling Instruction) try to disrupt these patterns, field-level professional norms are inherently insti tutionalized and taken for granted as something tha t just is, which makes them strong modes of reproduction.

## Discussion and Implications

Examination of the findings in this study illuminat es information about how holistic and non-holistic science representations are reproduced within this classroom and points towards efforts that could help reform s aiming for scientific literacy. The interplay between mode s of reproduction for holistic representations and those that only reproduced inclusion of SK and SI and how these supports manifest at many levels show that reform efforts need to look beyond just what the teacher is doing, or other single-level changes. Within the overall course findings summarized in F igure 2 and Table 2, NOSK is the part of science that was least represented and reproduced. Modes of repr oduction influencing how science was represented in  the course spanned levels from individual to scientific field, emphasizing the need to consider action beyond teachers. However, at the individual level, framing and role based modes of reproduction acted through the cours e team, emphasizing that although there is a need to consider the wider system, the instructors are still central to classroom practice.

The findings also show examples of how this course uniquely involves cross-department and college collaboration which affects what policies and professional norms reproduce classroom practices. Specifically, the freedom and flexibility within this course for inst ructors to innovate creates a diverse web of modes of reproduction that affect practice.

When looking at the specific example of NOSK being  represented more within earth and space units than within physics units, more detailed conclusions emerge. The specific background and training of the physics instructor provided the example of a specific past reform effort, in the form of Modeling Instruction, permeating into a space other than the original intended setti ng based on instructor transfer. This transfer, how ever, was constricted by the new classroom environment, causing the instructor to focus narrowly on specific goals. In this case, the focus on integrating SI and SK meant that NOSK was not supported within the physics units, especially the motion unit.

The split between earth and space and physics units ’ representation of NOSK also brought to light the deep institutionalization of professional norms fro m the different science fields. Things that are mos t taken for granted can most stand in the way of reform. For ex ample, one implication of this study is that, after  the investigated semester was over, I discussed possible changes in the motion unit with the regular physics instructor of the course and we decided to cut some very traditional content in order to be able to make other content more accessible. This challenged the professional norms of introductory physics content and opened the door  for changes in classroom practice.

ICLS2022 Proceedings 89 © ISLS The variation between the science disciplines also exemplifies that the specific classroom context and goals must be considered when formulating plans for implementing reforms. What comes easily in one discipline may be difficult in another. This pairs with the difficulty in transferring Modeling Instruction pedagogy between educational settings (i.e. high school physics to an undergraduate, interdisciplinary science course) to show how influential the specifics of the classroom and surr ounding system are to classroom practice. This show s how important it is to use research methods, such as the ones used in this study, to deeply look at what i s supporting classroom practice and understand the complexity of implementing change. Together, the findings suggest that, in order for t his course to implement reforms towards the goal of scientific literacy for all, particular attention is needed towards consistency in the representation of NOSK across disciplines. Possible actions include writing formal course goals and learning objectives that connect to NOSK, especially in the physics units by showcasing how h istorical work by scientists exemplifies NOSK, then examining content coverage and how this aligns with  course goals. These actions would attempt to estab lish course-level policies that balance out the lack of higher-level policy support of NOSK inclusion and create space for challenging taken-for-granted field-level professional norms. These findings also have important implications for  wider study of the complex, interlacing web that institutionalizes practices in the classroom. The c urrent findings provide insight into supports of sc ience representations manifesting on the timescale of a semester, but can also inform conceptualization and investigation of issues outside of the single course or semester.  Changing the unit of analysis from within one seme ster to looking at change across school years would provide  a different view with different affordances toward s wider change. What is considered unchanging or transient depends on the timescale of analysis (Lemke, 2000). Therefore, within a longer-term study, multiple snapshots of a longitudinal reform effort focusing on patterns and modes of reproduction could result in better unders tanding of the process of constructing and deconstr ucting modes of reproduction in service of lasting change.
