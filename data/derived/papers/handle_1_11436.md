# The Resilient and Transformative Journey of Teachers Through the Pandemic and Beyond

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
The COVID-19 pandemic presented unprecedented challenges for teachers, demanding adaptability, resilience, and growth. This study explores the experiences of secondary science teachers from a New England state, focusing on how they navigated internal and external challenges during remote instruction. Using the integrated framework of Teacher Resilience and Transformative Learning, we examine how educators relied on personal resources (mindset, optimism, and empathy), contextual supports (relationships with students, colleagues, and school leaders), and pedagogical strategies (streamlining and outsourcing), while critically reflecting to achieve transformative growth. Their narratives, highlight the interplay of resilience and transformation in adapting to crises. This study offers a novel framework to understand teachers’ experiences in challenging contexts—such as socio-political turmoil and natural disasters—while contributing nuanced insights to existing literature on teacher retention, and well-being. Caution is drawn to use this framework for action, not inaction.

## Introduction

The onset of the COVID-19 pandemic in March 2020 brought about unprecedent ed challenges for educators worldwide. Teachers across different contexts grappled with unfamiliar demands and untested solutions, exposing vulnerabilities in educational systems while showca sing educators' capacity for adaptation and innovat ion (Koh & Daniel, 2022). Although we are now beyond the immediate crisis o f the pandemic, an important question remains: What have we learned from the COVID-19 experience that can extend beyond t he moment and inform both research and practice on how teachers respond to crises? Teaching during crises is not new. Around the globe, educators regularly contend with challenges arising from socio-political upheavals, natural disasters, and resource scarcity. For instance, in regions affecte d by war, political instability or natural disaster  teachers often demonstrate exceptional resilience a nd adaptability to maintain access to education (Sharifian & Kennedy, 2019; Toole & Friesen, 2016). The past decade has witnessed numerous global disruptions,  such as the Syrian civil war, the Ebola  outbreak in West Africa, and devastating earthquakes in Nepal (World Bank, 2020). Curren t conflicts such as the war in Ukraine, the crisis in  Gaza, and ongoing violence in parts of Africa, alongside natu ral disasters like the recent floods in Pakistan, c ontinue to disrupt educational systems worldwide (United Natio ns, 2023). These events emphasize the need to learn from how teachers respond to crises. In this study, we b uild on that need by developing an asset-based framework to understand their experiences under such conditions.

The concept of teacher resilience—defined as the capacity to thrive in the face of ad versity—has been widely studied in the context of education (Beltman et al., 2011; Mansfield et al., 2016). Resilience is not simply about survival; it is about finding ways to adapt and thrive despite difficulties (Beltman, 2015).  This adaptive process often involves critical reflection, which can lead to growth. Transformative Learning Theory, introduced by Mezirow (1997), builds on this idea, highlightin g how critical reflection enables individuals to qu estion their assumptions, transform their perspectives, and grow in the midst  of challenging experiences. In this study, we bring these two frameworks—resilience and transformative learning—together. By combining these perspectives, we aim to examine not only how teachers adapted to adversity but also how their experiences led to growth and a reimagining of their perspective about teaching and learning (see Framework section for details). Adopting a resilience-transformative lens is particularly important becau se traditional studies on hardship often risk portraying educators as passive  victims of systemic failures. For example, Payne  (1996) provided a critical lens to understand the challenges faced in low-income schools; however, it has also been critiqued for reinforcing deficit-based narratives rather than highlighting the agenc y and strengths of individuals navigating challenging contexts. In contrast, view ing teachers’ experiences through a resilience-transformative lens acknowledges both the reality of adversity and the potential for growth. This framework helps balance realism with hope, offering a nuanced view of teacher exper iences. We also caution against undermining the systemic issues that exist in our current education systems in the name of resilience or transformation. Resilient-Transformative framework In the context of teacher education, resilience has been defined as “the capacity to adapt” (Beltman, 2015; Beltman et al., 2011), with literature identifying three cr itical contributors: personal resources, contextual  supports, and strategies (Mansfield et al., 2016). Alongside this, Mezirow’s (1997) Transformative Learning Theory o ffers a framework for understanding how adults critically reflect on unexpected challenges or disorienting dilemmas and reframe their perspectives, leading to personal growth. Combining these theories provides a comprehensive  lens to explore how teachers navigated the challenges of  teaching during the COVID-19 pandemic. Next, we will describe components of resilience and transformative learning in details. Resilience Personal resources form the foundation of resilienc e, with literature consistently highlighting the importance of motivation, self-efficacy, optimism, and emotional strength  (Mansfield et al., 2016). Intrinsic motivation, often linked to a strong sense of moral purpose, has been identified as a significant protective factor, buffering teachers against stress and burnout (Day, 2014; Gu & Day, 20 07). Optimism, including academic optimism, further contributes to resilience by fostering a positive outlook and hope for the future (Day, 2014). Additionally, empathy and emotional intelligence enable teachers to conne ct deeply with their stu dents and navigate emotional challenges effectively (Jennings et al., 2011). These elements align closel y with our research goal of understanding how teachers adapted and thrived during the pandemic. The resilience of educators is also deeply embedded  within their relational and environmental contexts. Research underscores the importance of supportive relationships among teachers, school leaders, and students as the base of relational resilience (Le Cornu, 2013). Strong relationships with leaders (Gu & Day, 2007), collaboration with peers (Hong, 2012), and a suppor tive school culture marked by trust and shared deci sionmaking (Gu, 2014) collectively strengthen teachers'  resilience during challenging times.  This element of our framework emphasizes how these contextual supports create a web of resources that sustain teachers during times of crisis.

Teachers employ numerous strategies to navigate adversity and maintain well-being. Research identifies problem-solving, help-seeking, and self-care as key strategies in building resilience (Le Cornu, 2013). Teachers who engage in professional learning, set boundaries  to avoid burnout, and practice mindfulness exhibit  greater resilience and adaptability (Tait, 2008). In the co ntext of teaching during the pandemic, strategies s uch as streamlining instructional objectives, utilizing creativity in assessment, and leveraging external r esources highlight the practical ways educators addressed challenges. Transformative learning Mezirow’s (1997) transformative learning theory emphasizes that adults grow most profoundly when confronted with disorienting dilemmas that challenge their established ways of thinking. Inspired by Paulo Freire’s (1970) notion of conscientization, transformative learning emphasizes self-awareness, autonomy, and the ability to act on reflective insights (Mezirow, 1997; Mezirow, 2018). For teachers, the pandemic presented a unique co ntext where rapidly changing norms required constant reassessment of practices and beliefs. By critically reflecting on these challenges, educators not only adapted but al so transformed their  views on certain facets of teaching and assessment, highlighting the potential for growth in adversit y. Our framework integrates three factors—personal resources, contextual resources, and strategies —that contribute to resilience with elements of transformative learning (disorienting dilemma, critical reflection, and perspective change). ## Research design and context

In this study we interviewed 10 secondary science teachers (6 male, 4 female) in a New England state, from 10 different schools. Participants were recruited using convenie nce sampling, followed by purposeful sampling to ensure diverse representation. Data was collected d uring the summer of 2020, shortly after the teacher s’ initial experience with remote teaching du ring the COVID-19 pandemic. Semi-structured interviews, conducted via Zoom and lasting between 40 minutes and 2 hours, ca ptured their reflections on teaching strategies, as sessment adaptations, and navigating challenges during this unprecedented period (for details see Raj, 2022). This study builds on our earlier work that emphasized contrasts in teachers’ experiences (Raj et al., 2024) by shifting focus to the similarities that emerged across contexts. By framing their narratives through a resiliencetransformative perspective, we aim to provide insights for researchers and professional development practitioners while challenging deficit-based narratives. Thematic analysis, guided by Braun and Clarke’s (20 06) framework, combined inductive coding (allowing themes to emerge naturally) with deductive refinement informed by the resilience-transformative framework. This iterative approach p rovided a rich understanding of how teachers leveraged personal resources, contextual supports, and adaptive strategies while engaging in transform ative reflection.

## Findings

Resilience via personal resources One teacher, Luke exemplified the power of a resilient mindset by having conversations with his students during the pandemic about the importance of tenacity and g rit. He reflected, “We talked about, you know, tenacity and grit with our kids. And I think what helped me the most was saying, you know, I'm gonna make up my min d that I'm gonna get this…I think it's probably the one thing that got me through.” This perspective highlights how Luke leaned on his personal resolve to create a sense of certainty in a situation that felt overwhelmingly uncertain. His emphasis on “grit and tenacity” illustrates that he values them as traits to deal with challenging situations. Luke’s deliberate focus on mindset aligns with well-recognized attributes of resilience, such as persis tence and determination.

Similarly, Hazel’s resilience was deeply rooted in empathy for her students, and her ability to prioritize their emotional needs over rigid academic goals. Reflecting on the early days of the transition to remote teaching, she explained, “I think I was trying to keep it as normal as possib le. Yeah, pretty quickly, my priorities shifted to the social emotional, like well-being of the students, because I knew what it was doing to me. And the weight that it felt like…it became a lot less about covering the same material that I would have normally and more about, I'm trying to get them engaged, maybe get them to smile.” Hazel’s reflection reveals how empathy guided her decisionmaking, allowing her to adapt her teaching practice s to meet the immediate needs of her students. Reco gnizing the emotional toll of the pandemic on herself and h er students, she shifted her focus from a cademic rigor to fostering engagement and well-being. These stories of Luke and Hazel highlight the essential role of personal resources in resilience. Whether through mindset  or empathy, these teachers drew on their inner stre ngths to navigate the challenges of remote teaching and prioritize what truly mattered. Resilience via contextual resources Building meaningful connections with some students became a source of resilience for many tea chers during remote learning. As the separation of physical spac e altered the traditional teacher-student dynamic, some educators found new ways to connect on a more personal level. Lydia shared, "In the Zoom meetings, by doing a one on one, by doing the office hours, I actually feel that there are some students that I got to know better. I know everybody's cat and dog now…on a personal level, I think I got to know them a little better.”  Lydia’s reflection highlights the relational depth that emerged with s ome students during the pandemic as boundaries betw een the classroom and home dissolved. These moments reflect how she used the resilience-building potential of teacherstudent relationships, where personal connection becomes a source of strength during challenging times. Collegial relationships offered a support for teachers navigating the overwhelming demands of  remote instruction. For many, collaboration with peers was  not just about sharing resources but also about em otional solidarity. Lisa reflected on how these connections became essential, “I think it was realizing that we couldn't stay in, or I couldn't stay in my bubble, that I really needed to reach out to my colleagues in the school …everyone really willing to share and, and be there for each other to support, our staff really, I think came together, um, and showed the best of themselves, to help our kids.” Lisa’s narrative highlights the importance of teacher-to-teacher relationships in navigating the demands of teaching  in a difficult situation. Her observation that the  staff “came together and showed the best of themselves” emphasi zes how collaboration and mut ual support became sources of collective resilience.  These stories from Lydia and Lisa illustrate the profound impact of contextual resources on teacher resilience.

Resilience via strategies Navigating the abrupt transition to remote learning, Mike focused on distilling his teaching approach to its essentials, finding resilience in simplifying both his teaching and assessment practices. Reflecting o n this adaptation, he shared: "So, I feel like the assessment the way that I figured out how to assess in the short time that I had to, you know, change everything, really made me look at what is the core vital learning..so, the teaching practice became very streamlined…Which then, of course, as you know, completely manipulated the assessment." Mike’s story highlights how resilience often involves i dentifying and prioritizing what truly matters. His focus on "core vital content" reflects a deliberate choic e to not adhere to traditional objectives  under the given time constraints. Mike exemplified resilience by utilizing the strategy of recalibrating priorities. Transformation via reflection For Hank, the pandemic teaching experience mirrored the ste ep learning curve of a first-year teacher, a period defined by uncertainty and rapid adaptation. He des cribed, "The learning curve was pretty steep, getting going with this. And I equated to the same experience tha t first-year teachers have …my advice tonew teachers was always strive for progress, not perfection …Because…the workload is tremendous…And I felt like all of us were back as first year teachers again, not quite starti ng from scratch, but pretty close."  Hank’s reflection reveals a unique perspective on the pandemic as a universal “reset” for educators, similar to the challenges faced by f irstyear teachers. His approach to value “progress, not perfection” signals a shift in minds et, valuing adaptability, and incremental improvement over unattainable stand ards. This highlights the importance of critical thinking in embracing change to gain new perspectives on existing challenges. While we have presented the four pillars of the resilient-transformative framework—personal resources, contextual resources, strategic adaptation, and transformative learning—through separate teacher narratives, a single educator’s story can often reflect all four dimensions. Heather’s account is one such example. Her critical reflection is evident in the way she rethought unif orm instruction after working with a struggling AP  student: “One of my AP students, this was a disaster for him to have to be online… And so, I ended up coming up with a completely different semester for him… And it was b eautiful. It worked perfectly… That showed me the opportunities that I might miss, if I'm really stic king with keeping everything the same for everybody … I think I will be more comfortable making bigger shifts.” In addition, her resilience via personal resources emerged through her intentional focus on joy during a difficult time: “Look for moments where you or your students can feel really just excited, full of wonder…like, really hang on to those things, and kind of let go of so many of the other things.” As a form of strategic adaptation, she shifted from making everything herself to using existing materials: “There’s a lot of good materials that are made by other people... I definitely am learning to use some things more than just make it… That was a helpful thing.” These practices were also embedded in a broader context of relational support, as she prioritized student needs and individualized  learning over rigid curriculum. Together, these el ements demonstrate how Heather’s story weaves together res ilience and tra nsformation in response to the complex demands of teaching during crisis.

## Discussion and conclusion

The experiences of teachers like  Mike, Lisa and Luke illustrate how the COVID-19 pandemic prompted both resilience and transformation. Drawing on personal resolve, relational support, and strategic adaptation, teachers navigated uncertainty with deliberate optimism, col laboration, and streamlined practices.  These examples align with global findings on teacher resilience (Fox & W alter, 2022; Marenyenya, 2023; Taylor, 2024). At the same time, teachers like Hank and Heather experienced de eper shifts in mindset. Hank compared the crisis to  a “firstyear t eacher” reset, and Heather reimagined her assessment and instructional flexibility after rede signing a semester for one student. These stories echo resear ch showing how disorienting dilemmas —such as the pandemic—can spark transformative learning (Shaik, 2023; Young et al., 2022). Our study integrates resilience and transformative lear ning to offer a more comprehensive account of how educators respond to crises. Rather than viewin g these processes as separate, we show how a single  story— like Heather’s—can illustrate all four elements: personal resources, contextual support, strategic a daptation, and critical reflection. This dual lens avoids romantic izing teacher perseverance and instead highlights b oth the emotional labor and structural challenges involved. As Taylor (2024) cautions, uncritical celebration of resilience risks masking systemic inequities. Our proposed fra mework, therefore, calls for an asset-based yet critical approach—supporting teachers while acknowledging the broader  need for structural change. Beyond the pandemic, this framework has implications for teacher educati on, retention, and crisis-response efforts, offering a tool for understanding educator growth in context s of uncertainty, whether  those are natural disasters, socio-political turmoil, or everyday institutional challenges.
