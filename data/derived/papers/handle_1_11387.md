# Desiring Learning in a Hospital School Arts Education Program

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study analyzes desire as a dynamic force driving learning in a children’s hospital school arts education program. Data come from an ongoing, three-year co-design project addressing a challenge for hospital schools in designing learning opportunities that move students to participate through ongoing social and emotional disruption, and that maintain continuity in students’ sense of themselves as learners. Analysis shows how students’ affective revelations while working collaboratively on a map-based art project are inseparable from the ongoing production of desiring learning that is essential to their elective participation. This conceptualization of affective revelations contributes to learning sciences research theorizing how affect relates to learning and learning ecology design. Drawing on critical design-based research in the learning sciences, the project challenges infirming narratives of children undergoing hospitalization, and it illustrates how coming to feel differently is an essential dimension of coming to know differently while learning in the arts.

## Introduction and rationale

This paper addresses a problem of practice in children’s hospital school s through the conceptual development of desire as a primary force in learning. Hospital sch ools provide essential services for K-12 students, supporting continued education during prolonged hospitalizatio n. The biopsychosocial impact of chronic illness, i ncluding emotional distress, often diminishes students ’ sense of agency as learners through the disruption of everyday connections to school and peers (Plage et al., 2022). Hospital schools therefore design learning opportunities that support academic progress while fostering a sense of normalcy to encourage students to see themselves as learners despite the uncertainty inherent in hospitalization (Boff et al., 2021). Still, participation in these activities remains elective, dependent on medical status and daily preference for participation. Hospital schools are therefore challenged with designing learning opportunities th at move student  participation through ongoing social and emotional disruption, and that maintain continuity in students’ sense of themselves as learners. We draw on a history of social theory that conceive s of desire as immanent to unfolding activity to understand how desire drives students’ participatio n in collaborative artmaking in a children’s hospital sc hool. Our conceptualization of desiring learning contributes to ongoing efforts in the learning sciences to theorize how affect, or variations in intensities of felt partic ipation in activity (Ingold, 2013, 2014; Lemke, 2023), relates to learning (e.g., Leander et al., 2023) and learning ecology design (e.g., Potvin et al., 2024). We draw from a lineage of affirmative theorizations that challenge post-Freudian conceptualizations of desire as the want f or something lacking (e.g., Tuck, 2009 a). Affirmative conceptualizations of desire developed from Spinoza (1632/ 2016), through Vygotsky’s late turn to emotion via Spinoza (Bakhurst, 2007), to contemporary theorists (e.g., Massumi,

2021) and critical, design-based researchers (e.g., Tuck, 2009b). Desire is the “expression of the creative impulse

towards human flourishing. Desire refers to being awakened, excit ed, energized, eager, ready to act” (Leitão, 2022, p. 256). Desire is understood as the primary force of pro duction in social life, moving us forward not in search of a lacking object but in relation to how unfolding events affect us. With this theorization, we participate in the ongoing participatory turn in design-based research (DBR) (Bang & Vossoughi, 2016) that works alongside communities to co-theorize and co-create opportunities for learning and change (e.g., Nxumalo & Tuck, 2023). We wonder how being open to how children with chronic illnesses desire might change how we think about hospital school curricula and learning in the arts.

To follow students’ desiring through artmaking we contribute the concept of affective revelations, which describes how social life comes to feel different as we learn and create, in and over time. Our analysis shows how students’ collaborative artmaking produces affectiv e revelations integral to desiring learning. We foc us our analysis on an activity from the second year of an ongoing three-year co-design project, HEAR_T C_ART, uniting researchers, artists, and hospital school teachers and students in a large South Korean city. As students collaborate in making a model map of their experiences in the h ospital and the surrounding environment (see Figures 1 & 2 below), we analyze how learning to create art changed students’ feelings of place and social connection and moved them toward continued and deepened participation in scho ol activities, both in the moment and over time. We argue these affective revelations evidence not only the ongoing production of desire in activity, but also essential outcomes of arts-based learning. Tracing affective revelations illuminates how learning in the arts can be distinct from learning through the arts, the latter of which is a more common focu s in the learning sciences, particularly learning STEM content through art practices (Halver son & Sawyer, 2022). Conceptualized in this way, le arning in the arts includes the potential to change how li fe feels and to drive desires toward continued crea tion, production, and being, which we also see as essential to the work of children’s hospital schools. Producing desire and affective revelations while learning in the arts Many learning scientists have considered learning t o make art objects through constructivist paradigms, which analyze students’ construction of physical objects as a part of their gradual building of conceptual representations (Halverson & Sheridan, 2022). From this perspective, art objects and perfo rmances have been conceptualized as representational objects evincing learners’ understanding and, in their capacity as physical and embodied artifacts, tools for mediating discussions of students’ ongoing learning across disciplines through artistic production. Beyond learning representational processes for spec ific material mediums, learning scientists have described identity development and fostering creativity and collaboration as outcomes of learning in the arts. For example, in their review of out-of-school time arts education, Halverson et al. (2023) posit ed collaboration as an outcome of collective learning in the process of bu ilding representational objects rather than as a “m echanism to individualistic outcomes…” (p. 362). Similarly, Leander et al. (2023) described how actors participating in longform dramatic improvisation learn an affective sens ibility of in-the-moment responding, or what they call “collaborative affective attunement,” as they produ ce emergent stories without pre-formed intention s. These researchers found that improv actors learn to sense  and follow transmitted energies, or affects, in co-producing amodal dimensions of an improvised performance, such as its tone. We are inspired by this and other learning sciences  research that understand affective ways of knowing as a learning target in their own right (e.g., Vea,  2020). Building on Halverson et al.’s (2023) propositio n for collaboration as an outcome of collective learning in the arts that exceeds representation in an art o bject, we see affective revelations as individual and/or collecti ve outcomes that emerge through the process of artmaking. In the context of art education, affective revelations  refer to moments of working with materials, indivi dually or collectively, when an awareness of social life comi ng to feel different emerges. For example, in our a nalysis students come to feel differently about clinical spaces in the hospital while constructing a collective map. To conceptualize affective revelations, we follow learning scientists who have drawn on nonrepresentational views of affect (e.g. Hollett et al., 2022), which theorize affect via Sp inoza (1632/2016) and his modern interpreters as “affections of the body by which the body's power of acting is increased or diminished, aided or restrained, and at the same time, the idea of these affections.” This embodied and relational theorization of affect helps us to understand how affective revelations relate to the ongoing production of desiring learning in the HEAR_T C_ART program. Feeling joy, awe, surpris e, defeat, or any affects increase s or diminish es our capacity for moving forward, for desiring. Affects and particular desires are formed together and are inextricable from the moments they are produced. In our analysis, we show how affective revelations are inseparable f rom, and productive of, the desires that move students f orward through the artmaking process and  compel their participation toward continued affective revelation. Through this analysis, we argue that affective revelations are an essential outcome of students’ learning about what art cando as a tool for social connection and transformation.

## Context and methods: Desire-based design in children’s hospitals

HEAR_T C_ART began in 2023 as a co-design project  between researchers, artists, educators, and children, combining concepts of listening to children ’s voices through artmaking (“Hear+Art”) and supporting their well-being through creative expression (“Care+Art”). This forms a desire-based design axiology, which, aligned with our theoretical perspective on learning,  informs how activities are designed to grow from  children’s affective experiences as learners. Our design axiology follows learning scientists suc h as Bang et al. (2016), who argued that axiological positionings emerge “ in the conceptual, emotional, and affective states that shape intersubjectivities and possible futures in interac tion…” (pg. 29). In tracing children’s desiring learning  across HEAR_T C_ART, we seek to understand how what we value in learning as researchers and arts educators comes into relation with what children value across affectively laden moments of learning in the arts. In practice, o ur axiological positionings  as researchers and art educators emphasize the proc ess of artmaking over the finished, representational artifact. In the second design cycle of this in-progress, three-year, three-cycle project, we developed activities at the intersection of our positioning and children’s desires for social connection and community expressed during cycle one. Therefore, in the second cycle, activity design prioritized cultivating community building and social connectio ns through artmaking, shifting from the year-one focus on individual expression in representational objects. For example, this paper’s analysis focuses on three, one-hour sessions of a mapmaking activity held over three weeks. Seven students, aged eight to thirteen, participated in the activity. Participants were hospitalized with childhood cancer. Each session was led by one teacher alongside a researcher. Before each session, these teacher researchers  arranged a large blue felt sheet and blocks of pape rcoated Styrofoam boards on a table. The blocks were aligned in a path-like pattern, resembling roads. This initial setup served as a visual provocation, prompting stu dents to reflect on the materials’ potential uses. Teachers encouraged students to imagine what these materials could represent about their lives, and they demonstrated that the blocks were interactive and could be rearranged, colored, and layered to form v arious shapes and sizes. Equipped with materials such as markers, crayons, and watercolors, students were invited to draw directly on the blocks, expanding their collective visual dialogue while creating a shared map of their experiences.

## Data collection and analysis

Data collection included audio recordings and theor y-focused, multimodal fieldnotes that merged written notes with digital images. Reflection meetings were conducted after each class to discuss these notes and iterative design potentials. Recordings were transcribed and translated by Author 2, a native speaker. For this analysis, data were reduced to moments of affective revelation. Next, researchers deductively coded reduced data ac cording to the following theory-driven questions: How are desires produced through affective revelations that emerge during artmaking? How are desires reproduced over time in affective revelations that eme rge during artmaking? We identified two processes of affective revelations: placemaking affective revelations (PMAR)  and collective affective revelations (CAR). PMAR refers to moments of activity in which students reinterpret familiar spa ces, imbuing them with new  meaning that changes how they feel in relation to them. CAR encompasses moments where students create and respond to shared energy  through embodied activity in ways that change their feeling of being together, as well as their recognition of the emerging sense of their collective. Tracing affective revelations across the mapmaking activity in and over time Our analysis follows Barab’s (2022) suggestion of “storied truths” as crucial to DBR’s potential to simultaneously drive theory in the learning sciences and maintain impact in a local educational setting. Through “storied truths,” we argue that, as affective revelations, PMARs and CARs drive desiring learning for children in HEAR_T C_ART. As affective revelations unfold, students reinterpret spaces and co-create social connections that make them “ energized, eager, ready to act” (Leitão, 2022,  p. 256) in and over time. These moments of relational awareness highlight how learning in the arts fosters affective change as an ongoing outcome productive of desire. Collective affective revelations As with SA and SB’s recognition of their shared journeys to the hospital, over the course of each of  the three sessions, students discovered more  connections through shared experiences of places, images, and cultural artifacts, such as an Instagram reel (short video) of a cat impossibly walking on a well-known river. These shared recognition s established a collective narrative  that connected individual experiences to a collective in-the-making. The ‘cat reel,’ for instance, emerged as a shared cultural artifact while SB and SA were drawing blocks to represent the river in session one. After they drew the blocks, T1 incidentally drew a cat on a blue surface, playfully representing ice. As T1 placed the block on the map, SB began to sing the song from the cat reel, exclaiming, in response to T1 praising his singin g: “It’s the ability to change everything by singing […] It is a superpower from heaven. ” SB recognizes the potential of his singing to change the tone of discourse and the session’s affective atmosphere. In session two, the full complement of five students collectively iterated SB’s song in to a series of imaginative scenarios as they created the blocks fo r the map. This improvisational narrative  began from SB’s block representing a Panda and quickly coalesced around students’ shared knowledge and experience of the river: Student D: Student B: Student C: SB: SC: SB: SC: Fu Bao (Panda) is complete [singing] Fu Bao went to China Fu Bao walks over the frozen…River. [singing] Bridge is collapsed while walking. [singing] (Pointing at the block) Isn't this a cat walking on the frozen Han River? [singing] The cat can't walk on the frozen…River and its legs will break. [singing] When I hit Grenzo on the frozen…River into the Ssampokrak, the cat, which was disheartened, rode around and around like a mountain dog. [singing] In each turn of verse, students not only developed a narrative, but they also maintained a collective energy with each improvised sequence performed in similar tones (cf., Leander et al., 2023). Their desire to sustain this collective energy through the non-representational, affective tonality of their talk evinces the importance of shared feeling in maintaining their social connection and cohesion as a collective.  The latter was explicitly represented in language when SB was absent in the third session, and students struggled to produce the collective energy  of previous sessions. Realizing this, SA remarked: “I don’t think I can get used to it because there’s no [SB].” This remark indexes a series of moments  that evoke a CAR that emerged over time. The remark itself demonstrates how the collective affective energy generated durin g earlier interactions was felt even in its absence, reinforcing the group’s shared affective connection. Through their responsiveness to each other, stude nts formed a sense of togetherness that extended beyond individual contri butions, illustrating CARs as collaborative outcome s of learning in the arts  essential to the ongoing production of desire. In this way, desire is both productive of and produced by affective revelations. As these students’ CAR suggests, designing for affective revelations as learning outcome may foster desiring learning, sustain commu nity, and create continuity in students’ sense of t hemselves as learners.

## Discussion: Challenging Infirming Narratives through Desire-based Designs

Our analysis challenges infirming narratives of children undergoing hospitalization, contributing to an established history of critical design-based research in the learning sciences (e.g., Rahm et al., 2023). We have demonstrated how desire-based educational designs reposition hospitalized youth as desiring learners  who actively engage in the creative processes and are driven by curiosity,  connection, and the joy of creation. Addressing a problem of practice in hospital school education through a developed th eory of desire, we have shown how affective revelations are inseparable from the ongoing production of desiring learning that is essential to students’ elective participation in hospital school activit ies. Educators can facilitate desiring learning by notic ing and affirming moments of affective revelation, such as when students reinterpret clinical spaces or establish collective narratives during artmaking. Pedagogically, teachers can inten tionally design learning environments with open-ended materials and prompts that allow students to infuse  spaces with personal and collective meaning. Broadly, our analysis of affective revelations in these children’s experiences  illustrates that coming to feel differently is an essential dimension of coming to know differently while learning in the arts.
