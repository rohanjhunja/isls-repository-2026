# Embodied and Digital Network Visualization of Data Design for Elementary Students

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper explores how transitioning from embodied to digital representations of network-data supports elementary students in understanding and interpreting network visualizations. We analyzed students’ post-interviews and classroom videos to understand their perceptions of the embodied activity design and how moving between representations enhances their understanding. We used thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2012) to generate four themes from students’ interviews on the use of embodied and digital representations. We used interaction analysis (Jordan & Henderson, 1995) to reveal nuances of the instructional sequence.

## Introduction

There is a pressing need for elementary students to be data-literate in this data-saturated world (Jiang et al., 2022). Data literacy requires a set of skills for working with data in contextual ways, including the compreh ension of data variability, identification of aggregate patterns, drawing inferences,  and interacting with data visualizations (Rubin, 2020). Previous research on the Common Onli ne Data Analysis Platform (CODAP, 2019) demonstrate d that data visualization supported data literacy development. This study focuses on elementary students’ interaction with network visualization because prior research has found it to be an approachable yet powerful computational approach for exploring personalized data and connec tions between data points without requiring a focus  on the quantitative side of data that might be off-putting to some students (Zhou et al., 2023). Despite the affordances of data visualizations, young students benefit from an easy entry point to understand network visualization. This study designed an embodied network activity in  which students represent a node (a data point in a network) before exploring more complex data in digi tal network visualizations. We positioned this embo died network activity as a starting point on a traj ectory toward sense-making and interacting with more complex network in a digital network visualization tool. Students transitioned from this embodied network into  a digital network that faithfully replicates this embodied on e and then evolved into another  interconnected but expanded digital network. We anticipated that this design would alleviate some initial challenges students migh t encounter when they were introduced to a network visualizatio n tool for the first time. We focus on the design o f this progressive transition from an embodied network activity and two digital n etwork representations. We asked: 1) What were students’ reflections on using embodied a nd digital representations? 2) How did the instruct ional sequence and the facilitation mediate students’ reasoning of digital network representation?

## Perspectives

Supporting data literacy through embodiment Network visualizations appear distinct from data graphs that elementary students may be accustomed to. Our first design conjecture was that embodiment would serve as a resource for helping students’ sense-making about how network visualization represents relationships betw een entities. One way of incorporating bodily resources into data literacy education is to have bodies representing mathematical forms because positioning students themselves as (or a part of) a mathematical object provides mu ltiple metaphors and visual/verbal/kinesthetic representations for conceptual understanding (Ma, 2017). For exampl e, in DesPortes et al.’s (2022) data-dance unit, middle schoolers created embodied metaphors in dance to em body graphic representations, which led tonew ways of reasoning with numbers and conveying graphic data. Similarly, students in data theater activities who have choreographed data presentations using their own bodies recognized the value of this activity in sensemaking with data (Bhargava et al., 2022). Building on these methods, we designed an embodied yarn network activity in which students acted as individual nodes in a network, re presenting their own ideas /experiences. They then asked who else had a similar interest and “connected” to one of their peers who had a shared interest by passing them one end of a piece of yarn while holding onto the other, thus creating a literal connection between them. Progressively increasing network visualization complexity We presented students with two digital network visuali zations of the yarn network sequentially. These net works were variations of the same network with increasing complexity. This design was inspired partially by the idea of transnumeration, “forming and changing data represe ntations of aspects of a system to arrive at a bett er understanding of that system” (Wild & Pfannkuch, 19 99, p. 227). Prior research using transnumeration t ypically involved students in a dynamic process of changing representations to assist them in discerning divers e aspects/patterns of the data (Fitzallen et al., 2017). We adapt this practice into a network visualization context by presenting interrelated networks of increasing comp lexity to highlight dif ferent aspects of data relationships in the network. We conjecture that presenting interrel ated networks with incremental complexity would pro mote students’ understanding of the network.

## Method

Design The majority of this study had students utilize a digital network visualizatio n tool, Net.Create (https://netcreate.org/; Craig & Danish, 2018), in which nodes (depicted as circles) represent people/hobbie s/places and edges (depicted as lines) indicate connections between two nodes (Figure 1). This anal ysis focuses on day 1’s embodied activity (the “yarn activity”) and two computer-based networks that were intended to extend this activity to highlight the potential value of this instructional sequence. In the yarn activity, each student represented a node (themselves), while the yarn represented the edges that connected students with overlapping interests/experiences (Figure 2). Students took turns choosing a question  (e.g., where are you from?) that our  team developed. After answering one question, they passed the yarn ball t o another student who indicated a similarity. This process continued, creating one edge at a time. Meanwhile, researchers created two digital versions of the yar n network in Net.Create. Version one (Figure 1A) replicated the yarn network, with students connected by a yarn-typed edge. Version two linked students with yarn and their similarities (Figure 1 B). On day 2, a facilitator sequentially showed a picture of the yarn network (Figure 2 B) and two digital networks. Figure 2 Yarn Network

## Data and data analysis

Twenty-two fifth and sixth graders from a private school i n the Midwestern United States participated in this study. Eighteen of these students participated (as nine pai rs) in post-implementation interviews. The interviews covered students’ understanding of emerging network  literacy, reflections on the yarn activity, and id entity development. This analysis focuses on the first two sections. We conducted a thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke,

2012) of students' responses to all these questions to understand students’ experiences and reflections on the yarn

activity. We conducted an Interaction Analysis (IA;  Jordan & Henderson, 1995) of the day 2’ classroom video data, where a facilitator introduced three representations in sequence.

## Results

RQ1: Students’ reflection on embodied and digital representations in this study Theme 1: The embodied activity offers an easy and more intuitive form of participation Seven of the nine pairs noted how the embodied acti vity was helpful in introducing network visualizations and digital activities. Two pairs specifically appreciated the different modes of participation provided by the embodied activity. Two pairs felt the yarn network was easier to understand. One student suggested we keep the yarn activity for all age groups, especially for younger students, while this student, on day 1, suggested that they did not learn data literacy from the yarn activity. We interpret this attitude shift as an increased appreciation of the importance of embodied activity as an integral component in their learning trajectory toward understanding network data. Yet not all students found this entirely intuitive, with a few noting, “confusing” and “not helpful for data literacy.” Theme 2: The embodied activity socially and emotionally engages a small group of students Five pairs expressed enjoyment of the yarn activity. Three pairs appreciated the yarn activity as very socially interactive, allowing them to interact in real-time and get to know each other better. Additionally, several groups appreciated the embodied nature of the yarn network, including one that appreciated the physical sensa tion of holding the yarn. Thus, students’ enjoyment of partici pating in embodied activity reveals the inherent potential for affective involvement with data representations. Nevertheless, five pairs raised concerns about the exclusio n of many students from being connected with the yarn  due to time limitations and the choice to add only  one student at a time. One student also expressed concerns about the potential bias in only choosing friends to connect with. We aim to address these concerns in the future while acknowledging their role in helping students appreciate the ease and speed of digital networks in connecting more people. Theme 3: The digital space provides more detailed information in an organized way Four pairs expressed appreciation of the digital sp ace, although interview questions had not requested  them to. Among them, three pairs emphasized that digital spa ce provides access to a greater amount of information. Two pairs made a general comment about that advantage, “…the online, you candefinitely see a bit more,” while one pair provided more details, stating that the digita l space can reveal how people/things are connected,  “it can tell the connections, like are you interested in this one, or is this important to you.” We interpret this as students being attentive to the edge types used to connect nodes s uch as “interested in and important to”, which were explicitly specified in the digital space yet not fully articulated in the embo died activity, and the fact that the digital space included many more nodes. Lastly, two pairs highlig hted the ease of making connections in the digital tool, and one pair noted the representation of information in the digital environment as more organized. Theme 4: Social norms in embodied activity are more salient Students articulated some discomfort caused by the easy data creation in the digital space. Two pairs stated that some students added inappropriate information in th e digital space. During the implementation, one stu dent was connected to some insulting nod es created by peers. We coordinated with teachers a nd attempted to turn this incident into a chance to educate students about da ta autonomy and anonymity in online spaces. We are pleased that two pairs reflected on the incident and recognized social norms in the embodied space can regulate students’ behaviors and safeguard what data they want to pres ent. We view more autonomy in the embodied network as balanced by more contributions to data sharing in the digital space. However, this certainly suggests the value of additional discussions about privacy and autonomy when asking students to represent themselves in the network. RQ 2: Instructional sequence and the facilitation mediate network reasoning From the yarn network to the first digital replica Moving from the yarn network to the first digital network seemed to support students to notic e similarities and differences across the representations. This was the students’ first time viewing a digital  network in Net.Create. Students’ first comments focused on the overall shape of the network, calling it looks like a fish or constellation. This digital version afforded students an easy access to a third-person perspective of viewing the network data and supported their attention to the overall shape of the network, which can be an important step towards noticing larger-scale data patterns. S tudents also drew on their experiences of the yarn activity to make sense of the network. They noticed that nodes of people were con nected based on who passed to whom in the yarn acti vity. Sharing a similar experience in the yarn network and the following actions of connecting through a string of yarn were taken up by students to understand connections  in the network and to point out limitations of the  digital network for not capturing the direction of connections. While the process of constructing a yarn network was time-consuming, as mentioned in post-interviews, this activity served as a steppingstone in supporting students’ sense-making of the digital network. This is achieved through creating an embodied metaphor between the action of passing a string of yarn and the connection betw een nodes and offering students a space to elaborat e more on similarities, which potentially contributes to their deeper understanding of connections in the digital network. From the first digital replica to the second digital version Next, the facilitator displayed the second digital version of the yarn network. This second version wa s arguably more complex, as it contained additional node and e dge types. The facilitation began with a general qu estion asking students what they noticed in this network. Students immediately responded that this network showed what people chose/said in the yarn network. Then, studen ts were prompted to mention a specific example of w hat people said, “dogs, ” and its connection, “was it Student 3. ” This exchange evidenced that this digital network refreshed students’ memory about what people had shared in the yarn activity, and students were able to recognize that information in this digital network. The facil itator encouraged students to recognize that the ne twork encompasses not only people-to-people connections as they built in the yarn activity but also other types of connections, such as people-to-things, that a network can represent. The facilitator continued to orient students to notice the co nnection types between nodes by pointing out the ke ys at the bottom of the digital network. The facilitator highlighted the connection between Stud ent 3 and the dog (that students mentioned before) and noted that the connection between these two nodes is oran ge, which means are important to. The facilitator mediated students to attend to the specific connection types  (e.g., dogs are important to Student 3). The detai led stories students shared about their similarities in the embodied activity to validate the connections became the foundations for students to understand the connection types in the digital network. The facilitation mediated students to notice more information and finer aspects of the network compared to the first digital network.

## Discussion

This study  demonstrated that the instructional sequence that started with embodied n etwork activity and then proceeded to the exploration of two digital networks enhanced students’ ability to pay closer attention tonetwork features. The embodied activity fostered an enjoyable, social, and regulated space for students to develop a general understanding of network concepts. It served as an entry point for students’ sensemakin g because their first impression of the connections in a digital network was directly associated with the action of passing the string of yarn to people who shared similarities with them. The detailed stories about similarities were used by students to understand connection types between nodes. These stories provided us with resources to present students with the digital network’s affordances of showing beyond people’s interrelationships. Future work will explore the role of embodiment in data literacy because of its potential to contribute to the conversation about data visualization from a humanistic perspective (Lee et al., 2021) and challenge data objectivity (Desportes et al., 2022).
