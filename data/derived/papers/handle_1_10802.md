# Balancing Freedom and Guidance: Kindergarten Teachers' Strategies for Outdoor Play (in the Post-COVID Era)

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study investigates the balance between autonomy and teacher guidance in children's outdoor play post-COVID-19. Conducted at an early childhood research center in the Northeastern United States, it employs scaffolding strategies within the Zone of Proximal Development, utilizing observations and interviews for data collection. The qualitative analysis reveals seven key strategies for enhancing outdoor play in line with children's developmental stages: observation, reflection, withdrawal, supplying materials, safety and social support, involvement, and management. The study aims to refine outdoor play experiences for young learners by balancing safety, learning opportunities, and the advantages of free play with structured guidance. It offers insights into supporting children's comprehensive development in outdoor environments, enriching the dialogue on outdoor play strategies with a Vygotsky-inspired framework and underscoring the importance of withdrawal and social support in the post-COVID era.

## Introduction

Outdoor play is integral to young children's multif aceted growth, especially for young children who ha ve experienced reduced outdoor time and increased screen-based time post-COVID-19 (Kourti et al., 2021). Outdoor play provides a diverse, natural, and expansive spa ce that indoor play lacks (Maynard & Waters, 2007),  offering unique benefits and contributing to children's heal th and physical, cognitive, emotional, and social d evelopment (Kemple et al., 2016). However, opinions vary on the extent of children 's autonomy and the teacher's role in outdoor play. Trawick-Smith & Dziurgot (2011) propose that children's aut onomy can be fostered through a hands-off approach during playtime, emphasizing child-oriented, voluntary, and flexible free play principles. This approach enhances executive function by nurturing a utonomy in children. However, Little & Eager (2010) highlight potential safety concerns of free play. A dditionally, free play may not consistently provide  optimal learning opportunities as it frequent ly l acks structured scenarios that engage children in intera ctive learning experiences (Lemaignan  et al., 2017). Concerns regarding safety and limite d learning opportunities in outdoor free play underscore the necessity of adult supervision. Pyle & Danniels (2017) recognize that teachers can enrich children’s play experience by guiding and expanding on a child's initiative. However, they also acknowledge that teacher involvement might disrupt the essence of fr ee play. Thus, finding a middle ground is crucial t o ensure children candevelop independently and gain the full benefits of outdoor play.

To address this, researchers like Perry (2001) emph asize the teacher's dual role as an observer and facilitator, using environmental strategies to enhance play. Additionally, Scaffolding, rooted in Vygotsky’s (1976) social constructivism, supports children's developm ent within the Zone of Proximal Development (ZPD), "the distance between the actual developmental level as determined by independent problem solving and the l evel of potential development as determined through problem-solving under adult guidance or in collaboration with more capable peers"(Vygotsky, 1978, p. 86). Building upo n on Vygotsky’s theory, Trawick-Smith & Dziurgot (2011) proposed a model (Fig. 1) to advocate a dynamic interplay where teachers guide children's  play while respecting their autonomy. Although these perspectives offer v aluable insights, the discussion integrating outdoor play into educational theories and pedagogical strategies is still lacking. By employing a qualitative case study methodology, this study seeks to bridge this gap and provide a more comprehensive understanding of outdoor play. Focusing on balancing children's autonomy and teacher guidance through the lenses of ZPD and scaffolding strategie s, the study’s research questions are as follows: 1) What strategies do teachers identify as effective in enhancing children's outdoor play? 2) How do teachers utilize  these strategies in alignment with a child's devel opmental stage? By delving into these questions, this study sought to uncover practical and effective methods for educators to enhance outdoor play experience for young children, balancing autonomy and teacher guidance.

## Research method

This qualitative case study examines the dynamics of interaction between teachers and children in a PreK class at a Northeastern U.S. university-affiliated kindergarten. The class consists of eight children, aged 4-5, born in 2018 or earlier, with limited opportunities for in-person outdoor play with peers due to the pandemic starting in 2019. We observed two outdoor play sessions, collecting recorded audio data and written field notes. Two 30-minute semi-structured i nterviews were also conducted individually with a PreK classroom teacher and a co-director acting as a substitute teacher for PreK. With participants' consent, these interviews were conducted, recorded, and transcribed using Zoom. The data were analyzed thro ugh a three-stage coding process that utilized both bottom-up and top-down approaches (Denzin & Lincoln, 2011), alongside a detailed line-by-line coding method (Charmaz, 2000). To ensure the validity and reliability of our findi ngs, we triangulated various data sources and had t he coding validated by the second author during the an alysis phase. This process yielded 102 codes across  14 categories, which were then distilled into seven strategies (Fig. 2). The three-level coding process for the strategy of "Involvement" is detailed in Table 1, showcasing an example of how these strategies were derived and defined. ## Finding and discussion

The findings of this study illuminate the multiface ted role of teachers in fostering outdoor play amon g young children, addressing the RQ1. Key themes identified  include observation, reflection, withdrawal, suppl ying materials, safety and social support, i nvolvement, and management, all of which highlight the intricacies of teachers' roles in nurturing a learning and age-appropriate play environment. Addressing the RQ2, teacher strategies are categorized into two distinct phases: assessment and action (see Fig.2). In the assessment phase, teachers employ observation and reflection to discern the current dynamics of children's play. This critical phase informs the selection of strategies for the subsequent action phase. In the action phase, teachers either implement tailored strategies to improve outdoor play or step back to encourage children's independent play, guided by insights from the assessment phase.  The following sections provide a detailed explorati on of these strategies in the two phases. Interview excerpts are in italics.

Observation is the initial and recurring step for t eachers to comprehend the situation and collect information for subsequent tailored support. Ms. N explains, " We want to balance the teacher's support and the children's lead…so we watch them and listen to their discussion first..." Observation allows for gathering crucial information, such as the current context, c hildren's personalities, needs, and interests, and enables the selection of appropriate strategies. Similarly, Perry (2001)  also emphasizes the significance of observation in identifying effective strategies tailored to indivi dual needs and interests. Thus, this process remains critical for scaffolding and facilitating effective intervention within the ZPD at later stages, even without immediate action. Reflection. Following observation, reflection involves teachers  deciding on the appropriate scaffolding strategies based on their knowledge of each child, considering individual developmental stages, interests, personalities, and specific situational needs. For example, Ms. E notes that the choice of strategy “depends on the condition,” indicating that decisions are tailored to the unique circumstances of each scenario. Combined with the field note, this process involves assessing the need for action,  selecting effective strategies, and determining th e extent of interference. Further, Ms. N's interview reveals that her selection of strateg ies is consistent with state and national standards, underscoring the idea that effective scaffolding necessitates defined objectiv es to foster children's autonomy, a concept highlighted by Hammond and Gibbons (2005). Withdrawal. Contrasting with active intervention techniques, withdrawal is a strategy characterized by not intervening directly, relying instead on the te acher's reflection from observation. Ms. E underscores this approach’s value, asserting that children "need space to explore sometimes," particularly when deeply absorbed in play or require time to think. This perspective is supported by  Trawick-Smith & Dziurgot (2011), who argue that interactions between teacher and child are not always necessary. Recognizing that a critical aspect of outdoor play is fostering children's autonomy, teachers often step back to allow children to independently plan, carry out, reflect on, and monitor their activities (Vygotsky, 1976). Adding to this, Ms. N states, “We can also learn how to play from them!” This suggests that the withdrawal strategies employed by Ms. N and Ms. E align with the modern understanding of the crucial role independent play plays in child development. Supplying Materials. Providing m aterials is essential for  maintaining children's engagement and influencing their behavior, promoting physical exercise, offering challenges, and fostering peer interaction (Cetken-Aktas & Sevimli-Celik, 2023; Merewether, 2015). Teachers tailor materials to  children's interests to encourage ongoing  exploration. For instance, a teacher observed a chi ld's interest in painting and introduced paintbrushes, leading to greater involvement from other children, according to field notes. Innovative material use can transition children from passive to active engagement, such as when Ms. N brought sandbox toys to the slide area, as noted in the interview. Introducing new materials can revive interest in the outdoor setting when children's engagement begins to wane.  Teachers often provide unstructured materials like large blocks for gross motor experiences and natural elements like leaves, water, and soil, which have been found to enhance experi ences positively (Staempfli, 2009).

Safety and social support.  Ensuring physical and social security is paramount in outdoor play, particularly for children born during or around COV ID-19 who experience social isolation  (Liu et al., 2023). Teachers are vital in maintaining physical safety by managing risky situations. To provide social support, they employ strategies like offering choice (Ms. N) and assistance (Ms. E); for instance, supporting children on the balance beam to ensure a feeling of security (field notes). Teachers frequently reinforce safety protocols, like taking turns or initiating a countdown before using  the slide, to minimize injuries caused by disputes  or disregarding rules. Strategies like using “timers, expressions, and role-playing” are employed to alleviate social tensions (Ms. E). Both Ms. E and Ms. N emphasize inviting peers to enhance social interaction. Furthermore, Ms. E stresses the need for young children to have “the ir own space” to prepare for interactions with othe rs, a point particularly highlighted in this study.

Involvement. Stemming from observation and reflection, involvement is key in assisting the development of young children. Teachers facilitate this by either participating in children's current play activities or inviting them to try new ones. For instance, Ms. N actively participates by "joining them, chasing them, playing tag, and doing things together." Such involvement can extend to role-playing scenarios where, for instance, a teacher might adopt the role of a driver in a child's imagi native train game, as captured in field notes. This echoes Vygotsky's emphasis on the importance of imaginatio n in play. Additionally, teachers integrate various  subjects, such as art, writing, or science, into outdoor acti vities to enrich the learning experience, a practice supported by both observations and interview s. Employing verbal strategies such as questioning, cl arifying, and offering suggestions, observed during activities like sand digging, is vital for enhancing play, resonating with the findings of Waters & Maynard (2010).

Management. Teachers employ management mainly when they notice a general lack of motivation among children. Our research identifies  that effective teaching management involves strateg ically staff deployment, optimizing the use of space, and consid ering environmental elements. It also includes tail oring activities for individual students, groups, or the whole class. The key aspect of this strategy is space utilization, where teachers may alter their positions within the playground to motivate children to explore different areas. For example, Ms. E observed children lingering in the sandbox for to o long, so she might move to the climbing  area and coordinate with other teachers to encourage chi ldren to venture into other playground sections. This strategy often involves thoughtful organization of the playg round layout, promoting teacher collaboration  and communication, and adjusting activities based on  environmental conditions. As Ms. E noted, " skiing down the mountain (on grass hills) in winter is very popular with children... " whereas climbing on grass hills might be restricted in other seasons for safety reasons.

## Conclusion and implications

This study used model-based qualitative coding analysis to propose seven feasible strategies for teachers to intervene in outdoor play and explain its operating  mechanisms. The research model emphasizes teachers ' need to balance " safety support " and " giving children opportunities to grow." It notably addresses the withdrawal behaviors observed in children who experienced early social interactions during the pandemic (Kourti et al., 2021), which offers  insights into the importance of social spacing in f uture considerations. Additionally, the model underscores teachers' adaptability in responding to children's developmental needs dur ing outdoor play, proving particularly beneficial for preschools with limited resources for expanding play facilit ies or accessing natural environments. The results highlight the versatility of guidance strategies in harmonizing learning and development in outdoor settings. Therefore, future studies shou ld focus more on enhancing teacher training and gui dance for implementing effective outdoor play strategies.
