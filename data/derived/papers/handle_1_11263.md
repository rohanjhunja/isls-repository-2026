# Generative Role-Playing Game-Based Learning in a Higher Education Literature Course

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study examines the effectiveness of generative role-playing game-based learning in a college-level science fiction course. Twenty-three students participated in a semester-long role-playing game designed to enhance understanding of literary concepts and foster internal motivation to learn. The study incorporated seven theoretical frameworks, including constructionist theory, situated learning theory, and transformative learning theory. Findings reveal that role-playing games provided transformational learning experiences, particularly in identity exploration and critical thinking. The study also suggests that role-playing games can be leveraged as a powerful tool for transformational growth across various disciplines, encouraging educators to consider how identity exploration through role-play can enhance learning outcomes in different educational contexts.

## Introduction and background

In the Spring of 2024, 23 students in a college-level science fiction course developed a role-playing game designed to achieve course learning objectives and provide a transformational learning experience. Those course objectives include but are not limite d to: an understanding of landscape/setting and of the relationship between characters and setting, an understanding of narrative, identit ies, and perspectives, and critical thinking about difference, whether social or biological in nature. As the seco nd iteration of an ongoing study into the effective ness of generative role-playing game-based learning, this semester's iteration used the work of previous semesters as a starting point and a guide. The student work can be  found through the fandom wiki, which is completely moderated by the students. A description of the first design iteration can be found in Cooper et al. (2024). The current design differed from the previous in se veral ways. First, identity was leveraged in a more conscious way throughout the course, as fitting our general hypothesis that conscious, scaffolded role-play can be leveraged to foster internal motivatio ns to learn. Previously, students made characters t o role-play during the game, but role-playing opportunities were limited to game play. In this iteration, students were asked to read and interpret texts as their characters, thereby shifting their fictional identity into the real space of the classroom. The fact that their characters were often aliens only increased the creative difficulty of role-playing analysis. The actual game mechanics did not change much, except for the fact that this iteration began  with the work provided by previous students.

This study aims to evaluate the effectiveness of ro le-playing game-based learning in achieving course learning objectives, particularly focusing on how c onscious, scaffolded role-play can foster internal motivations to learn and enhance students' understanding of key literary concepts.

## Theoretical framework

We incorporated seven theoretical frameworks for this study. Those are constructionist learning theory, cognitive constructivist learning theory, social constructivi st learning theory, situated learning theory, self-determination theory, dynamic systems model of role identity (DSM RI) theory, and transformative learning theory. Constructionist theory, developed by Seymour Papert, emphasizes learning through making, where student s construct knowledge by creating artifacts (Papert &  Harel, 1991). Both cognitiv e constructivism and social constructivism also emphasize knowledge constructio n. Cognitive constructivism, rooted in Piaget’s the ories, focuses on the individual learner's internal knowle dge construction, whereas social constructivism hig hlights the construction of knowledge through interaction with others in the learning environment (Powell & Kalina, 2009). We also grounded our study in situated learning theory, which emphasizes the context and environment in which learning occurs. According to this theory, learning happens as students engage with their discipline’s community through interactions with the environment and others in the community (Lave & Wenger, 1991; Stein, 1998).

To address the identity-related aspect of the students, we included self-determination theory, DSMRI theory, and transformative learning theory. Self-determination theory focuses on fostering intrinsic  motivation, which leads to improved performance and deeper learning (Ryan & Deci, 2000). DSMRI theory views identity as a continually evolving entity, with individuals in a constant state of “becoming.” In this state, various experiences can shape and alter a person's roles, perceived pos sibilities for action, and self-perceptions (Kaplan & Garner, 2017). Similarly, transfo rmative learning theory aims to develop a learner’s  identity by making their frame of reference more inclusive. This is achieved through discourse that challenges and expands the assumptio ns held by learners and others (Mezirow, 1997).

These theories collectively inform our study design and analysis by providing a multifaceted lens through which to examine the complex interplay between generative role-play game-based learning, identity exploration, and knowledge construction in a literature classroo m setting. They guide our understanding of how stud ents engage with the course material, construct meaning and identity, and potentially transform their persp ectives through the role-playing experience.

## Methods

Our data comes from the reflection papers from 23 students, collected at the end of the semester. For analysis, we have conducted network analysis to explore the intricate relationships between the students’ learning experiences and theoretical frameworks that were used to design  a semester-long RPG-based learning activity in a university literature course.

The course’s gaming component consisted of four pha ses. First, students created races and cultures within the fictional universe started by a previous  iteration as a Wiki Fandom. They then developed fi ctional characters. These characters were grouped, and each  group created backstories explaining how their cha racters became friends. Finally, the groups designed scenarios, culminating in end-of-semester game-play facilitated by the instructor and student game leaders.

We analyzed the students’ reflection papers using M AXQDA analytics pro. Each sentence was coded according to multiple codes in the codebook when ap propriate. All the data were coded in-vivo for emergent codes (Corbin & Strauss, 2015) except for the categ ories based on the theories in which this study is grounded where we used a priori coding (Thornberg, 2012). Th en we incorporated axial coding, to relate the data  together to reveal the interconnections among codes (Corbin & Strauss, 2015). We ended with  56 coding categories based on principles from each learning theory, what students perceived to be beneficial in their learning experience, and their struggles. A correlation analysis was perform ed, resulting in a symmetrical correlation matrix w ith values significant at the p<0.001 level. This matrix was us ed to construct a network map in UCINET. Using Girv anNewman cluster analysis (Newman & Girvan, 2003), we identified four clusters and adjusted node size according to the betweenness centrality value (Q = 0.562).

## Findings

Our findings showed divergent and convergent cluste rs. One type of student experience, represented in the map by the gray cluster, responded specifically to the most immediate forms of gameplay —rolling dice, making decisions, taking on foes, etc. Though no t incorrect, students were asked to consider the “g ame play” more broadly, including the creation of characters, worl ds, and races. Still, this perspective provided val uable insights as to how someone new to role-play gaming might perceive the complexities  of a role-playing game system. Similar to the gray cluster, the red cluster also s hows some difficulties with the mechanics of gamepl ay. Such anxieties are common; also commonly, those anxietie s are alleviated after a single session of gameplay. Like many games, one must play to understand the dynamics. These struggles suggest that students should be exposed to small mini-games that allow them to explore game mechanics and  overcome their anxieties before the actual game-play session.

## Implications and conclusion

Previously existing scholarship on game-based learning in the literature classroom focused on generative gameplay in the form of "Choose-your-own-adventures" (Mills 2022). While such games allow students to design branching narratives, our study demonstra tes that generative role-play game-based learning takes this a step further. By giving students power and control over their identity within the game, role-playing games provide a transformational learning experience, aligning with  our findings on identity  exploration and transformative learning theory.

The power of role-playing games in literature or history classrooms is apparent from our results. Students can re-create and live in various historical or fictional worlds, learning about that world in a visceral way by taking on their own identity within it. This aligns with our situated learning theoretical framework, where learning occurs through engagement with the environment (Lave & Wen ger, 1991). For educators in other disciplines, the  key question becomes: What identities would you like your students to explore? Who would you like them to become as a result of the knowledge they gain in your clas sroom? Our study suggests that if identity exploration through role-play can be introduced, it will have a profound eff ect on how students see themselves in relation to possible professions or fields of study, supporting the dynamic systems model of role identity theory we employed. For researchers, the task is to study the efficacy of generative role-play game-based learning across a wide array of disciplines and knowledge types. For example, there's potential to use this approach to help at-risk youth learn life skills. By creating a "successful"  identity for themselves in a fictional world, stud ents might in turn see themselves as capable of navigating real-life challenges, a hypothesis that aligns with our findings on self-determination theory (Ryan & Deci, 2000). Finally, considering that role-playing games are often video games, future research could explore how combining design, engineering, coding, critical thinking, and narrative studies might offer a holistic, hands-on education that bridges the gap between art and science. While our study was limited to a college-level science fiction course, these findings sugges t broader applications that warrant further investigation. Fu ture research should explore the effectiveness of t his approach in different educational contexts and with diverse student populations.
