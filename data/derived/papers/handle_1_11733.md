# Delighted to Share: Youth-Centered Design Documentation and Portfolio Practices in Architectural Learning

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Architectural education often reinforces inequities through high-pressure critiques and subjective evaluations. This study explores how design documentation and portfolio creation, grounded in constructionism, fosters engagement and excitement in architectural learning. During a 4-day summer camp, youth (ages 13-15) created portfolios to document their design work. Findings highlight how integrating youth-centered documentation practices fosters an exciting learning environment, transforming portfolio creation into an engaging process that sustains interest in architecture.

## Introduction and background

Architecture has long been marked by labor inequities, social injustic es, and gender imbalances (Frichot, 2022; Groat & Ahrentzen, 1996). These challenges extend into architectural education, where high-stakes final project presentations and critique sessions (Flynn, 2020) often overshadow opportunities for meaningful documentation, sharing, and reflection. Consequently, students frequently perceive evaluations as subjective, non-transparent, and inconsistent (Elbellahy, 2020).

This study examines how portfolio assessment, grounded in constructionism, f osters engagement and excitement as an alternative to traditional evaluation. While portfolios  are well established in architectural education (Clarke & Boud, 2016), a constructionist perspective frames them as “objects-to-learn-with” (Fields et al., 2018, p. 214)—tools that support learning through iterative reflection and design exploration. Constructionism emphasizes learning through making, where students create and share  personally meaningful artifacts (Papert, 1980). In this view, portfolio assessment is not merely documentation but an interactive process in which students shape their narratives and articulate design intentions. By integrati ng sociocultural dimensions such as collaboration and community-building, portfolios foster ownership and agency (Peppler & Keune, 2019), offering a more equitable alternative to traditional assessments. However, portfolios can also be time-intensive and disrupt the creative flow (Peppler & Keune, 2019). Recent approaches emphasize the importance of embedding documentation w ithin the design and making processes (Tseng, 2016). Yet, it remains unclear how integrating portfolios into architectural education can foster excitement in documenting design work, particularly in counteracting the  pressures associated with traditional architectural critiques. Thus, this study asked: How does design documentation for portfolio creatio n foster engagement and excitement among young people in documenting the architectural design process?

## Methods

This study, part of a larger project on robotic printing in architecture, explores excitement in documenting design work for portfolio creation in a 4-day summer camp (6 hours per day) at a German technical universit y. Participants engaged in architectural activities while documenting their processes and sharing their designs. Each day, small groups of participants used tablets to capture photographs of  their design work and outcomes, which they arranged alongside reflective texts. They then shared their wo rk with peers and course facilitators. Participants had access to tablets with editing software (e.g.,  Adobe Express) and social media apps (e.g., Instagram, Snapchat). Additionally, they had access to a documentation station equipped with a photo studio, lighting, backgrounds, rotating plates, tripods, and instant cameras. O n the final day, groups used a portfolio template (Figure 1, left) to organize their reflections and highlig hts of the activities. Each portfolio page represented one of the robotic printing activities they had participated in, resulting in 12 portfolio pages. The final day was dedicated to completing the portfolios, improving prior documentat ion, and capturing participants' accumulated experiences.

The workshop involved nine girls (self-identified, ages 13-15), with seven consenting to participate, working in groups of three. We collected 81 hours of video data showing design documentation and portfolio creation practices. To analyze how the document ation process fostered excitement among participants, we thematically and iteratively analyzed and coded both the video data and the portfolios to capture instances where students explicitly conveyed enthusiasm about their work. Our inductive codes were: (1) smiles and laughter, (2) joking, (3) hugging, (4) taking selfies, and (5) youth media culture refer ences and memes.

## Findings

All groups exhibited excitement in multiple ways throughout the design docume ntation process and portfolio creation. Despite no specific instructions, two of the three groups  incorporated selfies into their portfolios, showcasing a spontaneous and playful approach to capturing their design experi ences. One group, consisting of three girls (Luna, Nora, and Sophia; pseudonyms), compiled their portfolio by arranging their photographs and reflections according to the provided template. As they worked, the group showed visible excitement through smiles, laughter, and joking. When prompted by the template to reflect on their design process (Figure 1, middle), Nora suggested, “Let’s include our self ie (under the text).” Luna and Sophia eagerly agreed, responding with a high-pitched “Yes!” and laughter, highlighting their enthusiasm for the task.. Similarly, another group, consisting of two girls (Ava and Clara; ps eudonyms), added a selfie to their reflection page. They took a playful selfie using a Snapchat filter  that added devil horns (Figure 1, right). In addition to incorporating selfies, this group further engaged with youth media culture by adding a caption referencing a viral social media meme: “Another day, another  slay, peeeerioooodtdtdtd.” This playful caption, originating from a viral video of a young girl’s catchphrase, emphasiz ed their enthusiasm and engagement with the portfolio-making process, turning it into a fun, culturally relevant activity.

## Discussion

Through our analysis, we identified that integrating youth-centered design documentation and portfolio creation practices—such as taking selfies, designing memes, and capturing moments of vis ible excitement—transformed architectural design documentation into an engaging and exciting process. T his approach contrasts with the formal, high-pressure environment typical of traditional architectural critiques, highlighting the potential of these documentation and portfolio creation practices to enhance learning and sust ain interest in architecture among young people. Designing environments where documentation is embedded as an exciting, creative, and reflective activity can help counteract the inequities and pressures of traditiona l architectural education, making the field more inclusive and engaging. By aligning with constructionist approaches, t he focus shifted from evaluation to reflective practice. Participants personalized their portfoli os using digital photography, editing software, and the documentation station, incorporating elements like selfies and memes. These artifacts fostered a sense of connection to their work, turning the portfolio into a personally meaningful project and creating a supportive and comfortable learning environment. The next steps include surveying or int erviewing participants to explore how their positive experiences and emotions shape their motivation to document  the architectural design process and their engagement with the field.
