# Recognizing Group-Level Epistemic Emotions during Knowledge Building Activities

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Epistemic emotions are vital in collaborative learning and knowledge building but are challenging to recognize and understand at the group-level. This study introduces EmoSense as an approach and novel prototype that leverages facial recognition and self-reported surveys to recognize students' epistemic emotions during knowledge building activities, demonstrating its feasibility in an authentic knowledge building environment. Through this study, EmoSense provided valuable insights into collaborative learning’s affective dimension, informing better design of learning environments. This research contributes to a deeper understanding of epistemic emotions and the influence on knowledge building processes, while informing development of innovative approaches for advancement towards group-based emotions.

## Introduction and background

Epistemic emotions, which arise from the information alignment during knowledge-generation processes (Muis et al., 2018), are integral to learning and knowledge creation (Muis et al., 2015). These include anxiety, boredom, confusion, curiosity, enjoyment, frustration, and s urprise. While positive emotions like enjoyment and pride are linked to achievements (Pekrun et al, 2017), negative emotions like confusion (D’Mello et al., 2014)  and frustration (Pekrun & Stephens, 2012) can be beneficial to learning. As emotions play an important role in collaborative learning, it is paramount to conceptu alize and investigate the role of emotions as part of social interactions and conditions for engagement or motivation (Ludvigsen, 2016). In k nowledge building, an approach focused on advancing community knowle dge (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2010), epistemic emotions like confusion can enhance collaborative discourse (Zhu et al., 2019) and the fostering of epistemic emotions is viewed to be crucial for high-level discourse moves and knowledge creation (Yang et al., 2024). Prior research explored epistemic emotions via multimodal data (Lee et al., 2024; Teo et al., 2022b) and examined epistemic beliefs as predictors (Rosman & Mayer, 2018), showing that advanced epistemic beliefs have positive emotional effects. However, the collection and analysis of student epistemic emotions remain complex and challenging, with no standardized methods for doing so at an individual or group level. This study focuses on identifying student epistemic  emotions and collating them at the group-level to provide understandings during knowledge building, in recognition of the community's need to adapt to members' cognitive and emotional needs  in order to enable advances across social, cognitiv e and emotional dimensions (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2021). Importantly, this research does not examine group-based emotion as experienced by individuals on behalf of a group (Niedenthal & Brauer, 2012). As group-level emotions are difficult to detect via content analysis or observation (Hou et al., 2023), this study introduces EmoSense, a prototype that recognizes students’ epistemic emotions and contributes to group-level understanding during knowledge building activities. It is built upon prior individual-level work using facial and physiological sensors (Lee et al., 2023) that identified active, bored, and interested states, but suffered from low accuracy, thus motivating EmoSense’s development. EmoSense combines a web-based facial landmark recognition application and a n Epistemic Emotion Survey (EES) for validation, deployed synchronously  to analyze student epistemic emotions. Pilot results from the latest iteration of a Knowledge Building Design Studio (sKBDS; Teo et al., 2022a) showed promising insights. As EmoSense aims to enhance awareness of the emotional dimension in collaborative learning, this research also addresses the research question: How can student epistemic emotions be recognized and contribute to group-level understandings during knowledge building activities?

## Method and analysis

Data was collected from a June 2024 sKBDS involving over 30 teachers and students (Grades 5 to 10) engaged in three days of knowledge building activities focu sed on sustainability. Students collaborated in groups of 5-6, aiming to present solutions and prototypical work at the end of the sKBDS. Epistemic emotions were periodically surveyed using an EES instrument adapted from Pekrun et al. (2017), and group discussions were recorded with a 360-degree camera. While two groups consented to the study, only one group’s data was complete for analysis. Student epistemic emotions were analyzed through (1) self-reported EES survey responses and (2) facial expressions during knowledge building. While audio data was captured, the focus was on visual facial expression analysis alongside EES data.  Specifically for this study, d ata from day 2 of the sKBDS was analyzed at three junctures after key events, namely, after the expert talk, reflection, and idea building (at EES2, EES4, and EES7 respectively). The EES data from one group of six students was aggrega ted and corresponding video recordings were flattened for 2D analysis. OpenFace (Baltrušai tis et al., 2016), an open-source tool for facial landmark detection and affective computing, was used to iden tify the six students and perform facial action unit (AU) recognition. These AUs represent observer-based measurements of facial actions (Cohn et al., 2007) and up to 18 AUs were identified from this study, with intensity ranging from 0 (non-presence) to 5 (maximum intensity).

## Results and discussion

Student epistemic emotion  intensities and EES responses were collated to gain a collective sense of what the group of students were feeling across the knowledge  building activities during the sKBDS. Through the EES, students indicated a heightened sense of curiosity, interest, and excitement, and were also generally happy during the knowledge building activities. Results show that the self-reported emotions by the group of students can be complemented and represented by the presence and patterns of AUs that were analyzed from the students’ facial features.  For example, collective curiosity, interest and excitement can be represent ed by a weighted combination of AUs 1+5+7 +20, which traditionally signals interest and tinges of surprise and fear. This is an interesting development and will be further investigated to better understand students’ affective states, not as individual silos of feelings and reactions, but rather as a mixed bag of epistemic emotions.

Although there is a possibility that this standalone observation may be limited to the sKBDS setting, the proposed EmoSense approach of investigating student epistemic emotion s in this study  has shown that it is possible for cross-context implementation with novel approaches and use of emergent technolog ies. Similarly, the absence of certain AUs  and combinations  can also be inferred to show that certain emotions of negative valence are not present during knowledge building activities, and this can be construed as a positive development. For example, combinations of AUs 1+2+4 that indicate fear, sadness, and confusion  are mostly not detected in this study, which means that knowledge building activities in sKBDS often evoke emotions with positive valence such as interest, excitement, and happiness.

Last but not least, the method and results from this study helped answer the research question, regarding how student epistemic emotions can be recognized and used for further understanding at the group-level during knowledge building.  As shown through this study, it is possible to do so in a feasible and potentially scalable manner, with considerations of existing pedagogical and technological limitations. It is anticipated that future and upcoming work could be built on this study to allow for investigations on a larger scale, and to reliably recognize and understand epistemic emotions across contexts in collaborative environments.
