# Emphasizing the Role of Coordination Class Theory on the Study of Student Learning With Representations

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Visual representations are a core feature of teaching and learning in STEM disciplines. In all STEM fields, visual representations are used to describe phenomena, support problem solving, and aid communication. A significant body of research has documented that STEM students face challenges interpreting, generating, and translating representations across STEM curricula resultant from deficits in students’ “representational competence” skills. In this paper we examine the theoretical underpinnings of representational competence (RC) and argue that the constraints of RC can be remedied by instead applying coordination class theory. Considering two examples of students’ problem-solving with representations, we illustrate how developing coordination classes better explain the observed challenges students face with external representations in STEM disciplines.

## Introduction

The teaching and learning of visual representations are key parts of education, particularly in STEM fields. How students understand representations is inextricably  linked to their development of content knowledge s ince a common way of communicating conceptual information is through external modes of visualization (e.g., images, videos, simulations). Typically, the analysis of students’ learning with representations is related to their conceptual understanding through the correct use of disciplina ry representations (Nitz et al., 2014; Stieff, 2011). The theoretical construct that often frames such resear ch is known as representational competence (RC) and  is a presumed barrier for learning in STEM (Kohnle et al., 2020; Kozma & Russell, 2005; Nitz et al., 2014).  RC assumes that as students develop their ability to u tilize disciplinary representations their learning of associated conceptual knowledge is facilitated. However, such an assumption neglects the myriad ways that novice learners interpret and understand the features of external r epresentations. We propose that reconceptualizing representational competence as developing coordination classes will effectively address the shortcomings of RC. Coordination class theory (diSessa & Sherin, 1998) offers a perspective on student learning with representations that considers internal cognitive processes beyond demonstrated skills that better characterize the ways students employ their prior knowledge to make meaning out of  representational features. While we do not present  an empirical study that directly tests our claims, we do utilize some exemplar cases from prior research conducted by the authors that demonstrate the application of coordination class theory as opposed to representat ional competence. By doing so we provide researchers with an alternative approach to studying external representations that may provide more productive outcomes for research and instruction.

## Theoretical framework

The representational competence construct was devel oped from work by Kozma and Russell who studied the ways novices and experts interpret, generate, and transform chemistry representations (Kozma & Russell, 1997). Invoking the Knowledge-in-Pieces framework (KiP; (diSessa, 1993), Kozma and Russell argued that chemistry learners develop a fragmented understanding of chemistry concepts because they lack prior knowledge based on observable phenomena in their everyday lives (Kozma  & Russell, 1997). While intuitive knowledge elemen ts originate from direct experiences in domains such a s physics (e.g., p-prims), chemistry instruction re lies more heavily on visual representations to describe the s ub-microscopic nature of phenomena. Therefore, Kozm a and Russell argued that conceptual change in chemistry is inhibited by students’ difficulty interpreting representations and inability to apply representations in problem-solving contexts. Their observations led them to develop a set of representational skills they believed necessary for the advancement of novices to expert-level conc eptual understanding in chemistry.

While the RC construct leverages several aspects of KiP, Kozma and Russell are unclear on how these elements function through the RC skills to promote conceptual change. Additionally, much of the scholarship that invokes RC as a theoretical construct and dimension of analysis only consider whether students demonstrate RC skills. This leads to binary classifications of whe ther students “have” or “do not have” RC, which nar rows the scope of interpretations and useful insights from such research. Coordination class theory (diSessa & Sherin, 1998) provides a perspective that can answer these how and why questions while also characterizing the connections students make between features of external representations and their prior knowledge.

While diSessa and Sherin explored the implications of KiP in physics, they recognized that there was a common issue of how concepts are discussed and stud ied in education research that fails to account for  how learners establish an understanding of a concept ac ross representations or phenomena. They argued that conceptual change for some core concepts in STEM is better conceived as the development of coordination classes that include two structural components. First, readout strategies describe how students interpret information from the external world; second, causal nets describe ho w students create meaning based on their interpreta tions and develop connections to their prior knowledge. The readout strategies students use to extract information from the world and their assumptions involving underlying knowledge elements that comprise causal nets are integral to understanding how students progress from novices to experts. Additionally, students can coordinate information through their readout strategies in two ways: integ ration and invariance. The former describes how one  must attend to different pieces of information within a situation to formulate a comprehensive understanding. The latter refers to how an idea can be presented in different ways across multiple situations. With respect to representations, a student must come to recognize how one representation communicates information about underlying phenomena through various features (integration), as well as how different representations may describe the same information but in different ways (invariance).

When comparing representational competence to coord ination classes, the former lends itself to representational skills being distinct yet related to content knowledge. It is ambiguous whether Kozma and Russell truly contest that RC is separate from content knowledge, yet later research that invokes the construct has come to treat RC and conceptual change as independent outcomes (e.g., Nitz et al., 2014; Stieff, 2011; Stieff & DeSutter, 2021). By considering the inherent connections made between representations and the concepts they communicate, coordination class theory allows us to better understand how students develop content knowledge through the use of disciplinary representations. Since the connecti ons novice students make between knowledge elements  and representational features are less likely to be as straightforward and canonical as the connections we see experts create, it requires deeper consideration of the ways in which students reason. As such, coordination class theory provides a more fine-grained approach by considerin g what the student interprets from a visual represe ntation together with how they apply their prior knowledge.

Reframing representational competence as coordination class theory In proposing this alternative to understanding how students engage with visual representations, we present a set of exemplars that demonstrate how researchers might apply coordination class theory. We describe how students interpret pre-existing disciplinary representations as well as how they generate their own representations through sketching. These examples are from prior research conducted by the authors to gain a better understanding of how students engage with chemistry representations, how ever, coordination classes were not a framing theor y or approach to analysis. Importantly, the discussion o f these examples is not intended to be a developed methodological approach for the application of coordination class theory. Rather we seek to broadly describe how coordination class theory could be invoked in research on external representations and the resultant implications. The first example is from the first author’s dissertation research that included semi-structured interviews with thirty first-semester general chemistry students. These interviews were conducted to understand how students use different chemistry representations when introd uced to a novel topic area. Students were provided with instructional materials that included a short readi ng passage introducing the topic of molecular geome try along with a chart of all fifteen molecular geometries with example molecules in one of three different representations (ball-and-stick, dash-wedge, or Lewis dot). The second example stems from a larger project involving high school students’ engagement with a design-based chemistry curriculum that employs workbook activities in conjunction with sub-microscopic simulations (Stieff et al., 2012). This specific activity prompted students to draw what they thought water would look like on macroscopic, microscopic, and sub-microscopic scales and modalities. ## Conclusion

This paper demonstrates the theoretical power of ap plying coordination class theory to the study of students’ engagement with disciplinary representations. Rathe r than taking the narrow focus of whether students can correctly use and interpret representations, coordi nation class theory emphasizes the ways in which students understand external representations and link them to underlying phenomena. Moreover, coordination class theory provides a more detailed understanding of how students engage prior knowledge that other frameworks, such as diagram comprehension (Zhang, 1997), do not investigate closely. Researchers may use this information to develop int erventions and targeted instructional practices that redirect the focus in learning with representations  to the connections formed between representational  features and the concepts they communicate, rather than the procedural use of disciplinary representations in s pecific contexts. Additionally, it would be of interest to investigate how different forms of instruction infl uence the development of readout strategies and causal nets around the use of representations. Specifically, how instruction that emphasizes and describes the connections betwe en representational features and underlying phenome na would help engage students in useful readout strate gies that will allow them to make further connectio ns and inferences in their causal nets. In doing so, students may be better equipped to identify aspects of invariance and integration in disciplinary representations as well as generating representations in novel contexts.
