# How does Gender Influence Math Classes?: A case of a Storytelling Activity about Learning Vector

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In this study, the teacher specifically uses storytelling as a tool for drama-based learning, which offers a space for students to co-construct meaning together. The teacher embeds the storytelling teaching method into a math class for a new educational lens. By collecting classroom data and trying to focus on the students’ understanding and engagement, we explore students’ different perspectives on learning the concept of vectors. Through classroom dialogues and students’ storytelling performances, we aim to figure out the relationships between students’ genders and storytelling activities. This study incorporates learning observation and interviews with three 8th graders.

## Introduction

Drama-based pedagogy (DBP) applies dynamic and impactful methods to involve students in academic, emotional, and artistic learning by fostering dialogic meaning-making across all facets of the curriculum (Dawson & Lee, 2016). In this approach, participants  are directed by a leader to envision, enact, and co ntemplate human experiences. We defined DBP include storytelling, role plays, pe rformance…Embedding storytelling into the class creates students an imaginary world that allo ws them to engage in learning i n a more equal way. Nevertheless, the degree of engagement fluctuates b ased on the gender of participants, and individuals concurrently navigate social roles in their daily l ives that coexist with any imagined roles (Goffman,  1974). The memory of personal experience, cultural backgrounds, race, gender, soc ial economics, etc., will all influence the position, frame, and roles they play in the drama activity. Certain scholars, such as Spender (1982), assert that the favoring of boys is a nearly constant practice.

This paper presents a qualitative study of a DBP-embedded math class. We concentrate on the only girl’s participation in a three-member group storytelling activity and the interact ion between her and the other male participants. By using discourse analysis, we find that instructors who utilize DBP in their classes need to consider the problem of gender inequity. The significance of this study is to contextualize the only girl’s experience in the activity as a way to better comprehend gender identities in the DBP class.

## Theoretical framework

Acquiring knowledge requires immersing students in real circumstances so they can engage in the activi ties to make their experience unforgettable. Through a soci ological lens, the words of Vygotsky and Bruner hig hlights that learning is a social activity. Vygotsky (1980) points out that “learning awakens a  variety of internal developmental processes that can operate only when the child is interacting with people in his environ ment and cooperation with his peers.” Ultimately, social con texts comprise collect ively established and endorsed interpretations of situations, influencing the soci al actions individuals undertake in response to tho se definitions (Erickson, 1984). Our study examines how human beings interact with each other through languaging in a classroom setting. The term is involved in every interaction or communication between individuals, whether it be collaborative work, ideological discussions, casual encounters in everyday life, political connections, and so forth (Volosinov, 1973). Words can al so function as aesthetic, ethical, or scientific me anings. Students’ language, identity, ideology, etc., were all influenced by th e large social contexts, including race, gender, cu ltural norms, socioeconomic and so forth, which we all defined as personhood in this study.

## Context

We go to one of the famous schools in a medium-sized district in the midwestern the US. According to the school’s website, girls constitute 48% of the student popula tion and others are all boys. The student-teacher ratio is 16:1. 50 students in the 8th grade volunteered to attend our after-class study. We randomly selected 30 students to join the study. They had already learned the coordinate system and had no previous knowledge of vectors. Fi rst, we paired them up as groups. Each group had 2-4 students, but each group had at least one boy and one girl. Most of them knew each other, and some were siblings or cla ssmates. Every group had 3-4 activities during a 2-hour session. We had 8 sessions in total. However, this paper only focuses on one group’s storytelling activity. Through the activity, they explored the concept of vectors. What they were supposed to learn was a sense of vector and its properties (i.e., direction and magnitude). Student s were notified that there was no learning pressure  as any of their ideas and thoughts would contribute to this research. All the activities included playing mini soccer, playing computer games, constructing stories, etc.

In this case study, we chose one of the sessions wi th a storytelling case inside to display the differ ences between boys and girls in learning performance. Not iceably, in this session, there is only one girl and two boys, so the two boys and the male teacher form male soli darity. The story they made were about: two boys pr etended they were Salah and Mane from Liverpool, and the gi rl was the gatekeeper in the opposite team. Finally, Salah and Mane kicked the ball to the girl’s head, and th ey scored. Our research questions is How do teachers and students, students and fellow students co-construct stories together to explain the learning objectives with contexts?

## Methodology

Micro discourse analysis Bloome (2021) points out that Micro Discourse analysis is the procedure of studying how people act and react to each other through language. “Practices that recruit one or more modalities (e.g., oral or written language, images, equations, symbols, sounds,  gestures, graphics, artifacts) to communicate disti nctive types of meanings.” (Gee, 2003). In this study, we will use oral dialogue as a bridge to identify students’ interactions and the n explore in what ways they interact differently.

## Data collection (tools)

All class sessions were audiotaped and videotaped. We collected data via class observation, documents,  and interviews.

This study contained fully participatory unstructured observation. We investigated the students’ dialogue and drama performance in our teaching process. Due to the open nature of unstructured observations, in addition to complete teaching video recording, this study had an observation record table that included the inf ormation of “who”, “interaction”, and “footnote.” Documents in this study include these items, such as the teacher’s course plans, teaching videos in the storytelling activity.

We conducted a one-on-one semi-structured interview with each participant after cl ass, each session lasting about 5-10 minutes to understand how they felt about the storytelling activity.

## Data analysis

What we want to understand is how teachers and stud ents construct stories, make meanings and interact with others through DBP. We specifically focused on girls’ language and performance. For analysis, all intervi ew recordings were transcribed, we use NVivo with manu ally coding from the bottom to the top. The analysi s of qualitative data consisted of three coding cycles. In the first cycle, the descriptive coding analysis allowed us to determine the terms for presenting the language int eraction between boys and girls, patterns such as “ salience”, “ignorance”, “openness” etc. were taken into consideration. We then conducted a second round of focused coding which allow us to identify the most frequent or sig nificant codes. In the third round, two coders put generated patterns into NVivo, and it gave us the final codes.

## Implications

Research indicates that DBP contributes to developi ng social skills, including conflict resolution wit h peers (Walsh-Bower & Basso,1999), and recognizing others’ emotio ns (Smith, 2010). By imagining social roles and engaging in dialogues, drama brings forth unexpected linguistic, imagistic, and semiotic capabilities in a changing world. Here, opportunities for rehearsing, deepening emotions, and transforming into writing and other modes of expression abound for both action and identity (Edmiston, 2014; Enciso, 2011). Students are supposed to engage in a different world and dress up like their characters, which help them create new positions that differ from other than reality. However, the girl’s inequitable learning experience begins to help us understand the multiple nuanced ways with which some students negotiate their identities in DBP class. There are power dynamics that negatively position girls (in this case P2 is the one who is positioned by other boys) within an oppressive environment, which could damage their dialogues.

Further research is necessary to discover what kind s of power and positioning are likely to foster the development of a girl’s gender identity. More research may reveal how DBP class researchers and teachers create and support such contexts. These topics are worth e xploring in future research to extend our comprehen sion of the girl’s gender identity and make DBP class a ric her part of students’ lives. Moreover, it will faci litate mutual understanding between teachers and students, as well as students and fellow students.
