# Expansive Framing as an Asset-based Approach for Transfer: A Theoretical Integration

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Expansive framing is a situative model for promoting learning transfer. The model shares several foundational assumptions with asset-based approaches for teaching and learning, including the Funds of Knowledge and Funds of Identity frameworks. Both expansive framing and the Funds of Knowledge and Funds of Identity frameworks recognize students as inherently knowledgeable, experienced social actors who bring valuable breadths of competence to classroom settings, and moreover, hold that students should be empowered to leverage their out-of-school knowledge and experiences to frame disciplinary learning opportunities in the classroom. This paper presents a theoretical integration to surface key synergies between expansive framing and the Funds of Knowledge and Funds of Identity frameworks. Drawing on scholarship focused on each of these frameworks, this theoretical integration aims to open pathways for applying these frameworks together in practice and ultimately to contribute to foundations for advancing equity-centered research and practice for supporting learning transfer.

## Introduction

Expansive framing (Engle et al., 2011) is model for learning transfer that emerged from situative views of knowing and learning (Greeno et al., 1993; Greeno et al., 1 996). Expansive framing contends that transfer is likely to take place when learners make connections between a given learning context and people, places, topics, and ti mes beyond that context, are positioned as authors of disciplinary content, and are held accountable for the ideas they author (Engle et al., 2012). An appreciation for le arners’ experiences beyond a given disciplinary learning environment as valuable for transfer is inherent in  expansive framing. This appreciation for connectio ns beyond classroom learning environments aligns closely with the Funds  of Knowledge (FOK; Moll & Greenberg, 1990; Moll et al., 1992) and Funds of Identity (FOI; Esteban-Guitart & Moll, 2014) frameworks, two well-known assetbased perspectives of teaching and learning. Given this a lignment and its potential for instructional application, expansive framing may open pathways for designing implementable practices based on  the FOK and FOI frameworks. This theoretical integration  surfaces these pathways by  highlighting alignments between  two historically distinct lines of inquiry: research on  learning transfer and research concerned with brid ging assetbased frameworks with instructional practice. The FOK and FOI frameworks aim to position learners ’ diverse breadths of experience and social and cultural knowledge as assets in educational settings  (Subero et al., 2017). FOK —and by extension, FOI—are characterized by  the foundational assumption that “people are compet ent, they have knowledge, and their life experiences have given them that knowledge” (Gonzál ez et al., 2005, p. IX). Further, these frameworks hold learners’ out-of-school knowledge and experiences as “valuable educational resources” (Esteban-Guitart & Moll, 2014, p. 5) capable of supporting success in school. These perspectives reject deficit orientations towards diversity that pervade systems of educational practice and policy whereby non-dominant ways of knowing and learning are positioned as detrimental for academic achievement (Valencia, 2010; and while there are distinctions between the FOK and FOI frameworks, a detailed review of these distinctions is beyond the scope of this paper). Expansive framing, while not explicitly designed to confront deficit orientations, shares salient foundational assumptions with FOK and FOI. Expansively framed learning environments rely on an inherently asset-based view of learners’ knowledge and experiences beyond formal classroom c ontexts as valuable for learning. The FOK and FOI frameworks encourage “teachers to draw on student experiences and priorities in schooling, thus validating student knowledge and life values, and enabling them to scaffold student learning from the familiar” (Hogg, 2011, p. 667). Expansive framing relies on the empowerment of students to forge these connections themselves as they engage in classroom learning activities, which offers opportunities to leverage the theore tical values of FOK and FOI in practice.

Distinctions in origin and intent Despite foundational synergies between expansive fr aming and FOK and FOI, the frameworks have differen t theoretical origins and emerged in pursuit of diffe rent educational goals. FOK —and eventually FO I—were theorized with the explicit goal of expanding asset-based perspectives of learners’ diverse ways of knowing a nd learning (Moll et al., 1992). The FOK framework ori ginated in anthropological research interested in t he household and labor practices of Mexican American f amilies in the American Southwest (Wolf, 1966; Véle zIbáñez, 1983; Vélez -Ibáñez, 1988). Scholars have since explored ways in which learners ’ funds of knowledge help them navigate educational systems (González et  al., 1995). In response to perceived limitations o f the FOK framework, the FOI framework was developed t o uncover knowledge, experiences, and practices that are more personally meaningful to school-aged learners themselves (Rios-Aguilar et al., 2011; Esteban-Guitart & Moll, 2014).

While expansive framing shares key assumptions abou t knowledge and learning with FOK and FOI, it emerged from a different tradition of educational research and was not explicitly designed to serve equity-centered goals. Expansive framing evolved from Engle and Con ant’s (2002) research on productive disciplinary engagement (PDE) to offer a situative approach for fostering transfer. Using data from Engle and Conant’s (2002) initial PDE study, Engle (2006) explored ways in wh ich framing may lead to learning transfer between an initial classroom learning context and a performance assess ment representative of a far transfer setting (i.e., a setting contextually far-removed from the initial learning setting; Barnett & Ceci, 200 2). This work prompted experimental research on relationships between expa nsive (as opposed to bounded) framing of learning experiences and subsequent transfer (Engle et al., 2011) and ultimately led to Engle et al.’s (2012) v iew that transfer may occur when learners experience intercontextuality (e.g., Floriani, 1993) in initial learning contexts and are positioned as authors rather than consumers  of disciplinary knowledge. Engle and colleagues (2 012) characterize expansive framing as a situative approach for fostering transfer because of its goal s of creating a broad social context encompassing of both a given initial learning context and possible future transfer contexts. Theoretical synergies Despite differences in origin and initial intent, Engle et al.’s (2012) five explanations for how expansive framing leads to transfer align closely with the asset-based views of out-of-school knowledge and experiences upheld by the FOK and FOI frameworks. Engle and colleagues’ (2012) explanations open pathways for bridging in-school learning contexts with students’ lives outside of s chool, which presumably are characterized to some e xtent by their culturally-based funds of knowledge and identity. The five explanations are as follows:

1. Students recognize that they will use what they are  learning in future contexts, both within and beyon d

school settings. This encourages them to adopt more robust and effective learning strategies.

2. Students recognize that prior learning is relevant to possible future transfer contexts. This recognit ion

encourages them to visualize possible transfer contexts.

3. Students view what they have previously learned as useful for current learning experiences. This

encourages them to transfer in what they have previously learned to support their engagement in initial learning contexts.

4. Authoring content in initial learning contexts supp orts students’ confidence in their ability to use (i.e.,

author) that knowledge in potential transfer environments.

5. Students’ experiences of repeatedly authoring conte nt in the initial learning environment generalize t o

future transfer contexts, helping them become comfo rtable authoring and adapting new knowledge in new environments.

The first three explanations posit that having stud ents experience intercontextuality while taking part in initial learning activities supports future transfer. When classroom environments foster intercontextuality, s tudents are encouraged to make explicit connections between dis ciplinary content and future transfer contexts (in and out of school), connections between what they have learned  previously and possibl e future transfer contexts, and connections between prior learning and the present learning environment. These connections between classroom learning contexts and contexts beyond the boundaries of the classroom environment lead to the subsummation of school contexts and students’ lives outside of school into one broad, interconnected social context. Students’ funds of knowledge and funds of identity naturally contribute to intercontextuality. When instructors position students to draw on people, pl aces, times, and topics beyond the classroom contex t as they engage in disciplinary learning activities,  students are given authority to make connections to  their funds of knowledge and funds of identity. Asking learners to  make connections to future contexts as stated in Engle and colleagues’ first explanation positions them  to recognize possible out-of-school contexts they may encounter in the future that are personally —and possibly culturally —relevant to them. For example, an expansively frame d activity encourages students to consider how what t hey are learning might be relevant to a future prof essional or social experience. These experiences could be characteristic of a student’s funds of knowledge  when they align with the household or labor practices of their home  communities, and they may relate to a student’s fu nds of identity if they are relevant to personally meaning ful experiences the student can envision themselves  pursuing (i.e., in potential transfer contexts). We can extend this to Engle and colleagues’ second explanation that framing prior learning as relevant for future transfer contexts helps students recognize such opportunities for transfer. Not only might potential transfer opportunities have ti es to students’ funds of knowledge and identity, hi ghlighting prior learning as relevant to future transfer opportunities strengthens connections between disciplinary  learning and the social and cultural contexts students visualize themselves navigating beyond the classroom. Engle et al.’s (2012) third explanation positions students to transfer in prior knowledge and experiences to frame classroom learning. Similarly to the first  two explanations, prompting students to transfer i n what they have learned and experienced in the past contributes to intercontextuality. Upholding prior knowledge as explicitly consequential for current learning may encourage  students to draw on relevant household and communit y practices, knowledge, and experiences (possibly cha racteristic of their f unds of knowledge and identity) as valuable for classroom learning, and by extension, conducive to transfer. In other words, expansive framing holds prior knowledge and experiences as “valuable educational resources” (Esteban-Guitart & Moll, 2014, p. 5), which fundamentally aligns  with the FOK and FOI frameworks. Through interconte xtuality, expansive framing effectively “link[s] curricular content with studen ts’ practices, experiences and daily life contexts”  (Llopart & Esteban-Guitart, 2017, p. 270) in ways that align in practice with the central goals of FOK and FOI. The explanations for fostering intercontextuality —built on the notion that students should draw connections between in-school contexts and their lives outside of school —are congruent with practical goals of FOK and FOI. This congruency is especially strong w hen students’ interncontextual links are relevant t o their “historically accumulated and culturally developed bodies of knowledge and skills” that afford their “functioning and wellbeing” in their lives outside of school (Mo ll et al., 1992, p. 133). Var elas and Pappas ’ (2006) study of FOK in an urban elementary school’s integrated lite racy-science classroom offers an example of instructors drawing on students’ intercontextual links to frame  disciplinary engagement. In this study, the author s documented one student’s tendency to frame disciplinary content from a scientific text on gaseous states with his experience boiling water to cook meals at home. When the student volunteered this link during a class discussion between his home experience and the course text, the teacher followed with a series of questions that allowed the student to further expand on this experience and to make more specific juxtapositions with the concepts described in the course text. Moreover, the authors highlight the teacher’s approach to mirroring the language of the student (e.g., asking follow-up questions using his exact language describing the ‘bubbles po pping out’ when boiling water for soup). The teacher’s approach in this cas e held the student’s household experience and language used to describe that experience as valuable for learning.

Varelas and Pappas (2006) conceptualize this exchan ge as an example of the teacher building on a student’s experiential and discursive funds of know ledge to support disciplinary learning. From the pe rspective of expansive framing, the exchange represents the t eacher’s approach to fostering intercontextuality b y actively supporting connections between the student’s life a t home and the disciplinary learning context. This scenario aligns closely with Engle et al.’s (2012) third exp lanation—here, the teacher encouraged the student to transfer in what he had learned from this previous household ex perience to help frame his (and possibly his peers’) engagement in the disciplinary learning setting. In an expansively framed learning environment, the teacher could have advanced the exchange by asking the student how the information from the course text might relate to future possible household experiences in addition to the p rior experience with the boiling soup (per Engle et  al.’s first explanation). In alignment with Engle et al.’s second explanation, the teacher could have highlighted connections between the student’s prior experience with the boi ling soup and future experiences representative of possible future transfer contexts. In this exchange and othe r examples in Varelas and Pappas (2006) of teachers  drawing on students’ funds of knowledge in practice, interc ontextuality gradually developed as teachers gave students opportunities to transfer in their funds of knowledge and identity to frame their engagement in the given classroom contexts.

Basu and Barton’s (2007) study of students’ funds o f knowledge in a middle school science context offers findings that align with intercontextuality even more strongly than those discussed in Varelas and Pappas (2006). Basu and Barton’s (2007) interactions with students, all of whom were members of minoritized racial and socioeconomic groups, during an after-school science program gave the researchers a detai led sense for the students’ funds of knowledge that framed their enga gement in school. Similarly to  Varelas and Pappas (2006), they found that students’ prior and ongoing househo ld and community experiences influenced their orien tations to science learning experiences in school. Basu and  Barton’s analysis, though, focused more specifically on how students made connections between disciplinary science content and their personal and professional aspirations— that is, possible future contexts. This focus aligns with Engle et al.’s (2012) first and second explanations for how expansive framing drives transfer. In one example, the authors detailed a student’s “admiration of the caring role that the women played in her family and in her comm unity and her own desire to care for others through helping to create a healthy lifestyle,” which may be rooted  in the “vital role that caring plays among African  American women in [her] community” (2007, p. 481). The stude nt was particularly drawn to lab equipment used to study the human body because of its connection to healthc are professions (e.g., microscopes used for examini ng hair follicles and saliva). Basu and Barton describe the  student’s project in which she designed a “healthy  yet tasty” (2007, p. 481) candy bar, an experience inspired by  her aspirations for a career in the healthcare industry and a general interest in helping others live healthfully.

Basu and Barton (2007) do not reference the idea of intercontextuality explicitly. It is clear, though, that the students they describe were experiencing intercontextuality by drawing on their funds of knowledge to frame their engagement in the disciplinary learning conte xt through making connections to past culturally-based experiences and possible future experiences where they may transfer what they are learning in school. The student interested in healthcare, for example, used their f unds of knowledge to bridge their home and school c ontexts. Rather than engaging in out-of-school contexts completely separately from in-school contexts, the students with whom Basu and Barton worked framed their engagement  in disciplinary activities in ways that created br oader social contexts encompassing of their lives in thei r homes, communities, the classroom, and future con texts of personal and cultural value.

Engle et al.’s (2012) fourth and fifth explanations  call for positioning students as authors rather than consumers of disciplinary course content. While perhaps not as directly as th e explanations related to intercontextuality, this tenet also aligns with the  FOK and FOI frameworks’ orientation towards studen ts with diverse backgrounds as knowledgeable social actors. The FOK and FOI frameworks recognize that students bring sophisticated breadths of knowledge and experience to classroom settings, and that their funds of knowledge and identity are resources capable of supporting learni ng in those settings. The FOK and FOI frameworks —like expansive framing—therefore hold that prior knowledge and experiences frame students’ engagement with school contexts and t he disciplinary  content they encounter therein. Rather than viewing  students as inexperienced passive consumers of disciplinary knowledge, FOK and FOI position students to engage with classroom learning contexts by way of the authority vested in them thr ough their culturally-mediated bodies of knowledge and experience—to become active producers rather than passive cons umers of knowledge (Subero et al., 2015). In practice, as demonstrated by the studies described by Varelas and Pappas (2006) and Basu and  Barton (2007), expansive framing may create pathways for students to assume positions of episte mic authority. Students’ funds of knowledge and identity may bolster such authority and support their authorship of disciplinary knowledge, and by extension, promote future transfer.

## Implications for practice

Expansive framing complements—and is complemented by—the FOK and FOI frameworks in practice. By giving learners the authority to draw connections between disciplinary content and their funds of knowledge and identity, expansively framed learning environments open pathw ays for integrating students’ funds of knowledge and identity into instructional practice; positioning s tudents to draw these intercontextual links based o n their funds of knowledge and identity gives them authority to v isualize how their social knowledge, backgrounds, a nd experiences are related to disciplinary learning ac tivities and contexts they may navigate in the futu re. The FOK and FOI frameworks also hold promise for complement ing expansively framed environments by surfacing a broader range of intercontextual links that student s may find personally and/or culturally meaningful.  In other words, expansive framing facilitates the integration for the FOK and FOI frameworks in practice, and encouraging students to frame classroom learning exp eriences expansively with their funds of knowledge and identity strengthens the asset-oriented potential of expansive framing. Instructors interested in using expansively framed approaches built on students’ funds of knowledge and identity should encourage their students to generat e their own personally relevant frames. Empowering students to draw their own intercontextual links (rather than relying on those offered by the instructor) reinforces expansive framing’s goal of encouraging students to author co ntent in the learning environment. Empowering stude nts to create their own frames also upholds equitable distributions of power  in the classroom and avoids positioning instructors to mistakenly ‘colonize’ students’ cultural knowledge and experiences (Hughes & Greenhough, 2006). This also helps instructors avoid making wrongful a ssumptions about students’ cultural backgrounds and identities, which would be antithetical to the asset-oriented goals of the FOK and FOI frameworks. Moreo ver, centering students as authors of their own social a nd cultural frames addresses barriers to implementi ng assetbased instructional approaches derivi ng from cultural differences between teachers and t heir students (e.g., Oughton, 2010), as well as differences in funds of knowledge and identity between students themselves in the same classrooms.

Towards an asset-based model for transfer Expansive framing and other situative models of lea rning transfer hold features of context as central to learners’ participation in tasks during initial learning and transfer opportun ities (Greeno et al., 1993; Greeno et al., 1996). Given their focus on learners’ cultural contexts, the FOK and FOI frameworks may offer promise for su pporting situative models for learning transfer. Scholars have criticized the field of research on learning transfer for seldom contending with questions of culture, cultural identity, and diversity as factors that influence likelihood of transfer (e.g., Closson, 2013; Brion, 2022). A model for tra nsfer that extends Engle and colleagues’ (2011; 201 2) principles for expansive framing to explicitly surf ace and build upon learners’ funds of knowledge and  identity could advance the field of transfer research towards more  fully understanding the impacts of cultural context on transfer. Moreover, developing approaches to instru ction and curricular design offer pathways for  supporting transfer in diverse classroom settings and —in turn—for supporting future transfer as learners navigate contexts beyond the classroom.

A transfer model that integrates expansive framing with the FOK and FOI frameworks in practice holds potential for fostering a more culturally sustaining model for transfer. Such a model could serve to prepare learners with diverse breadths of culturally-based knowledge and experiences for both backward and forward transfer; backward transfer wherein expansively framed classroom experiences prompt students to reconsider prior cultural knowledge or experiences as valuable, and forward transfer (or simply transfer in general) wherein prior learning influences participation in novel contexts (Hohense e, 2021). Building on the FOK and FOI frameworks to encourage students to frame learning activities exp ansively could position them to recognize experienc es from (and knowledge linked to) their own home and commun ity settings as assets for the learning activities at hand rather thandeficits by way of backward transfer. L ikewise, positioning students to use their funds of  knowledge and identity to frame learning tasks expansively could help them recognize opportunities to transfer what they are learning in the classroom to contextually diverse c ontexts in the future. Doing so will also advance expansive framing’s goal of thwarting bounded framing (Engle et al., 2011) whereby learning activ ities are  defined as narrowly relevant to the disciplinary content  of interest and affixed to the specific time and pl ace where the activities are occurring —and therefore will rarely lead to transfer beyond c ontextually similar settin gs. Rather than holding students accountable to classroom environments defined by narrow, arbitrary notions of expertise or proficiency, this integration of frameworks could  help students transfer their learning to diverse ranges of future contexts that vary significantly from their initial classroom learning contexts. Expansive framing as conduit for bridging asset-based theories with practice Reviews of empirical research using the FOK and FOI frameworks in practice have highlighted their promise for improving classroom instruction (e.g., Llopart & Esteban-Guitart, 2018; Hogg & Volman, 2020). Findings have shown that using these frameworks to inform curricu la and instruction positively impacts the school ex periences of learne rs from historically underserved cultural backgroun ds; instruction that builds on learners’ funds of knowledge and identity appears to support improved learning outcomes, be tter experiences in school, greater cultural awareness among teachers, and stronger rel ationships between schools and the communities they  serve (e.g., González et al., 2011; Esteban-Guitart & Vila, 2013; Moll, 2014). While the FOK and FOI frameworks have proven powerf ul for combatting deficit orientations on local scales, some scholars have identified limitations capable o f constraining implementation into practice (e.g., Oughton, 2010) due to the localized, ethnographic nature of the established methods for surfacing students’ funds of knowledge and identity. In contexts where teachers lack the time and administrative support necessary to learn about their students’ funds of knowledge and identi ty through home visits or focused study groups, it may be challenging to implement FOK and FOI-based learning activities that are effective and truly aligned with students’ funds of knowledge and identity. Further, implementation becomes more challenging when interested teachers do not share a cultural background with each of their students—as is often the case in diverse classrooms. Oughton (2010), making this case in the context of the Unit ed Kingdom’s national education system, highlights poli cy language that appears to take up a superficial understanding of the FOK framework: “Schools have much to gain from the experiences and understanding of pupils, t heir families and communities. Drawing on their fun ds of knowledge enriches a school in a range of valuable ways” (Department of Education and Skills, 2004, p.8). Oughton’s skepticism of this policy language derive s from a concern that the Department will appropria te the FOK framework with a lack of critical reflection and appreciation for the complexity of culture: It remains to be seen whether this represents a genuine shift in government discourse away from the deficit model, or merely a weakening of the cri tical power of funds of knowledge as a concept. Since the aim of the original funds of kno wledge project was to recognize, celebrate and utilize forms of knowledge which were not value d by dominant educational discourse, the adoption of the term by official rhetoric seems to present a danger of allowing dominant groups to stand in judgement of what does or does not constitute a fund of knowledge. (Oughton, 2010, pp. 67-68) In other words, to what extent will teachers and policymakers who do not take part in traditional methods to better understand their students’ funds of knowledge use the framework as it was originally intended? How can teachers avoid making assumptions about the unique cultural backgrounds of their students, especially when their students carry with them a range of diverse backgrounds that may—or may not—frame their engagement with schooling? If the institutional infrastructure is not in place to encourage teachers to spend time building trust with community members and understanding first-hand what constitute a student’s unique funds of kn owledge and identity, they risk making assumptions about cultures that perpetu ate harmful stereotypes and ultimately essentialize  the experiences of their students.

School contexts not prepared to support teachers as  they design instruction based on students’ funds of knowledge and identity therefore present serious ba rriers to practical implementation. Moreover, and p erhaps ironically, cultural diversity among students in th e same classroom settings presents yet another chal lenge to implementation; while asset-based approaches such as the FOK and FOI frameworks  hold diversity as valuable for learning, this diversity nonetheless could present challenges for teachers interested in drawing on all students’ unique funds of knowledge and identity in their classrooms. Expansive framing —intended to give students authority to draw connections to personally relevan t people, places, times, and topics beyond given cl assroom contexts—could offer a conduit for encouraging them to devel op their own culturally meaningful frames as they engage with classroom learning activities. Instruct ion informed by integrating expansive framing with FOK and FOI would position learners as knowledgeable member s of the classroom community and vest them with the authority to identify and draw connections between disciplinary content and their own unique funds of knowledge and identity.

Rather than teachers taking it upon themselves to b ridge instruction with students’ funds of knowledge and identity,  students should have opportunities  to engage in framing activities wherein  they are given the authority to engage directly with personal, social, and cultu ral knowledge and experiences they find meaningful. These activities could be arts-based, narrative-based, or conversation-based activities in which students access diverse forms of expression and engagement. Teacher s should participate in framing activities with stu dents and engage with any artifacts students produce during the activities. By familiarizing themselves with their students’ frames of knowledge, teachers should gradually deve lop a better understanding of their students’ funds  of knowledge and identity; and in turn, they can  begin to design disciplinary learning activities th roughout which students are encouraged to draw connections to culturally meaningful people, places, times, and topics beyond the bounds of the given disciplinary learning activ ities themselves. Instead of centering abstract disciplinary content at the outset, framing activities foreground students’ funds of knowledge and identity as valuable conduits for learning disciplinary course material. Over time, students should begin to perceive their culturally-based funds of knowledge and identity as enveloped within a shared, broader social context w ith disciplinary content  that transcends boundaries of place and time.

## Conclusion

To some extent, learning contexts that actively empower students to draw connections between disciplinary content and their  funds of knowledge and identity may support expansi ve framing inherently. Researchers and practitioners may find that this theoretical integr ation occurs naturally in practice as learners use their funds of knowledge and identity to frame their disciplinary engagement and participation in classroom learning contexts. Researchers should collaborate with practitioners to explore  specific ways in which expansive framing helps students draw on their funds of knowledge and ident ity, and similarly, how the FOK and FOI frameworks lend themselves to expansively framed learning environme nts. Empirical research using both qualitative and quantitative methods is necessary to explore the extent to which expansive framing and the FOK and FOI frameworks align in formal learning settings. Analyses of survey responses relevant to each of these frameworks could prove useful for exploring latent relations hips between learners’ perceptions of the  elements of expansive framing and opportunities to draw on their funds of  knowledge and identity as valuable assets for disc iplinary learning. Moreover, i nterviews with learners from a range of  cultural background s could offer more nuanced insight into the extent to which students feel expansively framed classroom activities help position their personally relevant funds of knowledge and identity as assets for academic success. A model for learning transfer  that builds on established frameworks such as FOK a nd FOI to expand conceptualizations of context holds promise for  advancing situative theories of transfer and instructional approaches emerging from the situative tradition. An explicitly asset-based framework for supporting transfer is necessary to prepare learners to navigate diverse s ocial, cultural, and professional contexts in their  lives after formal schooling.  This theoretical integration presents a possible way forward  for developing equity-centered transfer models, and by extension, strengthening ef forts to bridge asset-based approaches with instructional practice.
