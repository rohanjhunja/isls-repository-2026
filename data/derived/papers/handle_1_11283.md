# Pedagogies for Executing Impactful AI Literacy Programs

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Given the recent democratization of artificial intelligence (AI), it is imperative to equip educators with the essential conceptual knowledge and competencies required for success as professionals. We describe design and evaluation of two iterations of a two-day synchronous workshop for N = 62 in-service teachers, which offered a non-technical introduction to AI and its cutting-edge applications in education. Teachers’ readiness to engage and build with AI using pre-post validated surveys showed improvements of up to Cohen’s d = 1.63, reflecting the significant potential of brief well-designed AI literacy programs in upskilling educators.

## Introduction

Empowering all citizens to understand the impact of  artificial intelligence (AI) on human lives has be come increasingly important (Allen & Kendeou, 2024). Educators, in particular, need AI education to (i) navigate the complex space of teaching and learning with AI (e.g., evaluating how AI tools work, and if and whether  they might be useful, effective and ethical in particular learning contexts), and (ii) support their students in developing AI literacy (Sperling et al., 2024). However, structured opportunities for educators to learn the foundations of AI, its applications in education and engage in hands-on experiences of building with AI are scarce (Zhang & Zhang, 2024). For instance, a recent US-based survey of N = 1135 educators from October 2024 suggests that 58 % of them have received no AI training (EdWeek, 2024). To address this gap, we developed a two-day (thirteen-hour) workshop for in-service teachers from Singapore schools. The learni ng objectives comprised – (i) developing conceptual knowledge about what AI is and how it’s used in the real world, (ii) understanding various components of machine learning that power  AI systems, (iii) critiquing the benefits and risks  of AI usage, with particular relevance to educational contexts, and (iv) gaining  experience with developing and deploying an AI app lication for education (AIED). Here, we describe our pedagogical reflections from running two iterations of this AI literacy workshop over the past year and robust empirical evidence regarding participants’ improved AI readiness. Theoretical and empirical background AI readiness refers to the preparedness of educators, particularly teachers to effectively integrate AI technologies into educational practices. We draw on one recent conceptualization of this idea from Wang  et al. (2023), who emphasize cognition (knowledge and understanding of AI technologies), ability (practical skills for utilizing AI tools effectively in teaching and learning processes), vision (perceptions of opportunities and challenges presented by AI and the ability to foresee its impact on educ ational practices), and ethics (moral considerations and regulatory compliance related to the use of AI in e ducational contexts) as four interrelated component s of AI readiness. Training programs that foster higher levels of AI readiness in teachers can be expected to  increase capacity and foster a conducive environment for pedagogical innovation while reducing perceptions of AI threats (Sperling et al., 2024).  Empirically, the majority of AI literacy professional development work seems to have focused on K-12 education and personal/lay users (see Pinski & Benlian, 2024 for a recent meta-analysis), with very limited work on training teachers (e.g., Lee et al., 2022; Markus et al., 2024). Often, AI literacy workshops with educators focus on co-design of learning materials to enable them to teach about AI in their classrooms (e.g., Lin et al., 2021; DiPaola  et al., 2023), which differs from the focus of our present work. Quantitative impact evaluation of AI literacy training for teachers is also rare – for example, Lee et al. (2022), who designed a ten-week interactive book club engagement to create a community of practice, used focus group interviews to examine qualitative perceptions of their professional devel opment model. Finally, to the best of our knowledge, research on AI literacy training is scarce in the Asia-Pacific region, the targeted demographic of our present work.

## Participants

Across both iterations of our AI literacy workshop, we had N = 62 participants. Among the n = 57 who completed the pre-post survey, the majority were school teachers (77. 2%, n = 44) with a handful of participants serving as school leaders/heads (10.5%, n = 6) and a few serving specialist roles such as ICT  coordinator, early childhood specialist and IT manager (5.3%, n = 3). The remaining participants came from  tertiary education (7%, n = 4). Most participants had  more than 5 years of teaching experience (93%, n = 53). Many reported interacting with secondary school students the most (77.2%, n = 44). University ethics approval was obtained (IRB-2025-006).

During the two-day workshop, a brief formative assessment quiz tapping into conceptual understanding of AI for education was administered midway (only for the second iteration). ## Introduction to AI (module I)

Drawing on IBM’s AI curriculum framework (IBM Corporation, 2020), our AI literacy program began with a module on foundational knowledge every teacher needs to know to embed AI into their everyday teaching practice (part I – three hours) – (i) knowledge (what is AI, history of AI, applications of AI), (ii) skills (e.g., design thinking, data fluency), and (iii) values (ethical decision making, bias and fairness awarene ss). A suite of constructivist pedagogies balancing telling and self-directed exploration was, however, employed in this workshop to make it extensively hands-on and target various facets of AI literacy, as further described below. Participants were nudged to identify parallels betw een human intelligence and machine intelligence to initiate a discussion on how machines may learn new  skills – this discussion led to an interactive activity wher e everyone worked in groups to come up wit h their own machine learning metaphors  (data, algorithm, model, prediction). Three flawed data subsets deliberately designed to lead to suboptimal results (Sinha & Kapur, 2021; Fields et al., 2021) for an exemplar educational task of predicting test  scores were used for intuition building regarding the importance of data quality (validity and reliability) that powers impactful AI applicati ons. For instance, one data subset comprised irrelevant feat ures like fingerprints while another data subset co mprised noisy/biased features like teacher quality and household income. Neural networks, one common class of machine learning algorithms, were introduced with unplugged activities (Lindner et al., 2019) mimicking how the brain works – participants served as neuron s across different hidden layers that could be acti vated depending on recognition of specific patterns in input images (h andwritten digits). To make the learning experience  embodied (Dai, 2024), participants were instructed to stand or raise their hands with every activation opportunity. A curated set of digits with varying levels of illegibility was further selected to showcase the complex mathematical idea of backpropagation in neural networks, specifically how prediction err ors could lead to adjustment a nd iterative improvements in the activation patterns of the netw ork. This activity was followed by a brief telling component that consolidated understanding of neural networks along with their applications in image/text recognition. This focus on deep learning served as a segway for introducing large language models (LLMs) that are powered by variants of neural networks. Before goin g into the capabilities and limitations of LLMs, ho wever, everyone played with a newly designed interactive web-based simulation to develop intuition about probabilistic thinking that underlies LLMs. Inspired by Dhariwal & Dhariwal (2024), a dice-rolling analogy was used to draw attention to elements of randomness and uncertainty  associated with making next word predictions. With in this simulation, participants could change the number of  dice they could roll (simulating shorter or longer  sequences of inputs), adjust the number of faces per dice (si mulating less or a greater number of input combinat ions), add bias controls (simulating which input combinations were more likely to appear with every roll), and ev en adjust the faces to non-text-based inputs (e.g., numbers). This simulation, whic h allowed them to view changes to the probability distributions with every customization,  can be freely accessed at https://tinyurl.com/ILA0021LLM. Contrasting case study descriptions drawn from real-world examples were used to progressively nudge awareness of privacy considerations central to the design and  deployment of AI systems. To illustrate an educati onally relevant example of how bias in AI systems may impact high-stakes decision-making, participants experimented with another newly  created web-based simulation. Here, they could use control of variables strateg y to systematically understand which factors differentially affect the accuracy and fairness of college admissions (e.g., grades, test scores, gender, race, extracurricular activities). This interaction served as a stepping stone to discuss issues such as stereotyping, discrimination, ethical limitations of using scant or non-representative data, and how sometimes attempts to mitigate bias may in fact increase biases in our AI applications (e.g., including only STEMfocused extracurricular activity information as opposed to a more holistic range of STEM and non-STEM based activities). This simulation can be freely accessed at https://tinyurl.com/ILA0021bias.

## Introduction to AI for Education (module II)

Subsequently, the workshop transitioned to how AI systems can support and help teachers navigate the fascinating space of human-AI collaboration in education by co-creating with generative AI via effective prompting  (part II

– four hours). Big ideas from learning sciences like collaboration, personal ization, multimodal representations,

assessment, and building on students’ cultural and linguistic assets (Ruiz & Fusco, 2022) were used to anchor the class discussion. Drawing on Sinha (2025), p articipants were given opportunities to reflect on the relationship between (i) scale and pedagogy (e.g., if private tutoring is education’s holy grail, are we sacrificing pedagogical richness by scaling up?), and (ii) theories and technology design (e.g., what are implications of an instructivist, constructivist, or embodied theoretical lens for designing feedback affordances of an AI system for education?). The next segment of this module used a jigsaw collaborative learning script (Dillenbourg & Hong, 2008) to nudge participants’ reflection on designing an i deal interaction setup between students using an in telligent tutoring system. Participants were individually asked to work with on e of four critical components that affect human-AI collaboration in the context of tutoring – nature of collaboration, nature of situation, AI system characteristics, and human characteristics (CPAIS, 2019). Subsequently, they worked in groups w ith partners possessing complementary knowledge to develop a more holistic view of the complexity of design considerations for producing lasting learning during tutoring. In the final module segment on everyday situations involving human-AI collaboration, several worked examples of effective starter text/image generation prompts for teaching and administrative tasks scaffolded participants’ learning, however with an additional eye towards environmental concerns. This segment concluded with a consolidation on how participants could teach with AI (use AI as a tool to enhance teaching), teach about AI (teach students to be creators and critical evaluat ors of AI technology) and assess with AI (use rubrics to grade conversation quality with generative AI and provoke reflections on AI usage). Building with AI (module III) The second day of this workshop focused on particip ants working in groups of five-six to build an AI-powered recommendation system for a locally relevant educational problem and stakeholder (e.g., student, teacher, parent) of their choosing (part III – six hours). Drawing on materials from AiEDU (2024) and by using individual work as preparation for collaboration (Lam & Kapur, 2018), participants were facilitated in going through (i) iterative phases of problem-statement and user persona creation for their intended recommendation system, (ii) researching similar solutions to justify the novelty of their a pplication, (iii) pitching solutions within their t eams first and subsequently a t the whole-class level, (iv) devising a data collection and an ti-bias plan by reflecting on the sensitivity of each data point they intended to col lect, (v) collecting data (via Google forms) from workshop participants, (vi) training a pre-built supervised machine learning model (via Hugging Face), and (vii) conducting qualitative error analysis on the model’s output in terms of overall accuracy, average accuracy per class, most and least important features contributing to the prediction. At the end, the machine learning model underlying t heir recommendation system (logistic regression) was introduced in a jargon-free manner to illustrate how the model iteratively  comes up with the importance of input features by adjusting weights based on prediction errors. The technical concept of regularization, critical to avoid overfitting of machine learning models by ens uring that all input features are fairly considered, was explained via analogies of a teacher using penaltie s to encourage their students towards maintaining a balanced focus on all subjects instead of just their favorite subject. This workshop segment concluded by asking participants to reflect on how they might improve the prediction s of their recommendation system and apply their pr edictive modeling pipeline to other mainstream educational t asks. In summary, participants built on their own interests and educational problems they were passionate about to exercise agency in solving a meaningful problem.

## Measures

Adapting validated AI literacy frameworks for teach er education (Wang et al., 2023) and integrating re levant measurements from existing AI literacy curricula such as IBM Corporation (2020) and AiEDU (2024), we used a five-point Likert scale to measure participants’ (i) AI readiness (9 items, 𝛼 = 0.83), which captured whether they were more prepared to critically describe how AI works (e.g., "I understand the strengths and limitations of AI technologies", "I can analyze why and how data is collected for AI  applications"), (ii) AI for education readiness (20 items, 𝛼 = 0.86), which captured whether they were more prepared to integrate AI in their teaching and learning (e.g., "I can effectively integrate AI technologies into m y classroom routines", "I understand the ethical obligations and responsibilities teachers n eed to assume in the process of using AI technologi es"), and (iii) building with AI readiness  (5 items, 𝛼 = 0.7), which captured whether they were more prepared to build new applications with AI tools (e.g., "I can identify and break down a problem that can b e solved with AI", "I am proud of an AI application that I helped build"). Our ten-item multiple choice quiz administered at the start of the second day had satisfactory difficulty  – only 3 out of 10 questions were correctly  answered by > 75% of participants, with accuracies for the remaining moderately difficult questions ranging from 48.3% – 72.4%. Discrimination indices, which capture how well our items distinguished hi gh and low-performing students, showed moderate to strong discrimination  (all questions except one had indices that ranged from 0.22 – 0.66).

## Research question and analysis plan

Our evaluation-focused research question focused on assessing the change i n participants’ AI readiness, AI for education readiness and building with AI readiness  after having gone through the AI literacy program. Because our pre-post data collection was anonymous and responses could not be linked, a two-tailed independent samples t-test was used to evaluate the impact of the AI literacy program across the two iterations.

## Results and discussion

Results showed that during the first workshop iteration, participants significantly improved thei r AI readiness (Cohen’s d = 1.46), AI for education readiness (Cohen’s d = 1.04) and building with AI readiness  (Cohen’s d = 0.83). Stronger effects were seen for a new participant cohort during the second workshop iteration approximately six months later – AI readiness (Cohen’s d = 1.63), AI for education readiness  (Cohen’s d = 1.44) and building with AI readiness (Cohen’s d = 1.39). See figure 1 for summary of results, which show that in terms of common language effect sizes, there was up to an 87.5% chance of a randomly picked participant to have higher readiness post-workshop compared to pre-workshop. Taken together, our AI literacy program showed stro nger and more sustainable results compared to recent efforts in AI literacy training (e.g., Markus et al., 2024). Quiz scores during the second iteration suggested that participants demonstrated good conceptual understanding (M = 6.69, SD = 1.79, max 10). Qualitative participant feedback post-workshop highlighted the following areas for improvement: expanding the discussion of AI ED platforms beyond predictions (e.g., unsupervised le arning), optimizing time allocation between theory and hands-on practice, incorporating more practitioner perspectives, and providing accessible AI tools and usage recipes.  Participants also shared that creating tangible recommender apps whil e deliberately thinking through issues of bias, exploring AI tools for knowledge co nstruction, and developing prompts for generative AI were useful ideas they wo uld like to apply  in their future practice. More systematic qualitative data collection (e.g., via focus-group interviews) is imperative in future to probe into these nuances. Instead of prescribing new information using an expository pedagogical approach, our AI literacy training was designed against the backdrop of real concerns like  cheating, content and student safety issues that may intimidate teachers from confidently using AI as part of their workflow. However, beyond the mere provision of procedural support and guides for  using AI tools, the workshop encouraged experimentation with customized simulati ons to facilitate the knowledge of how such tools w ork. This raised several critical questions centered on what contributes  to the limited effectiveness of AI applications in modeling complex educational scenarios. By further giving opportunities to build a tangible AI application that aligns with their educational values and visions, t he workshop opened room for creativity and may have  given our adult educators a sense of ownership and contro l over their own learning.  Several teachers tapped into their work experiences to convincingly justify how their recommendation systems c ould fill a practical ly relevant educational gap, thus resulting in a motivating and  meaningful hands-on educational experience. The dialogic nature of this workshop, along with deliberately designed groupwork opportun ities to engage participants in convergent and divergent thinking, may have additionally offered vicarious learning opportunities. Finally, a high frequency of concrete analogy usage throughout the two workshop days for making technical AI concepts more accessible may have bridged the gap between these abstract ideas and participants’ prior knowledge. Existing AI literacy frameworks (e.g., Allen & Kendeou, 2024) describe the relevance of not only teaching content knowledge about what AI is, what it cando and how it works, but also emphasize contextualization (critically evaluating how AI should be used) through hands-on activities.  In that light, our efforts to make educators AI-literate using approaches grounded in the learning sciences is a small step towards empowering them in their teaching journeys.
