# “How Are You Solving That?”: Improving Learning in Virtual Reality With a Reflective AI Agent

**Conference:** ISLS 2026

## Abstract

Virtual reality (VR) environments provide rich contexts for learning, but learners often struggle to process deeper learning while using them. Reflective activities have been effective at increasing learning from VR, but few studies have engaged learners in reflection while in the learning environment. In this demonstration participants will use our bespoke VR escape room environment to demonstrate an AI-powered reflective agent who is modeled on escape room game masters. While solving logic-based puzzles, the reflective agent acts as a character in the escape room that encourages learners to discuss their problem-solving strategies. Participants in this demo will use the VR system and interact with the AI agent. Virtual reality (VR) is a technology that surrounds the user in a digital environment, immersing them with stereoscopic 3D images and sound that heighten their sense of “being there” in the virtual environment despite knowing where their physical body is (Slater, 2009). This increased sense of presence, along with heightening learners’ agency via full-body interactivity and a first-person perspective that allows for more “hands on” engagement, present affordances for learning that are distinct from other media (Dalgarno & Lee, 2010; Makransky & Petersen, 2021). However, studies of whether VR increases learning relative to less immersive media like video and text have found mixed results, with studies varying between negative, neutral, and positive effects that average out to a small positive advantage of VR (Wu et al., 2020). Recently, more research has looked beyond such a focus on the hardware to better understand the pedagogical designs within VR that support learning (Mayer et al., 2022). Further, there is a need for research to move beyond assessing only learning outcomes in terms of content retention and instead focus on higher-order skills and the process of learning in immersive digital environments. We demonstrate a project that tackles the question of whether VR can be designed to support learning of higher-order skills by integrating metacognitive reflection as learners solve problems in the immersive environment. We built a VR escape room in which learners must solve logic-based puzzles to uncover the secrets of a prior society themed around gardening and healthy eating. As the learner solves the hands-on logic puzzles they are prompted by a character in the game, a spirit who holds the secrets to longevity and used to live in this house, to engage in metacognitive reflection during and after solving puzzles. This AI-powered system aims to improve upon prior examples of reflective activities that increased learning with immersive technologies but required learners to reflect outside of the VR environment. For example, studies have shown that segmenting a VR experience and asking learners to summarize what they have learned between segments (Parong & Mayer,

2018) or having them discuss what they learned with a peer after (Klingenberg et al., 2020) increased learning

outcomes. Recently, some studies have found that discussions within the VR environment with peers is more effective than an individual reflection (Petersen et al., 2023). Our project looks to the potential of generative AI and large language models (LLMs) for encouraging such reflection without breaking immersion of the VR experience. Related work Reflection in virtual reality Reflection in learning is an established activity that encourages learners to engage with learning of deeper structures beyond the surface features of a task. Several studies show that reflection improves learning outcomes in a variety of contexts, including in extended reality. Specifically in VR studies, reflection often takes place after the completion of the VR experience, and outside VR. For example, Parong & Mayer (2018) asked learners to remove their headset and write summaries after each segment, which increased learning and intrinsic motivation (Parong & Mayer, 2018). Klingenberg et al (2020) used post-VR processing activities like having learners teach what they just learned to another student, which increased learning retention, transfer, and self-efficacy. McGivney (2024) held reflective discussions with peers after using a VR experience to promote deeper learning. A s effective as these approaches are, they often require highly structured moments that occur outside of the VR experience, rather than promoting reflection while immersed in VR and engaged in the task, termed in-action reflection (Kleinman et al., 2025). However, recent research has highlighted the promise of encouraging people to reflect while using VR, as engaging with the immersive environment can be a highly generative activity (Wagener et al., 2023). One recent study found that learner reflection during a hands-on task in VR promoted higher-order thinking and engagement (Huang et al., 2024). Our AI-powered tool builds on this work to better embed reflective learning into the VR experience. Current work: Design of a Reflective AI Agent for improving learning Virtual reality learning environment We developed an educational virtual reality escape room that utilizes puzzles to improve and measure logical thinking and problem-solving. The adoption of escape room as our learning environment draws from prior work by Kleinman et.al (2025), which recognized that escape rooms promote and require critical thinking and problemsolving skills comparable to traditional learning environments, and also have ‘game masters’ who facilitate events in the room but also provide hints or nudges to the players when they ask or become stuck in their progress. For this project, we created an AI agent based on the role and best practices of such game masters (Kleinman et al., 2025). The single-player VR escape room was developed using Unity, the Meta XR CORE SDK 77.0.0, and the Open XR Plugin 1.14.0. Participants explore an abandoned house where they can access a kitchen and a greenhouse with five logic puzzles related to cooking and gardening that the participant must solve following a hub-and-spoke style: They solve four simpler (spoke) puzzles with simple logical problem-solving insights and unlock clues to the culminating “hub” puzzle that requiring more advanced logical insights. The spoke puzzles use hands-on interaction with game objects (watering plants, pouring sauce onto pasta), while the hub puzzle uses a more traditional grid interaction. Interaction is via hand tracking, not controllers, including gestures for teleporting beyond moving in the physical space allowed. Figure 1 depicts the layout of the escape room. Reflection design This reflection design addresses the challenges of reflection in VR by embedding it within the tasks at hand and allowing players to reflect during the problem-solving process as well as after. Players are prompted to reflect both in-action, while engaging in solving a puzzle. Figure 2 depicts an example of an AI-reflective prompt in the escape room. The current version of the AI agent runs in the VR escape room via OpenAI’s API. It is not a hint-giving system, but a reflective dialogue agent designed to prompt metacognitive reasoning while being diegetic or embedded into the story. The agent adopts the persona of an elderly woman and maintains an emotionally neutral, grounded tone. It never breaks character, never references the system or environment, and never references itself. The interaction is a prompt system developed based on established practices by game masters (Kleinman et al., 2025). The prompts encourage the player to verbalize reasoning, evaluate their strategy, and articulate their assumptions about the puzzle (e.g., “What do you know that can help you solve the problem?”). Demonstration In this demonstration, participants will be able to try the VR escape room environment and interact with the AI agent. Typically, the entire escape room takes 20-30 minutes to complete, but for this demo we will offer participants a 5-10 minute experience. We will have 2-3 headsets available, and space permitting multiple people can use the demo at the same time. Typically, learners have freedom throughout the experience and must explore the space to discover the puzzles and objects needed to solve them. For this demonstration they will be directed to a puzzle, so they are able to begin solving it immediately and interact with the AI. Figure 3 illustrates the four “spoke” puzzles that participants can solve that utilize various interactions. Figure 4 illustrates a participant solving the “hub” puzzle being solved by a participant using a more traditional logic grid interaction. The AI is designed to prompt reflection based on the learners moves, such as idling, making irrelevant moves, or expressing frustration, and participants will be prompted based on these behaviors. Participants verbally respond to discuss their problem-solving process, and the AI will prompt them to elaborate when needed. Before and after they use the VR environment, participants candiscuss the experience with the research team to learn more about the project and give feedback on the system.
