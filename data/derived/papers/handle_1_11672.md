# Authorship Networks in Learning Sciences Research on Disinformation

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
We present a pilot study examining three years of conference papers in the proceedings of the International Society of the Learning Sciences (ISLS) Annual Meetings from 2022 to 2024 to explore the connections and collaborations among authors in three fields of investigation: data science instruction, misinformation/disinformation, and social media. We found initial evidence of a cyclical pattern indicating that at times, sub-communities collaborate, while at other times, they operate more independently in isolation.

## Introduction and literature review

This paper is part of a more extensive bibliographic study and literature review that seeks to identify connections between three fields of interest that learning scie ntists have addressed differently in recent years: data science instruction, mis/disinformation, and social media.  Our working hypothesis is based on the assumption t hat there are critical overlaps between those three fields, a nd Learning Sciences (LS) research can explore thos e overlaps in productive ways once they are mapped. Literature  in fields close to the LS has  explored those intersections in different ways, for example: ● Social media and other online spaces are part of an  ecosystem where much politically motivated mis-and disinformation spreads (Dan et al., 2021; Freelon et al., 2023). ● Disinformation often comes enveloped with a “do you r own research” claim (Russo et al., 2023, 2024), which relates strongly to data literacy and interpretation of evidence (e.g., Carmi et al., 2020). ● Some scholars focusing on data science instruction investigate the sociopolitical layer of mediation within data science practices (e.g., Gebre, 2022; Irgens et al., 2020; Lee et al., 2021). We build on previous work that examined LS scholarship, such as Zheng et al. (2023), who explored co-authorship networks from CSCL and ICLS proceedings and identified patterns indicating how topics disseminate within the community, facilitated by influential authors and the cliques in which they participated.

## Methods

Our initial corpus comprised 2,015 papers authored by 4,138 unique authors from the proceedings of ISLS Annual Meetings held in 2022, 2023, and 2024. The metadata we gathered from those years included author names, titles, and abstracts—specifically, the latter two variables were the target fields for our search mechanism. Our search queries (listed below) were developed in consultati on with a research and instruction librarian at our  home university during a research session consultation: ● search_pattern_data_science = r“(?:data literacy|data science|statistical literacy|statistics instruction)” ● search_pattern_disinformation=r“(?:disinformation|misinformation|fakenews|post-truth|bad science|pseudoscience)” ● search_pattern_social_media = r“(?:social media|instagram|tiktok|facebook|whatsapp|youtube)” We analyzed author networks using the Networkx package (Hagberg et al., 2008). The search patterns were designed to classify authors into distinct research  topic groups based on keywords extracted from the titles and abstracts of their work. RegEx was used to ensure that the occurrences of the keyw ords could be captured regardless of case sensitivity. The filtered corpus  had 29 papers with 135 authors in 2022, 36 papers with 107 authors in 2023, and 37 papers with 133 authors in 2024. We also identified authors in multiple topic groups to examine the intersection of different groups of aut hors, and a unique name with initials was created f or each author. Network graphs for each year were built wit h color codes for different membership groups. Top authors were identified based on the betweenness centrality  (Freeman, 1977)  and degree centrality (Nieminen, 1974). Finally, top authors’ Egonetworks (Freeman, 1982) were plotted to identify their roles in authorship networks.

## Findings

Alternating between isolated groups and collaborative communities Figure 1 displays network graphs for the three years. In 2022, a relatively large and central hub connected authors working across different fields, primarily between the Data Science and Disinformation groups. Authors  in the three pairwise intersection groups were also linked  to that hub, indicating some level of collaboratio n across disciplines. By 2023, the network b ecame much less connected, characterized by many sm all, isolated, and scattered clusters of single topics. However, a hub connected all three fields, with a prominent central node at the intersection of Disinformation and Social Media (we  elaborate on that author in the subsection below). In 2024, a highly connected authorship network appeared, sho wcasing interdisciplinary collaboration across all three topics. A centralized hub emerged (similar to 2022), but now that hub incorporated authors from multiple thematic groups, particularly those in Social Media. A central node surfaced at the intersection of Data Science and Social Media, along with a few interdisciplinary groups. T he evolution of the authorship networks reflects va rious patterns of collaboration among authors across the three groups. Notably, however, no author was part of all three groups simultaneously in any of the three years.

Their degree centrality was also measur ed to explore their influence based on the number of conn ections (for anonymity, we used pseudonymous initials for the authors.) In 2022, SW emerged as the most influ ential author in the network, with a degree centrality of 0.45 and a betweenness centrality of 0.07. SW was the to p author again in 2024, achieving a high er betweenness centrality of 0.15. There was also a shift in her focus on topic intersections: SW primarily connected with authors from the Disinformation group and its intersection groups with Data Science and Social Media in 2022. In 2024, SW’s connectio ns shifted predominantly toward the Data Science and Social Media group while maintaining some connections with intersected groups involving Disinformation. In 2023, another researcher, CT, became the top author as a member of the intersection group Di sinformation and Social Media. Compared to SW, CT h ad a relatively lower degree centrality (0.12) and betwe enness centrality (0.02), which is also evident in a less connected author network than in the other two years. CT connected with authors across various single-topic and mixed-topic groups, except for the intersection group of Data Science and Social Media. This highlights CT’s role as a connector across diverse research areas, but less than SW.

## Conclusion

This poster explores the authorship networks to identify how the connections and collaborations of authors in the fields of data science instruction, misinformation/ disinformation, and social media have evolved in th e last three editions of the ISLS Annual  Meeting. It provides insights into existing collabo rations and suggests the need to foster future cross-field partnerships within our collaborative research community. In the poster presentation, we will include charts and tables that were omitted fo r space considerations (each year’s top authors, their d egree, and betweenness centrality; egonetworks for top authors in each field). Future research may delve deeper into the content of papers within the identified sub-communities, especially those authored by superconnectors recognized in this work. Natural language processing technique s may offer an overview of connecting points betwee n those topics and can be combined with qualitative methods, such as thematic analysis, for a more detailed treatment of contents, findings, and methodologies employed by researchers in our community field.
