# Exploring an Interactive Technology for Supporting Embodied Geometric Reasoning

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Access to body-based resources has been shown to augment cognitive processes, but not all movements equally aid reasoning. Interactive technologies, like dynamic geometry systems (DGS), potentially amplify the link between movement and geometric representation, thereby deepening students' understanding of geometric properties. This study investigated the influence of access to a DGS on students' geometric reasoning and gesture production compared to the impact of only spontaneous gestures. Access to simulations did not significantly increase the likelihood of students producing correct mathematical insights compared to spontaneous gestures. However, when controlling gesture production, DGS access became a significant predictor for transformational proof production. We also found that access to DGS influenced the type of gestures students produced during geometric reasoning tasks. This study underlines the vital role of gestures in geometric reasoning and suggests that while DGS offers benefits, spontaneous gestures remain a crucial embodied resource for exploring geometric concepts.

## Introduction

Embodied m athematical reasoning and communication involve a w ide range of expressions, such as writing, speaking, gesturing, and interacting with physical or technological tools and resources (e.g., Arzarello & Robutti, 2010; Edwards et al., 2014). During geometric reaso ning and proof tasks, students can for m geometric objects through body-based actions, often employing spontaneous gestures to represent properties and transformations of objects as crucial embodied grounding mechanisms (A libali & Nathan, 2012; Nathan et al., 2021). Sponta neous representational gestures are an expression of cogn itive processing and have been found to contribute to an increased likelihood of co rrect mathematical insight and transformational pro of (McNeill, 1992; Nathan et al., 2021).

Interactive technologies  such as dynamic geometry systems (DGS) that support both reasoning and cognitively relevant movement may strengthen the conceptual connection of dynamic movements and geometric representations, improving students’ ability to rea son deeply about geometric properties (Hollebrands,  2007).

Using interactive technologies to explore a geometr ic representation involves movement, and there is g rowing evidence suggesting these types of movement can enhance learning outcomes (e.g., Alberto et al., 2022; Lindgren et al., 2016). However, not all movements are equally beneficial for facilitating reasoning (e.g., Fogel et al., 2023; Kim & Nathan, 2023; Walkington et al., 2022). Few studies have investigated the effect of interactive technologies in supporting students’ verbal geometric reasoning and proof production based on experimental evidence. Thus, it is unclear whether interactive technologies prov ide additional scaffolds for  students’ geometric reasoning in ways that spontaneous gestures alone do not. In the current study, we explored the impact of interactive technology in the form of a DGS on students’ geometric proof performance and gesture production. We investigated whether access to interactive geometric simulations increased the likelihood of  correct geometric reasoning and whether it impacted the likelihood of representational gestures during their reasoning.

## Theoretical Framework

The embodied nature of mathematical reasoning and proof Theories of grounded and embodied cognition (GEC) p ropose that cognitive processes are derived from perceptual and motor experiences (Barsalou, 2008; S hapiro, 2019). GEC posits that all concepts, even a bstract representations, are understood in inherently perceptual, body-based ways (Wilson, 2002). When examining mathematicians' and math teachers’ proof practices,  empirical studies reveal that proof “is a richly embodied practice” (Marghetis et al., 2014, p. 243; Schenck  et al., 2022). The embodied nature o f proof is also evident among secondary school students in classrooms, as they frequently present their proofs using verbal forms instead of formal, written forms (Healy & Hoyles, 2002). Bo th teachers and students use speech and actions to track the development of ideas when exploring mathematical conjectures (Nathan et al., 2011). As in other areas of mathematics, embodied interven tions have shown some promise for improving geometry and proof performance. For example, Smith and colleagues (2014) tracked elementary students’ movements as they learned different types of angles  by linking arm movements to canonical geometric representations of angles via a grounding metaphor of angles as space between sides. Students who made multiple dynamic movements (i.e., changing the size of the angles) to explore the grounding metaphor benefitted the most. Nathan and colleagues (Nathan & Walkington, 2017; W alkington et al., 2022) have explored the potential  of motion-capture technology in enhancing geometric reasoning. They developed a motion-capture video game that prompted students to perform mat hematically relevant bodily actions. Evidence from a series of laboratory and classroom experiments showed that performing mathematically relevant actions contributed to students’ superior mathematical insights and enhanced geometry proof p erformance (Nathan et al., 2022). These studies sug gest bodily action is essential for grounding and influencing students’ geometric reasoning. Gestures promote mathematical reasoning One form of bodily action that can convey and influ ence cognition is gesture. Gestures are spontaneous  arm and hand movements that speakers produce when communica ting (McNeill, 1992). A review by Alibali and Natha n

(2012) found that pointing gestures reflected the grounding of cognition in th e physical environment and that

representational gestures, those depictive of objects and processes,  exhibited students’ mental simulations of action, perception, and conceptual metaphors. As si mulated actions (Hostetter & Alibali, 2019), gestur es can highlight the spatio-motoric information from actions (Kita et al., 2017) and help schematize relevant information to facilitate mathematical transformations (Walking ton et al., 2022) and generalization (Novack et al., 2014). Gestures also offer specific affordances for mathematical representation and communication, such as a low barrier to entry, portability, flexibility, and typically being meaningful to the actors. A specific class of representational gestures, know n as dynamic depictive gestures  (Garcia & Infante, 2012), is particularly important for studying mathe matics learning. These gestures simulate transforma tions of a mathematical object through multiple states. For example, a dynamic depictive gesture can portray the invariance of the sum  of its interior angles by re-scaling (transforming) its size, thereby supporting  generalization and abstraction. Evidence has shown that the production of dynamic depictive gestures strongly predicted participants’ formation of generalized mathematical proofs, even when controlling for spatial ability (Nathan et al., 2021). On the other hand, non-dynamic depictive gestures that highlight static properties of objects predicted the generation of correct mathematical insights (i.e., gist) (Nathan et al., 2021). The role of Dynamic Geometry Systems in mathematical reasoning and gesture Dynamic geometry systems (DGSs) are interactive simulation environments acce ssible on computers, tablets, or mobile phones. A key affordance of DGSs is their ab ility to allow users to construct objects, transform the constructed figure through dragging, and instantly update representations (Hollebrands, 2007). Such “continuous, real-time transformation” (Goldenberg & Cuoco, 1998, p. 351) allows students to dynamically view and intera ct with invariant properties of  objects, thereby supporting students’ geometric rea soning about objects, properties, and relations (Hollebrands, 2007).  Another affordance of DGSs is the ability to preven t task-irrelevant manipulations by constraining the interactivity of mathematical objects (Barrett et al., 2015). Howeve r, some DGSs can initially induce higher cognitive loads when studen ts are first learning the interface (Reis et al., 2012) and prevent students from recognizing formal mathematical operations (Sarama & Clements, 2009). Although DGSs were initially developed without touch-based mobile devices, the use of DGSs in touchbased devices has shown potential for primary schoo l students. Touch-based DGSs facilitate direct, embodied interactions with mathematical objects (Dube  & McEwen, 2015; Pittalis & Drijvers, 2023). For exa mple, children’s swiping and tapping behaviors on an iPad can promote counting (Sinclair & de Freitas, 2014). Dragging in touch-based DGS affords new modes of thinking and interacting with mathematics by linking hand movements directly to the actions on the screen (Ng, 2019). Research has suggested that lear ners prefer tangible user interfaces, where objects are manipulated by intentional gestures, to typical graphic user interfaces using buttons and menus (Zuckerman & Ggal-Oz, 2013). Unlike using a keyboard and mouse that l acks gestural congruency – a link between the physical actions and the concepts to be learned (Lindgren & Johnson-Glenberg, 2013; e.g., rotating one’s hands to rotate a triangle), a touchscreen interface may enhance gestural congruency for DGS s. With gestural congruency present, physical actions may serve as perceptual anchors for learning (Abrahamson & Sánchez-García, 2016) and create  embodied resources and metaphors that can facilitate geometric reasoning (Nathan & Walkington, 2017; Smith  et al., 2014). While studies have explored the actions (e.g., dragging) users perform on touchscreens (Pittalis & Drijvers, 2023), little is know n about whether the use of DGSs can impact user’s gestures “in the air”  -- specifically those gestures produced during the exp lanation of geometric proofs. This study adds to a growing body of literature on the potential effects of DGSs on users'  reasoning and proof performance.

## Research question

We developed a series of dynamic geometry simulatio ns that students could interact with during reasoni ng tasks in collaboration with GeoGebra as part of a larger project. In this current exploratory study, we are interested in the impact of access to these  dynamic geometry simulations compared to access to spontaneous gestures alone. We specifically ask: (RQ1) Does access to a dynamic  geometry simulation impact students’ verbal geomet ric reasoning, as measured by mathematical insight and transformational proof production, and (RQ2) Does  access to a dynamic geometry simulation impact students’ gestures during geometric reasoning?

## Participants and procedure

As part of a larger study, 84 undergraduates were recruited from a large Midwest university. This sample had an average age of 20.45 years. Forty-seven students identified as female, 35 as male, and 2 did not disclose their gender. Fifty-one students identified as While//Non-Hispanic, 30 as Asian, and 3 as Other race/ethnicity. 83% of the students were native English speakers. Students received a $20 gift card upon completion of all study activities. Students first completed a set of pre-measures, including a demographic and background questionnaire, the Spatial Reasoning Instrument (Ramful et al., 20 17), a 12-question geometry assessment based on the Diagnostic Geometry Assessment (Masters, 2010), and  a math interest survey (Linnenbrink-Garcia et al., 2010). In this within-subject repeated measures research design, participants were asked to justify the veracity of a series of 8 geometr y conjectures. These geometry conjectures include bot h two-and three-dimensional properties of shapes that are commonly featured in high school ge ometry curricula. For the first four conjectures, participants only had access to the conjecture text, definitions  for key vocabulary, and any spontaneous gestures t hey made. After completing the first four conjectures, studen ts were asked to justify the veracity of four addit ional conjectures. For these conjectures, participants had access to unique simulations developed using GeoGebra’s 3D Calculator App, a free, widely used DGS for touch screens  (Figure 1). These simulations were set up for each participant and presented on a large Promethean board so participants could move and manipulate the simulations using their hands directly. Participants were shown  a 5-minute video tutorial on how to use the GeoGebra simulations. Conjecture order was counter-balanced using a Latin Square. ## Coding and data analysis

Video recordings of student’s explanations were seg mented by conjectures, resulting in 656 clips. All clips were then transcribed and coded for mathematical insight, transformational proof, simulation use, and gestu re by a research team member  using MAXQDA 2022 (VERBI Software, 2021).  All categories were given a 0/1 (absent/present) for the entire clip of each conjecture. Insight r efers to students’ conscious retrieval of mathematical properties and examples that are relevant and applied correctly to the conjecture at hand (Figure 1; Zhang et al., 2016). For multimodal transformationa l proof, proofs must be generalizable and involve l ogical inference and operational thought (Figure 1; Harel & Sowder, 1998). Evidence of operational thou ght included Conjecture: If two parallel lines are cut by a third line, the pairs of corresponding angles are congruent.

Corresponding angles are in the same position at each intersection where a straight line crosses two others.

Insight: e.g., “The transversal cuts both parallel lines at the same angle. If you know one of the angles is 30 degrees, you use theorems to show that every angle has to be either 30 degrees or 150 degrees.” Proof: e.g., “The transversal cuts both parallel lines at the same angle. Since the lines are parallel, you use a translation to move the intersection between one parallel line and the transversal on to the other no matter what the angle ” both speech and gestures that describe operations a nd transformations of objects. For gestures, we cod ed for two types of representational gestures: non-dynamic (representing mathematical objects and or their pro perties) and dynamic (representing a transformation of a mathematical object through multiple states). We also created a third gesture category, any representational gesture, which included the occurrence of either type of r epresentational gesture and pointing gestures that involved either the simulation or an imagined mathematical object. We used mixed-effects logistic regression models on the outcome measures of insight, proof, and the three gesture categories using the glmer command in the R software package lme4 (Bates et al., 2014). Effect size was measured using d-type measures using the logit method, and odds rati os (OR) were calculated by exponentiating the beta coefficients. Participant ID and conjecture were included in all models as random effects. Student characteristics were only retained in the m odel if they significantly reduced deviation. For t he models predicting insight and proof, gender and calculus c oursework completion were included as covariates. T he intervention condition, non-dynamic representational gesture, and dynamic repre sentational gesture were also added to the model. For the models predicting gestures, 634 observations were analyzed after 22 clips’ recordings were deemed insufficient to capture gestures. Students’ spatial reasoning scores, geometry assessment scores, and math interest scores were characteristics retained for these models.

## Discussion and conclusion

This study explored the impact of access to interac tive simulations on students’ embodied geometric re asoning and gesture production. The results showed partial support for the positive effect of DGS access on geometric reasoning (RQ1).  DGS access increased the likelihood of transformational proof when controlling for the impact of depictive gesture production. Transformational p roof requires students to move beyond simply produc ing correct mathematical insights to verbalizing them i n a way that is logical, generalizable, and operational. It is possible that access to the DGS does not help stude nts access their mathematical knowledge but improve s students’ ability to logically generalize and opera tionalize their knowledge  through instantaneous dynamic interactions. Also, the results provided empirical support for th e idea that gestures are a reliable resource for embodying geometric concepts. Across both of our study conditions, students were more likely to produce correct mathematical insights and proofs when they produced  depictive representational gestures, whether dynam ic or non-dynamic.

In addition, the results of this study revealed sig nificant differences in the likelihood of “in the a ir” representational and non-dynamic depictive gestures (RQ2). When students had  access to the DGS, they were more likely to produce at least one representational gesture. However, students were more likely to produce nondynamic depictive gestures when they did not have access to DGS. Ther e was no impact of either condition on the likelihood of dynamic depictive gesture production. The DGS has the affordance of providing students with a visual representation of the geometric objects, whi ch may explain why students produced less non-dynamic depictive gestures. These types of gestures often d epict static objects, which may serve as an offload ing mechanism for  students when they only have access to their sponta neous gestures. Furthermore, DGSs have perceptual affordances in the form of a visual repr esentation that acts as attentional anchors (Pittal is & Drijvers, 2023), which may have led students to do more pointing at the simulation, which manifested as a higher likelihood of producing representational gestures.

There are two possible explanations for the statist ically insignificant effect of DGS access on mathem atical insight. The first explanation is that not all students utilized the interactive components of the simulation. Though our directions were that students could move the simulations to help them with their thinking, this type of dynamic interaction was not required. In a post-hoc analysis, we found that only 63% of students dy namically interacted with the simulation before or during their verbal reasoning. We look forward to presenting analyses of this subset in future works that may shed more light on this im portant topic of how DGSs can support embodied geom etric thinking and learning. Another explanation derives from a combination of two factors: (a) the inverse relationship between DGS access and depictive gesture production and (b) the competing effects of DGS access and depictive gestures. Without access to the DGS, students relie d on non-dynamic and dynamic gestures to depict geometric objects (Table 2). On the other hand, the theory expects studen ts’ interaction with DGS – whether it is visual or physical – and depictive gestures both to influence geometric reasoning positively. Therefore, we believe that depictive gestures increased the likelihood of mathematical i nsight and transformational proof to the extent tha t they effectively compensated for the absence of DGS. We also believe that this argument is supported by the fact that the treatment effect estimates increased after controlling for non-dynamic and dynamic gestures (Table 1). Though this study was exploratory, this research provides a first step in possibly changing geometry instruction by building an evidence base for which approaches and technologies may have the most significant impact in supporting students’ embodied geometric r easoning. Conceptualizing geometry as being grounde d in the embodied transformation of objects  rather than as grounded primarily in logical relati ons could open the subject area to many more learners. Principles for teaching secondary mathematics in a n embodied way formulated from this study and others could be comm unicated in teacher education classes and teacher professional development. This research could also inform the design and effective use of new classes of DGSs in classrooms that may use computer vision to track and interpret students’ “in a ir” gestures, thus expanding our notions of educational technology to support students’ bodies, their full range of multimodal forms of expression, and the physical and virtual spaces they occupy.
