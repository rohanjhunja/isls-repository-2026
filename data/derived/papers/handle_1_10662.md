# Identify Negotiations of Reluctant Makers in STEM-rich Makerspaces

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Makerspaces are technology-rich environments that foster Science, Technology, Engineering, and Mathematics (STEM) -rich practices. Making is a cultural learning model founded upon constructionism and beliefs of STEM-mediated social mobility. Acknowledging the complex ways of participation and learning in makerspaces, this paper examines the identity work of four girls aged eleven to fourteen in a tinkering workshop. Using intersectionality (Crenshaw, 1989), this paper describes how the girls negotiated identities in relation to coparticipants, others’ expectations, and their self-understanding. Despite the commonality of the girls’ unwillingness to engage meaningfully in workshop activities and intense negotiations, only some girls negotiated positive identities. Analysis reveals that several factors affected their identity work, including others’ participation and perceptions, while robust identities in other domains presented opportunities and challenges. The paper underscores considering social and individual factors influencing engagement in makerspaces to accommodate diverse identities and encourages understanding the implications of seemingly inclusive technology-rich learning environments.

## Introduction and background

The Maker Movement in education facilitates technologically and materially mediated learning and participation in both open-ended and structured contexts. It has sparked interest in playful inquiry pedagogies within generous learning environments (Vossoughi & Bevan, 2014) and  various technologies facilitating Science, Technology, Engineering, and Mathematics (STEM) learning. K-12 makers position themselves as problem-solvers, scientists, and engineers, engaging in STEM-rich practices (Bevan et al., 2015). While research  demonstrates promising outcomes, making is a complex activity involving pe rceptions, emotions, and individual and social capi tal. Following sociocultural perspectives on learning, t his article expands the understanding of four girls ’ identity work while participating in a public library makerspace workshop about circuitry. The girls’ unique experiences, marked by minimal project engagement, open avenues for inquiry into factors beyond success that shaped  their identities.

Troubling notions of learning in makerspaces Grounded in constructionist (Papert, 1980) pedagogi es, maker education intertwines STEM concepts and practices, emphasizing technology education and design-based learning with a playful focus on creative ideation. Recent research suggests its potential for  advancing STEM skills, engaging diverse learners, a nd broadening participation in STEM fields. However, limitations exist in constructionist approaches, with maker environments relying heavily on adult instruction, complex tools that limit exploration for some, and emphasizing collaboration but overlooking personal challenges in STEM. Overal l, barring a few, most research on learning while m aking ignores questions of identity, social and material ecological, and relational issues. While making spa rks STEM interest, understanding learners’ becoming makers or staying away remains crucial. Makerspaces embody a cultural model of learning emp hasizing collaboration, exploration, and innovation (Halbinger, 2018). Current STEM learning ideals aim for inclusive access to technology and scientific knowledge, promoting social mobility and economic p rosperity. However, gendered perceptions of technol ogy use (Eckhardt et al., 2021; Tan et al., 2013) in ma kerspaces align with broader stereotypes in STEM fi elds (Hedditch & Vyas, 2021), reinforcing biases about g ender and technology skills. Overlooking po wer structures in tech-rich learning environments perpetuates misconceptio ns about those needing immediate access (Delpit, 1988). The prevailing notion of STEM-enabled socioeconomic mobility, too, reinforces con formity to dominant cultural norms, disadvantaging marginalized learners. As critics argue for a more equitable STEM education that challenges and redefines cultural norms to be inclu sive rather than expecting learners to conform, thi s paper examines learners’ unwillingness to learn while mak ing the goal of broadening our understanding of some constraints of makerspaces as learning environments.

Identities in making Identity is a dynamic process shaped by community r ecognition and negotiation and influenced by resour ces, context, and desires (Carlone et al., 2011). Set in  the context of an e-textile-based maker workshop, this paper examines four girls’ identity centering intersectionality (Crenshaw, 1989) and aims to understand factors shaping the girls’ participation. Research indicates that STEM identity formation is complex and affected by gender, race, ethnicity, religion, and class, presenting several challenges for individuals already facing biases. Inclusive STEM programs offer opportunities for identity work, age ncy, and empowerment, but deficits-based narratives persist, necessitating justice-oriented, intersectional approaches (Rahm et al., 2022). Research advocates validating lived experiences and building critical consciousness, underscoring the need to embrace diverse identities (Avraamidou,

2020) and complicating our understanding of identities and identity work. Based on the knowledge that attention

to a nuanced understanding of participation and the dynam ic, context-dependent nature of identity negotiation is crucial to a holistic knowledge of learning in any context, this work is attentive to relational aspec ts of STEM journeys focused on becoming. To this end, the rati onale for this research is grounded in the imperati ve to foster inclusivity and diversity in STEM. While we have ma de significant efforts to encourage girls’ particip ation in STEM fields, understanding how girls with wide-ranging interests, motivations, and abilities negot iate their STEM identity can be further developed. By focusing  on girls who engage minimally with workshop activi ties, this paper aims to uncover the subtle yet impactful dynamics at play within the maker workshop environment that underscore some conflicts at play in makerspaces. U ltimately, this is a step towards acknowledging and considering a wider range of learners’ experiences in shaping equitable, effective, and responsive educational practices. Building on the above theoretical framework and body of research, this article responds to the question: How do girls with minimal engagement with their mak er projects negotiate their STEM identity at a make r workshop?

## Methods

This paper, rooted in an interpretive perspective (Mehan, 1992), explores girls’ learning in a maker w orkshop to illuminate the dynamic construction of multiple ide ntities, power dynamics, negotiation, resistance, a nd conflicting ideas within the social and shared space as a persistent phenomenon. The rese arch is guided by understanding learning as a socially constructed and negotiated experience. The setting involved a tin kering workshop at a local public library makerspace in th e southwestern United State s, where eighteen participants created projects using paper circuits and e-textile circuits over the summers of 2016 and 2017.  The workshop spanned four consecutive days each week, with sessi ons lasting two hours. Participants had individual workstations, freedom to collaborate, and access to recording dev ices. Data included recording workshop activities, informal interviews with participants, and videos recorded by participants to showcase the ir project work. A university review board approved the protoc ols for the study. The four-day workshop design included the following format each day: tutorials on connect ing circuit components, a series circuit, and a par allel circuit; individual or small group work on at least one proj ect that included the circuit type for the day; sha ring projects with colleagues and discussing possible improvements; a brief interview conducted by the author who facilitated the workshop. The data were analyzed through the fo llowing steps. First, in a round of open coding, pe rsonal (effort, notions of success, doubt, agreement, hope, e tc.) and participation attributes (embracing uncert ainty, learning with others, preference for school-like learning, existing interests, etc.) of the participants were identified along with STEM learning attributes (inquiry method ology, connections to other STEM domains, etc.). Ne xt, themes (fear of failure and parental disappointment, preference for independent learning, strong belie fs about learning in one domain of STEM, etc.) were identifi ed in response to questions about the intersection of factors that situate an individual’s participation and how these factors shape the individual’s figuring of se lf in activity. Finally, narratives were prepared for each particip ant: Ava (fourteen, Caucasian), Jazmine  (twelve, African American), Amy (thirteen, Caucasian), and Valentina  (thirteen, Latiné). The choice of these four cases  was determined by the girls’ unwillingness to demonstra te an interest in workshop activities and enough ta lk and activities recorded during the workshop to conduct a thorough analysis.

## Findings

As the oldest workshop participant and a dedicated crafter, Ava felt out of place among younger, energ etic participants. “Everyone around me was young and ene rgetic; their projects were so cool, and mine was j ust craft with an LED.” Ava incorporated one LE D-battery unit into a felt craft project, and felt in ferior to younger participants, more energetic, and seemingly better at working with e-textile components. “Craft is rewarding for me. Sewing circuits is not even just sewing; I don’ t make the rules. My parents brought me here.” Desp ite her parents’ efforts to spark her interest in STEM by encouraging STEM workshops and museum visits, Ava struggled to connect with e-textiles and expressed disappointment in letting her parents down. “My Dad’s an engineer, and the encouragement is a bit too much.” Although sadd ened by her lack of success, she aimed to blend cra fts into STEM at her own pace. “I’ll just keep doing what I love.” Ava’s participation reflected her parents’ a spirations, her resistance tonew, independent, collaborative l earning environments, and her hope for a future rec onciliation of crafts and STEM. Overall, Ava identified outside of STEM. Jazmine, a twelve-year-old attending her first maker workshop, received si milar encouragement from her father, like Ava, to pursue a STEM career, spec ifically in engineering. “I like this as craft, but  craft got an upgrade and got difficult. Some kids were  making bug robots that could move around and stuff,  and mine was just meh.” Despite being more optimistic than Ava, Jazmine, too, felt inadequate in making artifacts with e-textile components. She compared her projects to others and decided her work was not good enough. She created a Harry Potter-themed felt tie with a series circuit of two LEDs b ut felt uncertain about her prospects in engineerin g. “I think I could ask for help. This was Dad’s idea, so  he needs to help me.” While marveling at others’ p rojects, Jazmine wanted to persist and gain confidence in circuitry with her father’s support. “I’m just going to try harder and see what happens.” Using her project as self-inquiry, she navigated her identity at the intersection of a crafter, a daughter, an optimistic yet school-aligned learner, and one seeking external affirmati on. Jazmine cautiously identified as a STEM learner.

Amy, a self-taught programmer, viewed circuitry as craft, a pur suit she disliked. “I would say that I am an eight, programming-wise, but a two, craft-wise. This is different from programming.” While sh e successfully created a basic series circuit on felt a t a workshop, she refrained from extensive projects. Amy valued her programming skills as an effective measure of her i ntelligence. She showcased a deep understanding of programming, emphasizing the distinctions between circuitry in terms of code editing and the requisite precision. “These two things are very different; the editing is different, and there’s a way to be careful. Circuits are difficult to fix.” Therefore, Amy resisted time-consuming or challenging crafting, identifying sole ly as a STEM learne r and programmer, not a tinkerer. “I don’t think I wa nt to try circuitry; I like programming better. It’ s easier for me.” Amy’s reluctance to delve into circuitry as a novice conflicted with her established programmer i dentity, reflecting a conscious choic e in her learning path. In a way, Amy resisted the identity of a crafter and stepped away from the possibility of failure in a new domain.

Valentina, a K-12 STEM program student undertaking pre-engineering coursework, confidently engaged in the workshop where she constructed a series circuit on a felt swatch. “I take pre-engineering, so I can relate to this very well.” Unlike Amy, she equally  valued craft and engineering, expressing interest i n a circuitry project for her school’s science fair without a specific pl an. “It’s a different kind of problem-solving, and I will need to learn this better. I will do this for my science fair project, (but) don’t know exactly what.” Despite acknowledging the need to learn about circuitry, Valentina remained optimistic about her STEM prospects and unaffected by the simplicity of her project compared to that of other s. Her secure self-understanding and affiliation with a STEM school likely contributed to her strong STEM identity at the workshop. Her willingness to identify with the craftlike circuitry elements indicates her openness to domains other than pre-engineering as taught at school. Valentina also lacked negative perceptions of craft.

The above analysis reveals that identities in makin g exist at complex identity intersections. While th e identities of an independent learner, comfortable with uncertainty, and technology savviness are more robust in a makerspace, negotiating identities suc h as that of a crafter or a non-STEM-focused learner posed challenges. In contrast, identities such as a programmer or attend ing a STEM school were deemed superior and required  nonegotiation. Lacking a positive prior STEM affiliation, Ava and Jazmine perceived their projects as less ingenious than their peers. Parental encouragement and suppor t also impacted participants’ experiences, with Ava  feeling heartbroken and Jazmine cautiously hopeful. Amy and  Valentina felt successful; Amy chose to program ov er crafting, emphasizing ease of correction, familiarit y, and established expertise. Valentine, fond of cr afting, planned independent e-textile projects for future science fairs but accep ted the need to further learning. Neither Amy nor Valentina mentioned parental motivation for  workshop participation but confirmed affirmation o f their skills from places such as school. These observations are pertinent to understanding STEM identities and identity work in makerspaces as intersecting with identity work undertaken in other areas of life. Against the backdrop of a lack of interest in making from all participants, such divergent and distinct identity work illuminates the complex process of choosing to become certain kinds of makers and STEM learners.

## Discussion

This paper delved into the intersectional identity work of four girls in a makerspace tinkering worksh op. Their negotiations involved various factors, such as inte rest, independence, willingness to seek help, stron g learner identity in other areas, and overall approach to le arning, such as taking chances or avoiding failure.  The girls' experiences were multidimensional, reflecting the c omplex nature of identity formation in maker contex ts. Feelings of guilt and inadequacy prevented learners from developing an interest while assuming a general interest in STEM, leading to unquestioned and overly optimistic perceptions of STEM learning that complicated learners' experiences (Rahm et al., 2022). Further, the assum ption that crafting and low-floor, high-ceiling domains such as e-textiles and craft circuits can serve as gateways t o STEM skills were challenged by the experiences of  these four girls. Participants noted  the differences between engineering and craft, noti ng that engineering's solution-driven nature took away the joy of crafting. The girls' preferences for specific domains highlighted the importance of recognizing and valuing diverse learning experie nces and identities (Master et al., 2021), as learners wh o felt less accomplished indicated the need to work hard a nd keep trying while feeling less confident and que stioning their presence (Gonsalves et al., 2013). Therefore,  complex challenges persist in suppo rting girls' identities in STEM and adjacent learning environments despite pro mises of generous and appealing learning environmen ts and social mobility.
