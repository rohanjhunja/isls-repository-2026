# Game-based Ambiguity Resolution to Promote Sustainability Ethics Dialog

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
While models of game-based learning have been used for disciplinary understanding and practices, ethics education poses a unique design challenge given its combination of subject matter content and values-informed decision-making. This poster presents a novel design and results from a sustainability ethics game designed to compel player discussion. Results focus on characterizing different ways in which ambiguity arose through play and suggest further work in exploring how ambiguity can support productive ethical deliberation.

Games for sustainability dialog Digital and non-digital games have been explored for sustainability education due to the way that games 1) engage players, leading them to learn more about climate c hange and 2) can model ecological complexity, a useful affordance for sustainability education (Lux & Budk e, 2020; Wu & Lee, 2015). Using games for sustainability ethics education poses unique challenges due to the way that content knowledge by itself may be insufficient for ethical action  (London, 2001). That is, sustainability ethics curriculum cannot focus merely on supporting an understanding of social, environmental, and economic tradeoffs, as knowledge of such tradeoffs does not necessarily guide one toward better, more ethical outcomes. Rather s ustainability ethics education must also address the way that real-world decisions must be presented, deliberated and reflected upon for assent by relevant stakeholders, drawing together multiple and diverse value systems that may not necessarily be congruent. One implication for the challenging nature of susta inability ethics education is that p rior game-based learning models and theories focused on disciplinary conceptual learning (e.g., Clark et al., 2015) may need to be adapted in order to be effectively applied to create sustainability ethics games. In this project, we begin to explore a new approach that departs from prior research, which has historically focused on: 1) disciplinary content and practices through clear models presented through in-game representations; and 2) student  learning through increasing alignment with accurate disciplinary pra ctices and representational understanding (Holbert & Wilensky, 2018). Instead, we explore ambiguity as a game design element, in which unclear representation and feedback are used to compel players’ in-game actions and reflection (Gaydos & Nardo, 2022). We conjecture that players’ need and tendency to resolve game-based ambiguity through discussion and action could be us ed as a basis for ethical deliberation. Though ambiguity has been recently theorized as a useful design principle for ethics education (Schrier, 201 7), particularly to embody the “wicked” nature of eth ics problems  (Gaydos & Nardo, 2022), its pedagogical role has not yet been theorized or supported empirically. Okunoshima: Design and Development This poster presents preliminary findings from a pilot study from the development of Okunoshima, a three to fiveperson card game designed for eventual use in a large (200+) introductory undergraduate course on sustainable tourism. Its focus is on sustainability initiatives affect ing an island in Hiroshima, Japan, where non-native domestic rabbits have been allowed to freely reproduce and roam, creating both a tourist attraction and ecological management challenge. The game is a deck of 43 numerically ordered cards, that include instructions, fictional individuals representing demographics affected by sustainabilit y initiatives on Okunoshima (“character  cards”), sustainability initiatives, and discussion prompts. To play the ga me, participants read and follow instructions printed on each of the cards. Generally, the game proceeds through four phases (Figure 1): 1) players review the six character cards, selecting the three “best” individuals to populate a governing committee that would decide on sustainability initiative s that should be enacted on the island; 2) players review eight sustainability initiatives, deciding which would most likely be supported by the committee they selected; 3) a new character is introduced, and players must replace someone on their current committee with the  new character; 4) players re-evaluate the initiatives, deciding which the new committee would  likely support. After the game, players discuss: “How is deciding who should be on the committee an ethical issue? ## Methods, analysis, and results

In our pilot, we conducted two playtests with eight students (two groups of four) recruited through word-of-mouth. Participants in the first playtest included three undergraduate and one graduate student. The second was comprised of four undergraduate students. The aims of the playtests were to 1) determine the feasibility of the game for eventual classroom use  and 2) begin to characterize the kinds of dialogue that would emerge through play, especially exploring the ways different and sometimes ambiguous values and worldviews represented through cards influence in-game discussion. That is, playtests were identical except that in the first playtest, players were asked to include a “local politician” card in their revised governing committee (Phase 3) while in the second play test, students were asked to include a “ rabbit spirit” − a card representing and advocating for the needs of  the island’s rabbits. The rabbit perspective was included to provoke reflect and discussion on the needs of non-humans in governance positioning students within an ecofeminist framework where the rabbits might be considered as agents with interests rather than tourism resources to be used and managed by humans (Usui & Funck, 2020). Playtests were video and audio recorded, and these recordings were transcribed. Transcriptions were first coded descriptively to familiarize researchers with  the in-game conversation. Transcripts were then re-coded, specifically focusing on identifying points during the game play where players confronte d ambiguity. These instances were categorized into three types: rule (R), endogenous (EN), and exogenous (EX). These types are differentiated with regards to their origins: R ambiguity arises from  confusion about game-appropriate actions; EN arises from confusion about concepts explicitly introduced in the game (e.g., ethics); and EX ambiguity arises from ideas introduced from player discussions  referring to game content not originally designed into the game (see Table 1).

## Discussion and conclusions

Results suggest that players confronted and overcame different types of ambiguity so that play could proceed, and in doing so, players occasionally engaged in dialog ue that may be useful for ethics education. While some observed ambiguity (e.g., R-ambiguity) may be important to minimize, other ambiguity (e. g., EN, EX) may be useful for compelling player discussion, especially  around players clarifying one another’s language a nd values. Further work is needed to explore how this ambiguity can be more deliberately supported as a useful basis for ethics education, especially to support dialogue that promotes unders tanding and clarifying stakeholders’ and players’ views.
