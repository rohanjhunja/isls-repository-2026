# Envisioning Social Justice through Future Headlines: A Framework for Analyzing Youth's Moral and Normative Reasoning

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In this study, we have built on critical and creative pedagogy approaches to invite youth to envision desirable futures by addressing social injustices in data-driven societies through creating Futures Headlines. We developed an analytical framework that distinguishes between moral and normative reasoning. These dimensions are elaborated into further categories that allow examining youth’s perceptions of social injustice, as well as how they envision justice. We claim the framework is a valuable tool for research and practice as it helps understanding youth’s thinking about justice as well as identifying areas for further discussion to foster youth’s critical awareness and reflection. Based on our findings, we advocate for embracing a transdisciplinary approach to justice and data literacy, paying special attention to aspects connected to historicity and systemic thinking.

## Introduction and background

In recent years, there has been an increasing inter est in promoting social justice in critical data literacy  (e.g., Dangol & Dasgupta, 2023; Irgens et al., 2020; Matuk et al., 2020; Polman et al., 2022), and social justice has been tackled in youth computing education informed by critical pedagogy (e.g., Lachney et al., 2021; Ryoo et al., 2022). Social justice is a complex concept with various competing definitions present in academic literature. In education, decades of research have theorized what it means to  teach in justice-centered ways (see Lingard & Mills, 2007), with important contribution s from critical pedagogy scholars. Despite this pri or work, recent research has advocated for further support for science educators  to plan and design learning within their classroom environments (Luehmann et al., 2024). There is also a lack of discussion on how youth understand and a pproach the complex concept of justice and how to introduce  and frame it in their education (Polman et al., 20 22). In the context of mathematics and civics education, scholars have called for the need to further explore the relationships between data literacy and social justice (see Knight et al. 2022). The current study aims to advance theory and practice by provid ing understanding of youth’s views on social justice in data-driven societies. We align with works that emphasiz e the importance of starting from youth’s perspectives to trigger discussions that foster critical awareness, reflection, and action towards social justice. While there is a growing corpus of research advocat ing for a justice-orientation in youth data literacy, few studies elaborate on social justice with clearly articulated frameworks informed by modern theories of justice. In this study, we want to highlight prior contributions combining data literacy with a focus on social, racial, spatial, and environmental justice, as we see them as opening a path for further development through theory and practice. Approaches to justice-oriented data literacy informed by critical race theory have advocated for developing culturally responsive strategies involving historically underrepresented minorities (see Payne et al., 2021), helping youth become both racially literate about data and data-literate about race by using da ta visualizations (Philip et al., 2016). Feminist perspectives have placed at th e center the ethical implications of practices rang ing from defining what counts as data, to data collection and use (see Lee et al., 2022). Contributions integra ting critical mathematics and critical pedagogy of place have loo ked at space as a matter of (in)justice (see Rubel & Nicol, 2020), and from an environmental lens, justice has been at the center of youth’s engagement with data to examine unequal impacts of climate chang e on social groups (Burns et al., 2023). All these works share an emphasis in going beyond data literacy as technical skills, fos tering inclusion and participation by making data r elevant and by embracing diversity of practices to foster critical awareness and reflection. Education's transformative power  is at the core of data literacies approaches inform ed by critical pedagogy. However, for transformation to happen, we  need to first imagine how a better, more just, and  equal future might look like. Influential authors such as  Paulo Freire, Henri Giroux a nd Maxine Greene have stressed the importance of imagination to envision how thing s could be otherwise. In an age in which dataficati on and algorithmic decision systems driven by neoliberal values pose a serious risk of becoming disimagination machines that disempower youth, there is a need to claim imagination as a radical and productive activity. Methodologically, our work derives inspiration from speculative and d esign futuring traditions  (see Dunne & Raby, 2013), which emphasize using design to imagine and explore alter native (utopian, dystopian, desirable) futures, to question prevalent notions (see Iivari et al., 2022; Wargo & Alvarado, 2020). We invite youth to envision desirable futures, i.e., to dream about more just and equal futures in  a data-driven world, while we acknowledge this dreaming exercise also tells about the present. As our resea rch question, we ask: “How do envisioning activitie s reflect youth’s thinking about social justice?” Our study contributes to the design of activities that aim to trigger critical debates on social justice in data-driven societies with youth by engaging them in envisioning futures.

## Research design

The study involved 14 teenagers (aged 15-16) in a two-day Critical Data Literacy workshop focused on soci al justice in data-driven systems. Participants were diverse in gender identity and cultural backgrounds, with several having lived abroad. This study focuses on the work shop activity "Future Headlines", which consists of  creating fictional news headlines set in a future where curr ent societal challenges have been resolved, with th e goal of describing how these changes came about. We chose t his technique because imagining future(s) helps explore present perceptions, which are also shaped by the past (see Cantó-Milà & Seebach, 2024). In groups, participants envisioned a desirable future where a specific soci al injustice in data-driven societies had been addressed. The data collected comprises background questionnaires (n=14), visuals and texts of five fictional “Future Headlines,” audio of participants’ presentations, and researchers’ field notes. Informed consent was obtained from all participants, adhering to ethical research practices and Finnish National Board on Research Integrity guidelines. The qualitative data analysis used an analytical fr amework to examine youth's social justice thinking, defined through an inductive-deductive approach and informed by existing frameworks in social justice education, critical consciousness, and ethical reas oning. The framework distinguishes between moral reasoni ng and normative reasoning. Moral reasoning is understood as changes in thinking following approved moral principles. This type of reasoning has been considered key for human and societal development since it helps people realize when change is needed (Killen & Dahl, 2021). In this analysis, moral reas oning categories focus on assessing to what extent youth identified the conditions of the social injustices mentioned in their news stories, and include social injustice, interests considered, context, power and privilege, level and temporality (see Table 1). Normative reasoning refers to how things ought to be. Since the participants envisioned futures where social injustices were addressed, the analysis categories aim to reveal their views on what a soci ally just society ought to be. We have built on Rippon et al.’s (2020) categorization of j ustice approaches in political philosophy, which include s grounds, shape, site and scope of justice (see Table 1). ## Findings and implications

This section presents findings on youth's social ju stice thinking using the analytical framework dimen sions (see Tables 2 and 3), along with suggestions to enhance their critical awareness and reflection. In the future headlines, youth highlighted social injustices like labor market discrimination, racism, xenophobia, and criminal justice bias, mostly focusing on social minorities’ interests. From a moral reasoning perspective (Table 2), participants  were able to articulate how social and cultural categori es such as sex and gender, age, ability, nationalit y, and race produced collective identities (#1, #2, #3), distin guishing which social groups might hold privileges. As one of #1 authors expressed: "My inspiration for this was jus t the story about how Amazon tried to [do hiring by  using AI to go through] job applications, but it just turned  out very racist and sexist because it [AI] was tra ined on poor data". All groups acknowledged discrimination and o ppression as exercised by individuals. Several also  pointed at the systemic level (#1, #3, #4), emphasizing the  underlying structures of oppression enabling discr iminatory practices to thrive. Examining youth’s contributions from the lens of normative reasoning revealed how their views on social justice influenced the kinds of futures and alternatives th ey imagined (Table 3). In all the news, the grounds  of justice were the respect for people as free and equal. Youth's views on justice, emphasizing equality and fairness, aligned with John Rawls' liberal theories. Many of the news aligned with strict egalitarian views of justice (# 1, #3, #4) and in two cases, cosmopolitan principles such as f air treatment across cultures were highlighted (#2,  #5). Interestingly, all the news that explicitly pointed at the role of data to promote social justice resonated with strict egalitarian ideas (#1, #3, #4), envisioning solutions to protect individuals from discriminat ion by revising data collection practices. For instance, in #1 suggested removing personal data from curriculum vitae  and de-identifying applications with a predefined identity (see Figure 1). In #3 and #4, accurate data collection was seen as the solution to dismantle decision-making based on bias and prejudice (for e.g in #3 authors wrote: “Data sets between white people and coloured people from the same income families were compared and it was determined that the percentage of people committing crimes was equal”) All the stories highlighted people’s rights and liberties: to access and participate in the work market (#1), to enjoy one’s culture without being discriminated against (#2), to due process and fair trial in just ice (#3, #4), and to access service of publi c accommodations such as pubs (#5). In many stories, youth reflected on issues concerning redistribution and recognition (#1, #2, #3) by advocating for equal opportunities and by acknowledging and respecting individuals’ and group s’ identities and cultural  differences. When the emphasis was on rights (#4, #5), it was possible to identify  some tensions regarding whose rights should prevai l. For instance, in #4 the implementation of a video-surveillance system in public spaces could be seen as violating individual’s rights. All news pointed at the actions of individuals as the main site of justice, and in thr ee cases (#1, #3, #4) the basic structure of social institut ions was also considered. Here, we could observe an  alignment with the level at which social injustice was perceived to operate since the cases that framed injustice as a systemic issue, also conceived justice from a structural per spective. Finally, when considering to whom the pri nciples of justice apply, youth tended to restrict the scope of justice to the nation state (#2, #4, #5). The analysis of Future Headlines reveals youth's mo ral and normative reasoning, showing their ethical thinking about current social injustices and envisi oning a more just and equal future.  We argue that envisioning is about exploring and shaping possible futures, as  well as influencing how we interpret and act in th e world (Stoetzler & Yuval-Davis, 2002). Thus, the Futures Headlines created a t the workshop showcase youth’s perceptions on social injustice and their conceptio n of justice. We claim the analytical framework (s ee Table 1) provides a major contribution to and advances critical data literacies research and practice by helping to examine participants’ envisioning about social justice. We believe the distinction between moral and normative reasoning is a valuable contribution since it brings insights  into how participants conceptualize social (in)justice a nd helps understanding how these views impact their imaginat ions of future justice-oriented societies. For educational practice, breaking down theoretical concepts such as social injustice and justice into specific categories may also be useful for identifying areas for further discuss ion and elaboration in the learning activities. Bas ed on our findings, we recommend educational activities to en hance youth's moral and normative reasoning about s ocial justice in data-driven systems. We advocate for embracing a transdisciplinary approach to justice and data literacy, paying special attention to aspects connected to hi storicity and systemic thinking to foster youth’s c ritical awareness and reflection. We acknowledge the small number of participants as a limitation, necessitating caution and further research.
