# “For the RE[STORY]”: How an Adolescent Black Girl Reimagines Computing Technology Through Electronic Textile Quilting

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Despite computing technologies being positioned throughout society as neutral, the dominant framing of narratives in the field through white, masculine perspectives has manifested in artificial intelligence systems categorizing Black women and girls using historically-oppressive labels. To design for and center Black girls’ multiple identities in computing education, I propose electronic textile quilting—creating quilt patches that could be programmed to blink lights or play sound using sensors—as a vehicle for engaging Black girls in the process of “restorying” dominant narratives about computing. After analyzing minoritized youth’s quilt artifacts, video observations and field notes, I present findings from one adolescent Black girl participant, Larry, whose quilt patch restorying a WNBA-inspired video game reframed dominant computing narratives from an exclusive lens to one of gender representation. Through analyzing how narrative framing shapes which stories are told, youth can better discern how dehumanizing ideologies manifest in the design of emerging technologies.

## Introduction

Despite computing technologies being positioned throughout society as neutral/objective, the dominant, “default” framing of the field through narratives centering w hite, masculine perspectives have manifested in art ificial intelligence systems (e.g., Google search and autom ated image labeling) categorizing Black girls using dehumanizing, historically-oppressive labels (Noble, 2018). This study aims to design for the multiple identities of Black girls while interrogating and transforming the ways they are socioculturally “coded” through computing technologies. To conceptualize how marginalized youth’s computing identities are raced or gendered, I draw from both disciplinary (Bell, Van Horne, & Cheng, 2017) and narrative (Sfard & Prusak, 2005) perspectives of identity. Broader societal narratives and their framing serve as identity resources in STEM and computing-related learning environments that position students relative to one another as well as the discipline at large, and through restorying, youth “narrate the word and the world, [analyze] th eir lived experiences and then [synthesize] and [recontextualize] a multiplicity of stories in order to form new narratives” (Thomas & Stornaiuolo, 2016, p. 318). In this study, I explored the restorying practices of Larry (a pseudonym created by one of the adolescent Black girls participating in this study) in her creation of an interactive quilt patch inspired by her personal interests in basketball and gaming, which she titled “For the  RE” (a play on the phrases “for the rebound” and “ for the restory”). As a medium and context for engaging Black girls in the restorying process, I propose electronic textile (hereafter, e-textile) quilting, which involves des igning and creating fabric-based, quilt patches tha t could be programmed to blink lights or play sound using sensors (e.g., Authors, 2021). As predominantly Black and Brown youth from a local, informal STEM program designed e-textile quilt patches, I ask: When restorying dom inant narratives about computing technology, how do youth frame those narratives? Through analyzing how narrative framing shapes which stories and perspectives are told (and which are silenced), youth and researchers can better discern how dehumanizing ideologies manifest in the design of emerging technologies.

## Theoretical framework

One of the resources people use to make sense of th e events around them are frames—available storyline s organizing their understanding of events—which help “specify how broader narratives come to have meaning in local spaces” (Gresalfi & Hand, 2019, p. 496). When  designing equitable computing learning environments for youth to learn disciplinary concepts and practices and develop practice-linked, disciplinary identities (Bell, Van Horne, & Cheng, 2017), it is important to consider how broader cultural narratives and frames (e.g., i deas and stories about computing people and marginalized com munities) shape youth’s computing identity construction. When viewed through a lens of techno-determinism, computing technology is framed as an objective tool that can affect society without being shaped by society itself (Benjamin, 2019); however, when framed through the lenses of racism and sexism, computing technology is revea led to uniquely marginalize women, people of color,  and gender non-conforming people (Jones & melo, 2020). In this study, I use the concept of framing to examine how marginalized youth make sense of narratives about c omputing technology, both dominant and reimagined. By providing Black and Brown youth opportunities to restory dominant narratives about computing technology, they will be able to deconstruct harmful narratives abou t those technologies (and how those narratives are framed in ICLS2022 Proceedings 1509 © ISLS popular discourse) in order to reconstruct both nar ratives and future technologies in speculative, emp owering ways.

In considering historical media and technologies fo r challenging oppressive narratives, Black women have used quilting as both an art form and sociopolitical tool to resist oppression and collectively record and share their cultural histories (Cash, 1995). In other words, Black women and girls have been restorying dominant and hidden narratives related to their experiences since their enslavement in this country, using quilting, fabrics, and each other to build sisterhoods and rewrite their stories (Cash, 1995). E-textiles, this study’s medium for quilting, are fabrics embedded with microcomputers, lights, a nd sensors stitched together with conductive thread  and programmed to perform various actions (Buechley, Pe ppler, Eisenberg, & Kafai, 2013). Given the success  of combining narratives and e-textiles in supporting girls of color’s STEM interests and identities (Pinkard, Erete, Martin, & McKinney de Royston, 2017), e-textile quilting serves as a novel approach for engaging marginalized youth in computing and restorying dominant computing narratives.

## Methodology

Through examining minoritized youth’s restorying pr actices through social design experimentation, this  study intends to “[identify] and [address] consequential issues [...] that limit opportunities for learning and becoming,” (Gutiérrez & Jurow, 2016, p. 593) particularly in t he context of learning computing. In collaboration with two graduate student interns, we designed a workshop for a four-year STEM program at a local science museum. We implemented the workshop over the course of two weeks during Spring 2021 (10 hours) and 3.5 hours daily during Summer 2021 (35 hours) with the goal of sixteen, 14-15-year-old minoritized youth designing interactiv e, etextile quilt patches that restory dominant narrati ves about computing technology. To support the iden tity and community building of participants, youth participa ted in regular icebreaker activities (e.g., creatin g “I Am” poems), as well as engaged in discussions about dom inant, hidden, and counternarratives about computin g technology and quilting, and how both can be leveraged as vehicles for restorying. Data collected includes youth’s artifacts (i.e., quilt patch with explanation video, online design journal, and exit tickets), video observations and field not es, and researcher memos, which were analyzed using constructivist grounded theory (CGT). A methodology  for situating research in “the historical, social,  and situational conditions of its productions” (Charmaz, 2017, p. 199), CGT uses comparative methods itera tively throughout the research process by inviting researc hers to alternate between collecting and analyzing data. I decided to focus the analysis for this study on Larry, who out of the five adolescent Black girls in the workshop expressed having the most prior interests and knowledge about computing technologies. Through conducting three iterative cycles of multimodal analysis on Larry’s restorying process, I reveal how narratives about computing are framed throughout Black girls’ lived and shared experiences, as well as how those experiences are drawn from in order to restory and reframe computing technology.

People m ake meaning about sociopolitical issues through “media-generated images of the world,” and the “lens through which [they] receive these images'' reveals the perspecti ves of those with the power to control the narrativ e (Gamson, Croteau, Hoynes, & Sasson, 1992, p. 374). When reflecting on the role designing her quilt patch played in helping her reimagine computing narratives, Larry shared that it helped her “recognize different narratives [from her everyday experiences] and break them down and see how changing something small would affect everything.” By sewing and coding an interactive quilt patch based on her identity as an adolescent Black girl who likes bask etball and video games, Larry was able to observe narratives surrounding these interests and frame those observations from multiple, distinct perspectives (including her pers pective, the perspective of others, and the perspec tive of her imagined audience).

“There wasn’t anything like that for us”: Reframing computing technology from a lens of exclusion to a lens of representation When framing narratives about computing technology like video games through lenses of exclusion and representation, Larry was able to tease apart both dominant and hidden narratives, particularly whose stories, identities, and interests are most reflected in the  design of certain video games. “These stories comp are to the history of computing and the belief that primarily or only men are involved in it. It’s a counter narr ative that women can be interested in video games, more specifically NBA2k games and that they also should have a game that includes their stories,” Larry shared halfway through the workshop in response to the question “How do those stories/experiences compare to the history of compu ting technology?”. This finding reflects both the underrepresentation of Black women in computing and gaming (Gray, 2020), as well as the need for more research examining their unique computing education experien ces (Solomon, Moon, Roberts, & Gilbert, 2018). When observing narratives surrounding video games she played personally—such as the “world-renowned, best-selling NBA 2K basketball video game series” as stated on t heir website—Larry did not see the basketball part of her identity included in the gaming industry, despite identifying with other Black women who play basketball (e.g., women in the WNBA). Through speculatively travelling back in time and designing an advertisement for a game that doesn’t exist (yet), Larry illustrates how res torying allowed her to (1) reframe computing throug h a lens of representation and belonging and (2) reimagine a technology that highlighted the stories of women and girls like her.

After being able to note and shift the way computin g technology was framed through restorying the dominant narratives affiliated with it, Larry also expressed a desire to “go more into depth about more dominant narratives,” so she could “teach more people about it and have more awareness of the issue” (7/30/21, exit ticket). Narratives connect to broader societal and cultural  belief systems, stereotypes, and imaginaries, and given that they are leveraged by people to make sense of thems elves and others (Gresalfi & Hand, 2019), providing minoritized youth with opportunities to restory def icitizing narratives can support them in developing  their identity trajectories. Upon completing the workshop, Larry shared that she felt more “comfortable” and  “okay” with being who she is around others. “The topic I made my quilt about heavily applies to me, and I have challenged this dominant narrative before without realizing,” she reflected in her final exit ticket. This finding illustrated how designing a computational artifact that framed computing narratives from a lens of representation allowed Larry to restory learning computing to learning how to represent herself and her community.

## Implications for the Field

The restorying approach centers the historical and present lived experiences of marginalized communities (in this case, Black girls) as its methodological contributi on for reimagining STEM and computing education thr ough social design experimentation (Gutiérrez & Jurow, 2 016), which argues that “the coordination of past, present, and future-oriented actions and identities sets the conditions for new forms of agency central to realizing possible futures” (p. 567). Traditional, normalized forms of  learning that are grounded in racist ideologies co ntinue to “spirit murder” Black girls and boys by “robbing dark people of their humanity and dignity and [leaving] personal, psychological, and spiritual injuries” (Love, 2019). Through centering disciplinary narratives and how  those ICLS2022 Proceedings 1511 © ISLS narratives impact the identity construction of Black and Brown youth, restorying provides youth opportunities to write themselves into computing stories. Methodologies that disrupt and think beyond dominant forms of learning (e.g., interdisciplinary activities such as e-textile quiltmaking) can provide not only authentic but also legitimate approaches for minoritized youth to imagine alterna te outcomes about STEM and computing, technology, a nd their communities.
