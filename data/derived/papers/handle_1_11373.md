# Promoting Culturally Responsive Teaching via Educational Game Design –Teachers as Educational Game Designers

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
As schools become culturally diverse, integrating students' cultural backgrounds into teaching practices is essential. Culturally Responsive Teaching (CRT) has emerged as a critical focus in professional development (PD) for educators. However, many PD programs inadequately train teachers in CRT, resulting in superficial cultural integration. This exploratory study investigates the impact of educational game design workshops on promoting CRT, positioning teachers as serious game designers. Nine elementary mathematics teachers participated in a workshop that involved designing culturally responsive educational games. Through thematic analysis of interview data, findings reveal that game design fosters teachers’ empathy and understanding of students' cultural contexts, enabling the incorporation of diverse cultural elements into their teaching. Challenges identified include cultural gaps and limited design skills. The study highlights the potential of collaborative game design in enhancing CRT, suggesting that hands-on, creative PD approaches candeepen teachers’ cultural awareness and instructional practices.

## Introduction

It is crucial for teachers to consider the culture b ackgrounds of students and incorporate them into th eir teaching practices as schools become more culturally diverse (Ambe, 2006; Banks, 2006). Culturally responsive teaching (CRT) has  become a  focus of professional development (PD) for both pre-service and in-service teachers. However, many PD programs fail to provide effective CRT training for teachers, leading to superficial integration of students’ culture into their curricula (Lam, 2022). The main challenge lies in the transformative nature of CRT, which requires teachers to engage in a deep reflect ion on their cultural beliefs and how they can use the diverse cultures of students as teaching assets (Fullam, 20 07; Gay & Kirkland, 2003). Educational researchers and practitioners have recognized the benefits of involving teachers in collaborative instructional design practices for PD (e.g., Fu et al., 2021; Voogt et al., 2015). Among these collaborative design approaches, involving teachers in educational game design has shown its unique training benefits. Educational game design helps teachers recognize the value of play-based learning (Li, 2012) and encourages them to  use students’ culture and experience as teaching resources (Kim & Johnson, 202 1). However, previous PD research involving educational game design primarily focused on how teachers integrate technology (e.g., Li, 2012). The impact of educational game-design on teachers’ understanding of CRT and the challenges they encountered in this design process remains unknown. Therefore, this exploratory study aims to answer the following three research questions:

1. How does educational game design influence teachers’ understandings of CRT?

2. How do teachers incorporate CRT into their educational games?

3. What challenges do teachers encounter in designing educational games for CRT?

## Theoretical framework: game design as a collaborative cultural production

A well-designed educational game offers a learning culture  that connects with students’ pre-existing habits and interests (Prensky, 2001). In essence, educational gameplay is experiential learning rooted in serious play (Lloyd, 2001). It immerses learners in meaningful contexts or scenarios, allow ing them to “explore, discover, and question, ultimately constructing concepts and relationships in authentic contexts ” (Yang, 2012, p.1). For teachers, educational game design is not only  an instructional design practice, but also a cultural production of a meaningful play-based learning culture and environment. This design process promotes an active culture reflection. The reflection  helps teachers acknowledge that learning is an acti ve process where learners construct knowledge based upon existing experiences, culture, and context (Hargreaves, 1994; Young, 2010). For instance, Garcia  and Gomez (2017) found that involving teachers in t he game-design practice s encouraged teachers to focus on students’ needs and consider e ngaging students in a deep and intrinsic exploratio n of the subject. Similarly, Kafai and colleagues (1998) hig hlighted that serious game design shifted math educ ators’ teaching focus from extrinsic motivation to intrinsic motivation. As suggested by Kim and Johnson (2021), game design motivated pre-service teachers to reimagine the classroom structure to support the kind of meaningful play that connects the culture of students with the classroom curriculum. Incorporating educational g ame design into PD also shifts the power dynamics from the trainer to teachers (Garcia & Gomez, 2017). Since the design p rocess relies on teachers’ active involvement, it improves the agency of teachers  and their learning autonomy (Li, 2012). Additionall y, the collaborative design allows teachers to learn from other teachers’ perspectives  on CRT, which encourages reflection on their own CRT practices based on peers’ comments (Chien et al., 2012; Nguyen & Bower, 2018).

## Participants and procedure

The study was conducted in a public elementary scho ol located in a predominantly Black neighborhood in the Midwestern United States. Nine elementary teachers participated in this study. None of them had prior training in game design before this study. After participating the workshop, they were interviewed about their game design experience and its influence on their understanding of CRT. Each interview lasted between 30 and 45 minutes. Workshop design The workshop consisted of three sessions (see Figure 1). Each session lasted about three hours. In the first session, teachers were introduced to CRT related challenges in math education, including the lack of personalized learning experiences and limited student agency. Teachers also learned about CRT strategies with s pecific examples o f how math problems and tasks could be presented to include sociocultural contexts making them more relevant to students. In the second session, teachers focused on educational game design and mechanics, as well as strategies to make game mechanics culturally responsive. At the end of this session, they were divided into small groups (23 teachers per group, 3 groups in total) to brainstorm concepts for their own educational games. The third session focused on game design and testing. Each group was provided with materials to create a paper prototype  to visualize their game ideas. They then used the game prototype to test their game ideas with other groups. Figure 1 Brainstorming Game Concepts (Left); Creating Paper Prototypes (middle); Testing Game Ideas (Right)

## Data analysis

Thematic analysis was used to analyze the interview data collected. Thematic analysis is an effective method for “examining the perspectives of different research participants, highlighting similarities and differe nces, and generating unanticipated insights” (Nowell et al., 2017, p. 2). The thematic analysis procedure followed those five steps (Braun & Clarke, 2006): 1) Familiarizing with the data and developing initial codes; 2) Coding the data; 3) Searching for potential themes; 4) Defining and Naming Themes; and 5) Producing Thematic Report. The interview transcripts were coded using NVivo 12. The data was first coded inductively by the first  author with purposes for searching all possible patterns for two research questions.  The initial codes were then used for the second-round coding. After iterative cycles of reading, coding, and grouping, 32 codes were grouped into 6 categories. Based on those categories, all the them es were generated and reported.  To validate the themes, peer debriefing was conducted in which the first author shared the themes and data with ot her two authors to collect their feedback and refine the interpretations.

## Preliminary results

Q1: How does educational game design influence teachers’ understanding of CRT? Theme 1: “Connection Making-View Teaching from Children’s eye” Since serious game design requires designers to con tinually consider how learners interact with the game, this design experience prompted teachers to adopt their students' perspectives, exploring how their teaching practices align with students’ interests, as one teacher note d during the interview, “Educational game design  encourages me to look through a child's eye. It motivated me t o think about what  our kids would be interested in and what things did we notice make them feel excited.” Another teacher also commented similarly, “I thought game design was really cool!  It took our thinking to a different level. It made me constantly think about who's involved, like who's my audience.” Those quotes suggest that educational game design encouraged teachers to adopt a studentcentered approach, which is the foundation of CRT. Theme 2: “An expanded view of culture” Educational game design requires designers to create meaningful narratives and stories that engage learners. When narratives are crafted to reflect players’ backgrou nds and experience, the game will be more likely to  resonate with them. This helps teachers recognize the value of students’ diverse experience and backgrounds, as suggested by a teacher, The way I perceive meaningful game design or teachi ng is something that we give them based on their own background knowledge. I can't do a game about skiing if they never skied before. I am looking for the cultural background that way they can make the connection. I haven't really done it before. But I really think about it now.

Educational game design processes also expand teachers’ view of students’ culture. During the workshop, we provide d teachers with culturally responsive examples showin g how various aspects of culture – such as experiences, community, tradition – could be incorporated into the design of educational games. This helped teachers realize that culture is not only limited to race, and that they can incorporate various cultural aspects into their teaching to make it more relevant to their students: Letting students see that there's a purpose behind what they're doin g and connecting teaching with their own lives is also culturally relevant. But we just don't think about it. When we say culturally relevant, we automatically think of like  black or white or races and ethnicities and that's not all it is.

Q2: How did teachers incorporate CRT into their educational games? Theme 1: Inclusive Game Characters Design Non-Player Characters (NPC) are characters in the game that are not controlled by the player or learner. When designing NPCs, teachers tr y to increase the cultu ral relatedness of those characters by including commun ity members who are familiar to students, such as the s chool principal or school nurses. For instance, one  teacher group designed a geometry learning game  where students learned various geometrical shapes by re-constructing buildings that exist the community. One teacher said, “I think if we incorporated people from our community into the game or  even places – I mean the geometric buildings are the same as the places in our community. That would make it more interesting to them.” In addition to NPCs, when designing player-controlled characters (avatars), one teacher group designed a character customization feature that allows stude nts to create their own avatars.  They hoped the customization option may make the game play experience more engaging and personalized. This demonstrate that teachers were able to recognize the importance of incorporating p ersonalization and student agency as part of cultur ally responsive teaching practices in game design.

Theme 2: Game Reward System – Promote meaningful learning Teachers aimed to create a game culture that promote d meaningful learning, as reflected in  their design of the game’s reward system. Instead of using the typical “points-earning” approach, they designed a reward system to promote students’ intrinsic learning motivation. For instance, one teacher group designed a reward s ystem that encourages students to see the impact of their achi evements on the community. “Once they [students] complete the challenge, a new building will pop up in the neighborhood or the c ommunity. It's something for them to see how much impact they made for the community.”  This approach reflected teachers’ efforts  to shift from a competitive culture (focusing on points and scores)  to a collaborative culture that encourages contribution to a shared community. As one teacher said,  “ [it] would be great if we could figure out ways to engag e them to collaborate with each other rather than competing with each other.” Q3: What challenges do teachers encounter in designing educational games for CRT? Theme 1: “Culture Gap” One of the primary  culture-related concerns expressed by teachers is the cultural gap between themselves and their students. When incorporating culture into game design, s everal teachers worry that their own experience may not align with those of their students  due to generational differences. For instance, one teacher highlight ed the difference in media preferences, saying, “When I was young, we did a whole lot of animated but sup erficial things. Everything now is kind of realistic…”.  This shows that while teachers  acknowledge the generational differences between themselves and their students, they find it challenging to understand students’ culture. Cultural differences further raised teachers’ concern about misrepresenting or oversimplifying students’ cultures, particularly for minority students whose backgrounds differ from their own. One teacher said, “We need to go on a journey of learning about their culture bec ause there are so many layers to culture. We didn't want to be offensive. We should examine our assumptions. I don't want to mis-speak on it.”  Despite of the culture gap, this quote suggests teachers’ willingness to know about student’s culture and their awareness of personal cultural biases and assumptions.

Another concern among teachers  is about the “appropriateness” of certain students’ culture.  Some expressed hesitation when it came to incorporating students’ culture—aspects they perceived as inappropriate— into the game, despite the fact that it would resonate with students. One teacher said, “You have to make sure that you don't cross that line to reach them because even though they're doing it and they may be allowed to do it. But, sometimes that's inappropriate. And, there's a problem.” Another teacher also expressed a similar concern: “What if the culture they appreciate is not appropriate, right? Should we follow that, or should we draw a line.” These quotes reflect teachers' struggle with navigating cultural boundaries and determining how to incorporate students' cultures into their teaching in ways that are moral and align with social norms.  On the other hand, as manifested by the games teachers designed, what they considered “appropriate” or felt most comfortable with was incorporating local environment, communities, and familiar figure s into game design. However, they were not able to engage in deeper cultural integration – such as underlying values, learning norms, or cultu ral meanings embedded in student’s live experiences.

Theme 2: “Design Gap-I am not a designer” Most teachers have game-based learning experience, but their experiences ar e only limited to the use of pre-existing games  designed by others, including physical game-based activities, card games, and digital serious games. Most of them lack confidence in the game design, particularly when it comes to visualiz ing their game ideas. As suggested by those comments, “I know I will neve r be able to make my own.”  “ I didn't have the experience of like actually building a game or thin king through it. ” Those quotes indicate that game-design workshops need to provide more support (e.g., game design training) and resources (e.g., prototyping t ools) to help teachers gain confidence.

## Discussion

This study explored the influence of educational game design on teachers’ understanding of CRT and their design experiences. The results indicate that educational game design practices enh ance teachers’ empathy for students, encouraging them to adopt students’ perspectives. T his aligns with previous research highlighting how educational game design motivates teachers to use s tudents' culture and experiences as teaching resources (Kim & Johnson, 2022). Moreover, our study found that game design broadens teachers’  understanding of students’ culture, helping them recognize how various cultura l aspects—such as their daily experiences, community, and traditions, and social norms—can be integrated into educational games and their teaching practices. On the other hand, teachers expressed their concerns about their limited understanding of students' cultures and the appropriateness of certain cultural elements.

While teachers were able to conceptualize culturally responsive games, most of them lacked confidence in their game design skills. This might be caused by their misconception between  “design” and “development”. During the workshop, teachers initially felt overwh elmed by the idea of designing a game. Once we expl ained that the focus was on their game ideas and introduc ed them the low-fidelity paper prototyping design technique, they became more comfortable with the design process.

Although this study is limited in its relatively sm all sample size, it serves as an exploratory study that reveal potential benefits of educational game desig n to CRT. More research, conducted in a variety of subject domains, is needed to understand the full potential  of educational game design to teachers and what sc affoldings may best support them in this process.
