# Dialogic Intelligence: Rethinking the pedagogical goal of CSCL in the context of Generative AI

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
As generative AI surpasses students in knowledge delivery and examination performance, education faces an unprecedented challenge in defining what human intelligence means. This theory paper argues that our response should be to reclaim, revalue, and rethi nk intelligence itself, advancing dialogic intelligence as a central educational aim. The dominant view of intelligence as an individual, measurable trait reflects print literacy's historical affordances, which have obscured intelligence's fundamentally relational nature. AI's emergence as a ubiquitous cognitive technology necessitates recovering an older understanding: intelligence is the capacity to support the emergence of truth and goodness through responsive relationship—whether between people, with nature, or with technology such as generative AI. While the CSCL community has explored teaching thinking, research has typically focused on collaboration as a means to enhance curricular learning. The dialogic responsiveness of generative AI suggests instead that we should design CSCL to promote dialogic intelligence as an end in itself. We illustrate this agenda through two CSCL design examples that prioritize dialogic intelligence over content mastery. Generative AI and educational aims Generative AI has forced education into a moment of reckoning. When systems such as ChatGPT can pass professional examinations, retrieve information instantly, generate plausible arguments, and simulate dialogue, it becomes difficult to defend educational models centred on individual recall, routine problem-solving, and the production of competent prose. Much early response has been defensive: detecting AI use, designing “AI-proof” assessments, or treating AI simply as a support tool. But these responses avoid the deeper question for the Computer-Supported Collaborative Learning (CSCL) community: what should education aim to cultivate in human learners? CSCL should move beyond treating collaboration mainly as a means of helping individuals master curriculum content and instead place dialogic intelligence at the centre of educational design. Dialogic intelligence is not an individual trait measured by speed, accuracy, or test performance. It is the capacity for meaning, insight, and judgement to emerge through responsive relationships: between people, between people and traditions of knowledge, and increasingly between people and AI systems. Historically, dominant ideas of intelligence were shaped by print culture. Earlier traditions linked intelligence to judgement, discernment, and wisdom exercised in context. Socrates, for example, valued the “living word” of dialogue because it could respond, be questioned, and remain open to revision, unlike the fixed and answerless word of writing (Plato, Phaedrus, 275d). Over time, however, writing and later print culture encouraged a more monologic conception of thought: linear, abstract, decontextualiz ed, and increasingly located within the individual mind (Ong, 1982; Olson, 1994; Toulmin, 1990). By the twentieth century, psychometric testing turned this into a scientific orthodoxy. Intelligence became whatever could be standardized, measured, and compared across individuals (Binet & Simon, 1916/1973; Spearman, 1904). Yet this was never a neutral description of human thinking. It reflected the cognitive demands of print-based schooling. Tasks involving vocabulary, abstract classification, analogy, and formal reasoning were elevated as signs of intelligence because they fitted the dominant educational system. Cross-cultural studies and the Flynn effect later showed how contingent this was: what counts as intelligence changes with cultural practices, technologies, and forms of education (Ceci, 1991; Cole & Scribner, 1974). IQ captures something real, but that “something” is not intelligence in the broadest human sense. It is performance within a historically specific literacy regime. Much CSCL research has significantly advanced understanding of how learners work together productively. However, it has largely remained tied to the print paradigm, treating collaboration as valuable because it improves individual outcomes such as knowledge acquisition, problem-solving, or reasoning performance. The deeper possibility is that intelligence does not merely happen inside individuals and then get expressed in groups. Rather, intelligence can emerge in the shared space of dialogue itself. 641 Research on collective intelligence points in this direction. Woolley and colleagues showed that groups candisplay a form of collective intelligence not reducible to the abilities of individual members (Woolley et al., 2010). Work in the Thinking Together tradition similarly found that when students were taught norms for exploratory talk, both group and individual reasoning improved (Mercer & Littleton, 2007). Close analysis suggested that explicit reasoning moves alone did not explain the most important moments of insight. Often the decisive shift came in shared attention, pause, tension, or a sudden moment of recognition before explanation could even be articulated. The talk moves mattered, but mainly because they helped open what came to be called dialogic space: the relational field in which new meaning becomes possible (Wegerif, 2008). Dialogic intelligence as an educational aim Dialogic intelligence is not best understood as a possession but as an event. It arises when participants hold perspectives in tension, attend responsively to one another, and remain open to something beyond their current assumptions. Bakhtin’s account of double-voicedness helps here: to think dialogically, one must inhabit one’s own perspective while also entering into another’s (Bakhtin, 1981). Mead’s notion of the generalized other adds that reflective thought depends on taking up the standpoint of a wider community (Mead, 1934/1967). But genuine dialogue goes further than simply reproducing communal norms. It also opens toward what Bakhtin called a superaddressee and what might be described more broadly as an Infinite Other: a horizon of truth, goodness, or understanding that is never fully exhausted by current knowledge (Levinas, 1969; Wegerif, 2025). Generative AI does not possess lived subjectivity, but it can function as a remarkably articulate simulated voice. It can represent the accumulated discourse of disciplines, professions, and traditions. In that sense, AI can give learners access to a power ful form of the generalized other. A student in dialogue with AI can encounter what counts as established understanding in mathematics, science, history, or law. T he educational value of this does not lie in receiving better answers more efficiently. It lies in using that encounter as a platform for further questioning. If students simply defer to AI, they risk conformity to cliché. If they engage it dialogically, they can use it to test assumptions, reframe problems, and move beyond what is already establi shed. This distinction can be illustrated by the provisional findings of two design-based research examples which we have conducted at Cambridge. In one study, AI moderators were introduced into small-group collaborative learning. Early versions failed because they addressed students individually and did not support genuinely shared inquiry. After iterative refinement, however, students reported that the AI began to connect viewpoints, bring ideas together, and raise possibilities the group had not considered. It appeared to support depth, breadth, momentum, and inclusion within the discussion. Importantly, these gains did not register on standard critical thinking measures, suggesting that dialogic intelligence may require different forms of assessment from those used for decontextualized reasoning (Michaels & O’Connor, 2015; Hennessy et al., 2016). In a second project, AI was designed as a dialogic partner for leadership training in difficult workplace conversations. Rather than acting as a compliant assistant, the system was configured to challenge, resist, and force participants to reconsider their habitual responses. Participants reported that this kind of difficult AI interlocutor was valuable precisely because it created the tension needed for growth. In both studies, the key issue was not AI capability in itself, but the quality of the relational space created through interaction. These examples also clarify the difference between dialogic intelligence and what is often called hybrid intelligence. Hybrid intelligence usually refers to combining the strengths of humans and AI so that each compensates for the other’s weaknesses. That is useful, but dialogic intelligence points to something deeper. The most important educational moments do not occur because two separate agents contribute separate capacities. They occur when a shared space of inquiry opens in which new understanding emerges that belongs fully toneither the human nor the machine alone. The implication for CSCL is substantial. The task is no longer simply to design collaboration so that learners achieve predefined curricular goals more efficiently. It is to design environments that open, sustain, and deepen dialogic space. That means fram ing AI not mainly as answer-provider or tutor, but as a dialogic partner that can represent established knowledge while also provoking learners to move beyond it. It also means rethinking assessment so that it attends to relational capacities: how learners connect perspectives, sustain uncertainty, reshape questions, and generate insight together. Generative AI exposes the narrowness of the print-based conception of intelligence on which much modern education has relied. What remains distinctly human is not superior information processing, but care: the capacity to care about truth, to remain answerable for judgement, and to sustain inquiry beyond what is immediately given. If CSCL can make dialogic intelligence its central aim, AI becomes not a threat to education but an opportunity to recover and extend intelligence in its deeper sense: as participation in a shared, open-ended dialogue through which we learn, create, and orient ourselves towards what is true and good. 642 References Bakhtin, M. Generative AI and educational aims Generative AI has forced education into a moment of reckoning. When systems such as ChatGPT can pass professional examinations, retrieve information instantly, generate plausible arguments, and simulate dialogue, it becomes difficult to defend educational models centred on individual recall, routine problem-solving, and the production of competent prose. Much early response has been defensive: detecting AI use, designing “AI-proof” assessments, or treating AI simply as a support tool. But these responses avoid the deeper question for the Computer-Supported Collaborative Learning (CSCL) community: what should education aim to cultivate in human learners? CSCL should move beyond treating collaboration mainly as a means of helping individuals master curriculum content and instead place dialogic intelligence at the centre of educational design. Dialogic intelligence is not an individual trait measured by speed, accuracy, or test performance. It is the capacity for meaning, insight, and judgement to emerge through responsive relationships: between people, between people and traditions of knowledge, and increasingly between people and AI systems. Historically, dominant ideas of intelligence were shaped by print culture. Earlier traditions linked intelligence to judgement, discernment, and wisdom exercised in context. Socrates, for example, valued the “living word” of dialogue because it could respond, be questioned, and remain open to revision, unlike the fixed and answerless word of writing (Plato, Phaedrus, 275d). Over time, however, writing and later print culture encouraged a more monologic conception of thought: linear, abstract, decontextualiz ed, and increasingly located within the individual mind (Ong, 1982; Olson, 1994; Toulmin, 1990). By the twentieth century, psychometric testing turned this into a scientific orthodoxy. Intelligence became whatever could be standardized, measured, and compared across individuals (Binet & Simon, 1916/1973; Spearman, 1904). Yet this was never a neutral description of human thinking. It reflected the cognitive demands of print-based schooling. Tasks involving vocabulary, abstract classification, analogy, and formal reasoning were elevated as signs of intelligence because they fitted the dominant educational system. Cross-cultural studies and the Flynn effect later showed how contingent this was: what counts as intelligence changes with cultural practices, technologies, and forms of education (Ceci, 1991; Cole & Scribner, 1974).  IQ captures something real, but that “something” is not intelligence in the broadest human sense. It is performance within a historically specific literacy regime. Much CSCL research has significantly advanced understanding of how learners work together productively. However, it has largely remained tied to the print paradigm, treating collaboration as valuable because it improves individual outcomes such as knowledge acquisition, problem-solving, or reasoning performance. The deeper possibility is that intelligence does not merely happen inside individuals and then get expressed in groups. Rather, intelligence can emerge in the shared space of dialogue itself. 641 Research on collective intelligence points in this direction. Woolley and colleagues showed that groups candisplay a form of collective intelligence not reducible to the abilities of individual members (Woolley et al., 2010). Work in the Thinking Together  tradition similarly found that when students were taught norms for exploratory talk, both group and individual reasoning improved (Mercer & Littleton, 2007). Close  analysis suggested that explicit reasoning moves alone did not explain the most important moments of insight. Often the decisive shift came in shared attention, pause, tension, or a sudden moment of recognition before explanation could even be articulated. The talk moves mattered, but mainly because they helped open what came to be called dialogic space: the relational field in which new meaning becomes possible (Wegerif, 2008). Dialogic intelligence as an educational aim Dialogic intelligence  is not best understood as a possession but as an event. It arises when participants hold perspectives in tension, attend responsively to one another, and remain open to something beyond their current assumptions. Bakhtin’s account of double-voicedness helps here: to think dialogically, one must inhabit one’s own perspective while also entering into another’s (Bakhtin, 1981). Mead’s notion of the generalized other adds that reflective thought depends on taking up the standpoint of a wider community (Mead, 1934/1967). But genuine dialogue goes further than simply reproducing communal norms. It also opens toward what Bakhtin called a superaddressee and what might be described more broadly as an Infinite Other: a horizon of truth, goodness, or understanding that is never fully exhausted by current knowledge (Levinas, 1969; Wegerif, 2025). Generative AI does not possess lived subjectivity, but it can function as a remarkably articulate simulated voice. It can represent the accumulated discourse of disciplines, professions, and traditions. In that sense, AI can give learners access to a power ful form of the generalized other. A student in dialogue with AI can encounter what counts as established understanding in mathematics, science, history, or law. T he educational value of this does not lie in receiving better answers more efficiently. It lies in using that encounter as a platform for further questioning. If students simply defer to AI, they risk conformity to cliché. If they engage it dialogically, they can use it to test assumptions, reframe problems, and move beyond what is already establi shed. This distinction can be illustrated by the provisional findings of two design-based research examples which we have conducted at Cambridge. In one study, AI moderators were introduced into small-group collaborative learning. Early versions failed because they addressed students individually and did not support genuinely shared inquiry. After iterative refinement, however, students reported that the AI began to connect viewpoints, bring ideas together, and raise possibilities the group had not considered. It appeared to support depth, breadth, momentum, and inclusion within the discussion. Importantly, these gains did not register on standard critical thinking measures, suggesting that dialogic intelligence may require different forms of assessment from those used for decontextualized reasoning (Michaels & O’Connor, 2015; Hennessy et al., 2016). In a second project, AI was designed as a dialogic partner for leadership training in difficult workplace conversations. Rather than acting as a compliant assistant, the system was configured to challenge, resist, and force participants to reconsider their  habitual responses. Participants reported that this kind of difficult AI interlocutor was valuable precisely because it created the tension needed for growth. In both studies, the key issue was not AI capability in itself, but the quality of the relational space created through interaction. These examples also clarify the difference between dialogic intelligence and what is often called hybrid intelligence. Hybrid intelligence usually refers to combining the strengths of humans and AI so that each compensates for the other’s weaknesses. That is useful, but dialogic intelligence points to something deeper. The most important educational moments do not occur because two separate agents contribute separate capacities. They occur when a shared space of inquiry opens in which new understanding emerges that belongs fully toneither the human nor the machine alone. The implication for CSCL is substantial. The task is no longer simply to design collaboration so that learners achieve predefined curricular goals more efficiently. It is to design environments that open, sustain, and deepen dialogic space. That means fram ing AI not mainly as answer-provider or tutor, but as a dialogic partner that can represent established knowledge while also provoking learners to move beyond it. It also means rethinking assessment so that it attends to relational capacities: how learners  connect perspectives, sustain uncertainty, reshape questions, and generate insight together. Generative AI exposes the narrowness of the print-based conception of intelligence on which much modern education has relied. What remains distinctly human is not superior information processing, but care: the capacity to care about truth, to remain answerable for judgement, and to sustain inquiry beyond what is immediately given.
