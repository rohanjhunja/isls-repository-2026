# Epistemic Diversity as a Design Principle in Computer Science

**Conference:** ISLS 2023

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper considers epistemic diversity as a design principle in a coding camp for middle school students. We examined the creation of student final projects for the camp to determine how they engaged with the discipline of computer science while working on projects that mattered to them. Our findings suggest that epistemic diversity as a design concept on its own is insufficient in engaging diverse students in disciplinary practices, and one way to increase such engagement is by designing computational models that allow students to interact with them in ways that encourage multiple opportunities for decision making.

## Introduction

A prevailing concern in STEM education in the United States involves a recur rent pattern of inequity. Despite some changes over the decades, STEM careers continue to be male-domina ted and include few people of color (Foundation, 2017), due to differences in access to coursework in K-12, and hostile learning environments that drive students out of STEM. Efforts to improve STEM learning have begun to exa mine what students have opportunities to learn, along with whether students feel that their identities are welcome inside the classroom, due to the ways that STEM learning environments can easily become racializ ed and gendered (Gholson & Martin, 2014; Leyva, 2017). One approach to addressing inequities in classroom involves making space for multiple ways of knowing (Bang & Medin, 2010; Espinoza et al., 2020). If students are offered open and diverse ways to engage STEM content, making room for their identities and reasoning, learning environment s might not only be more welcoming, but more epistemically diverse.

Agarwal and Sengupta-Irving (2019) define epistemic diversity as engaging with multiple ways of knowing and doing within a discipline, highlighting the possibility that different approaches, values, and practices can exist in the same field. Epistemic diversity involves a balance. On one s ide is a traditional learning environment that emphasizes a single (Western) way of knowing about STEM. On the other side is a risk of only emphasizing students’ home knowledge. In such cases students risk leaving a learni ng space without having grown or developed new skills. This is especially pertinent to marginalized stu dents, who often operate outside of the culture of power (Delpit, 1988). To not engage marginalized students in w ork that demystifies the culture of power is to remain complicit in their separation from it and their continues marginalization. This paper examines the work of a summer coding camp for a diverse group of middle-school stude nts, where epistemic diversity as well as disciplinary learning were taken seriously as design principles. By analyzing student progress on final projects for the camp, we ask, how were certain tools and coding models that were utilized at the camp effective at supporting both disciplinary learning as well as epistemic diversity?

## Theoretical framework: Connected and productive disciplinary engagement

We draw from Agarwal and Sengupta Irving’s (2019) framework for Connected and Productive Disciplinary Engagement (CPDE). CPDE is a modification of Conant & Engle’s (2002) Productive Disciplinary Engagement (PDE) framework, which describes engaging with disciplinary content by positioning learners as agentic meaning makers. CPDE offers a critical framework that foregrounds power and ide ology, to “[wrangle] with the political and ethical complexities of what disciplinary knowledge and practices are value d, whose ideas are valued, and toward what ends disciplinary learning is mobilized” (p. 349).  PDE begins with four guiding tenets. Problematizing involves giving students opportunities to engage legitimate intellectual challenges.  Authority involves ensuring students are positioned in such a way to be able to address those problems. Accountability emphasizes that students’ work must make sense and be responsive to others in the discipline. Finally, resources focus on the tools and norms that are required for this engagement to occur. While CPDE focuses on these same four dimensions, Agarwal & Sengupta-Ir ving add two considerations: epistemic diversity, and historicity and identity. Epistemic diversity refers to diverse ways to think and engage with a discipline, which necessarily vary based on personal experience and cultural norms and values. The idea of epistemic diversity is that is invites inquiry into the nature of  knowing, and asks what disciplinary knowledge looks like if we separate the canonical ideas and practices of Western education and look expansively about what knowledge might look like in practice and outside of Western traditions (Bang & Vossoughi, 2016). Historicity and identity refer to the ways that disciplinary learning involves a  process of becoming (Lave & CSCL 2023 Proceedings  © ISLS 278 Wenger, 1991), which is never separate from the myriad intersectional identities that carry with them meaning, opportunity, and connections to broader narratives. Adding these dimensions serves as  an expansion of the four elements of PDE, such that the four elements must respond to who the learners are. This work takes the goal of designing for epistemic diversity, and explores to what extent that goal was realized by looking specifically at student engagement. In so doing, we ask the follo wing research questions: 1) to what extent did the tools and models used in the camp create spaces f or students to personally connect with their project (and coding more broadly); and 2) to what extent did the tools and models used in the camp support students’ engagement with traditional ideas of coding?

## Methods

Data comes from a free week-long summer camp hosted in 2018, called Code Your Art, that introduced middle school aged students to coding by engaging in artistic projects using NetLogo (Tisue & Wilensky, 1999). Students engaged in different coding activities and explored different models designed by the research team, resulting in a final project. Students were recorded while coding through laptop webcam and scr eencapture software resulting in two captures: 1) students’ activity on their laptops, and 2) thei r faces and talk. This was the primary source of data used in our analysis. The focal students in this analysis were two boys, Baa re and Bryan, and one girl, Brianna. Baare was a Black rising 7th grade, Bryan was a Latino rising 7th grader, and Brianna was a Black rising 7th grader. Brianna worked mostly alone, while Baare and Bryan worked together on their project. I selected Brianna, Baare, and Bryan as focal students due to their projects’ connections to the larger findings detailed below. Analysis To conceptualize disciplinary engagement, we draw from the Use-Modify-C reate framework (Lee et al., 2011). Use-Modify-Create (UMC) can be thought of as a progression in computer science education environments where students first start out in the “using” phase, of exploring models or code built by someone else. Students are then able to “modify” or adjust someone else’s code in ways that make it  suitable for their own purposes. Finally, learners “create” or write their own code to meet the desired needs of their project. This trajectory, from using, to modifying, to creating, is one way to view a student’s disciplinary engagement as it becomes deeper over time, and students gain experience and confidence in coding. We coded video of all students’ final project creation (13 total) based on the content of the codes and the nature of their use —whether they were using, modifying or creating the code. After this initial coding, we looked across all cases for patterns in using, modifying, and creating code. Finally, we found examples of extremes (high incidence of using versus high incidence of modifying or creating) and selected three case studies of engagement with different computational models, resulting in different disciplinary engagement through the lens of the UMC framework.

## Discussion and conclusion

As stated, the field of computer science has a long history of excluding mar ginalized students, with learning environments becoming unsafe spaces for students of color and girls (Margolis et al., 2012). Therefore, designers of learning environments have a dual responsibility: inviting and sustaining diverse  identities, and giving marginalized students access to disciplinary tools in ways that enlighten them to, rather than suppress them by, the culture of power. One strategy is to engage learners in activities tha t are personally meaningful to them, but alone this is insufficient. While the final projects demonstrated ample evidence of students articulating their own goals, they did not all create opportunities to engage with ideas central to the domain of programming. The access that students have to specific resources as they were engaging new conc epts seemed to influence the types of disciplinary activities students were engaged in. Of particular importance was the authority students had over their code to make repeated decisions, resulting in engaging beyond simple use of others’ code. Of course, the Use-Modify-Create framework is just one way to look at student engagement, and this trajectory is not the entire story. Even though Baare and Bryandid not express high levels of “creating” and decision making in their final projects, for example, it does not mean that they learned nothing about coding, but that their learning was not as explicit or readily observable as it could have been if they were interacting with ot her models. Ultimately, this analysis suggests that to support learning, students need activities that encourage self-expr ession while engaging with disciplinary practices. A model that allowed learners to engage in decision m aking became a crucial site for disciplinary engagement and epistemic diversity. Future work might examine as pects of inclusive computer science learning environments outside of repeated opportunities for decision making  that allow for learners to engage with the discipline while still honoring their ways of being in the field and the world.
