# A Design Space for Articulating and Addressing the Risks of Integrating Generative AI in Education

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Generative AI in Education must be approached with caution. While it might offer learning benefits, it also poses risks. Ethics frameworks have proliferated to guide responsible design, but they often lack enforceability and can be vague or overwhelming to implement in practice. We draw inspiration from Human-Computer Interaction to argue for the development of a systematic and community-driven design space to articulate, prioritize, and address the risks associated with Generative AI systems in Education.

## Introduction

While there is debate about the merits of generativ e AI (GenAI) as one of the most transformative tech nologies of all time—with some cautioning against exaggerating its capabilities and urging us to recognize it for what it is: advanced predictive system t echnology (Bender, 2021), and others considering it  a turning point in our technological evolution (World Economic Forum, 2024)—the reality is its applications and impacts are expected across nearly every field (Kinder et al., 2024). In  education, these expectations range from discourse s of inevitability (Williamson et al., 2024) to more gro unded speculations, proposals, and calls for a more  cautious approach to designing GenAI-mediated learning environments given the numerous r isks it presents (e.g., Giannakos et al., 2024; Yan et al., 2024). These risks i nclude potential decline in cognitive skills (Heersmink, 2024), psychosocial harms disproportionately affecting certain communities (Vassel et al., 2024), risks related to anthropomorphizing GenAI systems,  especially those with conversational capabilities (Kurian, 2024; Placani, 2024), concerns about a lack of regard for truth (Hicks et al., 2024), and huge environmental impacts (Crawford, 2021).

In response to these concerns, frameworks have emer ged to guide ethical and responsible development of educational applications of GenAI and AI more broadly (e.g., Barnes et al., 2024; Holmes et al., 2022; Whi te & Scott, 2023). While both terms, “ethical” and “re sponsible,” are contested, with no consensus on wha t they truly represent or who gets to define them (Goellner et al., 2024; Munn, 2023; Stahl, 2023), these frameworks are important as they provide “guidance on rights and w rongs; what should be done  and what should be avoided” (Rességuier & Rodrigues, 2020). However, they often fall short in practice —frequently criticized as being ‘toothless’ due to their lack of enforcement mechan isms and real-world impacts (Munn, 2023; Rességuier & Rodrigues, 2020).

Substantial work in the learning sciences focuses on educating both learners and educators about human, societal, and environmental impacts of GenAI systems (e.g., Logan, 2024; Morales-Navarro et al., 2024; Williams et al., 2023). Yet, how exactly are these ethics and responsibility issues being integrated into our own design work as learning scientists? Many of us in the learning sciences and beyond are deeply attuned to these con cerns, yet often feel conflicted. We envision the potential of GenAI technology, yet we grapple with uncertainty concerning how to minimize both anticipated and unforeseen con sequences. For instance, one of us experienced a st riking conversation with a high school student who could list all the social and environmental costs of GenAI, but when asked if those steep costs were enough for the student to abandon the technology, the student said no, because the chatbots provided help in ways the student struggle d to obtain given the student’s strained relationsh ips with teachers. Revelat ions such as these may give us pause about how to p roceed with powerful GenAI tools in education.

GenAI’s influence and impacts become heightened by an entrepreneurship and big tech driven economy enthused to partake in the alleged trillions of dollars in new value to be created with generative AI transformations of industry sectors such as finance, bi g pharma, aerospace, transportation, manufacturing,  legal services, and, of course, education (Chui et al., 2023). There have b een calls to enhance the ethical consideration and expression of research ethics in scholarly outputs by incorpor ating cross-disciplinary collaborations, aiming to integrate diverse concerns arising from different disciplines into shared guidelines for authors to address ethical dimensions of research involving emerging technologies like AI/GenAI (Knight et al., 2024). Yet, even these efforts can feel both vague and overwhelming, failing to alleviate a nxieties about where to start with what priorities. It is simply unfeasible to address everything at once or attend to all the moving parts within the socio-technical system of GenAI in Education. So, what shall we do? How should we navigate our responsibilities as learning scientists and designers without reducing ethical and responsible considerations to a mere “box-ticking” exercise (Kijewski et al., 2024), disconnected from real-world effects? How might we more transparently and intentionally articulate our rationale for addressing and prioritizing risks  associated with Generative AI technologies in educ ation settings? Moreover, how can we better explain d ecisions not to address certain risks or acknowledg e situations where we are unable to do so?

For progress on these matters, the motivations behi nd specific design choices addressing risks must be made explicit, along with a rationale for why address ing others were not pursued, and that may create a very lengthy list. Greater accountability is nee ded from the design community to clearly articulate  why certain prioritizations were chosen. Beyond designing from theoretical principles and embodying them in specific designs (e.g., conjecture mapping, Sandoval, 2014), the learning sciences community must transparently demonstrate how they navigated these challenges, engaged with the communities they are designing for, and fully considered these risks.

Towards a design space We argue that the field could benefit from a design space to better articulate the dimensions defining the risks of integrating generative AI into education systems and learning environments. As in other industry secto rs, a well developed design space can serve as a common framew ork for a broad community to engage in reasoning ab out and addressing these risks. A design space should not simply be a list of risks; there are aspects of these risks that have been insufficiently theorized. Similarly, a design space analysis should go beyond a box-checking exercise. It should establish common ground for a community s eeking to identify which risks to address and to fa cilitate understanding the reasoning behind such decisions. This design space analysis should aspire to offering a shared vocabulary and structure for both promoting understanding and guiding action. It should manifest a dual emphasis on the epistemic and the deontic, focusing not only on mapping risks but also on prioritizing them, along with the theoretical, practical, ethical reasons for their prioritization. A design space analysis will enable learning scientists to say, “Here’s what I plan to do today, and here’s why I’m making that choice.” In their design space argument, one then makes the case fo r why they believe it is more important for our att ention than these other choices and with what warrants and reasons.

Design space analysis (See Figure 1) originates in Human-Computer Interaction (HCI) (Card et al., 1990; Card et al., 19991; MacLean et al., 1991). It  provides a systematic approach for articulating the  rationale behind design decisions and importantly, evolves alongside  the design of the designed artifact itself. A desi gn space analysis explicitizes design processes, supporting both the current design process and future redesign s while facilitating clear reasoning about design choices, potential modificatio ns, and their expected consequences. A design space —the resulting artifact —provides the infrastructure for design argumentatio n. It incorporates evidential warrants to justify why one design is be tter than others for specific reasons, with hedges and counter arguments. The design space is useful for outlining  the critical dimensions of a design, thereby openi ng up possibilities for future exploration of dimensions that were either previously neglected or could be c onsidered in different combinations.

Figure 1 Fragment of the Design Space for the Xerox Common Lisp (XCL) Scroll Bar (MacLean et al., 1991) For example, another research community created a d esign space for AI-supported writing assistants. Lee et al. (2024) considered the research landscape  for writing assistants increasingly fragmented acr oss various research communities, so tackled the chall enge by proposing a design space as a structured wa y to examine and explore the multidimensional space of intelligent a nd interactive writing assistants. Through global c ommunity collaboration, they examined five aspects of writing assistants: task, user, technology, interaction, and ecosystem. Within each aspect, they defined dimensions and cod es by systematically reviewing 115 papers, leveragi ng researchers’ expertise across disciplines. Their design space proffers researchers and designers a practical schema to navigate, comprehend, and compare various possib ilities of writing assistants, aid in designing new  writing tools, and offer a community foundation for integra ting emerging scientific findings about uses of int elligent interactive writing assistants (Fig 2).

Figure 2 Design space for AI-writing assistants (Lee et al., 2024) Interdisciplinary literature and community input for design space development A similar approach could be profitably applied to a rticulate and address what is becoming an increasin gly fragmented and overwhelming landscape of risks associa ted with GenAI in education. Lee et al.’s (2024) wo rk can serve as a model for creating a design  space for supporting the development of a shared vo cabulary for discussing these risks, fostering a deeper understa nding of their associated implications, establishin g criteria to guide efforts in prioritizing them (e.g., heavily w eighted criteria might include the degree of harm t o minorities or potential damage to the environment), and option s for addressing risks (e.g., if psychosocial harms  are identified as a critical challenge, strategies like  curating diverse datasets or employing fairness au diting could be assessed for their feasibility and broader implicat ions. Similarly, if anthropomorphism risks are prio ritized, solutions might include designing less humanlike AI  interfaces and simultaneously educating students o n AI literacy.) This design space could be created through a review  of interdisciplinary literature,  and collaborativ ely developed by researchers, educators, developers, po licy makers, and students, those most directly infl uenced by the designed learning environments. What do we believe are the most important dimensions for our communities to incorporate into the design space we will build together? Substantial work is required here to full y theorize these risks and illuminate their pertinent dimensions. Such a GenAI-in-education design space would serve as an anchoring common ground (Clark et al., 1991) for Ge nAI system designers, enabling design space analysi s supported by a community-driven approach emphasizing principled action —decisions grounded in clear principles, w here one can justify why one is choosing certain ac tions over others. Ultimately, a design space analysis should also help identify where GenAI syst ems are not the best solution to the educational problems learning scientists seek to address.
