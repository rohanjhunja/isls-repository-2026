# Distributed Sensemaking Among Formal and Informal Educators Working Together to Support Nature-based Learning

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Nature-based learning has many documented benefits, but it is inequitably accessible to learners because of infrastructural barriers, including lack of professional learning for educators. This study brings together 33 informal and formal educators and examines how they learned with and from one another and the more-than-human world during nature-based professional learning. Findings from analysis of videos, focus groups, surveys, and field notes show how educators shared distributed expertise and engaged in distributed sensemaking.

Nature-based learning (NBL) is learning not just in  or about nature but from nature (Chawla, 2018). It has many documented benefits, including science learning (Ardoin et al., 2020), critical thinking and problem-solving (Kuo et al., 2019), connectedness to nature (Liefländer et al., 2013), and physical and emotional wellbeing (Chawla et al., 2014). However, access to clean, safe, natural spaces and NBL are not equitably available to all learners (Rowland-Shea et al., 2020). One reason for this “nature gap” (Rowland-Shea et al., 2020) is lack of supportive infrastructure—a critical factor in scaling, sustainability, and equitable access to learning  environments (Penuel, 2019; Pinkard, 2023).  For NBL, infrastructure includes natural spaces, transportation, material resources, curricula, alignment with  standards, and educator preparation. Here, we focus on educator preparation. Many formal educators never participate in or learn to facilitate NBL. For those who do, professional learning (PL) often takes a didactic approach that does not prepare them  to utilize nature effectively (Ayotte-Beaudet et al., 2017). There is a corresponding gap in PL opportunities for informal educators, such as naturalists, who often rely on ad hoc, self-directed learning, rather than intentional PL (Dwolatzky et al., 2021), which may not support pedagogical growth (Gong et al., 2021).  The goal of our project is to bring together informal and formal educators  for PL to support the statewide spread of a NBL program called School of the Wild  (SOTW). We examine how educators learned from one another and the more-than-human (MTH) world (Abram, 1996; Marin & Bang, 2018), shared distributed expertise (Brown et al., 1993), and engaged in distributed sensemaking (Ramey & Stevens, 2026). Our analys is is grounded in distributed perspectives on thinking and learning, including distributed cognition (Hutchins, 1995) and Actor Network Analysis  (Callon, 1984; Latour, 2005). These approaches focus on how thought and action are distributed across humans, non-human artifacts (e.g., tools and representations), and for our purposes here, the MTH world. Specifically, we take a distributed sensemaking (Ramey & Stevens,

2026) approach, which conceptualizes learning and implementation as contingent upon sensemaking interactions

between humans and non-humans that shape understandings and actions. This approach highlights the sharing of distributed expertise (Brown et al., 1993) and how infrastructure both shapes and is shaped by sensemaking.

## Method

Over two summers (2023-24), we implemented and studied PL workshops that brought together formal educators and state and county naturalists for experiential NBL. Thirty-three of these educators agreed to participate in our research (26 teachers, 1 principal, 5 naturalists, and 1 outdoor educator with prior K-12 experience; 24 female and 9 male; 30 white, 2 multiracial, and 1 Latine/x). The PL began with a one-day workshop at a state or county park, where educators played the role of students while SOTW staff modelled nature-based facilitation practices in one or more habitats (woodlands, wetlands, prairie). This hands-on learning was followed by time for discussion and planning for each school’s SOTW implementation. Afterward, the SOTW team shared a collection of curricular resources, scheduled a meeting or phone call with each team to finalize plans, and when possible, sent a staff member to accompany schools on their first day of implementation. Following the PL, teams of formal educators and naturalists facilitate d SOTW for students in grades 4-6. Typically, this involved 4-5 consecutive, day-long trips to one or more state or county parks. Each educator chose or was assigned to a specific habitat or theme and 2719 corresponding NBL activities that they facilitate d repeatedly for groups of 10-15 students, who rotate d through multiple sets of activities. Therefore, while informal and formal educators went through PL and planned together, they were alone while facilitating NBL. Data collection comprised of a pre-and a post-survey, video from chestor visor-mounted cameras worn by educators and students, focus group interviews, and field notes. We analyzed data using cognitive ethnographic (Hutchins, 1995) and interaction analysis methods (Jordan & Henderson, 1995). Our analysis yielded four ways in which expertise and sensemaking were distributed. First, sensemaking was distributed across human actors, but this  distribution was not symmetrical. Naturalists were positioned (by themselves and teachers) less as learners and more as experts on nature, the ir specific parks, material resources, and outdoor pedagogy. This positioning made sense when it came to knowledge about nature and local parks. However, while SOTW staff modelled inquiry  learning, some naturalists modelled what one naturalist called  a “telling” approach to teaching, rather than an “asking” approach. This led to pedagogical confusion for teachers. For example, one teacher asked a naturalist who had done a lot of telling, “You have a lot of this knowledge, just, you know, locked away. And so, for the rest of us, um, you know we’re going to come out here, uh you said there’s curriculum and stuff. Are those kinds of facts going to be involved in that? Or are we researching that?” Second, sensemaking was  distributed across human-made tools, but asymmetries of knowledge and power existed here  too. Naturalists introduced digital (bird and plant identification apps)  and tangible (water testing kits, bins of animal artifacts) tools to teachers, while teachers introduced few of these tools to naturalists. Having these tools afforded educators and students different types of exploration and learning. However, naturalists showed more comfort using them. For example, during one PL, when a naturalist invited teachers to throw atlatls (an Indigenous hunting tool), several teachers expressed reluctance. Teachers also had to communicate with naturalists to obtain materials. B reakdowns in communication led to failures to assemble necessary material infrastructure. For example, a school group arrived at one park expecting binocular s and bird books to be available but realized that they weren’t, because they hadn’t notified the naturalists in advance. Third, sensemaking was distributed across elements of the more-than-human (MTH) world. The MTH world both contributed knowledge to sensemaking processes, and (re)distributed power. While educators were in nature, they were able to learn directly from the MTH  world, which guided their sensemaking about how to facilitate NBL. In one episode, a naturalist  found a toad. When she picked it up to show teachers, they made observations, such as  “he blends in so well, you might step on him,” which sparked a conversation about the difference between frogs and toads and how a discovery like this could be leveraged for  student learning. The knowledge held in the toad supported teacher learning in ways not only mediated by naturalists. Finally, sensemaking w as distributed across time and space. During PL and SOTW, we observed multiple cycles of convergence and divergence of sensemaking. Educators converged during PL but then diverged for a period of asynchronous communication  and temporally and spatially distributed sensemaking. During follow-up meetings, groups converged again, then diverged, distributing final preparation tasks. They converged as school groups arrived at parks but diverged as individual educators led learning for small groups. Finally, educators converged for shared reflection. As teams converged and diverged, so did their sensemaking. Divergent time made space for individual sensemaking. For example, one teacher  had a conversation with her own child, after the PL, about microplastics and water pollution, that she said solidified her understanding of how to facilitate students’ learning at  SOTW. However, when  there was little communication, understandings diverged, as occurred with the school described above that neglected to communicate with naturalists about needed materials. By examining how formal and informal educators worked and learned together  to implement SOTW, our analysis showed how educators shared distributed expertise, how information moved through the network of humans, non-humans and more-than-humans, and how it supported sensemaking and the assembly of infrastructure. Our analysis showed how sensemaking w as distributed across human actors, human-made tools, elements of the MTH world, and time and space. It also showed how asymmetries in perceived expertise or comfort with and control over non-human actors created power asymmetries. These findings contribute to literature on both the role of distributed sensemaking in implementation and how to design PL  for NBL. For example, early distributed perspectives (Hutchins, 1995) focused on how human-made tools offloaded cognitive work or held and represented information. The present analysis highlights how the MTH world can play a similar role. Our analysis also highlights  how distributed sensemaking unfolds during periods of  convergence and divergence during PL and implementation, suggesting a benefit of short periods of divergence but frequent convergence to maintain shared understandings. Our findings build on prior work highlighting the problems with asymmetrical power relations during sensemaking and planning processes  (Severance et al., 2016).
