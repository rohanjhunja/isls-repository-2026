# Syncretic Designs for Young People’s Social and Ecological Imagination across Virtual–Natural Worlds

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study explores how syncretic designs for game-based learning that interweave digital and physical modalities, human and more-than-human actors, and imaginative and embodied forms of knowing can nurture young people’s social and ecological imagination. While such approaches have been examined in literacy contexts, their potential to cultivate socio-ecological imagination across virtual and natural worlds remains underexplored. Drawing on a Social Design-Based Experiment (SDBE) conducted in an out-of-school program, twelve youth engaged in sustainability design challenges in Minecraft alongside outdoor ecological explorations. The study investigates: (1) how syncretic designs for learning can support youth’s ecological and social imagination across digital and real-world nature contexts; and (2) what pedagogical possibilities emerge when virtual world-making intertwines with embodied experiences in natural environments. Findings show that youth envisioned sustainable futures grounded in their cultural repertoires, designing virtual neighborhoods that reimagined the ecological and social possibilities of their communities.

Despite increasing global attention to socio-ecological sustainability, young people, especially those from nondominant communities, remain largely peripheral to related discourse and practice. Often portrayed as passive recipients of adult-led initiatives or as “future citizens,” they are seldom positioned as present actors capable of imagining and shaping social and ecological transformation. In response, scholars have called for youth-sensitive and holistic educational approaches that recognize young people as investigators, storytellers, and agents of social and ecological change (Renshaw & Tooth,  2018; Kumpulainen et al., 2022). Building on this premise, this study examines how syncretic designs for game-based learning, defined as pedagogical (re)configurations that interweave digital and physical modalities, human and more-than-human actors, and imaginative and embodied forms of knowing can nurture young people’s engagement in social and ecological imagination. Drawing on sociocultural and posthuman perspectives, we view game-based learning for sus tainability as a relational and material process that unfolds across interconnected human –technological–natural s ystems (Barad, 2007; Kumpulainen et al., 2023). In line with recent scholarship that calls for moving beyond nature-culture divides that separate humans from their ecologies (Vossoughi, Marin, & Bang, 2023), our work recognizes that knowledge emerges through relationships among people, places, and more-than-human beings. Within the global climate crisis, there is compelling evidence that environmental devastation will continue to fall most heavily on non-dominant communities, deepening the inequities that already structure social and ecological life (Bullard, 1996; Pulido,  2018). These inequities highlight the need for pedagogies that foster the ecological imaginations (Kumpulainen et al., 2023) of students from non-dominant communities. Within these systems, imagination is not an isolated cognitive process but an embodied, affective, and socio-material practice through which learners reconfigure relations with the world and envision alternative futures and ways of living within it (Greene, 2000). Our work aims to leverage game-based learning to support students to engage in critical acts of futuring that surface and contest persistent  forces and structures that lead to inequities (Chang & Philip, 2025). By taking the imagination of youth from non-dominant communities seriously, the study seeks to explore how educators and youth from non-dominant communities can leverage game-based technologies and the socioecological relations that they develop in their everyday lives to denounce current systems and announce new and more sustainable futures for their communities.

## Purpose and theoretical framework

This study draws on sociocultural notions of play as an activity that enables children to act beyond the immediate constraints of their environment by imagining alternative social roles and realities (Vygotsky,  1978; Chaklin, 2003). Video games such as Minecraft enable children to externalize their imagination into action (Vygotsky,

1967) by constructing digital ecologies that simulate real-world architectures. In this sense, sand-box game worlds

that afford digital experimentation, tinkering, and co-design experiences create play contexts that 1887 support youth and educators to develop their social and creative imagination. Drawing from Fesmire’s (2010) notion of imagination as a deliberative, contextually situated form of ethical reflection, we conceptualize social and ecological imagination as a way of perceiving the interconnectedness of living systems and of envisioning possibilities for transformative action among humans and nonhumans. In educational contexts, such imagination allows learners to think, feel, and act with awareness of their entanglement in social  and ecological relations, a critical capacity for responding to the complexities of the Anthropocene. Taken together, more robust conceptualizations of play and ecological imagination open possibilities for designing learning ecologies that support youth to transform the material and ideological worlds they navigate. Syncretic designs for learning offer a generative  means to cultivate imagination in ways that are rooted in the diverse cultural repertoires of practice (Gutiérrez & Rogoff, 2003) of students from non-dominant communities. Following Gutiérrez (2014), syncretic learning is understood as a process of sense-making that brings together and subverts hierarchies between everyday and academic genres, texts, and conventions.  In this study, syncretic learning also refers to the movement and reorganization that occur  across digital and natural environments, bringing these seemingly distinct and contradictory dimensions into relation as generative sources for socio-ecological learning. Through such designs, learners candraw on multiple semiotic, material, and ecological resources to create connections between digital and natural environments. In this process, sensory, emotional, and imaginative experiences flow between virtual world-making and embodied encounters with nature, generating new awareness, empathy, and agency (Kumpulainen et al, 2022). These hybrid learning spaces position young people as imaginative and relational agents who co-construct socio-ecological understandings and negotiate their place within complex human and more-than-human systems (Kumpulainen et al., 2023; Fesmire, 2010; Gruen, 2009). While syncretic learning has been widely examined in literacy and multilingual contexts, its potential to support children’s social and ecological imagination remains underexplored. In particular, little is known about how syncretic pedagogical  designs can connect children’s engagement with nature-themed digital environments, such as Minecraft, with their embodied experiences in real-world nature. Addressing this gap, the present study explores how young people navigate and integrate digital and natural encounters through syncretic learning experiences that foster imaginative, relational, and place-based understandings of the more-than-human world. Guided by this purpose, our research addresses two research questions:

1. How can syncretic learning designs support young people’s socio-ecological imagination across digital

and real-world nature contexts?

2. What pedagogical possibilities emerge when virtual world-making intertwine d with embodied

experiences in natural environments?

This study was conducted as part of an after-school program, designed by the first author, in New York City, where young people engage with sustainability-themed worldmaking (Holland et al., 1998, Chang & Philip, 2025) on Minecraft. The focus of the study was to leverage students’ everyday ecological experiences and local placebased knowledge to support game design within and across digital and natural environments. The study draws on a Social Design-Based Experiment (SDBE) methodology (Gutiérrez & Jurow, 2016), in which the researcher and educators co-designed and participated in virtual worlds alongside youth to co-construct virtual neighborhoods. Across twelve 90-minute sessions, Latinx and Afro-Latinx participants worked in teams to design digital neighborhoods in Minecraft while also engaging in journaling, family interviews, and ecological field explorations. Drawing on the concept of funds of knowledge (Moll et al., 2006), the design encouraged youth to bring community ecological knowledge into the digital environment. Data sources included video-recorded gameplay, focus group interviews, journals, ethnographic field notes, and screenshots of Minecraft builds. The primary unit of analysis was the design episode, defined as a bounded stretch of collaborative talk-and-design in which youth planned and co-constructed virtual builds in relation to design challenges around sustainability. Design episodes were selected for close video analysis when participants connected design decisions to community histories, ecological relat ions, issues of accessibility. Interaction analysis (Jordan & Henderson, 1995; Hall & Stevens, 2015) traced how student talk, avatar movement, and co-design processes were coordinated during these episodes. A complementary content analysis of journals, interviews, screenshots, and field notes identified recurring themes across modalities, including ecological interdependence, collective well-being, and relational understandings of more-than-human life. Codes were refined iteratively through comparison across episodes and artifacts to examine how socio-ecological imagination was expressed in both discourse and students’ virtual design. 1888 Preliminary findings indicate youth and educators reimagined sustainable design by drawing on their own cultural repertoires and histories to design imagined worlds on Minecraft. Through collaborations with educators, youth co-constructed virtual infrastructures, negotiated shared goals, and imagined systems that reflected ethical commitments to the collective well-being of their communities. One major finding was that the students embraced multiplicity in their design that addressed the diverse needs of  their communities. For example, the students integrated public signs that were multilingual and accessible to vision-impaired people, created accessible public gathering spaces, and designed infrastructures that reflected their transnational lives and everyday ecologies. Their design processes further illustrate how they wove notions of cultural and ecological sustainability to imagine spaces that affirmed diverse ways of being,  including those of more-than-human actors. The students honored family migration stories and Indigenous relationships to land by building parks, greenhouses, and monuments that carried deep cultural-historical significance. They also designed a community museum that served as a space to narrate their collective histories and documented the lived experiences of their neighborhoods. By mapping family histories, youth countered discourses that reify gentrification and cultural erasure and re-membered their cultural histories through design.

As such, youth imagined sustainable futures grounded in their cultural repertoires and histories and designed virtual neighborhoods that reflected and  reimagined the ecological and social possibilities of their own communities. For example, across the sessions, we observed how youth designed architectures and embedded imagined technologies into their designs that focused on the well-being of their own cultural and ecological communities. In this case, syncretic designs that drew on the cultural and ecological knowledge of students and their families generated opportunities to design alternative sustainable futures. Through neighborhood walks and youth journal entries that reflected on the plants around their neighborhoods, youth learned the names  and impacts that these plants have on the soil and climate. 1889 Before coding these plants into Minecraft, students learned about the ecological conditions and relationships that enable these plants to flourish. According to students, the imagined purpose of the Minecraft greenhouse was for further scientific inquiry and so that the imagined people in their city could learn about and interact with these plants.

Our study is situated within evolving sociocultural research that conceptualizes youth as historically situated and agentic participants who, when afforded meaningful spaces and tools, can imagine and enact more just and sustainable socio-ecological futures (Rivero & Gutierrez, 2019; Kumpulainen et al., 2022). It contributes to the growing body of work among the learning sciences by exploring how young people’s ecological and social imagination can be fostered through syncretic designs for learning, and pedagogical approaches that intentionally bridge everyday and academic practices across diverse learning ecologies (Gutiérrez, 2014; Gutiérrez & Jurow, 2016). Methodologically, this study offers a novel approach by developing a multimodal design, which allows to investigate how children construct socio-ecological meaning and engage in future-making by moving across game-based learning spaces, and natural environmental contexts. Theoretically, our study adds to the current literature by extending the concept of syncretic learning across virtual and natural worlds. By examining how youth navigate between virtual digital play (e.g., Minecraft) and real-world outdoor experiences, we explore how syncretic pedagogical designs afford opportunities for youth’s social and ecological imagination and worldmaking. In doing so, we offer a novel perspective on how youth construct socio-ecological meaning across media and contexts, and how such learning experiences can e mpower them as imaginative and agentic participants in shaping more sustainable futures.

Within a wave of renewed interest in game-based learning for sustainability, schools and out-of-school learning environments are exploring games and design challenges to support the development of systems thinking and climate action among youth. Research within this  area demonstrates that games focused on waste sorting, water management, and agricultural ecology candeepen participants’ understanding of these domains, while energy-related games enhance awareness of energy devices and encourage energy-saving behaviors (Chen et al., 2024). However, more research is needed on how to design technology-mediated, youth-sensitive, and holistic educational approaches that position young people as historical actors in social and ecological transformation (Kumpulainen et al., 2022; Renshaw & Tooth, 2018). While recognizing the affordances of games that focus on environmental problem-solving, our study seeks to disrupt the nature –culture split (Vossoughi, Marin, Bang,

2023) that positions ecosystems as technical systems  separate from peopl e’s lived relationships with land and

more-than-human beings.

Responding to these gaps, we drew on a Social Design-Based Experiment (SDBE) approach and the guiding principles of syncretic learning  to integrate sociotechnical making with socioecological perspectives for multilingual Latinx and Afro-Latinx youth in urban contexts. Through these approaches, we found that game-based learning about sustainability can be paired with the  socio-ecological knowledge that students bring to a learning ecology to design for sustainability that is responsive to their cultural histories and everyday experiences. In addition to gaming technologies, neighborhood ecological walks, journaling, and family interviews supported the development of more expansive ecological imaginations (Kumpulainen et al., 2023) among both youth and educators. By expanding their social and ecological imagination, students began to see themselves as historical actors in sustaining their local environments. Overall, the study posits that syncretic approaches to game-based learning for sustainability can help youth grapple with the complexity of what is being sustained and for whom, enabling them to envision sustainable futures that are consequential to their everyday lives and the well-being of their communities.
