# Personal and Group Identity Processes of Americans and Israelis in a Cross-Continental Educational Journey

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In this research, we explore the processes of identity change that occurred among nine Israeli adolescents during a unique educational program. A group comprised of both Israeli and American adolescents embarked on a journey across continents aiming to strengthen both their personal and Jewish identities. We investigated the processes of change experienced by the participants and the factors they reported that led to these changes. Participants indicated that the processes of change were influenced by the places they visited but, more significantly, by their interpersonal and group interactions. They reported changes in perceptions of personal and familial identity, as well as in perceptions of religious and national identity. The findings provide insights into how encounters with others impact identity formation processes, facilitate self-exploration, and foster changes in both personal and broader dimensions of identity.

## Theoretical background

Identity is composed of various elements, including individual characteristics, dominant biographical points, and various group affiliations. Identity begins to develop in childhood as part of emotional development, and is influenced by various circles surrounding the individual, from  the nuclear and extended family, extending to the social environment in which one grows, and expands to the broader circles of society, country, and hum anity (Anderson, 2006). Sometimes, the social context may have a greater impact on the identity formation process than the values instilled in the family environment (Hod & Ben-Zvi, 2018). Encounters with others who are different provide an opportunity to explore beliefs and opinions and can serve as a catalyst for identity formation processe s (Firer et al.,  2023). Critical stages of identity formation are often assumed to occur during high school years, co nstituting a meaningful milestone in the process of adolescence (Erikson, 1994; Marcia, 1966).  This period, characterized by separation processes from parents and families of origin, magnifies the potential of the influence of the encounter with a peer group on identity processes on personal and group levels (Crocetti et al., 2008).

Different educational environments, both formal and informal, play a role in shaping identity in various dimensions (e.g. Cohen et al., 2023). One of the prominent programs in the Israeli education system is the journey to Poland, which encompasses visits to  towns where Jewish communities existed for centuries and to concentration and extermination camps. The aim of the journey is to preserve the memory of the Holocaust and to strengthen the students' connection to their Jewish and national identity. Additionally, there are various student programs aimed at strengthening the connection between Jewish students from Israel and communities worldwide. The goals of such programs are to strengthen a sense of belonging to the Jewis h people and through interaction with others, facilitate processes of change. Encountering others, especially when it pertains to collective identities such as nationality or religion, exposes to different perspectives, fostering the development of a worldview and the consolidation of opinions and perceptions, especially during stages of adolescence (Shulman, 2008). The current study explored a unique educational pro gram that combined a journey to Poland with an encounter between Jewish adolescents from Israel and the United States. The goal of our research was to trace processes of identity change that occurred during t he journey and identify the factors that influenced  these processes by answering the following questions:

1. What did the participants perceive to be the primary factors that influenced the process of change  they

experienced and in what way?

2. According to the participants, i n which identity domains did the process of change manifest itself, and

how did these domains influence one another?

## Research environment and participants

The research followed participants in a non-formal educational program called "Journey for Identity" (JFI). The program was a collaboration between the Jewish community in Rochester, New York, and the city of Modi'in, in Israel. The collaboration was a part of an initiative by the Jewish Agency, known as Partnership2Gether, aimed at strengthening ties between Jewish communities through shared educational and social projects. JFI took place in 2022, across three continents for a duration of three and a half weeks. It originated in Rochester, NY, continued to Poland, and ended in Israel. The goal of JFI was to enable the participants to explore their personal identity as well as broader affiliat ions such as group, family, religion, and nation; to develop leadership skills; to expand their understanding of others different from them; and to increase their knowledge of Jewish history, in particular the Holocaust. The group learned about the Holocaust through guided exploration in Poland, examined their Jewish identity through shar ed immersive experiences, and gained an understandi ng of each other’s community and life through home-based hospitality. Eighteen adolescents from16 to18 from Rochester, New York and from Modi'in, Israel participated in the program. The current research focused on the nine Israeli participants-four girls and five boys. T he Israeli participants were selected from a group of candidates who had applied to the program through media outreach. The selection process included personal and group interviews. Participants from Modi’in were then paired with partners from Rochester based on gender and areas of interest. F our months of advanced preparation were held at separate meetings in Rochester and in Modi'in. The meetings of the Modi'in groups included social activities, an introduction of basic concepts and key historical points related to the Jewish people, specifically t o the Holocaust, and preparation meetings regarding cultural differences. Throughout the journey to Poland, group meetings enabled participants to process their experiences. A final group session was held in the last day of the journey, in Israel, before the participants returned home.

## Data collection and analysis

The two main sources of data collection included nine pre and nine post semi-structured interviews with the Israeli participants and documentation of the program including observations  of the first author, who accompanied the program and the journey, and recordings of part of the group meetings. The pre-journey interviews averaged twenty minutes and included questions regarding participants' expectations, concerns, and sense of belonging to different identity dimensions. The post-journey interviews averaged forty-seven minutes and included retrospective questions concerning the realization of expectations and concerns, significant events during the journey and insights attained as a result. The interviews underwent a thematic analysis conduc ted by the two researchers to identify and conceptualize the main themes in the process of cha nge and the factors that influenced these processes. Subsequently, the researchers looked for examples of events to illustrate these themes within the information documented during the journey itself.

## Findings

The analysis of the data revealed that participation in the journey gave rise to changes in various personal, social, and religious-national aspects  of identity. We categorize d the findings into central themes extracted from the interviews—dominant factors that influenced the process of identity formation, and identity dimensions in which the change unfolded.

Influencing factors The interviewees referred to a variety of elements in the journey that had had an impact on them and triggered processes of change. These elements can be categorized into those affiliated with the historic knowledge, personal stories, or sites visited and those associated with interpersonal interactions. Participants reported specific places that left an impression on them, evoked thoughts, and emotions, and led to a shift in perception s and renewed insights. For example,  visiting extermination camps in Poland  that actualize the horrors of the Holocaust  for them (Ohad: "It's just completely different, you can't describe  it. Until you get to Poland, you don't understand. And I'm very glad I came."). At the same time, the interviewees attributed a greater impact on changes on their identity to interpersonal interactions. Participants referred to interactions on different levels. Some reported interactions at the personal level. They referred to m eaningful conversations with their partner, with members of the host family, and with other group members, that affected them, played a significant role, and contributed to insights about their personal and group identities (John: "I think the people are what made the journey for m e; the people are what changed it for me").

Participants also referred to interactions at the group level. These interactions involved the whole group, or subgroups (the Israelis, the girls). Events like a sleepover organized by group members were seen as significant in terms of interpersonal and group connection.  Group meetings during the journey that aimed at processing the experiences of the journey also provided insights for participants. Identity dimensions Participants described processes of change that occurred on several key identity dimensions: Personal Identity-The journey enabled participants to explore their beliefs, to  identify what is meaningful for them, to learn what helps them in moments of crisis, and to recogn ize their own abilities and strengths. Because of the distance from home, they had to cope in situations without parental assistance, which contributed to the self-inquiry process. The emotional burden of the trip to Poland further e nriched this self-inquiry. Some participants became more aware of when they needed a time out from the group and a moment of solitude, and when they felt ready to rejoin the larger group. Some of the interviewees mentioned the challenge of distance from home and taking care of oneself as a significant dimension that promotes self-development. (e.g., Orit: "I learned that I really can go through such an experience; I can be far from home. I have it, like I can be independent": John: "The distance from home, the fact that you are alon e... It gives you insights about yourself, about how you manage on your own").

Family Identity-Participants examined their relationship with their family and with their familiar family structure. As mentioned, the journey  created both a longing for and a separation from home. The geographic distance facilitated this examination alongside the exposure to different cultural and family models  at the host’s home. One participant, for example, was surprised when his partner asked for parental permission to go out with friends from the group, whereas he was used to being responsible for managing his own schedule. Social Identity-The intensive interaction with  their partners and the group, fostered changes at the personal and group levels. Some participants expressed concerns at the beginning of the journey that being away for a period of three and a half weeks might cause them to disconnect from friends who did not participate in the journey and expressed fear of missing out. By the end of the journey, these concerns were alleviated. Most participants felt that the detachment from the familiar environment and the ne w relationships gave them insights into previous social relationships. For example, one participant reported that the connection with her partner was unlike her relationship with her other friends. It was muc h deeper and more meaningful. (Rachel: "I think it was obvious that we have to support each other, and as if the closeness we created between us in Rochester also helped us deal with Poland") Religious Identity  – T he journey introduced participants to alternative Jewish lifestyles. Some of the Israeli participants had initially expressed a perception of Judaism as a set of commandments, to which they did not connect, or to specific life events like Shabbat and holidays. However, in the concluding interviews, there was a shift in perception, and participants described a strengthened sense of belonging to the Jewish people, along with an understanding of Judaism as a culture and community allowing some of the participants to embrace new connections to their Jewish identity. A few of the participants said that attending religious services at the Reform synagogue in Rochester was a highlight that deepened their connection to Jewish identity, revealing the diversity of ways to be Jewish.

National Identity-The participants reported on two aspects of the journey that influenced the formation of their national identity. The first was the visit  to Rochester, which exposed them to American cultu re and highlighted differences with Israeli culture. The second was their own hospitality of the American partners in their homes and the joint trip with them across Israel, which prompted them to view their own country through the eyes of their partners, offering them a new and previously unfamiliar perspective  (Toni: "In Israel, I don't go to synagogue. And now I think to myself that maybe if I lived abroad, say, in Rochester, then maybe I would actually go to synagogue, because it's like a community and it's nice"). After the journey, participants described a connect ion to Judaism through a shared destiny with the Jewish people following the visit to Poland. The se cond, and stronger connection, was formed through the visit to the Jewish community in Rochester, where they ga ined an understanding that being Jewish also means being part of a community. The strong connection between the group members, coupled with the experience of visiting Jewish communities in Poland and Rochester,  and the experience of hosting the visit in Israel, gave the Israeli participants new insights into their Jewish identity and deepened their connection.

## Discussion

This study investigated the processes of identity f ormation and change that occurred among Israeli adolescents who participated in a cross-continental educational journey. The purpose of the journey was to create an environment that encouraged participants to examine aspects of their identity in various contexts. The participants visited each other’s community, gained an understanding of the family, community, and nationa l culture and values of the other through home-based hospitality, and undertook a shared journey in Poland, focusing on the collective memory of the Jewish people. We interviewed participants before and after the program  and documented the journey. We conducted a thematic analysis on the participants' reports, to examine the factors they perceived as influencing the change process th ey underwent. Additionally, we examined in which identity dimensions changes were facilitated and the interaction among them. T wo types of factors were perceived as influencing the process of change. The first type included places and historical knowledge that left an impression on the participants. The second was related to interpersonal interactions between individuals, or as a group, throughout the journey. Participants recognized interpersonal interactions as the more meaningful and dominant force in the journey. P articipants reported a chan ge in perception  of various dimensions of  their identity  at the  personal, familial, social, religious, and national levels.

The design of the journey included several combined elements, which created an optimal environment for identity exploration. These elements included departing from the comfort zone, interacting with the other, and visiting places of historical and cultural significance. P articipants were away from their natural environment, their families  and friends. The distanc e was a challenging experience for some participants, but also provided them with a renewed opportunity to explore their perceptions and beliefs, liberated from familiar patterns. The week they were at home hosting their partner, provided an opportunity for self-reflection.

The personal, familial, and group-related encounters, which occurred in various significant locations, gave the participants insights into  their personal, religious, and national  identities. The journey facilitated a n encounter with the 'other', who, alongside the 'otherness', was also similar and equal. The two groups came from different countries, yet participants were the same age and shared similar experiences and challenges during th e journey. They also shared the connection to the Jewish people, and to a collective historical memory, particularly the memory of the Holocaust. However, within those common dimensions, there were also differences among the group members. Although the members were the same a ge, while the Americans were preparing for college,  the Israelis were preparing for military service. Israeli group members came with a basic knowledge of the Holocaust they had learned in their history classes, American group members  did not. Moreover, in terms of religio n, the exposure to different streams within Judaism was ne w for some of the Israelis, since in Israel the mainstream of religion is Orthodox. In contrast, Rochester has a diverse community and a sense of co mmunal solidarity among all streams in Judaism. In terms of learning processes, the encounter with the 'other', who is both di fferent and similar, enabled the process of change. The fact that the differences among the participants were not great, enabled them to develop and learn from one another. Indeed, Participants reported that this interaction with the other was transformative.

The places visited during the journey also served as catalysts for change. The encounter with the Jewish community in the U nited States and the visit to the synagogue led to a change in p erception regarding religion and a renewed connection of the Israelis to Judaism. The journey to Poland was emotionally charged, required coping with the emerging emotions as individuals and as a  group and created a strong bond and a sense of solidarity among participants. The journey in Israel strengthened the sense of belonging and pride, enabling Israeli participants to present Israel to their partners in a different light. Although t he participants overwhelmingly reported that the interactions they experienced were the most meaningful aspect of the journey, the setting of the journey allowed them to explore their identity and provided the framework for the process of change that occurred.
