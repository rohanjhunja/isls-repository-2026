# “Push Forward and We Will Help”: Teacher Autonomy and System Support in the Context of Teaching Displaced Students

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In this paper, we address the under-investigated topic of teaching displaced students and at the same time build on this unique teaching context to theorize about teachers’ sense of autonomy and support. While it is generally presumed that respecting teachers’ autonomy is important, there is a need to refine our understanding of how and why a sense of autonomy is not always embraced by teachers or perceived as valuable. The study builds on 14 interviews with teachers in provisional schools for displaced students in Israel. We articulate and illustrate a preliminary framework that elaborates on the notion of teacher autonomy by distinguishing the autonomy that teachers are granted from the support they receive to deal with the challenges that might come with such autonomy.

Objective Educators around the world increasingly face emergency conditions due to climate disasters and regional conflicts, including teaching displaced students (International Displacement Monitoring Center, 2023). This study is part of a larger research project taking place in the context of the Israeli school system amidst the war in the Middle East and the displacement of approximately 164,000 people li ving near the Israeli southern border with Gaza and  the northern border with Lebanon (Weissblei, 2023). The  goal of the broader study is to explore the challenges and adaptations of teachers in schools for displaced students in three institutional configurations. The first configuration (and the context of this paper) features provisiona l schools designed to provide immediate short-term support. These schools were formed in hotels, cultural institutions, and other locations. They re lied mainly on volunteer teachers, including many former teache rs, from the cities to which the displaced students had been moved. The two other configurations (not discussed in this paper) feature existing public schools that  received displaced students into their institution and the o riginal schools of the displaced students during th eir re-opening period after households moved back home. Against th is backdrop, this paper describes findings from the provisional schools that highlighted the overall ch aos at the beginning of the war and teacher autonom y amidst that chaos. We take a complexity theory lens to study teaching and teacher responses within these circumstances. In our prior analysis (Marco et al., 2025) we found that teachers in these schools typically practiced  extensive pedagogic autonomy. These prior findings prompted us to qualitatively distinguish between di fferent profiles of teacher autonomy and sense of support, comparing them to what we currently know from the literature regarding teacher autonomy as a field. We ask: How did educators in the provisional school setting s perceive their autonomy, and how did it interact with their sense of support? Theoretical and conceptual framing Complexity theory Educational researchers have built on complexity theory to study a long array of topics, including leadership and policy (e.g., Osberg & Biesta, 2010), philosophy of  education (e.g., Gilead, 2023), teacher identity a nd attitudes (Patterson et al., 2022), and teacher learning (e.g., Cochran-Smith et al., 2014; Ehrenfeld, 2022). Complexity theory concentrates on analyzing change in complex adaptive systems in which ordered and disordered phenomena coexist and influence each other (Morin, 2007). These systems exhibit unique features and pa tterns of behavior that make them hard to analyze, predict, and control (Mitchell, 2009). In this paper, we m ainly build on two aspects of complex systems theory. First, we  build on the notion of self-organization, which is the ability of complex systems to develop structures and patter ns from the bottom up with little or no external co ntrol (Colander & Kupers, 2014). Second, we build on the idea that disruptions can initiate changes in systems, which can be reactive or transfo rmative. Reactive responses try to restore or maint ain existing patterns in light of perturbations. In practice, they often stem from th e perturbations being perceived as a threat (Novali a & Malekpour, 2020). Alternatively, transformative reactions seek ways to self-organize and re-organize the system along new patterns or forms of order. In practice, these can be linked to disruptions that are viewed as opportunities (Ehrenfeld & Stengel, 2025; Folke et al., 2005). Si nce the response to a disruption or crisis can only  partially be top-down dictated, understanding the response to a cris is requires examining the interplay between system independent elements and their self-organization processes (Prigogine & Stengers, 1997). Therefore, an essential aspect of this research is understanding teacher autonomy and its effects on teachers’ work and school. Teacher autonomy In this paper we follow Nguyen et al.’s (2021) noti on of teacher autonomy as discretion or freedom to make decisions regarding the selection of content, teach ing methods, or learning assessment. On the one hand, studies of teacher autonomy highlight that granting teacher s with autonomy is essential for teacher motivation  (Börü, 2018), engagement, and emotional well-being (Skaalvik & Skaalvik, 2020), as well as for t heir overall professional development (Horn & Garner, 2022). On the other hand, reforms in educational systems (oft en implicitly) question the value of autonomy and dimi nish teacher autonomy by imposing scripted standard s that undermine teachers’ ability to innovate (Lundström,  2015). Notably, research has mainly considered aut onomy under ordinary educational circumstances. Considering teacher auto nomy in a time of crisis from a complexity theory perspective allows us to identify nuanced ar guments about its nature. For example, we wish to p oint out when, how, and why, granting autonomy can lead to problematic consequences for teaching and teacher learning. One such instance is when providing much autonomy (Hong & Youngs, 2016) with little support to realize  it. In that sense, the case of teachers in schools for dis placed students offers an extreme case of extensive  self-organization to theorize about teacher autonomy.

## Research context

We interviewed 14 volunteer educational staff members who worked in nine different provisional schools in Israel between November and December 2023. Note that unlike other cases of displacement in the  Middle East due to the war, all provisional schools and displaced students in our study had uninterrupted access to food, water, shelter, and medical supplies. At the same time, many of them, in particular those from the southern regions, have suffered from emotional hardship, personal losses, and more generally lack of conditions conducive to learning (see Marco et al., 2025). ## Data collection

In November-December 2023 we conducted 14 semi-structured Zoom interviews (30-60 minutes each) in which participants were invited to share their backgrounds and motivation to volunteer in the provisional schools. They were then asked to describe their roles and daily e xperiences in these schools, including their expect ations of the system, their challenges, and their strategies for addressing them. Subsequently, we inquired about th eir success and failure stories, the impact of their work on them, and the lessons they have learned from their experiences.

## Data analysis

The data collected in the interviews was previously  analyzed in Marco et al. (2025) using directed qua litative content analysis (Hsieh & Shannon, 2005). In that analysis, we relied on the pre-defined constructs of challenges, disorder, autonomy, and responses. Interviews were coded using the NVivo software, and the predefined themes were inhabited by emergent codes stemming from the data, organized as clusters of codes into larger ca tegories. In that previous paper, one repeating finding was t hat all teachers had a strong sense of chaos, and r elatedly, a strong sense of autonomy. However, we noticed that the teachers varied across the positive and negative meanings they attached to that sense of autonomy, and as the  code “no support” under the category of “challenges” hinted, many of them complained about having no support. In this paper, and in this phase of data analysis, we delve into the interaction between those two dimensions of aut onomy and (no) support. In other words, we wonder i f one possible explanation for these differences in the way the high levels of autonomy was perceived, is the difference between having autonomy with the support to realize  it or not. For this reason, our goal in this paper  is to start conceptualizing that difference and illus trate it from our data. We do that by articulating four interactions of high/low sense of autonomy, with high/low levels of support, naming them, and illustrating the distribution of our participants among them with examples from the interviews.

## Results: Illustrating cases of teacher autonomy and support

Note that we focus on the teachers’ sense of autono my and support rather than on objective working con ditions. Given the unique research context, all of our participants perceived their autonomy level as high and it is the sense of support that distinguish ed their cases (see Table 2). Empowered Practice teachers, who described high levels of autonomy and support, discussed salient support in terms of the hosting towns or institutions (e.g., museums), support from the principle, peer support, and support from their professional network outside of school. In general, they discussed flexible and adaptive types of suppo rt. Isolated Practice teachers, who described high levels of autonomy and low levels of support, discussed salie nt aspects of support that were missing as counseli ng professionals, support mechanisms from the Ministry  of Education, and guidance towards pedagogical vis ions and desired processes. We did not have cases of low  levels of autonomy we could describe as System-Dictated Practice (with neither autonomy nor support) or System-Guided Practice (with low levels of autonomy and high level of support), but it seems like these cases are more common in the literature (e.g., Collie, 2021) and in typical schooling experiences, which should make them (1) e asy for readers to imagine and comprehend; and (2) worthwhile to include in our framework. ## Discussion

In the cases we analyzed, autonomy was not willingl y granted through deliberate decisions but imposed by the circumstances. Under these circumstances, our data reflect both the benefits and concerns associated with teacher autonomy (e.g., Börü, 2018; Hong & Youngs, 2016). O ur data show that by getting support and overcoming  the challenge of excessive autonomy, some teachers navi gated the unique schooling conditions well and redu ced some of the disorder it implies. This aligns with complexity theory, which regards bottom-up self-organization as a crucial element of stabilizing systems (Colander & Kupers, 2014). Our findings suggest that providing extensive autonomy may put heavy responsibility on teachers’ shoulders, which may be experienced as a hardship. However, providing flexible support may alleviate the challenges involved in extensive autonomy and constitute an essential element of the ability of a system to adapt. While Jonathan reported being given multiple  types of support along with the encouragement to be autonomo us (“push forward and we will help”), Ethan and Dap hne wished their autonomy be accompanied by explicit guidance. The main question that these findings raise is what kind of support teachers need to realize extensive autonomy. We argue that a first step towards answer ing such questions about autonomy and support is to distingu ish between these tw o notions. To this end, we articulated and illustrated four profiles that emerged from the  interaction between levels of autonomy and support  in the context of teaching displaced students.
