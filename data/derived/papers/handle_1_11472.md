# Leveraging Simulations in Higher Education to Transform Learning and Professional Practice

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Despite the growing popularity and educational potential of simulation-based learning, a notable research gap exists in understanding the role of theoretical frameworks and research-based analytical models in facilitating learning through simulation in the context of work. This gap is critical as the complexity of organizational systems increases, necessitating a comprehensive understanding of how in situ simulation candrive individual, collective and interdisciplinary learning. To address this gap, this symposium presents studies on in situ simulation in higher education settings, focusing on sociocultural studies that support practice change, collective learning and the development of the simulation participants' transformative agency. Understanding the simulation participants as active transformative agents or world-makers of the future is essential for developing effective in situ simulation interventions that can enhance high-quality education and promote students’ learning.

Keywords: simulation, organizational complexity, sociocultural approaches, collective learning, transformative agency, practice change Session summary In the field of learning sciences, a primary aim is to conceptualize and design learning environments that enhance learners' opportunities and contexts for professional learning activities. Additionally, there is a strong emphasis on broadly implementing carefully nurtured pilot innovations that successfully meet these goals. The use of gamebased techniques and simulations in professional and higher education is on the rise, providing innovative learning environments that align with educational objectives. In overall, simulation environments enable learners  to explore and train, with the advantage of no real-world consequences from error. Within simulations, learners are facilitated to understand and manage complex real-world phenomena, reconstruct realistic situations, and practice crucial skills, such as negotiation, knowledge cocr eation, and interaction processes, to prepare them for working life opportunities (e.g., Kneebone et al., 2004; Fe nt et al., 2015; Gormley & Fenwick, 2016; Chernikov a et al., 2020; Muukkonen & Kajamaa, 2024). For example, medical  education simulations enhance students' diagnostic competency and problem-solving abilities (Heitzmann et al., 2019; Bligh & Bleakley, 2006), soft’ or ‘non-technical’ skills (Hofmann, Curran & Dickens, 20 21), while simulations allow them to apply discipli nary knowledge and real-world skills in practical interactions (Hertel & Millis, 2002). In situ simulations (i.e. simulation based educational activities in the naturalistic work environment) have been effectively employed to assess and enhance the  operational preparedness for emergency situations,  and for treating COVID-19 patients in intensive care units, showcasing the potential of these simulations (Fregene et al., 2020). Simulations locat ed in real workplace settings have been proven espe cially effective for “contextually testing” the current organizational system, for exa mple, to identif y challenges that participants face and their possible coping strategies, ultimately help buildin g strategies for better managing complexity (Gormle y & Fenwick, 2016), and to target organizational learni ng and transformation efforts (Gormley et al., 2020; Conn et al., 2023).

Traditionally, simulation environments have been de signed to emphasize  skill development at the individual level (Gormley et al., 2023). However, research also indicates that simulated s cenarios requiring collaboration within computer-supported learning systems, can be particularly useful for extending beyond limits of individual capability in a scaffolded, supportive and safe way (Gormley et al., 2020). Such computer supported collaborative systems open up for learners to generate a greater number of ideas (Csanadi et al., 2021) and shared knowledge practices (Muukkonen & Kajamaa, 2024; Hakkarainen, 2009) before reaching a solution. Also, sociomaterial modalities present in collaborative simulation situations can effectively raise awareness of gaps in shared understanding, enhance students’ responsibility tak ing, reveal the new knowledge generated, and prompt reflection on the groups’ performative progress (Abrandt Dahlgren, et al., 2016; Damşa, 2014; Van Aalst, 2009), enhancing the participants’ collective problem-solving abilities and learning outcomes (Muukkonen & Kajamaa, 2024; Zhang et al., 2018). For example, in the context of teacher education, when facilitating pre-service teachers' learning of diagnostic reasoning, collaborative tas ks can enhance their learning of complex diagnostic  tasks, problem solving and critically evaluating others' arguments in simulated scenarios (Sailer et al., 2023; Csanadi et al., 2021).

However, potential users of a collaborative  approach need to be guided in how best to introduce  this form of simulation. For example, in the context of medical education, there are typical phases includi ng instructing/briefing for participants, participating in the simulation  exercise/scenario and engaging in the post-scenario debriefing, ensuring that their psychological safety is developed and maintained (Gormley et al., 2020). Although tailored feedback is resource-intensive for educators, exploring partial automati on of this feedback offers both potential and challenges, underscoring its importance in educational simulation processes.  Even though simulation-based learning is considered a highly promising instructional approach for learning (Codreanu et al., 2020; Cook et al, 2013), collaborative task s inevitably can create tensions, disagreements, and confusion (Muukkonen et al., 2010). For instance, students ma y struggle to establish trust and collaboration due  to their multiple disciplinary backgrounds and the complexit y involved in simulation-based learning (Gormley & Fenwick, 2016; Kiesewetter et al., 2020). Thus, spe cific and supportive feedback are crucial for increasing the students’ performative learning potential (Sailer et al., 2023), and for enhancing their t ransformative agency, in other words, the students’ breaking away from historically formed frames of ac tion and collectively taking agentive actions to address and overcome tensions and contradictions in their collective learning processes (Virkkunen, 2006), in our case in taking place in various simulation contexts in higher education. Despite the growing popularity of these methods, and the acknowledged educational potential of simulation processes, there is a notable knowledge gap on the role of theory (Chan, et al., 2024; Eppich & Reedy, 2022; Berragan, 2011; Dieckmann, 2012), and researc h-based analytical (and pedagogical) models (Gormley et al., 2020) in facilitating the collective creation of knowledge and learning through in-situ simulation. This gap is critical as the complexity of organizational system s continues to increase, necessitating a more compr ehensive understanding of how in situ simulation candrive i ndividual and interdisciplinary learning. To addres s this gap, this symposium presents studies on the implementation and impact of in situ simulation in higher education settings, with a particular focus on sociocultural approaches that support practice change and the development of the participants transformative agency, and of becoming tomorrow’s practitioners. Understanding their theoretical premises and dynamics is essential for developing effective in-situ simulation interventions that can enhance high quality education and improve the students’ learning.

The meaning of material artifacts in social Interactions: Transformative agents in healthcare simulation exercises Camilla Wiig, Anu Kajamaa, Gerry Gormley Bringing theory into simulation research would be i mportant for recognizing and analyzing complex heal th care systems and analyzing how students learn in educational simulat ions prior to entering the working life (Chan et al., 2023; Gormley et al., 2020). In response to this need, we draw from sociocultural theorizing (Vygotsky 1986; Vygotsky & Luria 1994), that provides us with analy tical tools to understand complex activities, such as health care simulation exercises— sociocultural theorizing yet has gained little attention regarding simulation. We apply a sociocultural lens on tool-mediation, which widens our understanding of how hu mans learn, develop and transform their local environment through tool-mediated activities (Vygotsky 1986; Vygotsky & Luri a 1994). From this view, material-semiotic tools (i.e., tangible/physical artifacts and concepts/ language) are perceived as constitutive of human activity, development and learning. They are also crucial for the development of the human mind, collective thinking and culture, carrying cul tural knowledge both for the individual and collect ives about their history and purpose (Vygotsky, 1986).  Furthermore, h uman knowledge is externalized, stabilized, and further developed through artifacts (Grossen, 2010). Material and digital affordances can also function as semiotic means for the interaction among participants to establish joint meaning and to co-create new, innovative artifacts (Kumpulainen et al., 2019; Mäkitalo, 2011). From this perspective, the learning and development of individuals, organizations, and societies are dependent on how artifacts mediat e knowledge and skills and how the artifacts are made available to participants, including new generations (Säljö, 1999). To provide new knowledge on the transformative use of artifacts in simulation-based medical education, our study focuses on how the students and instructors interact and make use of the materialities across 30 authentic simulations in a Nordic simulation center. Our empirical data consists of 48 hours of  naturally occurring video recorded interactions of nursing students (N=40) and their instructors (N=10), in three advanced simul ator suits. The compulsory simulation course is part of a Bache lor in Basic Nursing offering basic training of deteriorating health conditions such as angina pectoris, postoper ative bleeding or cardiac arrest before students’ first clinical placement. Groups of 6 – 10 students were engaging in a total of six scenarios over a 2-day period. We decided to focus on analyzing the video recordings of  the 15-minute simulation exercise, because digital and hands-on tools and artifacts are particularly critical in this phase of the process, when the nurse students are interacting with the manikin and other medical digital equipment. In this study, we ask the following research questi ons: How material artifacts and tools are used in the studied simulation educational exercises? What characterizes the sociomaterial relations among students and instructors in the simulated educational exercises? Our results display that  instructors monitoring and manipulating the manikin and other medical digital tools provide hints and prompts to engage students start expected nurse care performances during the exercises, in situ. They introduced and used eight kinds of artifacts both human and non-human materials  such as co-students, nutrition, medical objects, texts, maniki n, communication tools etc. These artifacts were used both restrictively, to avoid engaging in unprofessional nurse-care procedures during the simulation, and expansively, to initiate and support actions of performing appro priate nurse care procedures and for developing pro fessional expertise. More specifically, the restrictive vs. expansive use of an artifact  refers to that in some cases an artifact/the manikin was used to avoid going beyond the standard script  of the scenario (restrictive use), whereas in other cases even the same artifact /manikin could be used to facilitate and support going beyond the standard script of the scenario (expansive use). We also detected  incidental vs. planned use of an artifact, which refers to that in some cases an artifact was introduced durin g the simulation exercise in an ad-hoc or improvisational manner (incidental use), whereas in other cases the  artifact was introduced based on a plan made befor e the exercise (planned use). By planned, we refer to an explicit mentioning of the artifact in the written scenario, or an explicit reference to the planned use of the artifact in the talk of the participants. In the symposium, we will present selected episodes that illustrate our analysis of t he restricted vs. expansive use of artefacts and pl anned vs. incidental artefact use. Our study provides new insights into the role of material artifacts in healthcare simulations, emphasizing the importance of their strategic use to enhance students’ learning. By applying sociocultural theory, the research reveals critical dimensions of tool and artifact use that can inform the transformative a gency of practice development in medical education. The findings suggest that the mere presence of artifacts is insufficient for ensuring student learning; their use must be st rategically integrated by participants actively transforming the simulation exercises. This research contributes to a deeper understanding of the socio-material dynamics in healthcare training, offering valuable perspectives  for educators and practitioners.  Understanding the simulation participants as active transformative agents or wor ld-makers of the future is essential for developing ef fective in situ simulation interventions that can enhance high-quality education. Conceptualising and assessing communication and teamwork skills in simulation-based medical education to support professional collaboration in practice Riikka Hofmann, Ruby Woodward One of the perceived key tasks of HE is preparation  for workplace practice and learning. Collaborative communication and interprofessional teamwork skills – part of what is often referred to as non-technical skills (NTS) – are widely acknowledged as central to this (Römgens  et al., 2020). NTS, and collaboration skills more specifically, are well-established in medical educat ion curricula internationally, but they are of wide r relevance in HE: there is increasing acknowledgement in the f ield that all graduates need a range of non-technical competences, or ‘employability skills’, for effective professional practice in increasingly complex organizational systems (Kornelakis & Petrakaki, 2020). Simulation-based learning (SBL) is increasingly used to enhanc e communication and collaboration skills but despite its growing popularity and educational potential, l ittle is known about its effectiveness (Chernikova et al., 2020). The field lacks robust outcome models and clarity about educationally relevant theoretical/conceptual dimen sions of such skills to support simulation developm ent (Kostusiak et al., 2017).

Hereby w e need to consider the temporal and institutional d istance between professional learning interventions and their (various) outcomes  (Hofmann & Ilie, 2022). The ultimate goal of SBL is to impact on workplace practice. However, evaluating impact pathways from learning contexts to such more distant outcomes is challenging. It requires theory (Hofmann, 2024) – theoretical understandings of the more proximal lea rning outcomes (like individuals’ skills) that may be con nected with more distant (organizatio nal) outcomes and the kinds of mechanisms that link those (Hofmann et al., 2024). This gap is the focus is this symposium. This presentation addresses this gap through the fo llowing questions: 1. How are communication and teamwork skills conceptually understood in existing  undergraduate and postgraduate medical education/t raining curricula in the UK and how are those skills theoretically linked with wider outcomes? 2. How are individual and organizational learning outcomes related to communi cation and teamwork understood in studies of SBL in medical education/training and what gaps  exist? 3. What role could sociocultural learning th eories play in designing and evaluating SBL to overcome the challe nges identified in (2) and support translation of t he skills identified in (1) into sustainable collaborative workplace practice? The aim of this study is to contribute to theoretically inf ormed understandings of how we candesign and evaluate simulation-based learning interventions to support the develop ment of communication and teamwork skills in healthcare contexts. To answer these questions, this presentation synthesizes findings from three analyses:

(1) a qualitative content analysis (Mayring, 2019)  of learning objectives (LOs) related to communication and

teamwork skills in UK medical education/training-curricula, (2) a Scoping Review (Peters et al., 2017) of outcome measures used in research on the effectiveness of S BL on communication and teamwork skills in medical education/training, and (3) a pilot impact evaluation of a mixed reality simulation utilising holographic patients. The curriculum analysis identifies and synthesizes the conceptual dimensions underlying communication and teamwork skills in the curricula (cf., Hofmann, Curran & Dickens, 2021), which this presentation recontextualizes in in relation to sociocultural perspectives within the Learning Sciences. However, the Scoping Review reveals a significant lack of conceptual and methodological c onsistency in designing and evaluating SBL to suppo rt the development of the as-identified communication and teamwork skills in medical education/training. Building on these findings, a novel theory-informed (Grossman et al., 2009; Heitzmann et al, 2 023) mixed-reality simulation is then presented to discuss a more theoretically r obust approach to designing and evaluating SBL to s upport the development of these skills.

This presentation contributes to our conceptual understanding and  theory-informed assessment of collaboration-focused learning outcomes SBL. It hereby also contributes t o the readiness to generate a rigorous evidence base for using SBL to develop communicatio n and teamwork skills in medical education, which c ould inform a development of SBL-interventions for professional collaboration in HE more widely. SimLab: Harnessing the power of in-situ simulation to promote organizational change in preparing for medical emergencies Sarah O’Hare, Richard Conn, Anu Kajamaa, Gerry Gormley Globally, healthcare is becoming increasingly compl ex. With rapid advancements in medical diagnostics and treatments, shifting population healthcare needs, a nd finite resources, there has never been a time to  ensure healthcare workers are prepared to meet these chall enges and deliver safe, effective care. As in many industries, including healthcare, high-acuity events (e.g., a patient experiencing cardiac  arrest) are an important and harsh reality. These events are often fragile situations: time-critical, with narrow margins for success or error. At worst, they can result in the death of a patient who might have survived with timely and effective treatment. Given their low frequency, healthcare professionals  are rarely afforded opportunities to develop and ma intain their skills through real-world exposure to such events.

Simulation offers a solution, enabling healthcare p rofessionals to learn through simulated experiences  to prepare them for real-world situations. High-acuity events require a timely, coordinated, and ad aptive approach to optimise outcomes. Simulation not o nly enhances individual responses but can also fost er collective team responses. Healthcare environments are laden with l atent errors and influenced by various spatial, mat erial, cultural, and social factors that can either enable  or inhibit effective responses to emergencies. The refore, we speculate that simulation has the potential to supp ort organizational learning and create the conditio ns for teams to deliver optimal emergency care.  Using an activity-theoretical framework, our research sought to desig n, implement, and evaluate an in-situ simulation program (SimLab) aimed at fostering collective, expansive learning and driving organizational change to improve emerge ncy preparedness. Our study focused on addressing t he following research questions: 1) What contradictions arose during the SimLab process, and how did they influence organizational change ? 2) In what ways did the SimLab intervention enhance th e interprofessional team’s expansive learning?

Activity Theory was applied through the development and implementation of an adapted Change Laboratory intervention utilizing in-situ simulation (SimLab). As an instance of a ‘medical emergency’ we crafted paediatric emergencies as a topic for these in-situ simulations (i.e. a child experiencing life threa tening anaphylaxis and another child experience meningococcal meningitis).  This study was conducted in a suburban general pra ctice. An interprofessional team (medical staff, nursing s taff, pharmacy staff a nd administrative personal) from this general practice participated in the SimLab, which included four workshops: two insitu sessions and fo ur facilitated group discussions. These group discussi ons were audio-recorded, transcribed, and analysed using activity-theoretical analytical tools, including the activity system model, the cycle of expansive learning, and the concept of contradictions. Participants followed a learning process that progressed from questioning their existing practices to analysis, modelling, consolidation, and the generalization of collaboratively developed new practices. This process enabled the team to foster collective expansive learning and adopt a systemic perspective  during their discussions. They were able to concentrate on  improving emergency care preparedness within the s afe, supportive setting of simulation, which in turn promoted both organizational learning and change. This research contributes new insights into theory-guided design and instruction in in-situ training, integrating social and cultural factors to support the developm ent of participants’ expansive learning and future orientation.

The study provided tools to  enhance interprofessional training and enriches our  understanding of how to effectively pedagogically support insitu delivery. The simulation process demonstrated in this study h as the potential for transferability, offering valuable learning opportunities in various clinical settings and contexts. Transformative agency as a driver of change in simulation-based learning in higher education Hanni Muukkonen, Anu Kajamaa Despite the increasing use of simulations to enhanc e students' learning processes in higher education,  many of these efforts still focus on acquiring established skills and competencies seen as relevant for adapti ng to curriculum requirements and educational r eforms. This adaptive approach often neglects criti cal historical and contextual factors, specifically the cultural-historical development of higher education institutions and the diverse contexts within which they operate (Miettinen, 2022). Moreover, it tends to overlook the mediated, transformative, participatory, and situated dimensions of collective student learning. To truly transform educational practice, it is essential for simulation-based learning environments to promote collective a nalysis of interactions, contradictions, and the construction of context. Th erefore, a significant research gap exists in explo ring participatory methods of simulation design that facili tate students’ transformative agency, enabling student communities to broaden their possibilities for action by redesigning their collaborative learning activities. Responding to the demanding  co-creation r equirements and taking initiatives and actions to t ackle such challenges calls for student agency, which is a particularly useful conc ept for studying and explaining student s’ interdisciplinary learning and development during such transformations. Drawing from a CHAT-based theory and method developed by Haapasaari and colleagues (2016) and Sannino (2015), this study analyzes expressio ns of transformative agency in the discourse and actions of simulation participants within the sociocultural  context of higher education institution. We hold that transformative agency manifests itself  in discourse and social activity that breaks away from a given frame of action and/or takes initiatives to transform it (Virkkunen, 2006). Further, we define transformative agency as a reframing proc ess evidenced in students’ initiative and commitmen t to transform their activity and its context(s) for personal and/or acad emic ends (Kajamaa and Kumpulainen, 2019; Rajala et al., 2013; Stetsenko, 2008). Transformati ve agency and expansive learning, t hat is a collective, non-linear learning process without  predetermined end results (Engeström, 2015), are theoretically and conceptually closely connected, even inseparable: they both require and enable each other in the sense that expansive learning is a process of formation of transformative agency (Haapasaari et al., 2016, 243). Expansive learning and transformative agency are always connected to a spe cific contradiction in a specific historically evol ved activity system. They enable overcoming the contradiction by introducing new, more adequate mediators to transform the collective activity and its local sociomaterial con text. The newly created solutions can for example, enhance knowledge sharing, collaboration in a novel way (Ha apasaari et al., 2016). This process requires time and continuous efforts, as transformative agency develops and is maintained in collective interaction over time when agentive actions gain their meaning, their conseque nces and their continuity in the interplay between individuals and their collective (Engeström, 2007).

We ask as our research questions: 1) What types of expressions of students’ transformative agency can be found in the simulation exercises? and 2) How do th ese contribute to the interaction, proceeding and o utcome of the simulation exercises. Our empirical case study was undertaken in four iterations of a master’s-level course on organizational psychology. We conducted an organizational simulation where stu dents developed a business concept and HR service offer for a client. The data  includes 11 hours and 22 minutes of video from two  teams, recorded during nine team meetings. The course feat ured lectures and a simulation exercise where stude nts from diverse backgrounds, namely educational psychology, engineering, economics, hum anities, and education, worked together. Over six weeks, 3.5-hour weekly sessions were held, beginning with a 45-60-minute lecture on organizational psychology themes such as dynamics, mobile work, and trust. Teams then spent 90-120 minutes planning and leading their teamwork, followed by 30 minutes of reflective discussions. In our analysis, we identified the number and propo rtion of occurrences of the six types of transforma tive agency, namely resisting, criticizing, explicating,  envisioning, committing to actions and taking acti ons (Haapasaari et al., 2016), in the video dat a and the transcription of the simulation exercises. We primarily relied on the video data, using the transcriptions as a supportiv e coding tool. Transformative agency expressions we re typically assigned to individual speaking turns. Wh en a speaker repeate d the same idea after an interruption, it was not coded as a new expression, as the intention  remained unchanged. Occasionally, a single speakin g turn contained multiple consecutive expressions of transformative agency. In instances of rapid, like-minded dialogue with overlapping speech, we considered it a single expression, given the shared intention and meaning.  Beyond discourse analysis, we focused on participants' use  of novel mediating artifacts and representations. This multifaceted approach allowed us to capture the dynamic and nuanced nature of tran sformative agency in action in the simulations.

Our findings show how when resisting, the current status quo was challenged, and new alternative suggestions were made by individual students by identifying problems in current ways of teamworking. As the students were explicating new possibilities or potentials in the  joint activity, they related to their past positive  experiences or examples of professional practices. In envisioning,  they ideated new patterns or models in the activit y and made future-oriented suggestions or presentations of a new way of collective working. Finally, some of the student s committed to taking concrete, new actions to change  the group activity, using digital tools and resour ces. Furthermore, our findings illustrate the explanator y power of transformative agency in the analysis of organizational simulation exercises, providing a new perspective on the understudied interplay between individual and collective learning in simulation-based education. By emphasizing collective actions and systemic change, which emerge and evolve over time, this approach tr anscends the examination of individual students and  their situational actions. It expands the current understanding of interdisciplinary learning and development in higher education. However, this expansive learning process  represents a unique form of collective learning and was not without tensions. As students were designing and enacting  collective interdisciplinary learning, they were often faced with contradictory views on adequacy or impor tance of some action, leading to a need to envision s transformations and new solutions, This study provides new insights into the role of s imulations in higher education, emphasizing participatory methods that facilitate transformative agency. The study also shows that simulations can also support organizational learning for the future, though implementing such environments involves inhe rent challenges and tensions. Overall, integrating transformative agency and expa nsive learning in simulation-based education can significantly enhance students' interdisciplinary learning processes, equipping  them with the skills needed for complex professional environments. Future research should continue exploring these transformative lear ning approaches across various simulation-based contexts.
