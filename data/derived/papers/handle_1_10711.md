# Electrodermal Activities During Self-Regulated Learning Relate to Learning Performance Within a Technology-Rich Environment

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study employed computer trace data and electrodermal activities (EDA) to examine students’ temporal dynamics of self-regulated learning (SRL). Specifically, 106 students accomplished a diagnostic task in an intelligent tutoring system. During the task, we recorded students’ interactive activities within BioWorld and EDA signals. Then we coded the forethought, performance, and self-reflection phase of SRL from computer trace data and extracted four EDA features for further analysis. The results of Friedman tests revealed that students’ had higher skin conductance levels (SCL) and more skin conductance response (SCR) peaks in the forethought phase. However, the average amplitude and duration of SCR peaks were significantly higher in the performance phase than forethought and self-reflection phases. Furthermore, Graphic Gaussian Model indicated that students’ SCR features during the self-reflection phase were more associated with learning performance. These findings provide evidence regarding using EDA to identify temporal dynamics of SRL processes.

## Introduction

Self-regulated learning (SRL) has been widely acknowledg ed as a critical factor to realize high learning achievements in technology-rich learning environments (TREs; Azevedo & Taub, 2 020). While TREs offer learners personalized guidance, they also pose seve ral challenges. The information involved in TREs is always massive and non-sequential, requiring sufficient SRL abilities to determine what information to process and how and when to process it  (Azevedo & Taub, 2020). SRL is typically defined as a dynamic and multidimensional process whereby learners proactively monitor and regulate their behaviors, cognition, emotion, and motivation to achieve learning goals (Zimmerman, 2000). A large body of research has confirmed the positive effects of SRL activities on academic performance across learning contexts ra nging from text comprehension, mathematical problem-solving, to clinical reasoning  (Molenaar et al., 2023). However, it should be noted that not all students are highly self-regulated learners, and we should provide immediate  scaffolding and guidance to facilitate the occurrence of effective SRL. To do so, the dynamic fluctuations of SRL processes should be captured as  they unfold over time. More importantly, students’ cognitive and emotional states during different SRL activities may differ in predictive effects on le arning outcomes, which still need further investiga tions. Drawing on this, the current study sought to employ electrodermal activities (EDA) to reveal the differences across SRL phases as well as the relationship between EDA features and learning performance.

## Theoretical framework

According to Zimmerma n (2000), SRL unfolds over three loosely sequential phases: forethought, performance, and self-reflection. Learners analyze the task, set task-specific goals, and make plans during the forethought phase. During the performance phase, they utilize multiple  learning strategies to solve the task and monitor learning progress and performance. In the last self-reflection phase, students reflect on their learning processes and make attributive judgments and adaptations.  During the process of SRL, students may exhibit var iations in behaviors, emotions, and cognitive engagement. For instance, Zheng et al. (2021) found that students were more likely to feel curious and confused during the forethought phase but were more bored in the self-reflection phase. Students put more cognitive engagement in the performance phase than the other two phases (Li et al., 2021). To further understand these variations in SRL, rese archers have turned to physiological measures, such as electrodermal activity (EDA), for more insights.  EDA is regarded as a measure of arousal, a component of individual affects (Boucsein, 2012). EDA signals can be decomposed into skin conductance level (SCL) and skin conductance response (SCR). SCL is the slowly changing component and provides a baseline for skin conductance (Boucsein, 2012), while SCR refers to the rapidly changing phasic component and  reflects responses to specific psychological or environmental changes (Boucsein, 2 012). Higher SCL is indicative of more attention and predisposition to receive and analyze information (Hernández-García et al., 2017).  SCR features, including the rate of peaks (SCR_Rate), the average amplitude of peaks (SCR_Amp), and the average duration of peaks (SCR_Dur) within task periods, can represent the di spersion, intensity, and duration of physiological arousals (Vanneste et al., 2021).

Moreover, educational researchers have extracted students’ SCL and SCR features and investigated their relationships with engagement levels, cognitive loa d, sustained attention, academic emotions, and learning performance (Di Lascio et al., 2018; Pijeira-Díaz et al., 2016; Yu et al., 2023). For instance, Di Lascio et al. (2018) established machine learning models to predict students’ emotional engagement from EDA features. Vanneste et al. (2021) found that SCR_Rate significantly increased with elevated cognitive dem ands. In the study of Dindar et al. (2020), researchers employed the synchrony o f EDA features to indicate students’ cognitive regulation in collaborative learning settings.  Despite the existing evidence, few studies examined students’ EDA features in fine-grained SRL phases nor investigated the association s between EDA features across SRL processes and learning performance. Therefore, the current study aims to address this r esearch gap by answering the following research questions: (1) How did students’ electrodermal activities differ a cross three SRL phases? (2) How did electrodermal activities during three SRL phases relate to learning performance?

## Participants, learning context, and procedures

A total of 106 undergraduate medical students participated in this study, with an aver age age of 22.3, SD = 1.67. This study required students to solve the task in B ioWorld, an intelligent tutoring system simulating virtual patients to help medical practitioners train SRL and clinical reasoning skills. Students first read a case summary describing patient information and symptoms. Then t hey could order medical tests or search the online library embedded in BioWorld to collect more evidence. Thro ughout the task, students could formulate  diagnostic hypotheses. Bioworld also enables students to assess the supportiveness of gathered evidence item to monitor the task progress and evalute the possibility of proposed hypotheses. Afterwards, they entered a final hypothesis and wrote a reflective summary of the task process. BioWorld then would automatically generate performance feedback based on the submission to indicate whether students reached an accurate diagnosis or not. Prior to data collection, students consented to participate in this study. Then they were equipped with the wearable sensor (Beijing Huixin Technology) to record EDA signals, with a sampling rate of 40Hz. Before the experimental task, students did a sample task to familiarize themselves with BioWorld. During the experimental session, students diagnosed the virtual patient Amy. Their operations in BioWorld and corresponding timestamps were recorded in log files.

Data processing and analysis As depicted in Table 1, this study adapted Zimmerman’s (2000) cyclical ph ase model and identified three SRL phases, i.e., forethought, performance, and self-reflection, and corresponding timestamps from BioWorld log files. Regarding the processing of EDA signals, human inspections were first performed to exclude low-quality EDA signals based on the proportion (i.e., at most 20%) of artifacts and air electricity.  The EDA signals of 10 0 particpants were included in the final analysis. To further remove noises and smooth EDA signals, we performed a low-pass filter with a 3Hz cutoff frequency and a 4 th order Butterworth filter, using the “NeuroKit 2” pa ckage (Makowski et al., 2021). Then we employed “NeuroKit 2” to decompose EDA into SCL and SCR. The minimum threshold by which to exclude SCR peaks relative to the largest amplitude in the signal was.10 (Makowski et al., 2021). In particular, the present study involved four EDA features (see Table 2; Vanneste et al., 2021). To answer the first research question, we first segmented EDA features based on the timestamps for three SRL phases. Given the violation of normal distribut ions, non-parametric Friedman ANOVA tests of differences among repeated measures and post-hoc analys is were performed to examine whether students genera ted significantly different EDA features across three S RL phases. To answer the second research question, we conducted Gaussian Graphic Model (GGM), an undirected network of partial correlation coef ficients, to explore the relationship between EDA features during three SRL phases and task performance. ## Findings

RQ1. How did students’ electrodermal activities differ across three SRL phases? As shown in Table 3, the results of Friedman  ANOVA test revealed that all four EDA features sign ificantly differed across three SRL phases (SCL_Mean: χ2(2) = 17.20, p <.001; SCR_Rate: χ 2(2) = 75.27, p <.001; SCR_Amp: χ2(2) = 36.06, p <.001; SCR_Dur: χ2(2) = 171.66, p <.001). More specifically, post-hoc analyses revealed that students had significantly higher SCL_Mean values in the forethough phase compared to  the self-reflection phase. In terms of three SCR features, all post-hoc analyses exhibited significant results. We foun d that students had the highest  SCR_Rate (Median = 9.00)  during the forethought phase, followed by self-reflection (Median = 5.07) and performance (Median = 1.67) phase. In contrast, students’ SCR_ Amp (Median =.06) and SCR_Dur (Median = 3.48) were significantly higher in the performance phase than the other two SRL phases. ## Discussion and conclusion

This study captured students’ EDA features to reveal the temporal dynamics of their SRL processes and indicated the relationships between EDA features across three  SRL phases and learning performance. More specific ally, we focused on SCL_Mean as a measure of sustained attention and used SCR_Rate, SCR_Amp, and SCR_Dur to reflect the dispersion, intensity, and duration of arousals induced by cognitive and emotional stimuli. The findings revealed that SCL_Mean was higher in the forethought than the self-reflection phase, indicating students’ overall attention levels were higher in the forethought pha se. Given the complexity of clinical reasoning task s, it was reasonable that medical students put sustained atte ntion to identifying task demands and critical info rmation at the preparation stage. Regarding the occurrence of arousals, we found that students experienced more SCR peaks in the forethought phase, suggesting that they encountered more frequent stimuli, such as unfamiliar information and cognitive discrepancies. During the performan ce phase, students collected and analyzed interconn ected evidence to propose diagnostic hypotheses, which led them to experience intense and long-lasting arousals. Furthermore, EDA features across three SRL phases exhibited distinct relations to learning performance. As aforementioned, frequent SCR peaks corresponded to more cognitive discrepancies, thus it is unsurpr ising to find that more SCR peaks during the forethought pha se were negatively associated with performance. Similarly, students’ sustained attention during the performanc e phase, indicated by SCL_Mean, was positively asso ciated with learning performance. Most interestingly, we f ound that all three SCR features were positive indi cators of performance, which reflected the significance of th e self-reflection phase of SRL in affecting students’ effe ctive learning. Overall, this study recognized the plausi bility of using EDA to identify temporal dynamics o f SRL processes, and future research can combine more multimodal data to reveal a more comprehensive picture of SRL.
