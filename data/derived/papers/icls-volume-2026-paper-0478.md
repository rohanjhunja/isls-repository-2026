# Making Theory Visible: A Visual Model for Resource Theory and Mechanistic Reasoning

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Understanding conceptual change requires examining how students activate and coordinate cognitive resources while reasoning about a task. Resource Theory (RT) describes how reasoning emerges from the activation and coordination of fine-grained cognitive resources in context, while Mechanistic Reasoning (MR) characterizes how students construct causal explanations by identifying entities, their properties, and their activities. In this paper, we propose a visual model that brings these frameworks together to illustrate how framing, resource activation, and coordination interact during problem solving. The model serves as an analytic tool for tracing how different configurations of these processes can lead to productive mechanistic explanations or to more superficial reasoning.

A central goal of science education is to understand how students reorganize and refine their knowledge as they engage in building explanations and problem solving, a process often described as conceptual change (Posner et al., 1982). Research shows that students’ reasoning depends not only on what knowledge they possess but also how they activate and coordinate that knowledge in specific contexts. Resource Theory characterizes knowledge as a collection of fine-grained cognitive resources that can be flexibly activated and coordinated during reasoning (diSessa, 1993; Hammer et al., 2005; Sherin, 2006; Philip, 2011) and Mechanistic Reasoning (MR) focuses on how learners build causal explanations by identifying entities, properties, and activities that produce a phenomenon (Russ, 2006; Russ et al., 2008).  Although both traditions provide rich descriptions of student thinking, their relationships and interactions are often presented in dense descriptive passages. Here, we propose a visual representation integrating Resource Theory (RT) and Mechanistic Reasoning (MR) to illustrate how framing, resource activation, and coordination may interact during reasoning episodes. Our goal is not to propose a new theory but rather to offer an analytic perspective that  will allow researchers to track student thinking as their reasoning unfolds during problem solving.

## Theoretical framework

Resource Theory (RT) conceptualizes knowledge as a collection of fine-grained cognitive resources that can be activated in different contexts (diSessa, 1993). These resources may be conceptual, procedural, or epistemological in nature. Activation refers to the process by which certain resources are cued in response to features of a problem, while coordination refers to how multiple resources are linked together to support reasoning. An important aspect of RT is epistemological framing, which refers to how students interpret the nature of an activity and what kind of knowledge they believe is appropriate to use (Hammer et al., 2005; Scherr & Hammer, 2009). Framing influences which resources students activate and how they coordinate them during reasoning. Mechanistic reasoning (MR) describes how learners construct causal explanations by identifying entities, properties, and activities that produce a phenomenon (Russ et al., 2008). These elements are coordinated into causal chains that explain how and why an outcome occurs. From a re source perspective, mechanistic reasoning can be understood as emerging from the activation and coordination of multiple cognitive resources. For instance, identifying entities, activities, and properties within a phenomenon can be interpreted as the activation of conceptual resources, while linking these elements into causal chains reflects the coordination of those resources into an explanatory structure. In this sense, RT provides a lens for understanding the processes that give rise to mechanistic explanations, while MR characterizes the structure of those explanations. 2862 A model for tracing reasoning during problem solving We developed a visual model that highlights how epistemological framing, resource activation, and coordination interact during problem solving. When students encounter a task, they draw on available cognitive resources to make sense of the situation (Hammer, 2000). Which resources become relevant depends partly on how students frame the task epistemologically. For example, students may frame a task as an opportunity for sensemaking, activating resources that support explanation building and causal reasoning, or  as recalling information from authority, leading them to rely more heavily on memorized formulas or facts. Once resources are activated, students may coordinate them in different ways. Coordination can involve linking ideas, reconciling conflicting explanations, or checking the consistency of multiple resources. In productive cases, students integrate these res ources to construct mechanistic explanations that connect entities, properties, and activities to account for the phenomenon being studied and arrive at the correct/normative answer. In unproductive cases, students may fail to activate the correct resources, coordinate them in constructive ways, or construct appropriate mechanistic explanations.  However, framing is not fixed during reasoning, and students may iteratively revise their problem-solving approach. As students interact with a task or with peers, their interpretation of the activity may shift, redirecting which resources are activated and how they are coordinated, leading to different reasoning trajectories. The model provides a way to track and analyze these shifts in reasoning. Importantly, the model is recursive and should not be interpreted as a fixed sequence through which reasoning must proceed. Rather, it is meant to function as an analytic heuristic for tracing interactions among framing, resource activation, and coordination.

Illustrative example from the literature To illustrate how the visual model operates, we draw on the example of classic sunlight and ice (Hammer, 2000). In this task, students are asked to describe what happens when a piece of ice is placed in a glass of water and left in the sunlight. Students often activate more than one line of reasoning: for example, one student may argue that “the sunlight can only add energy, so the water must get warmer.” In contrast, another argues that “the system will reach thermal equilibrium, so the ice and water will stabilize at the same temperature.” As Hammer explains: Given an unfamiliar problem, you need to search through your resources, perhaps trying several of them out before you arrive at those you find to be useful. Often, as may happen with this problem, you have active at the same time multiple ways of thinking about a problem that conflict with each other, and much of the work you need to do is to reconcile that conflict” (Hammer, 2000, p. S53) The visual model can make the dynamics of this episode visible. When presented with the  task, students frame the problem as one requiring explanation, which cues the activation of relevant resources. In this case, two competing resources are activated: “sunlight adds energy” and “systems come to equilibrium”. Coordination then follows, in which the students need to monitor and reflect on their thinking as they notice the conflict between these resources and attempt to reconcile them. In a productive pathway, students may engage in consistency checking, asking whether both ideas can hold simultaneously, or attempt causal chaining by linking entities (such as sunlight, ice, and water), their properties (energy and temperature), and their activities (melting and energy transfer). Such coordination can lead to a mechanistic explanation: sunlight transfers energy, the ice melts, and the water eventually stabilizes at an equilibrium temperature rather than heating indefinitely. However, our model also highlights alternative outcomes. A student who frames the task as recalling authoritative knowledge may invoke “thermal equilibrium” without constructing the causal chain, resulting in a superficial explanation such as saying “The water and ice will reach thermal equilibrium, so they’ll just balance at the same temperature.” Thus, even with the same initial activations, framing and coordination pathways can lead to divergent reasoning outcomes, which can be tracked in the visual model.

The proposed model connects Resource Theory and Mechanistic Reasoning by visually representing how framing, activation, and coordination interact during reasoning episodes. By making these processes explicit, the model provides a framework for analyzing how students’ reasoning develops during problem solving. For researchers, the model offers a tool for tracing how reasoning progresses or stalls during a task. It may help identify whether difficulties arise from limited resource activation, challenges in co ordinating ideas, or shifts in epistemological framing. Over time, applying the model to discourse data could reveal patterns in how students construct mechanistic explanations across tasks and contexts, yielding information to guide the development of eff ective instructional approaches.
