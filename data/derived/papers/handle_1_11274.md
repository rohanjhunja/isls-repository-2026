# Fostering Familial Presence in STEM Education through an Identities-in-Practice Perspective

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Supporting parental/familial presence in STEM education requires forging new parent-teacher relationships and epistemologies to bridge home and school. Through the FamJam project, using participatory critical design ethnographic methods, we collaborated with an urban public-school teacher, immigrant students, and their mothers to foster familial rightful presence in a STEM unit focused on engineering for sustainable communities. Our study employs the “Identities-in-Practice” framework to investigate how relationships among mothers, teachers, and students cultivate emerging epistemologies and new social learning practices. Findings reveal how teachers and mothers acted as allies, developing new relations that yielded three powerful practices: 1) fostering porous boundaries in STEM; 2) incubating new epistemologies into STEM; and 3) authoring new identities/positions among teachers, mothers, and students, while disrupting normative perspectives in STEM learning. We argue for recognizing, supporting, and leveraging new relationships among identities-in-practice to expand opportunities for parent engagement and advance justice in STEM learning.

## Introduction

A key structural inequality in STEM education is th e undervaluing of minoritized parents and families in school settings. Deficit thinking and hierarchical power s tructures often limit parents’ ability to support t heir children’s learning (Ishimaru & Takahashi, 2017). Schools frequently overlook valuable home-based practices and families’ experiential knowledge (McWayne et al., 2021). Addressing this requires anti-racist, asset-based approaches that recognize minoritized parental knowledge and invite  families to contribute meaningfully to STEM learni ng designs (Stoehr et al., 2022). We define rightful familial presence as authentic family engagement that legitimizes community cultural capital and bridges families and  schools. This presence includes parents c reating new roles and epistemologies, disrupting traditional physical and cultural boundaries between home and school. These shifts challenge teachers to rethink relational dynamics and support family-centered contributions to education. To understand how teachers and parents' relationali ties emerge in the FamJam project and how they shape opportunities for learning, we employ the len s of “Identities-in-Practice.” This framework captures how individuals make meaning of themselves within sp ecific contexts and in relation to others. Thus, ou r research question: How do relations among mothers, teachers and students that support their identities-in-practice allow for new social learning practices and “incubating epistemologies” (Tan & Faircloth, 2023) to emerge?

## Theoretical framework

Our study is grounded in the Rightful Presence Framework for justice-oriented education challenging traditional parental involvement models that often reflect White, middle-class power structures, perpetuating racial and class disparities (Paredes-Scribner & Fernández, 2017). These models confine families to the role of guests and impose dominant practices that dictate ways of knowing and  being (Tzou et al., 2019). Consequently, families lack meaningful opportunities to leverage their roles and social identities in supporting their children’s STEM learning. Rightful Presence Framework contains three intercon nected tenets: 1) Allied political struggle is an e ssential to STEM learning; 2) Rightfulness is claimed by making justices and injustices visible; and 3) Disruption guest-host relationships require amplifying the sociopolitical. Rightful Presence emerges when students, teachers, and families/communities collectively examine, disrupt,  and transform power dynamics in daily practices, n orms, spatial configurations, and relationships within the learning community (Calabrese Barton & Tan, 2020).  We also draw from the Identities-in-Practice framework to emphasize that identities take shape a s one engages in social practices within a community. Holland et al.'s (199 8) identities-in-practice theory focusing on how identities are socio-historically and contextually situated through four integral dimensions: figured worlds, positionality, spaces of authoring, and making of new worlds. This framew ork conceptualizes identity as a continuous process influenced by history, power, and politics, and is mediated by an individual’s agency, creativity and imagination (Rahm & Moore, 2015). Identities are not merely labels to describe oneself. Instead, identities are the ways in which individuals figure themselves and are figured by others as they author their presence in “figured worlds.” Authoring identities-in-practice involves both narrative and performative w ork, consisting of the narrative s individuals tell about themselves and the actions they take (Tan et al., 2013).  We posit that this framework, which involves narrating and performing or embodying iden tities-in-practice, enables us to more precisely understand the roles and relationships of families, parents, t eachers, and students as they engage in activities designed to legitimize their rightful presence and support students' STEM learning.

## Methodology

Drawing upon participatory critical design ethnogra phic methods, we collaborated with a  teacher, and four 6th-grade classrooms of immigrant students and families  to adapt and implement a STEM curriculum, focusing  on engineering for sustainable communities. Our project took place in an urban public school, located in an industrial area and surrounded by factories on all sides. The school serves a vibrant Arab community with ongoing migration from the Middle East. The teacher, Ms. Hened shares  a common backgr ound with her students. Across her four 6th-grade classrooms, 90 students participated, 89% of whom are in economically disadvantaged circumstances. Although the school uses English as the instruction al language, we established a supportive environmen t for students and their mothers, encouraging active use of their native language. Fourteen mothers particip ated in the project, two of whom are proficient in  English. Most participating mothers did not complete high school and typically spent their time caring for their families at home. In FamJam we enacted the Rightful Familial Presence approach through four collaborative activities: 1) exploring familial capital for STEM learning, 2) adapting curriculum through familial capital, 3) enactments, and

4) reflective dialogues. During co-design meetings, the teacher, mothers, and researchers co-explored science and

community cultural wealth related to energy and sus tainable communities. Data were co-generated through field notes, video/audio observations, mothers’ photo voi ce and storytelling, students’ artifacts and group interviews, child-mother interviews, and teacher interviews. Analysis  involved multiple stages of coding based on consta nt comparison procedures. The codes include 1) roles/p ositions (e.g., learner, cultural capital explorer, supporter),

2) refined roles (e.g., advocator for sustainable c ommunity), 3) connections to school, science, and c ommunity

(e.g., insider/outsider), 4) new social learning pr actices (e.g., designing artifacts, collectin g ethnographic data) and 5) incubating epistemologies (e.g., storytelling, lived experiences of energy pollution).

## Findings

Our findings illustrate how emerging relations among the identities-in-practice of mothers, teachers and students yielded three powerful practices that supported familial presence in STEM learning. These practices include: Storying families’ experiences to foster porous boundaries in STEM During community-of-practice planning meetings, mothers played a centra l role by sharing their experiences, expectations, and aspirations in the context of the engineering for sustainable communities curriculum. Alongside the teacher, they engaged in the figured worlds of sustainable communities and energy pollution, working together as allies to foster porous familial-community-STEM boundaries. Mothers’ positionalities were highlighted through a “photo voice” activity and storytelling, constructing narratives of their experiences as minoritized immigran ts and as residents facing daily environmental chal lenges in their neighborhoods. They shared photos of air p ollution from local factories, taken at different t imes of the day, and voiced concerns about their children’s health and well-being. “There is no house without a child suffering from allergies and asthma, ” Lena noted. Mothers described struggles with healthcare costs, keeping homes clean, and odors lingering on their children after outdoor play. Karema shared, “There’s a strong smell that resembles a fire, making it difficult for the children to breat he. Even after five minutes outside, it’s  impossible for them to keep their clothes on [when they come back into the house].”  The mothers also reflected on their cultural values and roles in building sustainable communities, emphasizing principles like “respect” and “helping others.” Ferial cited a Hadith: “To smile in the face of your brother i s an act of charity,” highlighting that small actio ns foster sustainability and community well-being.

Ms. Hened, the teacher, shifted between her roles as a learner, explorer of familial capital, and a fellow immigrant community member. She shared her own expe riences and empathized with the parents, recognizin g the inequities in resources and experiences that her community faces. She expressed: I feel like how the parents felt. I would just take my kids, all I had was like the  neighborhood park. And even in the neighborhood park, it was so limited […]  I felt the inequity, that day, when I took my kids just to my own neighbor because I live right here. And I was like, so unfair, that most neighborhoods, like if you go to like the  East end or the West end, within walking distance, they have these beautiful parks, with ben ches and multiple playgrounds and all the space and a walking path. They don't have that here. I'm starting to understand, like the limitations of our parents that have.

Reflecting on the mothers’ shared experiences, Ms. Hened articulated the need for justice in creating sustainable communities: “We have a right to breathe clean air, we have a right to have green space, we have a right to  have all the services [...] And a lso kids have all the things they need, necessary t o be successful.” Bringing these perspectives and experiences together fostered a deeper engagement with the concept of sustainable communities in the children's education. Mothers as co-designers in these  collaborative efforts broadened the definition of sustainable communities to include a healthy environment and cultural values, creating new educational resources to adapt the engineering for sustainable communities curriculum. Incubating new epistemologies into STEM The adapted curriculum provided students with acces s to diverse science learning spaces and ideas, emb odying various epistemologies and identities-in-practice that disrupted dominant modes of learning and being in the classroom and in STEM. Students took on different r oles, such as ethnographers and engineers while bei ng community insiders as they engaged in their science investigations.. First, students explored their mothers’ views on po llution, reauthored community experiences, and identified environmental challenges. They  engaged in the question: “What messages are the mot hers conveying through these photos?”   Malek, for instance, chose a photo taken by his mother, stating,  “This is the view that I see every night from my house window.” Other students reflected on a nighttime photo showing factories working non-stop, expressing thoughts like, “This photo makes m e think that pollution ha ppens all the time” (Hanady). This activity brought their lived experiences of po llution to the forefront, identifying environmental  challenges they face daily.  As students analyzed the mothers’ photographs and l ayered on their own interpretations and experiences with living in a polluted space, they e ngaged in the process of incubating epistemologies,  fostering “iterative, dialogic connections between community and community-driven science that produced new characteristics of each, with new artifacts with po tential to seed new social interactions and new way s of being” (Tan & Faircloth, 2023, p. 1648). Such new artifacts can be seen in what the students chose to engineer to address the community concern of unrelenting pollution.

Second, students’ engineering artifacts embodied a rightful familial presence as they progressed from ideation to prototyping. Their designs emphasized s ustainability and showcased at least two energy transformations, incorporating materials like coppe r tape, LED lights, renewable energy sources, and renew able resources available in their classroom, school, or home. At the same time, their projects embodied the  insights and desires of their families as they sought to eng ineer in ways that made their lives m atter. For instance, one group created a solar-powered, illuminated "Oxygen Factory" to promote plant growth and enhance CO₂ absorption.

The ‘Filter Robot’ project was designed to cleanse the air and foster a healthier environment. The ‘Basketball Bin’ promoted recycling in an engaging way, activating a light-up score counter when recycled paper was successfully tossed into the bin. They explained: “The students said they wanted to be more fun, positive and entertaining. Parents said they want to be a cleane r, more sustainable community.  So we put those together, and made it more fun, positive. We made it to reality.” Another group created a "Ramadan Board" to make lun ch breaks more engaging for fasting students by teaching them about Ramadan and its values in an en joyable way. During Ramadan, Muslim students fast f rom sunrise to sunset, and families emphasize learning about empathy for the less fortunate and charity. T he students who designed the project connected it to their reli gious and cultural needs. Basma shared, “It fits ou r religious needs […] and makes our community more sustainable.” The board featured a quiz in Arabic and English, mosque model, speaker, and LEDs that light up for correct answers. The students noted how recess became long and boring during Ramadan since they don't spend time in the cafeteria eating. Hanady explained, “We want to teach the meani ng of fasting during Ramadan and make it fun.” With  Arabic writing for newcomers and voice recordings for young kids, the project highlighted energy transformations, sustainability, and cultural sensitivity, blending education with enjoyment.

As students assumed engineering roles and designed solutions, they addressed technical needs while considering their community’s well-being. Amal shared: “As an engineer, I learned that  engineering is not just about doing something; it's about improving it  and making it better so humanity and customers can have a better advantage of the things.” Asad showcased how personal engineering skills can enhance projects, highlighting his role as a team member and contributor to community-focused problem-solving. I've seen my brother sketch ideas before fixing car s, and he applies the same approach in his work. When Mrs. Hened allowed us to study on School ogy and provided resources about FamJam, I started learning how to sketch ideas for our project. As a team, we discussed the problem and how to address it [...] we thought this would be a fun and effective way to help the community.

Authoring possible selves and practices in STEM learning Mothers and the teacher challenged traditional view s of parental engagement, emphasizing their vital r ole in STEM learning. One mother shared, “Because I come t o school [for FamJam], people think my daughter has problems,” reframing her involvement as an opportunity to co-learn and co-design, legitimizing lived experiences as learning resources. Ms. Hened echoed this, notin g, “District-wide, parents are always the first to blame when kids aren't doing well,” but emphasized parents’ “u nseen contributions” to  their children’s success. She highlighted the need for better structures to suppo rt meaningful involvement and shared her positive e xperience integrating familial knowledge into her lessons.

We could still look back at these pictures [photos mothers shared] and be inspired and say, Wow, look at this, I would never have thought to take a picture like that, like I'm referring back to this constantly throughout the day […] I feel like having the parents involved in this way was really great. It was positive. They were comfortabl e. They wer e not intimidated. They knew what they had to do. And the kids were happy. And i t was win-win it was, so the class was so engaged Ms. Hened found co-designing with community members to be powerful pro fessional development for herself and redefined her role to help students understand the importance of caring for their community. She said: “I feel like I have a mission that I need to open my students eyes up to understand that, like, we need to care about this place.” Another mother, Wasela, mentioned how parental involvement exposed her child to a variety  of learning experiences, stating, “They were able to experience  new projects, ne w learning, new education, everything. And us being here, they feel more excited and happier to learn.” Mothers and the teacher served as supporters and ad visors, offering feedback to refine student designs. One student, involved in creating the Basketball Bin project to promote recycling in an engaging way, shared how his mother's feedback helped him explain how to use  the bin as a game. He said: “My mom said it was a smart idea [...] and suggested adding a sign for the r ules.” Ms. Hened highlighted parental encouragement, noting, “For example, Anees, he’s usually not engaged in cl ass, so when I saw him with his mom, how he was wor king with his circuit. And I also was like, really impressed with him.” As the project progressed, students developed posit ive connections with science, valuing its role in helping their community and gaining agency as contributors. One student reflected, “it was hard and I barely knew anything about science. But after the Fam jam came, I started knowing a lot about science aft er liking it, and I learned that it is creative and entertaining.” Another student remarked “this [project design] shows we are creative, we improvise, and we actually care about our community”

## Conclusions and implications

This study contributes to understanding the identit ies-in-practice and evolving dynamics of immigrant parent-teacher engagement in fostering Rightful Parental P resence in STEM learning. Our findings suggest the importance of recognizing, supporting, and leveragi ng new relationships among identities-in-practice to expand opportunities for parent engagement. Mothers were p resented with unique spaces to author new identitie s-inpractice, imbuing them with additional status and empowering them to actively contribute to the design and enact STEM learning. This transition moved them from peri pheral participants to positions closer to “master practitioners” in community-of-practice (Lave and Wenger 1991). By elevating paren tal presence, novel and empowering various identities-in-practice were authored, enabling students and teach ers to become “insiders” in science practice for sustainable communities. Such practices legitimize participants in the various figured worlds of science class and advance justice in science learning.
