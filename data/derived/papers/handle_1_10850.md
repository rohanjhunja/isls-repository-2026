# Re-Mapping Possibilities for Educational Justice through Arts-Based Research Methods: A Cross-Field Poster Session

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This session aims to curate a collection of posters by cross-field scholars who integrate arts-based methods for equity-oriented education research in and beyond the learning sciences. Historically, education research has perpetuated colonial approaches to knowledge production that have harmed non-dominant communities under the guises of “rigor” and “objectivity”. This session will provide attendees with concrete examples of how scholar-artists are using arts-based research methods to 1) unsettle what it is to “know” in education research from diverse locations in the field and 2) pursue humanizing directions alongside historically-marginalized communities. Given educational research’s complicity in perpetrating harm against nondominant communities, this session aims to provide a space at ISLS for attendees to imagine alongside presenters how arts-based methods can be mobilized for justice.

## Significance

With governments stripping the rights of historical ly-marginalized communities and environmental destruct ion continuing to rage on globally, education research must address the shadows that issues of futurity ha ve cast on the present while also creating sy stems that imagine more humanizing futures. While r esearch is understood as the pursuit of knowledge, education research has pl ayed a significant role in perpetuating colonial re lationships among people, practices, and land (Patel, 2016). Co nsidering positivism is the dominant paradigm shapi ng knowledge construction as a supposedly neutral, value-free process (Collins, 2006), “education is a culturally and socially constructed institution for an imagined co ntext with purposes defined by those who are privil eged to be the deciders, and their work has not always been fo r the benefit of the masses [understatement]” (Batt iste, 2013, p. 159). We bring together justice-oriented scholars in and beyond the learning scien ces working with nondominant communities across unique disciplinary/field-based contexts in order to excavate the ways learning contexts maintain epistemic injustice by excluding,  silencing, or distorting who counts as knowers and  what counts as knowledge.

Our session foregrounds scholars who employ arts-based methods in their educational research. Arts-based research methods center diverse modalities in  inquiry processes (e.g., drawing, painting, dance,  film, poetry), thereby centering “affective experience s, senses, and bodies, and imagination and emotion as well as intellect” in knowledge-making (Finley, 2008, p.72). By inviting expansive ways of knowing and being into processes of knowledge production, arts-based methods have provided pathways for histori cally-minoritized communities to make their perspectives known in research (Bhattacharya & Payne, 2016). Even as scholars have begun to theorize the liberatory potentials of arts-based research methods in education and in the learning sciences more broadly (Cahnmann-Taylor & Siegesmund, 2017), our session aims to curate a space at ISLS 2024 in which scholars situated in diverse areas across the education field—and oriented, accordingly, towards diverse standards and conventions of knowledge production—discuss how they mobilize arts-based methods for equity. It will also advance the aims of ISLS by inviting attendees to contemplate how arts-based methods can be mobilized to dismantle colonial, raci st, epistemic, and many-dimensional hierarchies, pursue social justice in the field, and offer routes toward methods that might contribute toward collective healing of historical injustices. Publishing and production as student empowerment: Facilitating knowledge through community sites of cultural transmission Kalonji Nzinga, Marlene Palomar, Shawn O’Neal and Alexander Williams Research into wellness outcomes for youth of color shows that minoritized youth face systemic inequiti es in accessing care and establishing wellness routines. This same research, however, often views minoritize d youth and their communities from a deficit lens. Deficit models attribute racial and socioeconomic disparities in mental health outcomes to a culture of poverty (Moynihan, 1965)–the notion that minoritized communities lack the cultural proclivities to adequately prioritize and pursue wellness. This leads to the implementation o f invalid assessments based around Eurocentric standards of w ellness, often failing to measure what youth of col or know about mental and socioemotional well-being.

This study is a hip-hop design-based research study built around an asset-based approach to wellness learning, where we construe ontology as knowledge and culture as the transmission of this ontological knowledge. We draw on Spencer’s (1997) PVEST model, which invi tes researchers aiming for resilient youth outcomes  to identify the social, cultural and institutional protective factors that have evolved within the community, as well as adaptive resources that enable trajectories of wellness. We investigate how learning environments, rooted in hiphop based education and critical literacies (Freire 1990), can facilitate opportunities for Black & Brown youth to learn (and share) narratives of healing that are en dogenous to their cultural communities. We trace h ow the learning, re-writing, internalizing and revoicing of culturally-rooted wellness narratives (in a hip-hop format) is related to the development of wellness outcomes for  low-income urban students. The intervention focuses on students from Latinx, Indigenous, AAPI and Black co mmunities impacted by historical forms of cultural exclusion from a major, mountain western city.

Within this study, youth gain expertise in one of the most prominent storytelling genres in contemporary language arts: hip-hop. Instructors introduce the history and conventions of the artform, including lectures on the 5 elements of hip-hop (knowledge, graffiti, emceeing, djing, and brea kdancing). Youth learn the conventions of hip-hop storytelling, and then use those skills to re-write the wellness narratives of their traditions in a genre that is relevant to global youth culture. Research on hip-hop pedagogy (Stovall 2006, Alim 2007, Petchauer 2009) has established how this genre acts as an intellectuall y rich set of practices in which to frame multi-disciplinary learning. Since 2021, this study has conducted mult iple interventions to collect qualitative  and quantitative data on these arts-based inquiries and methodologies. We utilized multimodal discourse analysis of the artistic projects in this anthology and found that youth participants  construed wellness in various culturally endogenou s ways including: (1) a reconnection to the soul, (2) representati on of their intersecting identities including race and immigration status, (3) the importance to take care of the self, (4) art as joy and resistance, (5) the journey of their ancestors, (6) connectedness to community despite gentrification, (7) family recipes used for generations, and (8) cross cultural movements against gendered violence.  We explore how these themes can be utilized as des ign principles to assess knowledge that BIPOC youth hav e of endogeno us wellness practices and tensions between these wellness practices and the deficient lens applied to their respective communities. Beyond objects: Making, self-making and worldmaking as a transdisciplinary artistic sensibility Melita Morales and Teyona James-Harris There are many presumptions about what we mean when we say the arts. These ideas often permeate educational projects that adopt the arts in ways that reinforce  academic arts as the starting point for meaningful  encounters with materials. This study challenges the essentialist notion that the arts are always anti-oppressive, anti-racist, or bring about healing and expressive opportunities. R ather, we focus on the careful design for transdisc iplinary activity, rooted in co-designed axiological principles that create the conditions for approaching arts activity as an opportunity for meaning making and world making. We imagine alongside Camnitzer (2020), “that first work of art did not respond to a need to make an art object, but was the application of a method for cognition …the consequence of an attitude” (p. 13). Through this s tudy we demonstrate how student making  crossed boundaries of arts production to encompass a sensibility, or a n attitude, for making sense of ideas and bringing oneself into being across domains.

Scholarship has long highlighted the need to move a way from disciplinary canons toward expansive, equity-oriented disciplinarity (Bang et al, 2018; Gaztabid e-Fernandez, et al., 2018; Warren et al., 2022; Gordo n, 2011; Wynter, 2003). What constitutes "the a rts" has received thoughtful, critical attention in  the last decade, including efforts to account for the political and ethical dimension of how and for whom knowledge is produced within and across disciplines processes and product ion boundaries (Barajas -López & Bang, 2018; Castellano, 2019; Gaztabide-Fernandez, 2014; Vossoughi et al., 2016; Camnitzer,  2020). We take up the arts as an essential element of a critical transdisciplinary that suppor ts a re-sensitizing to the world, a method of returning to the senses as cognition (Author, 2022).

Data for this study is drawn from a larger particip atory design research project, now in its seventh y ear, between a midwestern university and community-based STEAM organization serving Black, Latinx and Asian American youth (Erickson, 2012; Bang & Vossoughi, 2 016). Our corpus of data includes 500 pages of mult ivoiced fieldnotes, audio-video recordings, debriefs, and student interviews and artifacts. Using ethnographic methods we analyze macro aspects of program design and micro-interactional moments to ground larger ideas in the concrete and everyday (Vossoughi et al., 2021; McDermott & Raley, 2012; Sharpe, 2019). Tracing the discourse and iterative development of students’ work, we share how activity design, pedagogy, and interactions weave together to play foundational roles in what is made possible to be made. Sharing images, written text, transcripts of conversations, and presentations, we connect dots between often-severed forms of thinking and being. We demonstrate how individua l and collective thinking-through-making across domains demonstrated students’ ethical orientations and age ncy to reframe purposeful disciplinary learning, cu ltivating a sensibility toward making and worldmaking.

Given the disciplinary silos present in most educat ional environments, there are few opportunities to learn about how students make meaning across domain  areas, particularly how topics meaningfully emerge  and are understood. This poster offers the field connec tions between key moments of activity design and ed ucator facilitation that support 1) disciplinary porosity through multiple forms of exploration and expressio n, and 2) political and ethical dimensions of learning and artistic expression. Exploring arts-based approaches for critical participatory co-analysis Melissa (Mez) Perez, Day Greenberg, Angela Calabrese Barton, Aliyah Williams and Youth Researchers The future is not a stagnant thing to be discovered; it is something that is created as people act, think, believe, and come together in moments, over several intersecting  presents (Perez et al, 2023). Youths’ roles in imagining and bringing about more just futures cannot be understated. They are often at the forefront of global social movements, and are active in their local communities, making a  real difference in the lives of the people around them (Uttamchandani, 2021).

Through our work, we aim to challenge the notion that there is an age-restriction to bringing about more just futures. We do this through our discussion of how youth participate in research via artistic practices. We use the framing of Critical Participato ry Co-analysis (Greenberg, 2019), an orientation to youth-engaged research wherein the research process is designed to be resp onsive to youths’ interests as well as being develo pmentally appropriate. We specifically focus on how youths’ drawings impact the analysis of data.

## Arts-based data analysis and critical participatory co-analysis (CPC)

Art making can be a powerful meaning-making and signifying process (Furman, 2022; Cahnmann-Taylor & Siegesmund, 2017), and especially so when engaged by youth from marginalized backgrounds. Thus, our contribution builds primarily on the work done arou nd arts-based methods for analysis, such as collage and theater. Both focus on the co-construction of meaning using multiple modalities, often that transcend language through embodiment or creation (Gerstenblatt, 2013;  Greenwood, 2019). We extend this idea through the use of drawing, which had been an existing practice of the youth who were involved in the research. Through Critical Participatory Co-Analysis (CPC), youth researchers co-analyze research data about them, which is often challenging given the rigid st ructures typically used for data analysis (Cammarota, & Fine, 2008). Arts-based approaches to CPC support rigorous data analy sis in ways that are meaningful and developmentally appropriate for youth.

## Methods

The analysis sessions took place on Zoom over 5, 1. 5 hour sessions during the summer of 2023. The 4 yo uth researchers, aged 12-16, had participated in an after-school STEM-rich makerspace (the Green Club) during and beyond the 2020-21 school year. During zoom sessions, participants drew/wrote a response to a prompt pre-written by Author 1. Then, we engaged in an artifac t-mediated discussion that led to identifying key mom ents remembered from 2020-21 programming, which adult researchers supplemented with video data. What we analyzed: Green Club during COVID-19 Youth and adult partners discussed youths’ experien ces during the height of social distancing in the p andemic, with special attention to how they experienced the Green Club during this time. We offer three dimensi ons in which arts-based data analysis supports CPC: memories, identif ication of key moments, and synthesis. These three dimensions enhanced data co-viewing and cross-case analysis across the team. Firstly, drawing sup ports remembering key moments and narrating them from the youths’ poi nt of view. Secondly, these memories  point to key moments, some of which the adults had also highlighted. In this poster, I offer a Queer Blackgirl Autoethnographic analysis of making prompted by the theme of “Black Life and Technology.” Blackgirl autoethnography (Boylorn, 2016) suggests that Blackgirls through self-construction can make their own homes, removed from the circumstances of racism, classism, and misogyny. I queer Blackgirl autoethnography to consider how Blackgirls make gender, so that they can be more at home with themselves. This requires looking at Blackgirls in a genderexpansive  way. This analysis considers how those who identif y with Blackgirlhood, and may now be Black Women, non-binary, or trans, construct home for them selves. Home for Blackgirls is “A place where we can be our full-bodied selves and not be under surveillance. Home is a place we create with our own hands and experience when nobody is looking.” (Boylorn, 2016, pg. 45). Over the course of 6 hours, I video recorded myself making and talking about the process and took field notes. During the session, I used reflective acryli c and transparent acrylic to develop an underwater motif that responds to the question, where on earth is life for Black Folks? Next, I transcribed the video recording and began to code for themes of Blackgirlhood and home space-making. Emergent open codes refer to themes such as  a) home space-making, b) making / crafting, c) Black Life, d) gen der, and e) creation. These codes and excerpts were then used to create analytic memos (Saldaña, 2021) around these emergent themes. Initial noticings include that the location depicted is often one void of technology and within an environment that makes it impossible for human life to breathe. I explore articulations of the siren, Mami Wata, and momentarily enslaved folks who chose death in the sea over a lifetime of enslavement. From the analysis, I am constructing a series of narratives, poems, and prose that inform how one candocument the connections between Black Life and Technology as well as make art about the topic.
