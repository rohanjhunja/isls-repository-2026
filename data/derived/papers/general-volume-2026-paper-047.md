# Empowering Learner Agency in Writing with GenAI: A Web-based Platform

**Conference:** ISLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Recent advances in Generative AI (GenAI) have led to increasing attention in how chatbots and agentic tools may support students’ self-regulated learning (SRL). However, existing works primarily focus on short-term interventions on adult learners, offering limited insights into how GenAI can meaningfully foster SRL and learner agency in K-12 education. To address this gap, we introduce GAILA, a writing platform that integrates stage-specific AI agents with real-time learning analytics dashboards (LADs) to support the forethought, performance, and reflection phases of SRL. For students, GAILA offers structured writing workflows, task-aligned AI agents, and real-time analytics to encourage informed self-monitoring and behavioral adjustments. For teachers, GAILA provides fine-grained configurations of writing stages and agent visibility for pedagogical control. Together, these features enable sustained, classroom-integrated use of GenAI tools and open opportunities for research on how GenAI can support SRL among secondary school students.

Learner agency is “the power to originate action” (Bandura, 2001, p. 3). It is essential to learning because it drives motivation, autonomous actions, and self-directed growth, enabling learners to take initiative, persevere through challenges, and adapt effectively. Generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) may both empower and hinder learner agency. On the one hand, it expands learning opportunities and provides ongoing support that fosters proactive and agentic learning behaviors (e.g., Ng & Lai, 2025). On the other hand, it may induce the automatization of learning, irresponsible offloading of cognitive work, and metacognitive laziness, which negatively impact learner agency (e.g., Darvishi et al., 2024). Given the mixed blessings, it is important to conceptualize and create learning experiences that could harness the affordances of GenAI while circumventing its potential haphazards for learner agency.

## Related work

Agency is the will and capacity to act that changes the course of actions (Giddens, 1984). It involves creating action plans and executing the plans with motivation and regulation (Bandura, 2001). Thus, agency is fundamentally intertwined with self-regulated learning, which emphasizes engaging learners in proactively managing their cognitive states in a circular, regulatory manner (Zimmerman, 2000). Agency and self-regulated learning are mutually reinforcing, where agency fuels self-regulation and self-regulation serves as the practical mechanism through which agency is enacted. Thus, to empower learner agency in GenAI-mediated learning experience, we resort to self-regulation as a potential solution. There is an increase in research attention to the potential of supporting the self-regulation processes with GenAI agents, especially for English as Foreign Language (EFL) tasks in undergraduate and graduate studies (e.g. Han et al, 2023; Xiao & Zhi, 2023). However, as Wu and colleagues (2025) commented in their analysis, there is limited attention from recent research on secondary education settings. Moreover, recent literature lacks longterm implementations that allow deeper understanding of the impacts and sustainability of chatbot-facilitated SRL.

Learning analytics play an essential role in augmenting the relationship of learner agency and selfregulation, as learning analytics dashboards (LADs) visualize progress and strategies, fostering self-efficacy and adaptive regulation. Researchers have incorporated LADs into GenAI-mediated learning platforms. For example, both Kim and colleagues (2024) and Li and colleagues (2025) generated real-time feedback with ChatGPT on an essay writing system. However, most of these studies visualize student-initiated behaviors reactively, lacking prescriptive analytics that may serve as interventions to motivate behavioral changes (Susnjak et al, 2022; Koh & Hu, 2023). As such, only a few studies have demonstrated meaningful effects of LADs on SRL (Kaliisa et al, 2024).

G AILA system design and theoretical backgrounds In light of the research gap, we introduce GAILA, an acronym of “GenAI and Learning Analytics.” The platform aims to use self-regulation and prescriptive LADs to empower learner agency when interacting with GenAI for writing. It provides distinct interfaces and functionalities, fueled by multi-agent, tailored for both students and educators, supporting a structured, multi-stage assignment flow. It is designed for secondary level writing that allows longer-term investigation of student behaviors across writing stages. Students’ interface Following the three-phase self-regulatory cycle, including the forethought phase, the performance phase and the self-reflection phase (Zimmerman, 2002), we propose a three-stage workflow respectively for each writing assignment, including Goal Setting, Essay Writing, and Reflection. In the Goal Setting stage, students are prompted to set both the goal for writing and that for their interaction with AI. For each goal, they are prompted to set the respective tackling strategies. A goal-setting AI agent is made available to support students in setting Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, and Time-bound (SMART) goals and plan relevant strategies. The goals and strategies will remain visible throughout the writing process, and students are reminded to mark strategies as completed before the final essay submission. In the Essay Writing stage, the writing process is further divided into three sub-stages: outlining, drafting and revising. At each sub-stage, we provide different AI agents to aid students in their writing process. Each agent receives a different prompt-based configuration to ensure their output alignment. Learning analytics dashboard (LAD) To further support students’ self-monitoring process and teachers’ assessment, we provide LADs to students and teachers, which captures and visualizes process-level data in real-time. A sample interface of the LAD is shown in Figure 2. The analytics are divided into two categories: writing performance and AI Interaction. For writing performance, other than word count and goal progress, we adopt the complexity and accuracy metrics from the Complexity, Accuracy and Fluency (CAF) components and follow the measurement implementations of Hwang (2025). The measurements include: ● Lexical Complexity: measured with Moving Average Type-Token Ratio (MATTR; Hwang, 2025; Covington & McFall, 2010) ● Syntactic Complexity: measured as log 10 [type frequency of constructions + 1] (Hwang & Kim, 2023) ● Lexical / Syntactic Accuracy: measured as # of lexical or syntactic error-free T-units / total # of T-units (Larsen-Freeman, 2006; Polat and Kim, 2014; Park’s, 2014) Figure 2 Student’s Interface of the Learning Analytics Dashboard For AI Interaction analytics, we include 1) AI usage chart, 2) ChatGPT copying detector, and 3) Prompt Category charts. In the AI usage chart, the agent usage count and follow-up prompt counts are displayed in a bar chart format to promote balanced agent usage and encourage follow-up prompts. In the ChatGPT copying detector, passages detected to be from chatbot output are highlighted. To avoid false detection, we ignore overlapping segments of 30 characters or fewer, approximately 5 English words. We then calculate the percentage in length of suspected segments to the whole essay. This aims to discourage overreliance on AI output (Gu & Yan 2025; Li, 2025) and support feedback discussions between teachers and students on responsible AI use. In the prompt category charts, the GenAI prompts recorded from students were classified into a coding scheme illustrating key writing processes. With insights from Valtonen et al (2025) and Heil et al (2025), students' prompts were coded into two nature categories based on goal orientations (Table 2). The prompt category distribution of a student is displayed next to the class distribution to highlight their difference and promote a balance in categories. All dashboard analytics are visible to students in both the Essay Writing stage and Reflection stage. Additionally, if students are not on their first assignment, previous analytics will be shown at the Goal Setting stage to allow strategies on constant improvement and regulation. Teachers can create and modify assignments with detailed configurations on writing stages, including enabling or disabling the goal setting stage or reflection stage, as well as selecting the AI agents and chatbots available for each stage. They are also in charge of setting the assignment description and rubrics to guide both students and AI autograde agents. A chatbot is provided in the interface to aid them in designing the assignment rubrics.

As students work on their assignment and save their drafts to the system, teachers can see their draft content to monitor their progress. They can also check dashboard analytics and remind students with abnormal behaviors through the notification feature in the system. Examples of abnormal behaviors include high AI copying percentage and extended durations of not viewing the dashboard or using any AI tools. Notifications will be shown on the student's homepage when they login. After students submit their final draft, teachers are in charge of grading and providing feedback to the students. An AI agent is provided to autograde the essay. Teachers can review and edit the autograde and feedback contents before sending them to students. An example of the teacher’s assignment interface is shown in Figure 3. The LAD allows teachers to pinpoint the needs from individual students and the whole classes so that they could follow up in class to support students’ writing and agency with GenAI. Figure 3 Teacher’s Assignment Interface with Grading Features (Left) and Student’s Analytics (Right) Technical implementation The GAILA platform is designed for desktop web usage only. JavaScript and the React framework are used for frontend development. Assignment configurations, submissions and gradings, and trace logs for analytics are stored in a MySQL database with a Node.js server handling API and database read/write requests from the frontend. An additional Python service is implemented to integrate a large language model from OpenAI for AI agents and handle structured outputs for agentic tools (dictionary, autograde, revision). The model used for this setup is gpt-5.1, 2025-11-13 version.

Interactive demo In the 2026 ISLS conference, we will provide a functional prototype of the GAILA platform and a trial student account to experience the writing process from the student’s perspective. We will provide brief descriptions on the workflow of an assignment. Participants can browse the tools and agents available to students in writing and those for teachers as well.
