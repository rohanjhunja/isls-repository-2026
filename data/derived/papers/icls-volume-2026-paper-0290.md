# Mapping Mathematical Intent: Investigating Conceptual Conflicts in Integrated Math and Computational Thinking Lessons

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study investigates how second-grade students build connections between abstract mathematical concepts and concrete actions through the use of robot coding toys to support multiplicative reasoning. Drawing on the Onto-Semiotic Approach, we analyzed video data from three integrated mathematics and computational thinking lessons to identify semiotic conflicts, with a focus on conceptual conflicts. Our findings reveal that these conflicts emerged from four distinct variations: chunking, two loops in a row, usefulness of the last loop, and not locked in loop. These results underscore the critical role of the design of coding toys and offer practical implications for educators. Specifically, the study provides insights into how teachers can anticipate and mitigate these conflicts by being aware of their nature. This awareness can help facilitate students’ ability to connect abstract mathematical ideas, computational code, and concrete actions more effectively.

Robot coding toys are widely used to integrate computational thinking (CT) into elementary math classrooms (Clarke-Midura et al., 2024). While these tools offer a spatial and dynamic way to learn math (Shumway et al., 2023a), they also introduce a new layer of symbolic representation that young students must navigate (Shumway et al., 2023b). For example, a child may understand the mathematical concept of iteration yet struggle to program a loop command using a robot’s specific syntax. A central challenge arises in understanding how children translate their mathematical knowledge into the procedural rules required by these new tools. This paper uses the onto-semiotic approach (OSA) to frame these moments of misalignment (Godino, et al., 2007). Specifically, we focus on the conceptual conflicts that arise from this disconnect between a student's mathematical understanding and their ability to represent it using the robot's syntax rules. We argue that these conceptual conflicts are not mathematical misconceptions, but rather productive struggles in semiotic translation. They are valuable moments that reveal the nuanced ways students negot iate meaning between a known mathematical idea and the specific symbolic system required to enact it. To this end, this work is guided by the following question: what are the conceptual conflicts that emerge when triads of students use a tangible coding robot to solve math tasks on multiplicative reasoning?

Theoretical framing To analyze the conflicts that arise during these student-robot interactions, we employ the Onto-Semiotic Approach (OSA), a framework that views mathematics as a human activity involving practices, objects, and the signs used to communicate them (Godino et al., 2007). A central tenet of OSA is the distinction between personal  meaning (an individual’s unique understanding) and institutional meaning (the shared, accepted meaning within a context like a classroom) (Godino et al., 2009). In our context, the institutional meaning includes not only the mathematical concept (e.g., iteration) but also the expected practices for representing that concept using the robot's specific syntax (e.g., for loops and forward movement). Friction between personal and institutional meanings is what OSA terms a semiotic conflict, defined as “any disparity or difference of interpretation between the meanings ascribed to an expression by two subjects” (Godino et al., 2007, p. 133). In our research, these conflicts emerge from the challenge of semiotic translation: a student holds a personal meaning for a mathematical concept but struggles to represent it using the robot’s syntax. The conflict is not a misunderstanding of the mathematical idea, but a friction between the student's intended meaning and the procedural interpretation demanded by the tool. For this paper, we narrow our analysis to the specific conceptual conflicts that relate to how a mathematical concept must be mapped onto the robot's set of signs and procedures.

## Materials and methods

This study explores the integration of mathematics and CT in first and second-grade classrooms as part of a larger collaboration between university researchers and a five-teacher co-design team. We focus on the project's initial phase, where one teacher, Dr. J, piloted three lessons connecting multiplicative reasoning with loops. The lessons used Botley 2.0, a toy robot that students program with a handheld remote using symbolic commands. While most commands, like Forward (↑), are straightforward, the Loop (↻) function is more complex. A sequence of commands placed between two Loop presses defines a repeatable "unit." The act of closing this unit (with the second Loop press) also executes it once. To make the unit repeat, the user presses the Loop button again immediately after closing it; each additional press executes the unit one more time. For instance, the program Loop, Forward, Loop, Loop causes the robot to move forward twice: once for defining and closing the unit (Loop, Forward, Loop), and a second time for the final Loop press. Multiplier Rule   Each consecutive loop command placed immediately after a closing loop acts as a multiplier, adding one more repetition to that unit. Integrated math and CT lessons with Botley 2.0 The three lessons were designed to engage students in multiplicative reasoning by using Botley to iterate different combinations of 8 steps using only two codes: forward and loop. Students used Botley in previous math lessons. However, they only used Forward, Backwards, Rotate Right, and Rotate Left however, they had not used the loop code. In the first two lessons, students worked in groups to identify all the different ways they could make Botley iterate 8 movements. In the third lesson, students analyzed pre-created programs, showing the math and te sting their solution. This lesson was designed to help students navigate more precise and accurate interpretations of the Loop code, understanding it means different things depending on where it is in the program.

## Sample, data, and data analysis

The setting was a second-grade classroom in a rural school in the Western United States. All students (n=24) provided consent. Dr. J assigned students into groups of three or four. Majority of the time was spent working in groups interwoven with whole group discussion and sharing innovative solutions. Three groups of students (n=9) were randomly selected as focal groups and were videorecorded over the three class sessions. Data included ~5 hours of video, field notes, and student worksheets  completed over the three lessons. Analysis was a multi-step process of multi-modal interaction analysis (Jordan & Henderson, 1995; Goodwin, 2018), that involved iterative co-watching videos and annotating transcripts of the lessons  while attending to language modes (i.e., verbiage, inscriptions, gestures, etc.). First, we identified episodes of  semiotic conflicts (Godino et al. 20 07), specifically, moments where a student's verbal count, gesture, or written program revealed an interpretation of a loop command that differed from Botley's syntax. Then, we coded for mismatch between the math and how it was represented in the program. This was an iterative process where the codes were refined and collapsed (see Table 2). This study analyzed the conceptual conflicts students experienced while using a robot coding toy to explore multiplicative thinking. Framed by OSA (Godino et al., 2007), our analysis identified four key conflicts: Chunking, Two Loops in a Row, Usefulness of the Last Loop, and Not 'locked in,’ which we argue are productive struggles in semiotic translation. These conflicts originated from a disconnect between the students' personal mathematical meanings and the institutional meaning embedded in the robot's programming syntax. For example, conflicts like "Chunking" revealed that students understood the mathematical concept of creating distinct repeated units but struggled to express this using the robot's specific syntax, where a single command serves multiple functions depending on its position. These struggles appeared to be productive, and their frequency appeared to decrease across sessions, a pattern warranting further investigation.

Our findings have direct implications for both educational practice and the design of coding toys. For educators, understanding these specific conceptual conflicts can help in designing better support for students during open-ended exploration of loops, a known challenge in early education (Grover & Basu, 2017). The initial frustration experienced by students suggests that while open-ended play is valuable, the non-intuitive nature of the robot's syntax may benefit from  more targeted guidance. For designers, this study raises questions about  the need for developmentally appropriate input methods that prioritize a clear and distinct syntax for initiating, iterating, and completing a loop. The ambiguity of using one button for multiple functions proved confusing for seven-and eight-year-old students. The institutional meaning of educational tools should be designed to match the intuitive mathematical logic the intended student users already possess. Finally, this study also offers a novel extension to the OSA (Godino et al., 2007). In this context, the institutional meaning was enforced by the robot through its immediate feedback on the interpretation of the symbolic system. This creates a unique cont ext for studying semiotic conflicts, revealing the friction that occurs directly between a student's personal meaning and the procedural interpretation demanded by the coding toy.

## Discussion & Implications

In conclusion, this paper reframes common student errors with coding robots as valuable moments of negotiation. By identifying the specific conceptual conflicts tied to a tool's syntax, we provide a more precise way for researchers and teachers to understand how students translate mathematical ideas intonew symbolic systems.
