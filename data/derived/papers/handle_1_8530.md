# Towards Automated Analysis of Undergraduate Academic Writing Using Metadiscourse, Cognitive Level and Word Network

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Automated assessment of students’ academic writing can provide timely feedback and alleviate teachers’ workload. This study examined 199 undergraduate students’ essays through metrics of metadiscourse, cognitive levels, and word network, and the relationships between these metrics and performances. Metrics were calculated under the framework of Hyland’s metadiscourse model and Bloom Taxonomy whereas relationships were revealed by correlation analysis and classification with multiple algorithms. Findings show that students employed more interactive metadiscourse markers than interactional ones, and there were weak to mild positive correlations between some of these metrics and performance score. Highperforming essays involved more higher-order thinking and stronger connections among words. Besides, classification models combining all three types of metrics were most effective in differentiating high and low-performing essays. The three types of metrics evaluated can be potentially used to provide fine-grained suggestions for improving students’ academic writing.

## Introduction

Students’ performance in academic writing is a dete rminant of their academic performance, degree attai nment, and even an important graduate attribute. Academic writing instruction is therefore an essential component in higher education. In the field of learning analytic s, automated assessment of student writing has attr acted much attention. Besides marking, automated means can also identify and compare textual features and writing strategies in high and low performance writing. These could be  leveraged by writing analytic tools for providing timely, actionable feedback at scale to large classes of students (Gibson et al., 2017), and alleviating teachers’ workload (Hu et al., 2016). Towards automating analysis of s tudents’ academic writing, this preliminary study a ims to examine student essays from three linguistic and ex tra-linguistic aspects of writing: metadiscourse us age, cognitive levels, and word network. Specifically, we pose four research questions (RQs): RQ1: What is the relationship between metadiscourse usage and performance scores of student essays? RQ2: What is the relationship between cognitive levels and performance scores of student essays? RQ3: What is the relationship between word network metrics and performance scores of student essays? RQ4: To what extent can metadiscourse usage, cognitive level, and word network metrics predict high and l ow performances of student essays?

Results will reveal whether and how metrics of the three linguistic and extra-linguistic aspects of writing are related to writing performance and offer insights on generating automated feedback for academic writing. Metadiscourse As a universal feature of academic writing, metadiscourse represents textual devices for organizing text, engaging readers, and signaling writers’ attitudes (Hyland & Tse, 2004). Unlike content words, metadiscourse markers do not present the topic, but guide readers through th e text and help them interpret the content (Hyland,  2017). Hyland (2017) referenced the characteristics of previous metadiscourse models and proposed the interactive and interactional models. The former consists of featur es leading readers through the text by coherently o rganizing information, whereas the latter involves argumentation and expressing epistemic stance and attitudes to readers. Prior studies have analyzed metadiscourse usage in student writing, reporting that it facilitates writer-reader interaction and improves academic writing performance (Abdelrahim & Abdelrahim, 2020; Hu et al., 2016). Cognitive Level Academic writing entails different forms of writing targets, ranging from description to evaluation of information, which can reflect the writer’s varying cognitive levels (Qiao & Hu, 2019), and hence their thinking skills (Palmer & Lomer, 2016). Based on Bloom’s Taxonomy (Krathwohl, 2002), there are six cognitive domains of learning: knowledge (lowest), comprehension, application, ana lysis, synthesis, and evaluation (highest). As writ ing is a primary way of externalizing, evaluating and promot ing student thinking, these cognitive domains have been proposed for assessing quality of undergraduates’ w riting (Penner, 2013). At a finer granularity, Qiao  and Hu ICLS2022 Proceedings 1525 © ISLS

(2019) leveraged Bloom’s Taxonomy to classify students’ analytical writing into three cognitive levels based on

semantic, syntactic, and lexical features. There is  nonetheless a lack of studies examining the relati onships between these cognitive levels and students’ academic writing performances. Word Network In academic writing, some words are more likely to appear together (e.g., ‘data” and “analysis”), esta blishing patterns throughout a text (Hong & Cao, 2014) and f orming epistemic networks (Fougt et al., 2018). Epi stemic Network Analysis (ENA) is a method for the identification and quantification of ties between elements in coded data and their representation in dynamic network models. It allows quantifying the relations between elements of the epistemic frame (Shaffer & Graesser, 2010). ENA  has been used in analyzing student writing and vis ually differentiating their performance levels (Fougt et al., 2018). In this study, instead of manually codi ng student writing, we used word co-occurrences to form a word network (Oshima, Oshiima & Matsuzawa, 2012) for each essay and calculated the network metrics (e.g., density) for comparison across essays.

## Research Context and Methods

The student essays were collected from an undergraduate general education course with students from different majors in a comprehensive university in Hong Kong w here English is the medium of instruction. The cour se is on the topic of cultural heritage and digital technologies while the essays were submissions of an assignment on pros and cons of digitizing cultural heritage. As a  typical academic writing task, this assignment req uired the student-writers to put forward relevant arguments and support them with evidence, experience and/or literature. Students were suggested to keep the essays around 2000 words long. There were 199 essays (mean length = 2228.5 words) collected from two deliveries of the same course in the Fall semesters of 2018 and 2019. The course teacher assessed the essays from the aspects of addressing the topic, intellectual engagement with concepts and examples, critical thinking, and organization and language. The scores of the essays ranged from 25 to 100 (out of 100), with a mean of 75.3. The median of scores (75) was used to separate the essays into high and low performing groups. Those scoring higher than the median were regarded as high performing while those below were regarded as low performing ones. For RQ1, we used the Hyland’s (2007) metadiscourse  framework (Table 1) which is both comprehensive and concise. It covers ten categories of metadiscourse markers with five under interactive and interactional models respectively. A web-based tool, Text Inspector, was  used to detect metadiscourse markers based on this framework, and the frequency of markers in each category was used as metadiscourse metrics for each essay. We then extracted pairs of words that co-occurred (e.g., “virtual” and “reality”) in the same ICLS2022 Proceedings 1526 © ISLS sentence. Each essay was represented by a word network where each word in the essay was a node, and each tie between two nodes indicated the presence of this co-occurring word pair (i.e., “virtual reality”). For each essay, network metrics were calculated usi ng the Python NetworkX package: density, diameter, modularity, degree centralization, betweenness centralization, closeness centralization, and clustering coefficient. To answer the first three research questions, Pear son’s correlation tests were conducted to analyze bivariate relationships between these metrics of es says and the performance scores. Classification mod els were constructed to classify low and high performing ess ays, which can reveal multivariate relationships be tween performance scores and combinations of metrics (RQ4). Several commonly used algorithms were applied, including Support Vector Machines (SVM), Ridge Classifier (RC), Decision Tree (DT), Gradient Boosting (GB), Random Forest (RF), and Extra Trees (ET). Ten-fold cross-validation was conducted to evaluate the classification performances which were compared across different c lassifiers. To compare classification power of diff erent metrics, we also built classifier models based on each set of metrics, i.e., metadiscourse, cognitive levels and word network. Classification accuracy was used as the performance measure.

## Results

The most frequently used category of metadiscourse markers was Transition (2799 times, 31% of all metadiscourse markers), followed by Hedges (1338 times, 15%), Frame Markers (1060, 12%) and Boosters (909, 10%). In total, there were 5593 (62%) interactive m arkers and 3534 (38%) interactional ones, indicatin g that students preferred using interactive metadiscourse. The usage frequencies of Evidentials (r = 0.20; p = 0.004) and Hedges (r = 0.24; p < 0.001) had mild positive correlations with the score, which corroborated the common use of nuanced stance with external support in academic writing (Hyland, 2017). In terms of cognitive levels, Application had the h ighest number of keyword matches (2762, 40%), followed by Synthesis (1328, 19%) and Evaluation (893, 13%). If regarding the lowest three levels as lower-order and the highest three as higher-order thinking (Qiao & Hu, 2019), the total counts of matches were 58% and 42% respectively, where the highest two levels took 32%. These results indicate that students in general demonstrated fairly substantial higher-order thinking skills (Qi ao & Hu, 2019). Correlation analysis (RQ2) yielded weak positive relationships between scores and word matches in Analysis (r = 0.19, p = 0.008) and Synthesis (r = 0.15; p = 0.030), corroborating typical requirements of academic writing (Palmer & Lomer, 2016). The word networks turned out to be fairly dense (a ve. density = 0.68 out of 1), indicating many words were inter-connected. Relatedly, the average networ k diameter (2.12) was small, meaning that, to conne ct any two words in a network, on average at most one othe r word in between was needed. The average degree centralization (0.04) was low, indicating the connections between words were scattered and few words dominated the connections. However, no significant correlation was found between each network metric and the performance score. To help us visually examine the networks, ex ample networks of a high and a low performing essay  are shown (Figure 1). The color of the node indicates subgroups of words generated based on their connections. The node’s size is proportional to its degree centrality (i.e., no. of connections with other words), and the thickness of edges represents the frequency of word co-occurrenc es. As shown, the high-performing essay had more and stronger connections among words, constituting a ri cher network than the low-performing counterpart. U pon examining the actual words, connected words in the low-performing essay were mainly between topical words of this assignment, such as “cultural”, “heritage”, “digitization”, and “preservation”. In contrast, the network of the high-performing essay shows more varied connections such as those between “object”, “visitor”, “techno logy”, and the topical words.

## Concluding Remarks

This preliminary study explores metadiscourse usage, cognitive levels, and word network in students’ academic writing. For each of these aspects, we proposed met rics that are based on well-known frameworks and ca n be calculated through automated means. Results showed weak to mild correlations between few metrics and performance scores. The classification experiment i ndicates promising multivariate relationships betwe en the proposed metrics and writing performance, and such relationships can be revealed through automated mea ns (classification). Future work will continue fine tuning the parameters of the classification models for improving classification performances. We will also identify strong predictors in the classification models for providing feature-specific feedback on academic writing to students and teachers.
