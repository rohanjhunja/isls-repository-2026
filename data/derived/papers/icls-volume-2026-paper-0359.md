This symposium asks what it means to engage or practice community-based methodologies through the lens of relational becoming, especially in the foundational presence and power of communities in the Global South Side (Nzinga, 2023). Relational becoming for us is to cultivate interdependent orientations and ontologies to research. In contrast to colonial contexts of educational research, relational becoming is an ethic, a  way of living that purposes research as alliance-building work. Ethical design research  is not merely something we create but the learning environments we continually become as we make sense of ourselves and our unique responsibilities with lands and each other. (Meixi, 2022). Relational becoming as an ethical commitment informs our methodological choices and design  iterations so that they are  reparative in spite of the destruction caused by r esearch within empire. Relational becoming is based in “a profound care for each other and all life”; it is how we keep each other and ourselves alive (Simpson 2025, p. 29).

We use Global South Side (Nzinga, 2023) to refer to both “traditionally described formerly or currently colonized international regions, mostly in the southern hemisphere (including Africa, Asia, and South America)”, where the Global South Side highlights the ways in which colonization happens on hyperlocal scales, so that one side of town (i.e. the northside) can act as the colonizer and another side of town (i.e. the southside) like the colonized” (p. 17). Furthermore, this symposium considers the ways our research methods have been historically ontologically designed for the accumulation of knowledge and the development of theory for cognitive empire in the north (Takayama et al., 2017; Meixi & Nzinga, 2023). We use ‘ empire’ to characterize systems marked by humandominance and the explicit fracturing of nature-culture relations (Bang & Marin, 2015), with vast implications for knowledge building and intergenerational transmission, curriculum design, cross-community collaborations, research inquiry, and interview methods. Across various positionalities with human communities and lands (Jadallah, 2024) – as visitors, cultural insiders, teacher educators, and community members, we collectively consider what it means to bring relational orientations to our research practices, especially within participatory and community-based designs and methods (Bang & Vossoughi, 2016). Relational becoming as ontological design cycles 2437 We focus our inquiry and analysis of relational becoming here from an ontological approach to design (Escobar, 2018). Ontological design recognizes that in designing our tools, systems, pedagogies and practices, we inherently shape the kinds of worlds we inhabit and the kinds of beings we become. This circularity continues as “We design tools, and these tools design us back” (Willis, 2008; Winograd & Flores, 1986, as cited in Escobar, 2018, p. 110). The worlds we design also design us. “Us” here refers to researchers, scholars, students and artists of learning who almost always occupy liminal spaces and also desire to hold new centers. “Us” are the insiders and outsiders to community spaces, visitors, thought partners, analysts, and witnesses to unfolding human meaning-making and activity.

By designing us, we mean that our whole-self-spirit involvement in the places where our lives unfold guides us to grow our unique roles and responsibilities to lands, the Cosmos and each other. Through this, we cultivate practices and ceremonies that open our hearts, minds, bodies and spirits to enter into the work and receive the teachings that guide our lives and the offerings we co‑create. Our research practices become part of our own humandevelopment and ethical maturation as we co-create and live know ledge. This is especially critical in academia where the norm is to do work devoid of spirit. At the same time we intently protect sacred knowledges from being co-opted, appropriated, misread, and imported as static content in the academy. As Trujillo writ es in Paper 2, instead we find ways to invite, re-create, and re-imagine spaces and expansive practices that honor spirit and ceremony beyond where they are held. In this way, we create a bridge that reconnects spirit and reason, and allows ceremonial ethics and relational commitments to shape academic spaces while keeping the ceremonies themselves rooted in the lands and lineages that sustain them. As this bridge forms, we weave knowledges in relation, moving with them as they bloom into wisdom.

Extending this to focus on our methods of inquiry in Global South Side contexts, we consider (1) family, community, and land-based endogenous practices, perhaps ‘origin methods and ontologies’ that emerge from local and historicized understandings of self and place, (2) how they influence our methodological choices and research designs, what this affords, expands and limits in our research practice and design, and 3) and how the research designs we inherit and live too, design us.

This collection of papers examines the concept of relational becoming in ethnographic, community-based and participatory design-based contexts concerned with issues of transformative sociocultural change (Bang & Vossoughi, 2016). Across cases from Chinese international students in the U.S., Bantu communities in Malawi, the indigenizing of spaces through relational and ceremonial commitments in Denver, rural women entrepreneurs in Zimbabwe and Sub-Saharan Africa, and the coastal community of Playa Grande in Chiapas, Mexico, we explore community-based methodologies and intimately study our processes of becoming within our research. Specifically, we explore the tensions and possibilities of being in relation as consequential within interstitial spaces across learning ecologies-in entrepreneurial spaces, in teacher education in climate-vulnerable communities, government-run public schooling, and community life as educators, researchers, and families endeavor together. Relational becoming as a kind of ontological design includes learning to become medicine to each other, including learning who we are to each other, in ways that regenerate our intellectual, cultural, landbased traditions everyday as part of the deep substance of life. We ask the following questions:

1. What practices of relational becoming inform our methods and methodologies?

2. What methods and methodologies support and/or limit our possibilities of relational becoming?

3. How do these support the regeneration of the systems of relationships from within our research?

4. What are the characteristics and challenges of in relational becoming within empire?

## Overview of papers

Drawing from five different community contexts, a core assumption of our work is that cultivating healthier socioecological futures requires not just community-based and ecological knowledge but learning to cultivate relational systems that ask us to be w ith each other differently from within our research. First, we pay attention to how empire is constructed and negotiated toward settler ends, and disregarded within educational programs and systems. For example, large-scale development and schooling initiatives have often structured epistemic erasure, violence, damage, and dispossession in many homes and communities with unique contexts of empire and colonization. Second, we present a collection of methodological experiences with beloved communities that seek to be grounded in origin epistemologies, stories, and land-based knowledge systems. Finally, we offer reflections and openings, and moments of teachings for us that attend to power, historicity, and politicized trust (Vakil et al.,

2016) especially since unequal power paradigms can hinder authentic collaborations and perpetuate harm.

Additionally, we recognize the term “community” can flatten a diversity in whole groups of people in ways that in fact reproduce harm by not explicitly naming sociopolitical and historical analyses of power (Philip et al, 2013). We explicitly attend to con structions of community here and the ways that our ways of relational becoming account for communities’ mutual investment, member diversity, situated membership, and self-determined purpose that hold the members of a socio-cultural collective together (ibi d). Each of our papers examine a mix of methods (tools that we use to collect and analyze data), methodologies (our guiding theories, philosophies about truth, how we enter into relationships with our questions, ideas, and related responsibilities, and why we do what we do in the first place; Brayboy et al, 2011), and moments of relational becoming, paying attention to how these expanded possibilities of community-based collaborative efforts. The first paper develops an Umunthu-Ecopedagogy with teachers, elders, and community members in Malawi’s Mchinji District, where climate disruption and poverty are acute. It reframes teacher education as a restorative, relationship-centered practice and, through a small pilot with six in-service teachers, uses Story Cycles and Community Walks to braid school science with Indigenous and Local Knowledge. These methods challenge the coloniality of knowledge and validate community epistemic resources (Chilisa, 2020; Mbah, 2019). Adopting a Two-Eyed Seeing stance positions teachers as collaborators “in-between” classrooms and communities. The study concludes that cultivating reciprocity and shared agency strengthens place-responsive climate learning and enables locally meaningful adaptation.

The second paper explores Indigenous ceremonial practices as living epistemologies that transmit ancestral wisdom through embodied, land-based, and relational pedagogies. It develops the concept of Embodied Reciprocity to show how movement, ritual, and storywork co-transform knowledge into wisdom through love, intergenerational care, and decolonial healing.

The third paper examines the role of Ngano storytelling, embedded within Shona Indigenous knowledge systems, as a culturally specific methodology for entrepreneurship training among rural women in Zimbabwe.

The study foregrounds relational ontology and methodological justice to conceptualize entrepreneurship as a communal, ethical, and spiritual practice, rather than limiting it to economic dimensions. The fourth paper examines how progressive, socio-politically active students from China (trans)form their identities when pursuing higher education in the US. Against the backdrop of expanding nation-state projects and circulating global hierarchies of race, gender, and class, this study borrows intersectionality with a transnational lens to explore how participants resist these systems while developing identities with transformative socio-political possibilities. The paper also considered how affect is central to identity transformation amid the intensified emotional and psychological challenges of global identity politics today. Paper 1: Pedagogies of relational becoming: Umunthu-ecopedagogy and epistemic justice for climate education in Malawi Sunga Kufeyani In rural Malawi, large-scale climate education often perpetuates a disconnect between formal school knowledge and the lived realities of community life, resulting in epistemic violence. This disconnect diminishes the collective resilience required to address climate change (Shizha, 2013; Ndlovu-Gatsheni, 2018). Bridging this gap necessitates a re-examination of teacher education as a restorative process and pedagogies of relational becoming grounded in Umunthu-Ecopedagogy and epistemic justice. I frame this  issue through epistemic injustice, relationality, (Fricker, 2007; Chilisa, 2020) and the persistent coloniality of knowledge that marginalizes Indigenous and Local Knowledge across Africa (de Sousa Santos, 2014; Quijano, 2000). For example, the Mchinji District shows that children are taught abstract climate concepts while effective place-based practices, such as reading seasonal signs, phenological cues, and local forecasting, are neglected (Eybers & Oliver, 2023). Empirical research demonstrates that these community knowledge systems are valuable for adapting to seasonal variation, yet remain marginalized in formal education (Kalanda-Joshua et al., 2011). This divide undermines the coherence and social stability necessary for communities to address climat e disruption (Ndlovu-Gatsheni, 2018; Chilisa, 2020).

## Methodologies and context: Becoming partnering

For an equity-centered partnership, I used a small participatory design research (Bang & Vossoughi, 2016) pilot with six in-service teachers who practiced relational becoming (Meixi, 2022). Through Story Cycles and Community Walks, which reflect Indigenous  Bantu learning (Hoppers, 2002; Ramose, 1999; Metz, 2017), teachers re-examined their roles in relation to land, elders, and learners. These practices align with ecopedagogical goals of challenging harmful relations and encouraging place-based, democratic learning (Kahn, 2010; Misiaszek, 2021; Kahn & Richard, 2010). In Umunthu-Ecopedagogy, education means building ethical relations, reciprocity, and responsibility, not just delivering content. Learnings By shifting from preset content to context-based learning, the intervention reinforced the idea that relational responsibilities, as symbolized by the msangu tree’s stabilizing roots, are fundamental to knowledge creation. The pilot surfaced two main pedagogical tensions for further examination in our partnering processes. First, while teachers aimed to make climate education inclusive, their classroom methods inadvertently reinforced epistemic hierarchies. They often prioritized abstract scientific concept s, such as the hydrologic cycle, particularly in drought settings, thereby sidelining local, community-based practices like rainwater harvesting and reading local environmental signals. This approach continued to privilege formal science over local expertise, widening the gap between school knowledge and lived community wisdom, and hindering local adaptation (Agrawal & Arun, 1995). Second, teachers reported that community elders directly associated the erosion of cultural traditions with 2440 increased vulnerability to climate impacts. Elders specifically linked the decline of communal rain observances under the msangu tree with reduced food security, emphasizing that losing these practices is both an epistemic loss and a practical harm. These insights highlight that repairing education requires strengthening both ecological understanding and cultural continuity by reintegrating place-based knowledge systems into formal education (Ramose, 1999; Metz, 2017; Kayira & Jean, 2015).

The intervention demonstrated its value by positioning teachers toward a Two-Eyed Seeing approach, where they integrate scientific knowledge with Indigenous and Local Knowledge on equal terms and “desettling” climate education (Bang et al, 2012; Marshall, 2018, Bartlett et al., 2012) This enactment of epistemic justice highlights the importance of recognizing and prioritizing Malawian traditional ecological knowledge as a means to heal existing divides (Chilisa, 2020; Santos & B., 2014).

Contributions: Pedagogies of relational becoming Community-based methods work when they promote  this attitude of mutual responsibility and seeing between. Building teacher agency with, not for, the community shifts classrooms towards active responsibility. This kind of teaching is a real step toward equitable partnerships in climate education, where epistemic justice is needed for lasting socio-ecological futures in Malawi (Kalanda-Joshua et al., 2011; Misiaszek, 2021; Bang et al., 2016).  My positionality is an active "relational becoming," rooted in my lineage as a daughter of Malawian educators. This professional and spiritual inheritance allowed me to enter the work not as a detached observer, but as a "relative" and "guest" bound by Umunthu. My analytic memos served as a site of "desettling," where I documented the shift from analyzing data to witnessing epistemic loss alongside elders and teachers. Paper 2: Embodied Reciprocity: Pedagogies of Land, Spirit, and Story Karla Trujillo This research reclaims education as a sacred journey of relational becoming, where body, land and spirit co-teach through ceremonial motion. It confronts the ontological design of Western research as a tool of empire, marked by humandominance and the severing of nature-culture relations (Bang & Marin, 2015), and offers Indigenous ceremonial frameworks as urgent acts of repair. In this work, I distinguish between ceremonial epistemologies that guide ethical research design and ceremonial practices that remain held within specific lineages, not offered as academic methods, while also creating a bridge that invites academic spaces to be shaped by the relational and ethical commitments of ceremony without appropriating the ceremonies themselves. Through the duality of the Plumed Serpent and Tezcatlipoca’s mirror of introspection (García & Márquez, 2021; Carrasco, 2013), learners are guided to confront colonial hierarchies and restore balance. This work attends to how ceremonies and rituals like danza function as living epistemologies that connect the human body to the Earth and Cosmos and weave understanding through movement, memory and relation. These ceremonial ways of knowing transform fractured educational spaces into ecosystems of reciprocity,  where learning unfolds as a braided process of remembering, healing and co-creation. Education, in this framing, is not a linear path but a spiraling movement where knowledge is not extracted but nurtured through rhythm, relationship and respect. In this nurturing, knowledge becomes the flowering of wisdom, blooming through the relational commitments that sustain life across generations.

## Methodologies and context

This inquiry is guided by three interwoven methods that honor Indigenous epistemologies and relational accountability. Storywork, following Archibald (2008), treats stories as sacred pedagogical tools that transmit wisdom through oral, relational and intergenerational channels. It centers community voice and cosmological memory and resists extractive research by privileging collective narration and ceremonial dialogue. Autoethnography, grounded in the researcher’s role as danzante and ethnomusicologist, positions embodied reflection as a site of inquiry. It foregrounds ceremonial participation, emotional resonance and spiritual insight as ways of weaving understanding in the manner Kimmerer (2013) describes and it helps trace how movement, ritual and relationality shape healing and methodological design. Videography captures the nonverbal, rhythmic and spatial dimensions of ceremonial learning. Through interpretive analysis of gesture, sound and gaze (Heath et al., 2010; Knoblauch & Schnettler, 2012), it reveals how understanding circulates through movement and collective resonance. By supporting repeated sensory-rich engagement with ceremonial moments, videography allows for layered interpretation grounded in cultural protocols and community-defined meaning.

2441 Together, these methods support a way of learning shaped by ceremony without reproducing ceremonial practice for academic use and they help me attend to how ceremony guides my responsibilities, relations and ways of becoming within the research while offer ing a bridge that allows ceremony to shape academic spaces through relation, care and accountability while keeping the ceremonies themselves within the lineages that hold them. Learnings Several teachings emerged from this work. First, ceremony clarified that learning is not a discrete cognitive act but a relational, ethical and energetic movement. Ceremonies and rituals like danza function as living epistemologies that connect the human body to the Earth and Cosmos and they guide understanding rather than offer a technique to be replicated. Knowledge and wisdom are inseparable and knowledge becomes the flowering of wisdom as it moves in relation.

Second, the work illuminated how Western research designs often separate knowledge and wisdom from responsibility, creating a divide that ceremony refuses. Storywork, autoethnography and videography helped me remain accountable to community-defined meaning and cultural protocols and they supported a way of learning where knowledge moves toward wisdom through relationship. Finally, this inquiry made clear that while ceremonial epistemologies can guide research design, ceremonial practices themselves must remain within the communities and lineages that hold them. Naming this boundary is part of the work of relational becoming and it ensures that research contributes to repair rather than repeating the patterns of harm that empire has long enacted.

Paper 3: Being, knowing, and doing: Indigenous approaches to entrepreneurship training in Zimbabwe Enet Mukurazita This paper begins with the assumption that understanding what it means to be a person in the world, including relational, resilient, culturally grounded aspects, is crucial for designing equitable research. The ontological view of personhood, which in Shona culture focus on relationships, shape development and learning, while Western models instead, often emphasize individual autonomy. The study examines how rural Zimbabwean women’s lived experiences and Indigenous knowledge systems shape both entrepreneurship and the methods used to study it.

Using Indigenous approaches such as Ngano storytelling (Mukurazita, 2023), this study examines how ways of being and knowing are inherent in learning, and research in the context of   business training in rural women contexts in Zimbabwe.

Recent movements in rural entrepreneurship in Sub-Saharan Africa has often been a site to attempt connecting and integrating Indigenous and Western knowledge to promote mutual benefits and foster pluralistic understandings that go beyond dichotomies (Bang & Medin, 2010; Cajete, 2000). In fact, UNESCO (2023) reiterated Dickson (1985)'s emphasis on the link between education, culture, and development for relevance. While this was an emphasis in my study, I realized the need to ground our methodologies, our processes of inquiry in acts of relational becoming.

This study builds on Shona onto-epistemic and axiological perspectives of learning and becoming as a kind of cognitive (Vishvanathan 2009) and methodological justice, where justice goes beyond epistemic perspectives but are instead embedded within lifeworlds, learning ecologies, cosmologies, and ways of making lives, and livelihoods.   Learning is a lifelong process in African contexts, especially in Zimbabwe, which is mirrored in communal traditions such as songs, proverbs, and festivals (Avoseh, 2009; Amponsah et al., 2018). In other words, learning is part of daily life, storytelling, and spiritual practice, not a separate activity. 2442 Furthermore, the study is grounded in relational ontology, where knowledge, ethics, and identity are not individualized, but are derived from community and relationships (Wilson, 2008; Chilisa, 2012).   Shona philosophy emphasizes interdependence and moral responsibility in a manner similar to Ubuntu, an African ethic that emphasizes ‘munhu munhu nevanhu’ (a person is a person through others). This ontological grounding shaped not only the content of the entrepreneurship training but also the research methods, particularly the use of Ngano storytelling as a participatory and spiritually grounded method of inquiry." Specifically, I examine methodological justice as a critical methodological orientation to narrative inquiry, using Ngano methodology as an organi zing philosophy and methodology.

## Methodologies and context

The study was conducted in rural Zimbabwe, focusing on Wards 27 (Rukweza) and 29 (Mukamba) in Makoni District, Manicaland Province, among rural women who completed an entrepreneurship training program. Like many rural African communities, the area relies h eavily on agriculture but struggles with poor infrastructure, limited electricity, and restricted market access (Mutambara et al., 2020). It is culturally rich, with traditional values guiding conflict resolution, environmental stewardship, and knowledge transmission through rituals, storytelling, and community courts (Gwatidzo, 2019; Nanhanga, 2023). Women, who make up 53% of the population, face barriers such as low education, limited formal employment, and restricted financial access, and must navigate complex inequalities shaped by donor agendas, cultural norms, and economic uncertainty, making entrepreneurship a vital economic opportunity despite the dominance of Western business models that often marginalize informal and small-scale enterprise development. Nevertheless, women demonstrate resilience and innovation through communal resource-sharing, deep land knowledge, and experiential learning. This paper examines narrative inquiry methodology based on Ngano storytelling from Shona traditions as a form of relational becoming (Mawere, 2014; Chabata, 2019).Ngano is not merely a data collection technique but a worldview that places the interviewer i n relation to those involved.   Ngano, meaning “advice” or stories generally, uses oral cultural techniques, native languages, and visual imagery to convey knowledge, reflecting Indigenous epistemologies, ontologies, and axiologies with minimal academic filtering (Mapara, 2018; Osei-Tutu, 2023). This method acknowledges moral values, spiritual beliefs, and interge nerational wisdom, emphasizing storytelling as a technique that reflects and respects the individuality of participants. Traditionally performed by elders known as Sarungano, Ngano storytelling is communal and participatory, expressing moral values and spiritual beliefs through characters, songs, and proverbs. In this study, I highlight two practices that facilitated relational becoming with the Sarungano (participant) narrated entrepreneurial experiences through an individual story (rungano rwako) and group stories (gungano). Practice 1: Involving culturally resonant stories that integrate proverbs, idioms, and connections to land and spirituality (Chilisa, 2012). These narratives reflected Indigenous epistemologies and explicitly connected business pra ctices to land, ancestry, and communal values. The cyclical and metaphorical structure of Ngano storytelling facilitated mutual recognition and relational engagement. This approach fostered a sense of belonging and enabled knowledge generation within shared cultural frameworks. Storytelling functioned as a method of cocreation rather than solely as a data collection tool, affirming participants’ identities and lived experiences. It situated learning and entrepreneurship within the context of daily life and  spiritual practice. Practice 2:  Sharing their totem and ancestry. Before the narrative inquiry begins,the researcher demonstrated relational accountability, not as a procedure but a means of being with and building trust, co-creating knowledge, and respecting Indigenous voices (Owusu-Ansah & Mji, 2013). Participants were subsequently invited to share their own lineages, reflecting mutual respect and cultural alignment. This exchange facilitated trust and reduced hierarchical distinctions between the researcher and participants. The process established a culturally grounded environment in which participants experienced recognition and validation. The act of sharing totems affirmed both identity and the relational ethics integral to Shona ontology. This appr oach supported more in-depth storytelling and contributed to a research process that was both spiritually and culturally affirming. Learnings from Ngano Ngano storytelling as a research method revealed key insights into methodological justice, relational ethics, and co-creating knowledge in Indigenous contexts. This method challenged Western practices and affirmed Indigenous storytelling as a legitimate and embedded form of knowledge, not just data collection. The research shows that achieving methodological justice needs more than technical skill; it depends on relational accountability, humility, and ethical collaboration. By sharing totems and ancestry, researchers created a culturally grounded setting. Here, mutual recognition replaced hierarchies and fostered two-way engagement. This change moved research from being about participants to being with them. It produced authentic, spiritually rich narratives spanning personal, family, and ancestral stories. Collaborating through Ngano storytelling honored the dignity 2443 and agency of rural women and built more trust and richer stories than standard methods. In the end, Ngano prompted a new view of knowledge-making as relational, participatory, and culturally responsible. It respected Indigenous knowledge and challenged extractive research. Contributions This study contributes to the decolonization of entrepreneurship training and research methodologies by centering on Indigenous knowledge systems derived from Shona cultural traditions. Using Ngano storytelling reframes entrepreneurship as a relational, spiritual, and ethical practice situated within Indigenous worldviews rather than solely as an economic activity. Results indicate that knowledge production in this setting is fundamentally communal, arising from relationships with community members, the land, and ancestral knowledge. Western frameworks that emphasize individual autonomy and technical rationality were challenged by this approach. Methodological justice was achieved through relational accountability, where shared totems and ances try established trust, mutual recognition, and epistemic humility. This process shifted the research from data extraction to collaborative knowledge creation, affirming participant dignity and agency. As a researcher my positionality moved from insider/outsider to an epistemological mediator through the use of Ngano as the data collection became a trust-based process of story weaving. Furthermore, ethical access in Indigenous contexts, as demonstrated in this study, requires authentic engagement.  As a researcher I functioned as a relational weaver to guide the collaborative process ensuring that the knowledge produced emerged from shared meaning-making instead of an imposed agenda.

Paper 4: Unlearning hegemonies, relearning ourselves and the world: Identity (trans)formation among progressive Chinese international students in the us Xun Yu Amid rising far-right politics and political divides among the world (Snyder, 2018; Stanley, 2020), Chinese international students in the U.S. navigate a precarious sociopolitical landscape marked by intensifying geopolitical tensions between China and the U.S. This paper aims to explore how progressive and socio-political active students from China cultivate and enact their socio-political identities, as they confront the gendered, ethnonationalist, and racialized labels produced by US nationalism/white supremacist and Chinese ethno-nationalist discourse.

## Methodologies and context

To unpack the (trans)formation of socio-political identities among Chinese feminist students, I borrow intersectionality from Black feminist thoughts as a key conceptual framework. Crenshaw (1991) provides the framework of intersectionality for articulatin g how interlocking systems of oppression create the intersection of different forms of discrimination and privilege based on one's position in society. An important distinction in how I use intersectionality is that I take an intersectional approach that n ot only examines the intersections of social issues but also the power relations both between and within the US and China. To do so, I use a community based, multimodal life history-based narrative inquiry to explore how these students “understand and behave in the political world as they see it” (Crow & Andrews, 2019, p. 558). Employing one life history mapping workshop with two in-depth, semi-structured interviews, I have collected four life history drawings and conducted more than 40 hours of interviews.

Learnings The first finding was the various ways in which students embody intersectionality. They had very different starting points and identified diverse significant life events that sparked their socio-political unlearning and (re)learning. Participants draw on the language of identity to articulate their (re)learning about themselves yet encounter deep friction in doing so. The second finding focused on the affective undercurrents in identity (trans)formation, especially amid the heightened emotional and psychological challenges of identity politics worldwide. I found that participants' “negative” feelings, such as anguish from disillusionment about the government and confusion about violent events involving ethnic conflicts were long-lasting and central to their ongoing socio-political relearnings.

Rodgers, Xiaohan Liu, Joy Emmanuel Faculty and administrators face significant challenges as they contend with how to build more equitable systems of doctoral education, particularly for Black, Indigenous, and Latine students. Many existing efforts aimed at supporting students of color to navigate graduate education focus more on preparing students for assimilation into the dominant culture rather than identifying and designing interventions to address the root causes underlying the cultural, structural, and policy issues that perpetuate the ir exclusion (McGee, 2020; Slay et al., 2019). However, systemic change, defined as the fundamental and sustained transformation of aspects of an ecosystem, is notoriously difficult in graduate education (Cardoso et al., 2022). The design and implementation of activity in doctoral education is notoriously decentralized, with decision-makers often distributed across various organizational units like academic departments, school/college administration, and the Graduate School (Posselt, 2020). This decentralization offers both advantages and disadvantages:  it affords departmental leaders with autonomy and agency, allowing them to tailor their interventions to the specific needs of their social context. A downside to decentralization, however, is that people and resources are often siloed, leading to duplica ted efforts, organizational redundancies, isolation, burnout, and niche, short-lived changes rather than widespread and sustained ones (Felix & Castro, 2018). Additionally, systemic change requires strategic, comprehensive, and coordinated efforts across multiple stakeholders, which is made notoriously more difficult in a decentralized system. Given these complex dynamics, the contours of how systemic change efforts are best conceptualized, coordinated, and executed across multiple stakeholders in graduate education remain elusive. We report findings from a larger instrumental case study of the complex process of promoting equity-minded systemic change in graduate education in the Center for Organizational Learning and Change (COLC; pseudonym). Established in 2024 at a large, public, land-grab university in the Midwest, COLC aims to build sustaining partnerships with constituents in eleven physical science academic departments across the School of Engineering (Engineering), the School of Arts & Sciences (A&S), and the Graduate School (TGS) to develop learning communities of faculty, staff, and students and pilot meaningful interventions to adopt evidence-based practices to support Black, Latine, and Indigenous PhD students. Because the activities of COLC are conceptualized and coordinated by its steering committee, a diverse and interdisciplinary group of university faculty, administrators, and staff members across the university, this qualitative study explores how the steer ing 2451 committee describes the purpose and object of joint activity in COLC, the contradictions they encounter in their systemic change efforts, and the strategies they employ to navigate these contradictions. We assert that facilitating systemic change in graduate education is an organizational learning issue that requires both continuous self-reflection (Basile & Azevedo, 2022) and a critical evaluation of the many interconnected practices, policies, and structures that affect graduate student success and satisfaction (Dupree & Boykin, 2021). Thus, we employ Cultural Historical Activity Theory (CHAT) to frame our analysis. By situating rules (the norms of engagement), community (contexts, groups, constituents), and division of labor (how work is distributed within activity systems) as foundational for understanding change processes, CHAT provides researchers with the analytical tools needed to systematically observe how these aspects function interactionally as mediators to permit or constrain how learning and change happens within and across activity systems (Engeström, 2001). We hone in on the narratives of the COLC steering committee members as subjects working across multiple distinct, albeit interconnected activity systems (e.g., TGS, Engineering, A&S, and the 11 physical science departments). CHAT offers a framework for studying how the steering committee conceptualizes and carries out the work of building systemic change in ways that move toward transformative, equity-minded praxis (Sannino & Engeström, 2018).

We conducted all the interviews via Zoom and audio-recorded them with participants’ consent. We examine how racial politics and the political economy shape the teachers’ collective organizing strategies and learning processes within their pilot effort. We interrogate how the small group navigated dominant ideologies, addressed their subjectivity, and challenged taken-for-granted segregation as they engaged in internal and public conversations.

We weave together three frameworks to examine the political dimensions of teacher learning within their organizing. First, we utilize Diem & Hawkman’s (2018) conception of whiteness as policy, encompassing colorblind ideology, normalization of white identi ties, and minimization of racist legacies. Second, we draw on Hall’s (1997) cultural politics, attentive to how discourse, representation, and ideology shape group meaningmaking processes. Finally, we extend Pham and Philip’s (2021) pedagogies of organizing to examine the microlevel dimensions of collective action, the co-construction of goals and strategies within structural and historical contexts. This framework affords rich examination of macro (e.g., cultural, political, and economic contexts) and micro dynamics (e.g., interactions, sensemaking, and negotiations) that shape the teacher small group’s organizing strategies for school desegregation.

This qualitative case study stems from a larger research partnership in which the second author engaged in a year-long co-participant research study with an organizing group of teachers and parents engaging in local desegregation efforts in Oakland, California. The second author operated as both a researcher and resource provider, connecting participants with studies and resources on the history and effects of integration. Our multi-phase analytical approach builds on growing research efforts that integrate micro-moment analysis with an inquiry into the broader political, economic, and social conditions that shape the mechanisms and processes of learning within the context o f collective action for racial justice. Initial analysis includes a) analytic memos, b) index coding (event types, actors, process stages); c) recursive deductive (race discourse, identities, policy context) and inductive coding (interpersonal moves, resources). Selective moments are analyzed through discourse and interactive analysis to trace how the small group articulated, contested, and reworked racialized ideologies through their talk and strategic organizing actions (Fairclough, 2003). Our analysis explores three interconnected themes, demonstrating how teachers construct pedagogies of organizing to counter whiteness as policy. We explore these through interactional moments where the small group learning processes become visible. In the first illustrative moment, “Naming Race,” teachers strategically negotiate language within restrictive policy contexts, adjusting terminology to maintain a race-and power-conscious analysis while adhering to legal restrictions on race language. This moment unfolds during a planning conversation, internal to the organizing group, in which they discuss possibilities for framing their proposal while waiting for data from the district. Without reliable support from district officials, they use their own  informal knowledge and memories of other integration efforts to suggest framing. Through moments of correction, exploration of possibilities, and re-orienting to their goals, they co-construct possibilities. In the second moment, “Identity Work,” we examine teachers grappling with their shared white subjectivity while resisting the essentialization of skin color of minoritized individuals to politics (see Melamed,

2015) and instead center critical politics of justice, as they build multi-racial coalitions. This moment comes in

conversation with a member of another local organizing group, sharing about their efforts and intentions. Following a series of internal discussions about their role as white educators, what they can “add,” and combating the “white savior myth,” educators name their own identities and political commitments to justice, inclusive of and not limited to this pilot.

The affordances and constraints of strong political identification: Critical approaches to teacher preparation and student sensemaking within a mentorpre-service teacher dyad Veer B. Kothari, Thomas M. Philip, Andy Castro This paper explores an enduring question in educational research: How can teachers authentically engage with student thinking and sensemaking, within the context of their own strongly held views, while honoring students’ dignity (Scott & Philip, 2022)? Within inquiry-oriented approaches to education, there is a general consensus about the value of attending to student thinking and sensemaking—supporting students' own ideas and allowing them to arrive at their own conclusions. However, in critical pedagogical approaches, educators often do not authentically engage student sensemaking if students’ ideas run counter to the educators’ goals (Ellsworth, 1989; Philip, 2019). For example, teachers who are positioned as, and position themselves, as critical or skill ed at facilitating discussions of power (race, class, gender, etc.) may constrain their ability to listen to students or center their ideas (Philip, 2013; Philip et al., 2017). Building on this critique and through a case study of a pre-service dyad mentoring relationship, we investigate how strong political commitments, when they take on particular localized meanings, may constrain, rather than support, the dialogic, improvisational work of engaging students’ emergent ideas, especially when those ideas diverge from the teachers’ own political stances. Our study examines how social justice discourse was mobilized to flatten and homogenize, rather than support a complex, nuanced, and agentic view of students.

Drawing on theories of identification (Holland et al., 2001) that are attentive to the structural constraints teachers face as well as their agentic capacities, we elucidate how strong forms of political identification may limit interpretive flexibility and the ability to recognize the value in forms of reasoning that diverge from one’s own political perspectives.

Methodologically, this study was part of a larger year-long study of four mentor-pre-service teacher pairs, each committed to critical approaches in their classrooms. We present an in-depth case study of a Black queer in-service teacher mentor, Florence, and a White woman pre-service teacher, Vivian, who co-taught a ninthgrade Ethnic Studies class at a school where approximately 80% of students were Latinx, 14% were African American, and about 2% each were Asian American and White. Our data include three co-planning–observation– debrief lesson cycles and three interviews with each teacher. To examine mentoring dynamics within the dyad, we coded the lesson cycles for “educative [co]mentoring practices” (Wexler, 2019). Analysis proceeded through the identification of instances of ideological convergences and expansions (Philip et al., 2018) in which the educators made sense of their students. We examined how such constructions aligned or did not align across the data corpus. Analysis proceeded through ongoing writing of memos and conversations of emerging themes and findings. We found that the teachers expressed strong political commitments as central to their reasons for teaching, yet also struggled to respond when students’ perspectives diverged from their own. In her exit interview, Vivian explained, “The thing that drives me to the social studies classroom is…I get to enjoy such opulent living in Wilsonville while the person who made this (points at notebook) lives in terrible conditions. And that’s not by accident, there’s a historical process and systematic process.” Here, Vivian articulated that her primary motivation for teaching stems from a political commitment to illuminate the structural conditions that produce large-scale inequities in society.

She continued, “I had a student who said, ‘Why do we spend so much time studying oppression? I live it every day’ and that’s a student who gave us a lot of push back... I disagreed with what he thought and how he approached it, but I think he had some valid points.” While Vivian hedges her statement by acknowledging that the student “had some valid points,” she does not elaborate or express dialogic engagement with the student’s concern—that his lived experience of oppression rendered certain curricular content redundant or superficial. 2455 Similarly, Florence described in her first interview that she aspired for her students to be “the activist I want them to be in [the] world.” She continued, explaining that while many of her colleagues “might disagree and say it’s [students’] choice to be activists,” she disagreed. “I want them all to be [activists] in whatever field they go into.” Like Vivian, Florence articulated a strong political commitment as a teacher: she wanted all of her students to become activists. Rather than supporting the diverse and heterogeneous interests of her students, Florence’s commitment was oriented toward a predetermined outcome that was important to her. In another interview, Florence again reflected on the challenge she and Vivian faced engaging male students. She began by pondering her male students' lack of engagement: “If I had to guess and assume [why my male students are less engaged].” She then turned to possible explanations, suggesting "it could be us [the teachers]," but without pause and in the same turn at talk, landed on the explanation that societal expectations made male students believe they need to act in particular ways. She continued, “We  talk a lot about identity and society’s ideals around boys of color, they’re not told to be nurturers, to help each other out. A lot of it is about our patriarchal society. There’s a conflict between oppression and I’m weak…If I’m analyzing their words correctly, it’s about how is this going to make me money?” Florence made two high-inference claims (“If I had to guess and assume” and “If I’m analyzing their words correctly”) and hedges her first by noting “it could be us,” suggesting that the teachers themselves may play a role in the dynamic she describes. However, rather than examining how the organization of the classroom or her and Vivian’s pedagogy may contribute to disengagement, Florence flattened her male students of color into a homogenous category, explaining their disengagement through the discourse of patriarchy (Philip, Kothari, Castro, 2025). In her framing, “boys of color were not told to be nurturers,” and thus, caring about oppression would make them appear “weak.” Here, the social justice discourse of patriarchy was mobilized to explain both why male students resisted “caring” about Ethnic Studies and why they expressed interest in making money. This localized meaning—of boys being primarily associated with a desire for money—persisted over the course of the school year with Florence providing the same inference of their behavior on two separate other occasions. Capitalism was also invoked in conjunction with patriarchy to further explain her boys’ perceived interest in money rather than Ethnic Studies.

The educators' strong political commitments individually expressed, also shaped dyad interactions in ways that seemed to foreclose collective engagement and inquiry around students' thoughts in expansive and dignity affirming ways. Two thirds of the way through the school year, the educators were preparing for a photojournalist, Jose, to visit the class and discussed how to prepare students. Vivian had asked students to prepare questions for the visit.

F:  “I like that you had them think about questions for Jose…As far as naturally engaging them, sometimes I don’t know, there should be a line that ‘this guy makes bank, he gets to travel’. Our children really value money right now. This class, I don’t know, they do really value money.” V:  “Something I want Jose to talk about is he’s chased a lot of opportunity, which is something I respect about him as a person, and I want students to be like ‘damn this guy grew up in Mexico City, applied for this, somehow got wrapped up in this.” In this interaction, Florence and Vivian initially aimed to think collaboratively about how to engage their students. However, rather than considering the many possible ways a photojournalist’s work might resonate with complex and multifaceted students, Fl orence immediately emphasized Jose’s lifestyle—his income (“makes bank”) and ability to travel—as the aspects that would have appealed to her male students. The educators did not consider students' multiplicities of identities and sensemaking, but instead shifted to a discussion of classroom logistics. Drawing on the localized meaning that “boys valued money,” she reduced students’ potential interests to a single, homogenized motivation. Vivian then built on Florence’s framing, highlighting Jose’s pursuit of opportunity as the potential hook for student engagement. Taken together, these findings illustrate how teachers’ strong political commitments can shape how social justice discourses are mobilized in ways that unintentionally flatten rather than nuance or complexify students’ lives, interests, and perspectives. In interaction, these shared political commitments can converge (Philip et al., 2018) and limit dialogue that takes students' thinking and sensemaking seriously. Overall, critical approaches to teaching share important commitments with social justice and activism. Critical teacher education often emphasizes the “what” and “why” of teaching —questions most aligned with an activist stance—yet, as Philip (2013) argues,  teaching requires a distinct stance, one that may be informed by activism but is fundamentally oriented toward engaging students’ thinking. Focusing on a mentor-teacher dyad, this study contributes to scholarship on critical teaching and teacher education by arguing for a more nuanced approach to critical teaching and teacher education, one that supports teachers to authentically listen to and honor 2456 students' unique and idiosyncratic forms of sensemaking, not as a means to our ends, but as an end in itself. Such an approach — one that treats students' sensemaking with dignity and respect — is indispensable if we are to teach toward justice.
