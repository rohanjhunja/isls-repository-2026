# Math in the Messy: Breaking Down Norms “Queer”-ly

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Falling in line with existing visibility studies centering the queer experience in STEM, this paper zooms in on the lived experiences and perceptions of queer students in mathematics. This qualitative study is grounded in queer theory as aligned with constructionism. Drawing on interview data and through an iterative thematic analysis, this qualitative study highlights four themes, characterizing present curricular practices as inequitable: reinforcing the binary, reinforcing neutrality, math as fixed, math as gendered. Implications point to design principles for a queering of mathematics, aimed at the construction of more equitable learning spaces for queer, and ultimately, all youth.

## Introduction

This study zooms in on the lived experiences and perceptions of queer students in higher education mathematics, reflecting on their challenges and positive experie nces to gather insight into productive next steps f or the design of a “queering” of mathemati cs, that deconstructs and disrupts notions of mathematics as a space for dominant groups in the field (i.e., white, male, straight). While this study falls in line with existing visibility studies centering the queer experience in STEM (e.g., Forbes, 2022; L inley et el., 2018) and tackling issues of represen tation, discrimination, and persistence in the field (e.g., Freeman, 2020), it further aims to derive design principles for a productive transformation of curricular practices in mathematics that are perpetuating inequities. Within mathematics, what has garnered more attention with respect to issues of equity has been the role gender plays in mathematics. A more intersectional lens with a focus on the experience of queer students as such could illuminate the specific aspects of establishe d practices in mathematics that may be oppressive t o queer students and pave a way toward disrupting them. A f ocus on gender exclusively might be oversimplifying  the observed disparities and the factors at play whereas a queer theory lens which holds a view of identity as “unfixed, contingent, and in a process of constant reconstitution” could unveil unfolding complexities, as pertaining to queer students’ experience in mathematics (Rands, 2009, p.3; Butler, 1993; 1999; Sumara & Davis, 1999). Focusing the study are the following research questions: (1) How does queerness inform one’s perceptions of and experience in mathematics? What is signaling heteronormative s ensitivity within current curricular practices? (2)  How can we use a constructionist  lens, as aligned with queer theory, to design for a  queering of mathematics which meaningfully disrupts present inequitable practices?

Building upon earlier work (Yankova, 2023), I draw on interview data with queer underg raduate and graduate students to gain insight into present curr icular practices that are perpetuating inequities. Grounding this work in a queer theory lens, as aligned with constr uctionist learning theory, I engage in iterative co ding and thematic analysis, ultimately deriving four themes, characterizing present curricular practices  as inequitable: reinforcing the binary, reinforcing neutrality, mat h as fixed, math as gen dered. Envisioning new possibilities, I then propose design principles for more equitable learning spaces in mathematics.

## Background

LGBTQ+ and STEM In recent years, there has been a more consolidated effort to study LGBTQ+ students in STEM, who have shown to be among minoritized groups in STEM. The literat ure on the LGBTQ+ experience in STEM has fallen und er different categories, with some studies tac kling issues of representation, discrimination, and  persistence in the field, both in higher education and the workforce (e.g., Freeman, 2020; Hughes & Kothari, 2021). For instance, in their analysis of a large-scale data set from the Higher Education Research Institute at UCLA, Hughes & Kothari

(2021) investigated the retention of LGBTQ+ students in STEM, as compared to their heterosexual peers, finding

that after four years, LGBTQ+ students were more likely to have switched to a non-STEM major. Other studies have investigated the queer experienc e in STEM, taking a predominantly qualitative approach (e.g., Forbes, 2022; Linley et el., 2018). For e xample, drawing on interviews with students, Forbes

(2022) found that the marginalization of queer stud ents is like women's experience with respect to being driven

out of the pursuit in STEM fields, often associated with a lack of a sense of belonging. Other studies have brought in an intersectional lens of race and sexuality and more critical perspectives, challenging STEM’s neutrality (e.g., Leyva et al., 202 2). All these studies have provided visibility for LGBT Q+ students and people in STEM, with opportunities to follow up and investigate ways to address present disparities by taking more of a des ign-based approach, aimed at transforming current practices in STEM. A synergistic approach Drawing on queer perspectives within the learning s ciences has picked up pace in the last decade or so, as previously queer theory was often presumed to be be st fit for the work of philosophy or literary schol ars (McWilliams & Penuel, 2016). Yet, queer theory has much to offer learning scientists, especially those interested in the design of more equitable learning environmen ts for queer studen ts. Toward this aim, some existing work has outlined recommended design principles in support of more equitable practices, such as highlighting identity variance and varied identities, and taking an activ ist stance that engages disruption and contradictio n (McWilliams, 2016; McWilliams & Penuel, 2016). Addi tionally, where queer theory and sociocultural theo ries of learning meet is at the importance of social int eraction and cultural artifacts (McWilliams & Penue l, 2016). With its focus on design and artifact production, o n process over product, and on making sense of fail ure, constructionism (Papert, 1980) finds synergy with q ueer theory. I thus theorize the intersections betw een constructionism and queer theory as a productive sp ace and a path forward to de sign and re-envision more equitable mathematics spaces.

As the name suggests, constructionism engages learn ers in the construction of knowledge, which is in the process of becoming, in a state of flux, much like queer theory’s concept of knowledge as not fixed and progress as not necessarily linear (e.g., Butle r, 1999). Papert (1980) posits that learning occurs  best through the production of personally meaningful  artifacts, whether that would be physical or digita l artifacts, what he calls “objects-to-think-with.” It is in the creation and interaction with artifacts that new knowledge gets integrated with and builds on old knowledge, with artifacts providi ng a more tangible representation of one’s mental m odels, facilitating understanding of complex ideas. Under constructionism, learning is envisioned as a proces s, with no right or wrong answer, that makes sense of failure through debugging. Queer theory perspectives add a critical lens to constructionism, crafting a space to productively tackle an exploration of design principles for a “queering” of mathematics t hat aims to disrupt and deconstruct current curricu lar practices that tend to perpetuate harmful norms.

Foundational works from Foucault, Sedgwick, and Butler have laid the groundwork for queer theory’s burgeoning theoretical base, anchoring it in key concepts like performativity, heteronormativity, and a binary that is to be challenged along with existing social and political power structures (e.g., Butler, 1999; Fou cault, 1990; Sedgwick, 1990). Butler coins the notion of gender performati vity as “a matter of reiterating or repeating the norms by which one is constituted,” where femininit y and masculinity are performed as dictated by what  is deemed socially compulsory and acceptable (Butler, 2003, p.22). In a similar vein, Butler writes of a heterosexual matrix, a grid of one’s sex, gender, and presumed sexuality, based upon the former two, shown to represent what is stereotypically perpetuated as the norm in society (e.g., male and masculine = straight, but female and masculine = queer) and fueling homophobic notions (Butler, 19 99). Governed by binaries, the heterosexual matrix thus builds a hegemonic idea of sex and gender as unilat erally informing sexuality, which is a reductive no tion that must be challenged posits Butler (1999). Stemming from this idea is also the notion of pervasive heteronormativity (Warner, 1993) whereby heterosexuality is perceived  as the norm, which regulates “all social relations  and all forms of thinking that exist  with these relations” (Sumara & Davis, 1999, p. 202). Beyond sexuality, heteronormative culture spans life events and their  temporalities (e.g., marriage, reproduction), with  notions of “queer time” and “queer space” challenging the accepted heteronormative order (Halberstam, 2005). Queer pedagogy and queering the curriculum In line with queer theory, over the past few decade s, existing work has investigated the possibility o f a queer pedagogy and queer curriculum in education to challenge and disrupt heteronormati ve structures, positioned as the norm and perpetuating inequities. Beyond “inter ruptions to heteronormative thinking [which] assist  in the important work of eliminating homophobia and hetero sexism in society”, queer pedagogy aims to shed lig ht on new ways of knowing and learning (Sumara & Davis, 2 002, p. 205). Early w ork along this line of research has primarily been situated in the context of reading and literature, such as Britzman’s foundational piece, “Is there a queer pedagogy? Or stop reading straight,” which po sits that the following are essentially the tenets of a queer pedagogy: questioning norms, limits, boundaries, and reading practices (Britzman, 1995; Pennell, 2020). Over time, additional tenets of queer pedagogy have emer ged, all similarly aimed at subverting normative pr actices. For instance, some of these tenets have included bu ilding comfort with uncertainty and not providing s tudents with immediate answers; initiating talk about gende r and gender norms where fitting; and incorporating  group work in student learning activities (Pennell, 2020). Luhman, on  the other hand, cautions that queer pedagogy’s aim to disrupt norms might be overly ambitious and “queer teaching, rather than assuming and affirming identities, [should] take on the problem of how identifications  are made and refused in the process of learning,” as a more apt goal (Luhman, 1998, p. 152).

Existing work on queering mathematics education fro m the past decade or so has fallen into different buckets: addressing inclusivity aka “add-queers-and-stir” (e.g., Rands, 2009; Richard et al., 2022), in corporating elements of social justice in the math classroom (e.g., Rands, 2009; Pennell & Fede, 2017), considering a queering of mathematical content (e.g., Davis & Sumara, 2000), and situating a greater need for change at the l evel of teacher education (e.g., Yeh & Rubel, 2020). With t he sociopolitical turn in math education (Gutiérrez, 2013), Dubbs (2016) identifies a need for a queer turn in math education, calling for research on queer mathe matics pedagogy that “from a center of ethical concern for queer students, rejects the heteronormative systems, structures, and discourses” (p.6). What  is missing in the literature is a more consolidated  approach to a queering of mathematics toward more equitable spaces at large f or queer students, which would benefit all students  in mathematics as well.

## Methods

For this qualitative study I collected interview data to better understand queer students’ experience in mathematics and, more broadly, what is signaling heteronormativ e sensitivity within current curricular practices a nd bringing about inequities. The author of this paper is a white cis queer woman with a background in mathematics, who also brings expertise as a learning scientist and mathem atics educator. Through an iterative coding process  and thematic analysis, I aim to uncover themes that cha racterize present curricular practices in mathemati cs, as experienced by queer youth, and work toward putting  forth design principles toward a more equitable transformation.

## Setting and participants

Situated in the context of a 4-year minority-serving institution in Southern California, this st udy draws on sem istructured interviews with queer-identifying undergraduate (n = 4) and graduate students (n = 4). Participants were recruited through various channels such as recruitm ent fliers, convenience, and snowball sampling, and represented different majors, both in STEM and non-STEM fields.  Participants were at different stages  in their program of study and had taken advanced courses in Mathematics or St atistics (e.g., Calculus, Linear Algebra, Differential Equations). Two additional c odes did not fall under any of the above, as they were manifested across all (Intersectionality, Binary Oppositions). Dedoose was used to code the interview data, where IRR was achieved with 20% of the data. In the last stages of analysis, I engaged in iterative thematic analysis to synthes ize codes and excerpts into themes, depicting the q ueer experience in mathematics and shedding light on cur ricular practices that need to be disrupted toward more equitable opportunities for queer, and all, students.

## Findings

These four themes depict aspects of current curricular practices and mathematics spaces, at large, that perpetuate systemic inequities, which are to be interrogated with an eye toward design for more equitable practices and mathematics learning spaces. In this section, each of the themes is discussed in greater detail. In the following Discussion and Implications section, I look ahead to propose design principles for ways to mitigate the present inequities in productive wa ys, as also informed by participant interviews and visions for the future. Sophia continued, “Thi s gives us this math that is supposed to be th is simple, sequential, binary thing, or like it's something that always has a specific outcome.” What this ends up perpetuating is an idea of mathematics that could alienate stude nts for one reason or another. In his response, Mig uel tied his queerness to this binary aspect of mathematics, saying, “When you're queer, I feel you’re very much on a continuum, instead of vibrating in a kind of discrete way,” suggesting that one’s queerness, by virtue of being on a continuum, may not align with a notion of mathema tics that is binary, and static, as opposed to being fluid and on a continuum.

Ray, an undergraduate student with a programming background, mused on the reason why he finds more enjoyment in programming, which, he said, is similar to mathematics in its logical approach and problem-solving. He said, “With math sometimes, if it's taught a cer tain way, the professor wants it that way on the ex am, right? It's very, very, very specific and just like, oh, no! Like the creativity aspect of it is gone. (...) But that's why I feel like programming is really cool, because I do have that freedom of being like, oh, I can approach it from this way or this way or this way.” Eliminating binary divide s between a right and wrong approach made programming more appealing to Ray, due to the embedded creative  freedom in pursuing an approach of one’s own choos ing. Outside of the right/wrong binary, which we discussed in more depth, other divides and oppositions that surfaced across the interviews included applied math vs. abs tract/theoretical math; math person vs. not a math person; STEM vs. humanities, which, in the interest of space, will not discuss in detail. What such oppositions reinforce, like the right/wrong opposition, is an oversimplified idea, a black and white perspective, reducing complexity out of habit and convenience. An added concern stemming  from this theme is a subsequent fear of failure, h arbored by students and impeding their learning.

Reinforcing neutrality Similarly to reinforcing the binary, across the data, 75% of the interviews (n = 6) evok ed reinforcing neutrality, a theme pertaining to moments in the data where participants commented on aspects of identity being suppressed in Math, or STEM at large. The theme further addres ses the underlying factors that reinforce and perpe tuate neutrality, often painting a line of co ntrast between Math and non-STEM fields. For example, Miguel said, “Humanities classes let you see a bit more of the h umanity of other people. Like, most of them are dis cussion based. (...) In comparison, in math classes, actual ly, you are not supposed to bring anything of you o r from your background to the table because it’s just, this is about integrals, it’s not about you.” Miguel’s obse rvation is in line with prior studies (e.g., Forbes, 2022). Certa inly class structures and activities seemingly leav e no space for one’s identity in mathematics, including one’s gender or queer identity, but what reinforces an air of neutrality is deeply embedded in a broader system of oppression, silencing minoritized groups in STEM and perpetuating a perception of what kind of people should pursue math or STEM, more broadly.  Reflecting on the spaces he’s in, Luis, a graduate student in Electrical Engineering commented, “Most of the people don't even know that I'm gay. Unless we talk about that, so I can say yes, I'm married. I’m gay, I’m married to a man (...) Sometimes people feel surprised about that. And that's the moment they re alize the diversity in th is STEM group. And it happens very often.” Luis’ observation reflects an implicit, often unchecked, bias and image of who gets to be a math or a STEM person, where, indeed, one’s queerness is often made invisible. Participants further commented on the importance of  the environment, as constituted by their teachers, classmates, and the space at large, for leaving spa ce for or marginalizing aspects of their identity, toward perpetuating a certain mold of who gets to do math.  Highlighting the importance of the environment, Ra y said, “When I was in an environment where it was positive, I think that those walls came down, in terms of h aving to compromise my identity or to shield my identity. (...) But in environments whe re there was negative reinforcement, it would often cause me to have to p resent a certain way where it's like, ‘Oh, I have t o be more quiet, or kind of like pitch down my voice a lot, a nd fit a certain like mold, in order to kind of ble nd in, or else, you know, you're kind of targeted.” Though maintaining an air of neutrality, math spaces could be more har mful in isolating and marginalizing otherness, including one’s queer identity, in turn reinforcing an image of who gets to do math. Similarly, Farah reflected, “I was very gender-nonconforming in high school, like I had, I had a buzz cut. (...)  I was very butch, and basically I had one math teacher that I felt like she did not seem super comfortable with how I was presenting.” Farah’s response, like Ray’s, highlights the importance of teachers and the environment, for either welcoming diversity or reinforcing neutrality, leaving no space for one’s identity. Math as fixed All the interviews (n = 8) evoked math as fixed, a theme pertaining to moments in the data where mat h is considered fixed, or stagnant, along multiple axes.  One aspect of the theme pertained to math as perpe tuating a fixed mode of instruction and interaction with students. Reflecting on this, Alice, a graduate student in Mechanical Engineering, said, “Professors need to not be pure lecture. (...) You know, example problems especially are a great active learning opportunity, but in math that opportunity doesn't get taken, like, it's just, here's the problem. Okay, you saw it, moving on.” Alice’s response points out  the instructionist tendencies of present curricular practices, or teaching practices that center the instructor as the main source of knowledge and a certain model of interaction with students that is perpetuated over time in some, if not most, math classes. Alice reasons that tha t is the case since “those professors just teach the way they wer e taught when they were students decades ago,” impl ying a level of pre-determined, or fixed, approach, being repeated acro ss time and space. Rowan, an undergraduate student in Psychology also notes this instructionis t tendency, drawing on their own experiences, “The hardest math class I've taken here wasn't really in the difficulty of the content itself, but more, in how it was taught, when the instructor was just kind of like saying a bunch  of concepts, but it wasn't really allowing us to h ands on apply them, like practice for ourselves.” Similarly to Alice, Rowan notes a need to learn about mathematics in cont ext, and in the context of doing mathematics, and shifti ng from an instructionist practice to a more studen t-centered, perhaps, constructionist approach.

Another aspect of the theme pertained to the linear  sequence one is expected to take classes in and th e reinforcement of required classes, essentially acting a s gatekeepers for students. For instance, Max, an undergraduate student in Biology said, “So calculus was required. The reason I took Calc courses is because Calc II and also a statistics course was required for the Bio major. (...) But I don't really think the class was that valuable or worth it at all. Because I did it. I learned all  the stuff. I knew all this stuff at the time. And then I just had no reason to retain any of that.” Having a required se quence of classes imposes a linearity to math that may or may not be beneficial to students. While following a fi xed sequence is beneficial when aptly preparing stu dents for their fields of study, imposing linearity without added value may be more harmful than not. More broadly, Sophia reflected on the content taught in mathematics clas ses, remaining unchanging over time, “I think math,  the same way as other like STEM fields is, for me, I don't know, it' s the field that both pushes evolution, but still w ants to revolutionize the slowest, compared to other fields, if that makes sense. But it's the one that, in terms of bringing new types of knowledge, it's very slow to do that. It's like we've been done and tried, and we know it works, let’s continue doing it.”  It is not just the sequence of  mathematics knowledge that gets enacted in the sam e way over time, but also the content itself. The reluctance to change and remaining rooted in past practices, tends to privilege one way of doing mathematics over another, in a way reinforcing a model of mathematics preserved over time by dominant groups in the field.

Math as gendered Across the data, 62.5% of the interviews (n = 5) evoked math as gendered, a theme, pertaining to moments in the data where participants discuss mathematics as a sp ace either for, or privileging, males, often as intersected with other aspects of their identity (e.g., white, straight). Thus, this theme is essentially characterizing math spaces also as heteronormative. For instance, reflecting on the question if math privileges certain identities over others, Alice said, “I think it does. And a lot of that is from, like I mentioned before, what interests are girls and boys expected to have. Math, like a lot of STEM, is an old boy's field. It's expected to be a lot of white men teaching other white men.” Gender, as discussed by Alice, and societal expectations, have evidently privileged dominant groups in the field, perpetuating this privilege across space and  time, and marginalizing other identities. As a res ult, as Sophia notes, this affects what math is being taught as we ll: “And if that’s what’s in the field, then by con sequence, we can think of the fact that their perspective is the  one that is shaping math. The tone is so strong as  is supposedly the [presence of] hetero normative males in the field.” Outside of represent ation then, more importantly, the fact that mathematics has been established as a space fo r dominant groups, has further marginalized non-dominant mathematics practices, enacting a cycle of inequity in representation and practice over time. In considering gender and mathematics, Miguel said,  “It's not just that it’s a male-dominated field. It’s also that you are invited to perform masculinity. You are invited to advocate for the correct algorithm, or the most effective one… And maybe I am being heteronormative myself, but maybe not. I feel like these are a lot of things that are brought by men.” Going beyond representati on and delving into gender as performance, Miguel interrogates other societal expectations and precon ceived roles ascribed t o men, aligning masculinity with the already established binary aspects of mathematics (e.g., one right approach, or one approach that is most effective). Thus, performing gender in mathematics spaces perpe tuates ingrained ideas of both binaries within mat hematics and socially constructed gender expectations. Pavin g a way forward would thus inevitably require a bre aking down of ideas of masculinity and binaries within ma thematics toward an approach that opens up the spac e for other groups’ engagement and meaningful participation.

## Discussion and implications

Through an analysis of the lived experiences of queer students in mathematics and an investigation of the factors that contribute to heteronormative and inequitable practices in math education, this study identified four themes depicting math practices at present that need to be mitigated toward more inclu sive and equitable opportunities. Queer theory as intersected with constructionism is  a suitable lens to use in deriving design principl es that could be used to disrupt and deconstruct present inequiti es. Limitations of the study lie in not surveying s tudents majoring in mathematics, with plans to conduct more  interviews in the future. However, the benefit of interviewing students across a variety of other STE M majors is that it reveals practices in mathematics that may have remained unnoticed by mathematics majors who have elected to pursue advanced studies in the field, already suggesting an enjoyment and interest in mathematics.

First, regarding reinforcing the binary, embracing an approach that values process over prod uct, in the constructionist tradition, and a multiplicity of ap proaches to address a problem, along with embracing  failure as an option, without fear of it, promises to disrupt the pervasive binary opposit ion of right vs. wrong. As Rowan reflected, what they enjoyed about their math class  were the “experiences when you know, failing to ge t the answer isn't like punished because it's more the ef fort, [which] creates a low-pressure environment for learning where I actually was incentivized to put in the wor k.” Moreover, taking this a step further and unequi vocally embracing failure as a possibility can enable creative approaches and breakthroughs (Halberstam, 2011), valuable for the learning in mathematics. As shown, reinforcing the binary also tends to reduce complexity for the sake of ease in teaching a set process. But experimentation, tinkering, and an openness to failure, all hold the promise to transform present practices by embracing math in the messy instead. Second, regarding reinforcing neutrality, elevating the value of human connections as a key c omponent in math spaces holds the promise of meaningful transformation. Through embracing the strengths of good teachers and mentors, and an environment, where, in Ray’s wo rds, “ those walls [can] come down, in terms of hav ing to compromise my identity or to shield my identity,” m ath spaces can transform to a point where identitie s are not marginalized. As Miguel said, “It would be super co ol if you could bring you r humanity into the math class.” Thus, a path ahead is to consider a possibility whe re identity is not disjointed from the curricular a ctivities in mathematics.

Third, regarding math as fixed, embracing the converse, math as fluid in t he design of learning environments, aligns with many of the tenets of bot h constructionism and queer theory (e.g., knowledge  as not fixed, and in the process of becoming, and progress  as not necessarily linear). Furthermore, as Miguel  pointed out, transforming fixed participation structures in the classroom has the potential to change not only the way one participates in math, but the way one does math. He  said, “Maybe diversifying the way you participate in the classroom will help people of any gender identity o r sexual preference help configure safe spaces. Som etimes participating in math class is saying, ‘Oh, I think the answer is this. Oh, I solv ed the problem like this.’ (...) But maybe, math class is also a place where we explore colors. Maybe math class is also a class in which I  use my body in different ways.” Turning fixed math practic es from instructionist to constructionist, for instance, could create more space for students  to construct their knowledge through different part icipant structures, modalities, and materials for math learning across contexts.

Lastly, regarding math as gendered, a meaningful transformation – efforts and evidence of which have been the focus of existing research – would require the breaking down of notions of mascu linity and eliminating the need to perform masculinity as part of engageme nt with mathematics by perhaps investigating and qu eering the tools and materials of mathematics, what has be en left out of the discourse so far, but holds the promise to construct, from the ground up, new opportunities. Future work would investigate such a queering of mathematics.
