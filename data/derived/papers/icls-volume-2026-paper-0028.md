# Playing A/Part: Considering Directors in Ensemble Learning

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Learning in ensembles is “learning…that cannot be done alone” (Ma & Hall, 2018, p. 508). While researchers have examined the infrastructure necessary to sustain ensemble learning, they have yet to fully consider the role of the teacher(s) and the “part” they play in relation to th e ensemble. In this paper, we explore the range of ways directors participate in ensembles and examine how their practices position them in relation to the wider group. We ground our discussion in video ethnographic cases of a college choir and an undergraduate theatre ensemble. We present four critical cases: two in which ensemble directors call attention to their unique role in the ensemble and two in which directors’ participation positions them as “just another” member of the ensemble ––although within limits. These findings highlight the fluid nature of directorial participation and raise important questions about theorizing directors’ membership in an ensemble’s community of practice.

Learning scientists are increasingly exploring ensemble spaces because they recognize that learning in these contexts has particular properties that advance our theoretical understandings of collaboration. At their core, ensembles necessarily require participants to work together in order to accomplish their aim (e.g., Leander et al. 2023; Ma & Hall, 2018). These ensembles can vary broadly and encompass everything from dance troupes and multi-person theatre performances to orchestras and hockey teams. Ma and Hall (2018) call the learning that happens in these spaces ensemble learning and describe it as learning where “members understand the activity is done together, performance requires a group, and learning must happen in group performance” (p. 509). While not all ensembles have an explicit leader, many rely on a conductor, director, or coach. Even in professional (as opposed to school-based) ensembles, these leaders carry a pedagogical responsibility to the wider group. When examining the role of teachers in ensemble contexts, researchers have tended towards a causeand-effect model in which instructors’ behavior is studied on its own or in terms of how their choices cause changes in the ensemble’s learning and performance (e.g., Corbalán et al., 2023). However, these analyses reveal a conceptual slippage: Is the teacher part of the ensemble, or are they not? After all, when we speak of learning in a football team, college choir, or theatre troupe, we typically assume we are talking about the performers. Yet we readily accept that the ensemble would not be as successful without a director. It is not always clear to what extent the director should be seen as apart from the ensemble, and to what degree they should be seen as a part of the ensemble.

As we begin to understand ensemble teaching that emerges from within the ensemble (Hollett et al., 2022), we need scholarship that considers the participatory role of directors in ways that include, but are not limited to, their teaching function. In this study, we examine two different ensemble contexts—choir singing and devised theatre—to explore the roles that directors take on in relation to their ensembles. We find these practices to encompass a range from “apart from” to “a part of” the rest of the ensemble. We also note the way both of these modes retain participatory divisions: directors never truly become “a part of” the ensemble in the same way other members are; they only move closer to or further from this positioning through practices that are s imilar to or different from other ensemble members’ practices.

In exploring these two different ensemble settings, we therefore examine the research question: What is the relationship between ensembles and their facilitators? More specifically, what are the practices that ensemble directors employ and how do those practices indicate their participation in or relation to the wider ensemble? In our data, we explore two types of practices. The first involves explicit pedagogical work in which directors position themselves as distinct from the ensemble, either as necessary to its functioning or as separate from its eventual independence. The second represents a different kind of participation, in which directors move with the ensemble as they perform, as though they were “just another” ensemble member ––even as crucial distinctions remain. Looking across these practices highlights the range of ways directors are both with in and outside of the ensembles they direct.

Prior literature on instruction in ensembles In the choral education literature, there is significant research that focuses on the conductor. Much of this research centers around conductors’ pedagogical practices, such as their overall approach and perspective on choral 197 teaching (e.g., Corbalán et al., 2019), how they structure rehearsal and use scaffolding (e.g., Freer, 2009), how they organize giving instruction and feedback (e.g., Emerson et al., 2019), how they work to create community (Parker, 2016), and necessary skills and competencies (Durantt, 2017). This literature base illuminates how choir directors understand and organize choir learning environments, but it tends to focus on the instructor’s actions. Comparatively less work has effectively explored the implications of taking the ensemble as the unit of analysis. Across theatre education, similar literature examines the director’s pedagogical function. In her work on devised theatre, Mia Perry (2011) writes that Western theatre history witnessed an evolution from actor/actor-manager to playwright to director over the span of two centuries. She notes that this change over time created a hierarchical relation to the ensemble, one in which the director works with actors in “service” of the play––be that their own vision, or the playwrights’ (Perry, 2011). While other s cholars have likewise noted the “apprentice” - like nature of an actor and their (traditionally Western) director, scholarship seems to remain focused on how the director leads actors toward “greater independence, progression and creativity” (McNamara, 2018,  p. 155). Indeed, much of this literature is labeled “actor training,” implying a coaching relationship between the actor and the director in which  the director critiques the actor towards improvement in their skill (Kornetsky, 2017). Less literature has e xplored the pedagogical nature of devising practices, though research that does has kept a closer eye on the mediating role the director plays in negotiating ideas in rehearsal spaces (Perry, 2011). These bodies of research represent substantial fields of study with immense implications for understanding effective ensemble pedagogy. However, a learning sciences perspective on ensembles, which privileges studying learning in situ and in interaction, has been underrepresented in these fields. The learning sciences are well positioned to learn from and enrich these conversations through theories and methods attuned to how learning unfolds moment  by moment. A growing body of learning sciences scholarship has begun to fill this gap by examining ensemble learning as it unfolds in real time across a range of performance contexts. Ensemble learning involves rehearsing the coordination needed to perform as  a collective. Such coordination responds to changes in the wider ensemble and therefore requires increasing relational attunement. As examples, Ma and Hall’s (2018) study of a marching band examined how members learn to move as a unit through coordinated practice, revealing ensemble learning as a form of heavily infrastructured activity that requires specific material and social configurations. Hollett et al.’s (2022) study of a bal let variations class explored how shared movements contributed to a sense of the ensemble’s cohesion and noted the ways teacher direction depended on an affective and entangled relation to the ensemble. Leander et al.’s (2023) study of an improv troupe analyzed how ensemble members develop a capacity to “read each other’s energy” and respond in the moment. These studies have advanced a learning sciences perspective on the ensemble as a distinct (though perhaps anomalous; Perry, 2010) place of learning that involves developing interdependence and an increasingly tuned relational capacity to achieve joint tasks. Across these ensemble settings, learners often shift between individual and collective participation (Malm, 2020; Powell, 2005; Vogelstein, 2020). However, the treatment of teachers and directors across these studies varies considerably. In some cases, the instructor’s role is analyzed primarily as a pedagogical one, where an external coach scaffolds or structures the ensemble’s work (Ma & Hall, 2018). In other cases, the instructor appears more peripherally in the analysis, with greater focus on peer-to-peer interaction and collective sense-making (Leander et al., 2023). Looking across this work provides an opportunity to better advance our theoretical understanding of the role directors play in ensemble activity. To this point, when we theorize “ensemble learning,” how should we best understand the director’s contributions given both their participation and their unique role as part of that ensemble ?

## Context and methodology

Given the state of the field, we argue that it is necessary to better understand the kinds of things ensemble directors do and interpret their participation relative to what ensemble members do. In doing so, we seek to spark a conversation about the fluid nature of directorial participation and its implications for ensemble learning. A strength of our approach is that we examine what is common across two very different ensembles: choir and theatre. We were particularly intrigued by the fact that choir conductors remain with the ensemble during performance, visibly cueing and coordinating the group, while theatre directors “leave” their ensemble for the performance, watching from the audience as the actors perform independently. Both spaces are ensemble learning spaces that meet the criteria that Ma and Hall (2018) articulate (that learning is done together, performance requires a group, and learning happens in group performance). Thus, despite their differences, both contexts share a core structural similarity as learning environments, making them productive sites for conversation to examine the varied ways directors participate in ensemble work. We examine two ensemble contexts. Suraj’s setting was a college-level choir over the course of the semester, led by a conductor. Lana observed an undergraduate theatre ensemble over the course of a semester as they composed a devised piece inspired by Hamlet. The theatre ensemble’s work was supported by two movement 198 directors: Eleanor and Lynn. The data discussed below are drawn from these larger projects, which, in both cases, involved intensive observation, interviews, and video recording of rehearsals and performances. Our work draws on video ethnography of learning (Goldman, 2007; Derry et al., 2010) to engage in deep necessary (Jordan & Henderson, 1995). While this  paper is primarily conceptual, our analytic approach is grounded in empirical data from these two ethnographic contexts. For our analytical process, we first chronicled insights from each context that were relevant to ensemble learning practices. We then discussed how these phenomena related to one another and narrowed our focus to the teaching or learning mechanisms that were present in similar but different ways across both ensembles. This led to our choice of an analytic focus on the range of ways ensemble directors participated in their ensembles. Next, we carefully examined our entire datasets for instances that were critical cases (Patton, 1990) of the phenomenon of interest: directors’ positioning relative to the rest of the ensemble. We then specifically sought examples that represented extremes along a continuum of participation;  instances where directors explicitly distinguished themselves from the ensemble, and instances where directors’ actions most closely resembled those of other ensemble members.

Once we selected these critical cases, we analyzed each through a process of transcription, turn-by-turn case analysis (Bartlett & Varus, 2017), we found it generative to both examine each case, putting the two contexts into conversation to uncover crucial similarities and differences. The two types of cases ––apart from and a part of––cohered into our analytic categories. Throughout this process, we enga ged in dialogue as we analyzed each case to see emergent overlaps and tensions between the two settings. Our writing was also part of our analytic process (Richardson, 1994) and was a crucial aspect of putting our data into conversation with our theoretica l framework and analytic focus.

## Within the choir context: “Look at the director.”

In this extract, students are learning the last few measures of the piece Double, Double, Toil and Trouble (arr. Jaakko Mäntyjärvi, text by William Shakespeare). At the very end of the piece, after the last note is sung, all singers stomp one foot in unison.

Before this case begins, in this rehearsal sopranos, altos, and tenors have practiced the last few measures of the piece including the stomp. So far, the stomping has been synchronous to the conductor’s satisfaction, as evidenced by the fact that he did not comment on it. He asks the bass section to “add yours in there.” The basses sing their part, but when it comes time to stomp, two individuals’ stomps come hearably later than all the others. The instructor then gives this direction: “Get the timing on th e footstomp right or you're gonna be stomping solo (laughter).” This is the first time in any rehearsal that the conductor has given feedback on the stomp. In this utterance, the conductor positions the stomp as in need of improvement and, in conversation analytic terms, his comment is a bid to repair it (Kitzinger, 2012). By commenting at all, he calls attention to the fact that some aspect of the prior performance was not ideal. His bid to repair does not come with any particular directions to help students stomp at the correct time, but rather is an instruction to “get the timing right.” This instruction is somewhat redundant in that students know they have to get the timing right; the difficulty is in doing so. The conductor’s comment that otherwise “you’re gonna be stomping solo” is meant to be somewhat humorous, as evidenced by him and other members of the choir laughing after he says it. The comment also functions to outline a potential consequence of the unsynchronous stomp. Since in the choir the norm is not to be solo, but rather be part of a whole, it is clear that “stomping solo” is an undesirable outcome. The conductor then asks all sections to sing the last two measures of the piece. At the time of the stomp, he makes a stomping gesture with his hands, pushing his hands towards the ground. He also stomps along with the students. This time, all the stomps are synchronized. The conductor then directs them to do the last fourteen measures. This time, there are again two extra stomps that come hearably after the rest of the choir. This prompts the following interaction: Conductor:   So if you're not sure where to put the foot cue, what should you do? At least two students:  Not do it.

Conductor:   Not do it, or, there's another one.

199 Student:    Use your pencil Conductor:   (cups his hands around his mouth) Look at the director *laughing and muttering from the choir* Conductor:   that's kind of what I'm for is to let you know when to do that. So let me do my job.

As the interaction proceeds, the instructor is clearly seeking a particular answer, “Look at the director.” He goes on, “that’s kind of what I’m for is to let you know when to do that.” This sentence has several discursive functions. First, the conductor positions coordinating their timing as his purpose (“what I’m for”). He also explains the purpose of a conductor (“to let you know when to do that”), establishing a version of what the conductor’s role within an ensemble is as to cue synchronous sound-making. He continues, “so let me do my job,” further positioning such cueing as the role, job, or work of his alone and the role of the students as to orient to him in this way.

This series of events culminates with the conductor making a specific and explicit statement that he has a particular, unique role in the ensemble. That role is described as important and necessary to the ensemble’s success. Of course, the conductor has t acitly been separate from others in the group—he stands at the front and governs their rehearsals, after all—but he rarely calls explicit attention to it. In this case, he is prompted to do so by a repeated error, one which had appeared to be fixed but then broke down again. What is significant about this series of events is that the conductor calls everyone’s focus to his status as a special member of the ensemble, as having a particular job that is not their job. The move also functions pedagogically to get the synchronicity right. Unlike his first attempt at repair, which merely mentioned that there was a synchronicity problem and that students had to get it right, this turn provides an explanation for how students might get it right: by watching him. Indeed, this intervention seemed to work; the students’ stomps were synchronized throughout the rest of the rehearsal.

## Within the theatre context: “You are an ensemble.”

In an early rehearsal, Eleanor (one of the two movement directors, and the only one present in this scene) introduces the actors to an ensemble-building exercise called The Cup Game, where players stand in a circle and keep a paper cup in the air by hitting it upward, counting each pass. Over three rehearsals, Eleanor participates in the game alongside the actors while also providing instruction. As they develop a passing rhythm together, she offers guidance such as hitting the cup “gently, so that it floats” and passing “to our ensemble, not away.” Along the way, as the group experiences “tears” (Ma & Hall, 2018) that cause the cup to drop, she interrupts choruses of “nooo!”s and laughter with reminders to “resist distraction” and “stay focused.” After a series of poor passes, she has the group “scramble” positions and take a collective breath to settle the new formation. Her directions and repairs distinguish her as a leader, but her language of “our ensemble,” coupled with her physical position in the circle, marks her as another actor in the ensemble.

In the fourth rehearsal, Eleanor shifts her role dramatically. She brings out the cup but announces that she must step away to attend to “director-related” tasks. She passes the cup to the actors and says, “You  are an ensemble and you cando this without me,” leaving the group to direct itself. This marks a clear repositioning of her role in relation to the group: Eleanor’s language shifts from “ our ensemble” (which included herself) to “you are an ensemble” (which explicitly excludes her). By stepping outside the playing circle and naming her separate “director-related” responsibilities, she draws a boundary between her role and the actors’. Initially, the actors are hesitant, dropping the cup repeatedly and dissolving into laughter. One actor intervenes: “Guys! Ensemble!,” echoing Eleanor’s earlier directive to “stay focused.” As the game continues, other actors revive Eleanor’s instructions from previous rehearsals, reminding each other to “pass to your neighbor” and “float the cup.” Eventually, they also  use Eleanor’s reset strategy to “scramble and take a breath.” As the rounds go on, they progressively hit higher numbers of cup passes.

In this sequence, Eleanor makes a specific statement about her role in the ensemble: By telling the actors they “cando this without me” and physically removing herself from the circle, she positions the ensemble as capable of functioning independently of  her. While she obviously holds authority as a director, Eleanor’s participation in the circle blurs the distinction between ensemble leader and member. By declaring the actors capable and physically removing herself, she creates conditions for the group to direct itself internally, rather than with external guidance.

This reveals a pedagogical strategy for fostering ensemble autonomy. While Eleanor’s earlier interventions modeled specific techniques for playing well, her separation from the group encourages the actors to feel as though they already have what they need to succeed. The strategy proves effective; as actors retrieve and apply her earlier instructions, self-directing, they successfully play the game. In a later interview, Eleanor 200 revealed that while she did need to step away during this rehearsal, she finds it important to stage a moment where the actors have to play the game without her. “It’s kind of a test to see if the group candirect themselves,” she said, “They have to believe they can trust each other.” She views this as a pedagogical move towards nurturing the ensemble’s independent ecology ––a belief that the actors can rely on one another to accomplish something, even without a director in the room. This is particularly crucial for theatre, given that directors rarely ever perform with their actors once a show goes up. Setting them up with the right tools and then leaving them to do the work, she argues, is her way of teaching them to be prepared for that separation. Considering apart from In both contexts, these practices reveal a proleptic approach to ensemble teaching, that is, a focus on the ensemble’s future needs and a tailoring of teaching methods to address those needs (whether the goal is dependence or independence). Across both ensembles, we see how specific practices served as pedagogical scaffolding. In the choir rehearsal, the conductor scaffolds a future where he will always be present to cue the group. When the stomp falls out of sync, he explicitly positions himself as the solution. This builds the ensemble’s capacity for director-led performance and teaches them to depend on him. In the theatre data, by contrast, the movement director scaffolds a future where she will be absent. She positions the actors as the ensemble and physically removes herself from the circle. As a result, the group has to use the tools she’s previously given them to succeed on their own, teaching them to function independently of her. Despite these opposite aims, both approaches share a key feature: the director positions themselves as separate or different from the other ensemble members. The choir conductor does this by establishing himself as the person to watch and sync up with, while Eleanor does it by explicitly stepping outside of the circle and naming her “director-related” responsibilities. Both “you  can’t do this without me” and “you cando this without me” functionally demarcate the director as somewhat apart from their ensemble. Directors perform as part of the ensemble In this section, we examine cases where the director’s participation more closely resembled that of everyone else in the ensemble. Of course, the director is often working within the ensemble (Hollett et al., 2022), but the cases here are unusual in that the director also performs the actions that the rest of the ensemble does.

## Within the choir context: Dancing to the music

At the final performance, the students sang an upbeat piece called Down by the Riverside. As they sang, the conductor danced (moving his hips and hands to the rhythm) and mouthed along with all of the words. It is not possible to hear his voice specifically on the audio record, nor was it possible to hear it in the concert venue. Therefore, we conclude that he was mouthing along, rather than fully singing. Mouthing and dancing could play a pedagogical function in that they may help students synchronize their sounds. However, the utility of mouthing is debated (Bland, 2020). Conductors note its potential advantages as informing vowel shape during rehearsals. It also may help convey to students the desired facial expression or tonal quality of a particular piece. Its potential disadvantages include that students focus too much on the mouth and not enough on the conductor’s (presumably more informative) hand gestures and that it compromises the conductor’s ability to listen fully to the choir (Bland, 2020). In this way, even when pedagogical, this kind of mouthing (and dancing) could be substituted for better pedagogy (i.e., hand gestures). Nonetheless, even experienced conductors admit to mouthing often and unintentionally. As the conductor dances and mouths along, he may be seeking to convey a particular vibe to the students and elicit a specific (in this case, joyous) quality to the music they sing back. However, we speculate that there was also a kind of irresistibility in which the conductor simply could not help “getting into” the music. We support this speculation by the idea that this vibe could have been conveyed in a “smaller” way, with less intensi ve dance movements. In addition, there was also the emotional nature of the music that perhaps beckoned participation. As one choir director puts it, mouthing along during performance is “a habit I wish I never developed but joy is hard to contain” (W. Frankonis, personal communication, September 24, 2025). In this case, the conductor’s behaviors, and specifically his embodied behaviors, are the same as other choir members. He is not just instructing but also performing. But importantly, there are still limits. Even in such an uninhibited moment, the conductor is conscious to mouth and not actually sing. At the same time, his dances are, if anything, larger than the students’, as he is not constrained by bodies on either side of him or standing on risers. The conductor is certainly still conscious that he is not in fact in the ensemble. The conductor almost crosses over in this action from his role as a separate, powered member of the choir over to “just another” singer. Yet he retains his remove, demonstrating the limits of the conductor’s ability to position himself as a typical member of the ensemble.

## Within the theatre context: Moving with the lift

In a rehearsal a week before their performance, Lynn and Eleanor helped the cast establish their “flight call,” a practice run of a lift sequence where the ensemble raises the actress playing Ophelia above their heads. Before they run through the lift, Eleanor stresses that “how you rehearse it is how you perform it” and voices that both she and Lynn, as directors, won’t be present before every show so the group needs to “be able to feel comfortable” doing it on their own. This echoes Eleanor’s earlier positioning of the ensemble as independent from the directors. And yet, as the actors rehearse the lift sequence, both Lynn and Eleanor move in a “metronome” fashion with the activity (Ćosić, 2023). This entrainment (that is, the synchronization of the movement of one body with another) with the group is visible in th e way Lynn sways upward and downward with the ensemble as they lift and land: Lynn rocks forward on her left foot as Ophelia goes into the air and kneels downward with her right leg as Ophelia comes down, tracing the lift pattern into the air and back to the ground (Figure 1). As the cast runs through the sequence again, she sits and continues to follow the movements with her seated body as though she were doing the lift with them, even though she has no part in the action. At first glance, these movements could be understood as modeling pacing for the performers. However, the performers cannot see Lynn, nor should they be watching her when they need to focus on the person suspended above them. Instead, Lynn seems to be movi ng with the ensemble rather than for them. It is possible that this embodied tracking helps her monitor the lift’s momentum and trajectory, giving her information about the success of the lift sequence. Some of Lynn’s participation might also be explained by an affective attunement, that is, an embodied resonance with the intensity of the choreography. They seem to follow the movement with their eyes as much as with their bodies, responding as if  they too were launching someone skyward. Here, the boundary between director and ensemble member blurs. Lynn and Eleanor’s embodied responses suggest a kind of membership in the ensemble’s collective action. However, crucial distinctions still remain: the y sit apart from the actors, in the 202 audience, observing rather than executing. Even as their bodies move as if they belong to the ensemble, their actual physical positions being offstage mark them as outside of it. The directors approach, but cannot fully cross, the line separating their leadership role from ensemble participation. Considering A Part Of Across both ensembles, we notice an extraneous type of activity that is not necessarily pedagogical but almost feels like the director can’t resist being part of the performance. Both the conductor and the director moving with the ensemble indicates something about following the work that the ensemble is doing. On the one hand, there is something about the professional role of a director that include s a way to gauge the “success” of the work being done, to follow along with it and catch its progress. We see this in the way that mouthing would indicate a misplaced word or vowel, or entraining in a metronome fashion would catch a glitch in the rhythm of  the lift. However, given that these movements are not necessary to the performance at hand, we speculate that there is an element of this movement that has to do with the artistic repertoire of the director, a kind of siren call from the ensemble to entrain and participate.

In the choir case, although the gestures and dancing could help synchronize the group and convey a particular tonal quality, there is a redundancy to these actions; the choir is already synchronized by the conductor’s hand and has already presumably been instructed about tonal quality in a way that did not require the conductor to mouth along and dance through the entire piece. Similarly, in the theatre case, the actors cannot see Lynn, which means that her movements are not meant to pedagogically re-orient the actors, but rather to follow along with their movements, as though she were a part of the group. These are also perhaps affectively charged moments; the conductor is participating in the final performance of an upbeat song, and the movement director is following a moment with a great deal of tension  where the actress playing Ophe lia is at risk of falling. Across these two examples, the directors seem closest to being “just another” member of the ensemble—a part of the group—but both have limits: neithe r is actually participating fully, providing evidence that the directors have retained consciousness of their separate role.

In this work, we wondered: What are the practices that ensemble directors employ,  and how do those practices indicate their participation or relation to the wider ensemble? To address this, we examined four critical cases across two ensembles, a choir and a devised theatre group. The first two cases illustrated instances in which the director of the ensembles explicitly positioned themselves in a directorial role, separate from but related to the ensemble. In the choir case, the instructor noted that, as the director, he had a specific role to play in synchronizing everyone else. In the theatre case, the movement director exited a group exercise by noting that the group would be able to succeed without her. Both cases revealed instances in which the directors, in very different ways, called attention to the fact that their role in the ensemble was not like everyone else’s, and in so doing separated themselves from the ensemble body. The second two cases contrasted by examining scenarios in which the directors actually performed the embodied actions that other ensemble members did. The choir director mouthed the words and danced. While this played some pedagogical function––conveying t o the choir a particular tone, facial expression, and energy ––we interpreted this action as also a kind of getting lost in making music together, although he refrained from actually singing out loud. Similarly, the theatre movement directors moved their bodies with the ensemble, even though none of the actors could see them and thus there was no pedagogical function. We interpreted this entrainment as partially related to developing an internal sense of the ensemble’s rhythm during the lift, but also about the irresistibility of following the embodied actions that everyone else is doing in multi-party ensemble interaction. Both cases also seem potentially distinguished by an affective extreme. These findings benefit from comparing and contrasting two different ensemble contexts to create a more general understanding of ensemble direction. We demonstrate that ensemble directors engage in a range of participatory stances, from close to distant participation with/in the ensemble. Ensemble directing is a complex and social pedagogical practice that involves responsive participation with the ensemble. Directors fluidly participate as a part of and apart from the group they are directing. Of course, b y examining these critical cases, we risk simplifying the extremely wide range of ways directors could participate. Our goal is not to suggest a binary conception of in-ness or out-ness, but rather to highlight how different instructor actions can be conceptualized or understood in relation to those of the learners in the ensemble. What prompted our analysis was the question of if the director should be considered as part of the ensemble. Based on our analysis, we argue that they always are, even as they transition between close participation (part of) and more distant forms of participation (apart from). Participating in the ensemble as a director involves some form of this role-shifting. In our two contexts, we showed the directors distinguishing themselves from the 203 ensemble can also be a way of participating with the ensemble. Even as directors distinguish themselves as different from the rest of the group, they still scaffold the ensemble’s success: the choir conductor offers the cue, and the actors follow Eleanor’s previous directions. In both cases, the directors remain “present” within the ensemble, even if separated. Likewise, even when directors perform movements that make them appear like other performers, they still maintain a partially removed stance. They literally stand away from the group, seated in the audience or positioned at the front. We argue that there is more to be understood about the fluid and messy nature of leadership positioning in ensemble learning.

This work is significant for both learning scientists who study ensemble learning specifically, as well as the facilitation of collaborative learning broadly. We welcome further research on moments like the ones we illustrate above, in which directors do the performance actions of the ensembles. How do their identities as artists contribute to their entrainment with the ensemble? How do their own experience as non-instructor participants in ensembles color their performance-while-instructors? In building on this work, ensemble researchers might also further identify how directors make decisions about their positionings and the interactional process by which those positionings are responsive to their perception of ensembles’ performances.
