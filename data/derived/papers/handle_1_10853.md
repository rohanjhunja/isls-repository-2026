# Applying Syncretic Frameworks in the Learning Sciences

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Syncretism has been proposed as a framework that is valuable for understanding learning. Four papers across a range of formal and informal instructor-led contexts take up concepts from Gutiérrez (2014) like syncretic literacies and syncretic designs for learning in service of shared questions: How can teachers design for syncretic learning in practice? What does it look like to put syncretism in conversation with academic contexts/formal schooling in generative youth-centric ways? Where should the learning sciences gonext with respect to syncretic designs for learning?

Symposium overview Syncretism as a framework for learning Learning is not bound to any one context; in fact, learning is always happening as we move across diff erent learning ecologies that shape our lives and experiences (Gutiérrez, 2014). This view of learning is focused on how learners "[organize] for possible  futures" (Gutiérrez, 2008, p. 154) as they navigate  between spaces. Educators and communities designing for equity aim to make se nse of tensions between formal and informal learnin g experiences. Syncretic learning describes the expan sive forms of learning  that occur when youth integrate everyday and school-based contexts and practices (Gutiérrez, 2014; Gutiérrez & Jurow, 2016; Radke et al., 2022). This theoretical orientation values “horizontal lea rning,” or the ways that learners develop and integ rate knowledge across contexts, including out-of-school contexts, to build new ways of knowing and b eing in the world. Syncretic designs seek to reorganize the rel ationships and tensions between everyday and school-based practices while looking toward reorganizing for more just futures. Syncretic literacies and syncretism as a design principle in instructor-led educational contexts Syncretic literacies, social sensemaking practices that cross boundaries, help understand the re-mediation of activity systems to level traditional scientific-everyday hierarchies as educators and youth leverag e expansive linguistic repertoires across home and academic spa ces. Gutiérrez (2014) charges researchers to recogn ize and study syncretic literacies to consider interest-based repertoires of youth and “how individuals develop, repurpose, and reorganize [these] repertoires” (p. 48). More r ecently, Lizárraga and Gutiérrez (2018) advanced the concept of syncretic literacies in examining a critical case of everyday practices of Latinx working-class families, naming nepantla literacies  as, “literacies that thrive in the boundary, emerge  through negotiations with syncretic literacies” (p. 39). They argue that by engaging sy ncretic literacies, youth negotiate the creation of  liminal identities in boundary spaces that allow them to ta ke up their full “linguistic and sociocultural toolkits t o learn” (p. 40). The symposium papers here apply conceptions of syncretic literacies to participant learning. Syncretic literacies are not confined to any particular learning contexts, and consideration of “what takes hold” (Gutiérrez, 2014, p. 48) as students navigate  different spaces allows educators to better design  learning spaces allowing for processes of development, repurposing, and reorganization. As formal environments inhibit– or even prohibit–full expression of their linguistic and knowledge repertoires, students confront tensions that may not be recognized in learning designs. Syncretic design offers a frame tonegotiate how tensions can be generative for learning, particularly as they foster potential for something “new” to emerge. The papers in this symposium examine formal educati onal spaces: classrooms, online courses, and a school library. All the contexts are learning envir onments where “academic” language, tasks, and perfo rmance are often privileged, and where instructors help gu ide teaching and learning as designers. Educators a cross the cases show evidence of identifying and/or responding to tensions between the academic and everyday, in cluding unmarking such boundaries (Vogelstein et al., 2023). The symposium investigates how  everyday knowledge is foregrounded and emergent tensions are framed–and leveraged–as generative for learning. These include: 1) youth navigating syncretic ways of claim-making across disciplinary and fandom communities i n a formal classroom;

Following these presentations, Discussant Kris Gutiérrez will comment on the pedag ogies and designs across the cases and productive w ays forward for syncretic design in the Learning Scienc es and education more generally. Time will be held for conversation with attendees on a shared whiteboard open to both online and virtual participants.

## Significance

Designs for syncretic learning are mainly centered in literacy-related contexts, often in informal settings. However, Gutiérrez (2014) argues that leveraging th e everyday in school spaces is essential to address ing the disconnect between formal and informa l learning. Our goal is to speak to and influence f ormal educational contexts, particularly with respect to inequities. Thus, we ask: How can teachers design for syncretic  learning in practice? How do we put syncretism in conversation with academic contexts/formal schooling? Where should the learning sciences gonext with respect to syncretic designs for learning? Paper 1: Navigating syncretic claims across fan and disciplinary spaces Karis Jones, Scott Storm and Sarah Beck

## Purpose and theoretical framework

This case analyzes interpretive claim-making across fandom and disciplinary communities, drawing data from writing conferences in a U.S.-based fandoms-themed English course serving diverse students. Dis cursive claims emerged as a syncretic tool across fandom and disci plinary communities. Adolescent learners are expect ed to practice navigation (Moje, 2008) across different discourse communities as they move from one school subject to another. We use this metaphor of navigation to furt her theorize syncretic literacies that “put everyda y (from the home and community) and disciplinary literacy practices (from school and institutions) in relation to one another” (Lizárraga &  Gutiérrez, 2018, p. 39). Particularly, we are inter ested in how BIPOC youth hybridize practices between fandom communities and disciplinary literary scholarship.

## Methodology

This study took place in a twelfth-grade English literature class in a large urbandistrict in the northeastern U.S. In this course, youth and teachers iteratively  co-designed a curriculum focused on supporting youth navigation across fandom discourses. The designers (Author2 and Author1) conceptualized the discipline  of literary study as a type of fandom, thereby placing it on the same level as youth-selected fandoms. Our exploration of youth navigation across interpretive communities  comprises a series of  multi-participant writing conferences in which one of the co-authors (Author1) was acting in the teacher role, e ngaging in 1-1 dialogic assessment conversations and giving students space to carry on such conversations with one another. To trace focal students’ learning, we used an analy tic matrix from Bomer and Laman’s (2004) work on positionality in writing conferences to closely exa mine shifts in self-and other-positioning. We analyzed them through a series of analytic memos around evidence of convergent or divergent intersubjectivity in mom ents of dialogic assessment. We noticed a pattern: moments of divergent intersubjectivity often happened speci fically around claim-making. We looked for discontinuities in our focal student Jewel’s practices using claims across disciplinary and fandom systems —such as a moment where she stopped another student’ s claim-making, interjecting with “Listen!” and explaining the problem with that claim—as well as moments when such boundaries were more porous.

## Findings and implications

One of the goals of this course was for youth to understand the metalinguistic differences in language use between these two communities and critically consider ways to upend or remix linguistic structures. This presentation will examine a series of conversations between focal students Esmaily and Jewel about the TV show Lucifer, ranging from informal chats to official dialogic assessment sessions with Author1. We argue that the two students do not just navigate between practices but actually bring practices together. For instance, when talking about the casting of Lucifer, Jewel both shares her affective evaluat ion (“I just hate how…”) and a critical observation  about the show’s casting choices (“He's the only dark skin th ere. Like he stands out… besides mains, it’s him”).  We argue that Jewel is using her fandom experience to construct an original claim in the fandom discourse, a highly valued literary interpretive activity. The convergence of personal evaluations and critical observations implies that there is emotional charge to both the character and the way the character is constructed—that Jewel finds it problematic that this boring and frustrating character is the o nly Black character in that season of the show. We argue that Jewel is using critical too ls around race to think about reactions to content and make a unique claim about the (literary) effect of the creators’ choices on the viewers. Jewel’s analysis took up critical race tools—tools that she had both developed from her experiences as a Latinx  woman and used in class to analyze the course text  Their Eyes Were Watching God—to make a claim using hybrid discursive structures at the intersection of these different communities.

By mixing disciplinary claim-making practices with affectively-charged and colloquial fandom practices, Jewel blurs boundaries. We argue that ty pical educational spaces are unnecessarily bounded,  whereas hybridizing practices allows participation across b oth spaces. Reflective of social justice pedagogies that aim to coordinate “the tools for producing knowledge, expe rt subject-matter knowledge, and the knowledge that youth from a variety of backgrounds bring to their learning” (Moje, 2008, p. 33), these practices do not merely reproduce practices from various communities but transform them, leading to lifelong consequential navigation. Paper 2: “An interesting mental exercise”: Making space for teachers’ syncretic pedagogical content knowledge Lauren Vogelstein, Jasmine Y. Ma, Sara Vogel, Sarah  Radke, Christopher Hoadley, Laura Ascenzi-Moreno, Wendy Barrales, Joyce Wu and Felix Wu

## Purpose and theoretical framework

Syncretic literacies have been conceptualized (Guti érrez, 2014) as emerging from tensions that crop up  when educators and learners reorganize the typical hierarchies between everyday and scientific literacies. We often see syncretic literacies emerge in pe dagogical interactions between teachers and student s in learning environments. Given that designers of learning environments also draw on the “new pedagogical imagination” that sync retic literacies have inspired (Gutiérrez, 2014, p. 54; L opez et al., 2021;  Radke et al., 2022; Vogelstein et al., 2023), teachers as designers do syncretic sense-making around the different forms that knowledge ca n take and how different knowledges might be differentially valued  in and out of school contexts. Our paper explores how an educator engaged in syncretic literacies as she developed new understandings of a topic students brought into the classroom (hacking) in order to make pedagogical decisions about what knowledge and sources to bring in to her classroom in response (e.g., BrainPop, Wikipedia, and the Hacker’s Code of Ethics). From a syncretic literacies perspective, content knowledge is constantly under negotiation, thus teachers’ pedagogical content knowledge (PCK) –the knowledge, perspectives, and practices teachers  need to teach a particular content area (Shulman, 2015)–is also under negotiation. PCK has, in some domains, been taken to be common domain-specific pedagogical strategies to counter learner misconceptions. Others take PCK to be more centered on different ways students make sense of c oncepts in the domain (e. g., Gess-Newsome, 2015). Both models conceive of the content knowledge to be more  or less stable and agreed-upon. Theories of syncretic literacies that view learning as the “organization for possible futures’’ (Gutiérrez, 2014, p. 48) ope n up the possibility that  PCK is not a static set of ideas and practices, but  can be prospective, i.e., supportive of the emergence of new syncretic forms of discourses and content which challenge knowledge-power hierarchies. Computer science education (CSed) is an important c ontext to examine tensions related to knowledge and pow er. First, as a newer academic discipline with a bu dding position in school curricula, there are fewer normative, formalized, and/or potentially restricti ve approaches to CS literacy in schools. Teachers m ay find the content more unfamiliar or unstandardized, and the literature around pedagogical content knowledge in CSed is still nascent (Hubbard, 2018). Secondly, the domain  itself involves multiple perspectives which each m ay be dominant in different professional settings (e.g. hackers, corporate settings, start-ups, academia). Third, the ways that technology influences everyday life means that  not only le arners, but also teachers, may bring different everyday/non-dominant literacies to bear as CSed is implemented.  Because of these tensions, computer science may exemplify a domain where multiple everyday disc ourses and multiple scientific discourses exist in tension and contestation. We argue that it is because of these tensions and the positioning of CSed as a less formalized, relative newcomer to schooling, that it is a place of possibilities. To make this argument, our episod e illustrates forms of syncretism that go beyond the school vs. home dichotomy. T he purpose of this paper is to examine an episode of teacher discourse around CS teacher sens emaking to help us unpack syncretism beyond Gutiérr ez’s initial contrasting of “everyday” and “scientific” discourse (in the Vygotskian sense; Gutiérrez, 2014).

## Methods, findings, and implications

This paper analyzes a recording of a conversation b etween teacher, researcher, and intern members of a  design team that convened in summer 2021 as part of a prof essional learning community focused on K-12 multilingual learners in CSed. The team supported S andy, an elementary school librarian, as she planne d a unit to engage existing student interests in hacking within her CS  classes. Syncretic literacies offers an analytical  framework to help us understand the tensions that members of the  team grappled with  in attempting to define “hacking” (building content knowledge) in order to teach this  topic (applying pedagogical content knowledge). Ou r initial approach to analyzing this discourse looked for evi dence of traditional bifurcation and hierarchies of “everyday” and “scientific” knowledge relevant to syncretic fr amings. However, we quickly discovered that this bi furcation was not complex enough to account for different for ms of knowledge that were in tension in the discour se. For this, we used interaction ana lysis (Jordan & Henderson, 1995) to closely trace h ow conceptions of “hacker” and “hacking” were developed in relation to ideas about what CS concepts to teach and how to teach them. The design team grappled with a tension related to the definitions of hacking offered by a BrainPop video Sandy had consulted (which highlighted activities s uch as designing, programming, and remixing code) a s contrasted with definitions that one of the researc hers, Sara, found in Wikipedia and a podcast about hacking (which highlighted more subversive activities such as “hacktivism,” hacking crimes, and idealistic activities). During the conversation, participants’ goals shifted from attempting to define hacking, to describing what people labeled as “hackers” do in different settings, thei r beliefs, and  purposes. These descriptions were related to differing connotations in professional settings (e. g., in a tech company, an academic department), in computing discourse communities representing varying politica l agendas and generations (e.g. YouTube influen cers, Anonymous, Internet libertarians, open source activists) and in everyday settings such as the reactions of parents to children talking about hacking, or kids talking among peers about “hacking” as a descriptor of how they might win a videogame (whether or not it involves cheating). As part of the conversation, it became evident that neither school nor professional practitioner discourses had a single, consensual framing of hacking that align ed with our initial presumptions about scientific knowledge i n the syncretic sense. There were also multiple dis courses and sources of both “everyday” and “academic” knowledge. During the session, Sandy engaged in syncretic literacies by juxtaposing, evaluating, and attempting to recon cile various, and sometimes con tradictory, definitions and conceptions of hacking.

Simultaneously, she was developing PCK. She propose d activities that would support student sense-making about different discourses in syncretic ways, including leveraging formats like debate, guiding  students to develop their own definitions of hacking and  to create their own “hackers code of ethics.” Sandy  called this definition-building work “an interesting mental exercise” —we argue it was a syncretic one in which the teache r was navigating many everyday and scientific conceptions in juxtaposition with her role as a curator and promoter of school-based conceptions and literacies. By taking up a sy ncretic analytic frame, and looking for new discourses emerging from tensions across settings a symmetrical in status, we began to see ways in whic h the traditional conception of everyday vs. scientific discourse was more complex. This analysis also foregrounds the dialogic nature of PCK: PCK is an emergent type of knowledge which is not teacher-only, but exists relationally among teachers and students, and the discourses and literacies that swirl around them. Paper 3: Syncretic approaches to data science: Towards consequential learning Emily Reigh, Cherise McBride and Michelle Hoda Wilkerson

## Purpose and theoretical framework

Our work explores how students’ relationships to data influence their engagement in STEM learning. The present study explores a workshop on environmental racism (ER)–the disproportionate burden of pollution on communities of color –that was grounded in syncretic theories of learning. We ask “How do students build relationships to large-scale geospatial data and come to see it as consequ ential to their lives?” We present a case study of a student who engaged her everyday knowled ge of racism in the social world to reason about da ta on environmental factors and social demographics.

Datasets are not neutral; rather, they are construc ted texts that reflect their social, cultural, and political contexts (Lee et al., 2021). Students can engage wi th data, including large-scale data that others have collected, in deeply personal ways that are connected to their experiences of race and plac e (Philip et al., 2016) and family and community histories (Kahn, 2022). We employ syncretism (Gutiérrez, 2014) to design curricula that juxtapose students’ personal, cultural, and community-based understandings of the world, as recounted through narrative, with data-based representations and STEM practices. For example, students may analyze inconsistencies between their experiences and statistical trends or investi gate contradictions in how their communiti es are (or are not) represented in data collection and analysis. The goal is not for one set of practices to dominate the other, but rather for the negotiation of different practices to promote deeper engagement in learning and new analytic lenses on the world.

In our curricular designs, students create “data st ories” that make explicit how their own narratives and the stories of others can motivate decisions about selecting, transforming, and visualizing data. Students also show how findings from data analysis can reveal new insi ghts about their personal and community experiences. Data stories include traditional disciplinary practices, but also uphold the value and integrity of everyday practices, like narrative, that are often devalued in STEM. Through  juxtaposing disciplinary and everyday practices, s tudents author new forms of texts that use data to represent the past and to imagine paths towards more just futures.

## Methodology

This study is situated in one of a series of week-long, online summer workshops for middle school stu dents on the topic of ER. In the workshops, students engaged  with narrative case studies of ER-related advocacy around the world, listened to a talk from a  local African-American environmental activist, and investigated p ublic data sets on social indicators (e.g., income, race, asthma) and geospatial indicators (e.g., air pollution, water pollution, pesticides). Students developed a research question  that they explored through quantitative data and c onducted interviews and other forms of journalistic research.

This case study focuses on Juliana, a Latina studen t whose family recently immigrated to the United States. Data sources included transcripts of the cl ass sessions and one-on-one conferences with students, as well as students’ written work. We first created activity logs that summarized Juliana’s participation in the workshop. Then, we developed an emergent coding scheme that r epresented experiences (e.g., individual experiences, collective experiences of family/community), practices (e.g., posing questions, storytelling, selecting data, filtering data), and concepts (e.g., racism as interpersonal/social, racism as st ructural/located in the physical environment). Finally, we selected key moments in t he evolution of Juliana’s research project and iden tified the experiences, practices, and concepts that drove her  inquiry. We also analyzed the extent to which her work wa s supported by the curricular design and in-the-moment instructional decision making.

## Findings and implications

Our analysis showed that Juliana emphasized racism as a social experience, in contrast to the focus on the geospatial environment and health in the STEM curriculum. Over  time, she integrated these two foci in her analysis of the provided datasets and the construct ion of her final narrative. Juliana’s final data st ory addressed the question “How does air quality affect the health of construction workers in [my hometown] and especially the Latino community?” We trace the evolution of Julian a’s project, including how she reframed ER as a soc ial phenomenon, integrated geospatial variables into he r conception of ER, and oriented to data by connect ing her father’s individual experiences to those of a larger Latinx community. Reframing ER as a Social Phenomenon through Eliciti ng Personal Experience. Students first explored stories of ER around the world, then were asked to interview a family member about ER. In her work, Ju liana reframed racism as a social phenomenon experienced by her entire community by focusing on linguistic injustices, saying “Many of my family members get dirty looks b ecause they don’t speak English clearly [...] I thi nk this happens to many families.” She emphasized the need for social action against such injustices. Integrating Geospatial Conceptions of ER through Hearing Others’ Stories. Over time, Juliana expanded her understanding of ER to include the geospatial environment through stories told by others. After hearing from the environmental activist, she shared her shift in thinking: “Well, I thought that [environmental justice] was just someone/something getting justice but it is actually more about like air pollution or water contamination and what people did to get justice for that.” Drawing from another student’s reflection on her family’s exposure to pollution as farmworkers in a nearby rural area, Juliana made further connections to the Latinx community in her hometown and eventually her own father, who worked in construction. Orienting to Data through Connecting Individual and  Collective Experiences. Juliana analyzed data on asthma rates and race/ethnicity in two locations, h er hometown and the university town where the works hop facilitators lived. She found higher asthma rates in her hometown, which had a larger Latinx population. Finally, she asked “ Are most construction workers Hispanic?” and sought  data to compare her father’s individual experience to that of the larger collective of her community, ultimately arguing that exposure to air pollution disproportionately affects the Latinx community due to the nature of their work. The syncretic design of the curriculum, which paire d storytelling with data analysis, allowed Juliana to engage in ways that were meaningful to her family a nd community. Building on previous work in informal contexts (Kahn, 2022), we show how connecting perso nal, family, and community experiences to data can promote student engagement in instructor-led STEM learning environments. Within these environments, syncretism is reflected not only in curricular desi gn, but also in-the-moment instructional decision-making. Instructors play a key role in facilitating consequ ential learning by supporting students’ reframing o f curricular activities and concepts and by encouraging flexible use of everyday and disciplinary cultural practices. Paper 4: Syncretic doings of literacies: Walking to collectively design land-based additional languages curricula with a rural community of the colombian caribbean Tatiana Becerra and Enith Lambraño Purpose Decolonial scholars have pointed out the ways literacy and Additional Languages (AL) have been used as colonial tools to maintain the hegemony of European language s and cultures (Battiste, 2013). For instance, lite racy researchers have argued that most educational measu res and practices reflect the overvalued literacy p ractices common in elite communities (Gee, 2014) and exclude the literacy practices from non-dominant groups (Gutiérrez et al., 2009). Similarly, AL scholars highlight the  oppressive colonial  nature of teaching modern/colonial languages, adversely affecting minoritized learners (Heugh et al, 2021). In Colombia, where this study is situated, colonial  views permeate literacy and AL education and negatively impact learners from non-dominant groups (i.e., peasants, indigenous, and Black communities residing in rural areas) whose literacies and linguistic  practices are often deemed as deficient (Fandiño -Parra, 2021). Inspired by syncretic learning and literacy research (Gutierrez, 2014) this study explores the land-based literacies of a rural community and the ways youth and teacher s in a secondary school draw on these literacies to design literacy and AL curricula. In this paper, we descri be the process of walking the land to unsettle trad itional understandings of literacy and to jointly design land-based curricula with members of a rural community.

## Theoretical framework

Syncretic research emphasizes the need for researchers and educators to shift away from deficit views of learners from non-dominant groups. For example, Vossoughi (2014) explored the potential of linguistic repertoires as tools for scaffolding learning and fostering expression without fear of judgment. Gutiérrez (in Gutiérrez & Jurow, 2016) explained her uptake of idiosyncratic texts like testimonio to engage Latinx youth in curriculum genres and create new types of texts. Additionally, Davis and colleagues (2020) contributed strategies for reframing interpretations of learners' actions, challenging assimilationist a gendas in education, and supporting self-determination and freedom for racially marginalized learners.

The asset-based and expansive understandings of learning and literacy generated by this syncretic scholarship have informed this study –that is in itself syncretic, drawing from various t heories, disciplines and methodologies–to undertake research that allows to explore the literacies that emerge from daily interaction with the land and sustain life in a rural community in C olombia. Like syncretic approaches that mobilize th e wisdom of communities (Zavala, 2016), this study draws on participatory research methods like walking and communitybased design to co-create language and literacy curricula with youth, parents and teachers from the community. We frame walking as a methodology that allows to li ve and feel syncretic understandings of literacies,  which inform transformative, land-based language curricula.

## Methodology

Multisensory walks, our participatory data generati on design, is inspired by Fals-Borda’s feeling-thinking and participatory research (2002) which centers the epistemologies and voices of community members, and by recent uptakes of walking methodologies in the learning sc iences. Studies have shown the potential of walking   to account for the effect of material worlds and ecosystems in racialization and the perpetuation of inequities (Taylor & Pacini-Ketchabaw, 2017), to re orient participatory design that centers political and ethical action for more equitable futures (Vadeboncoeur et al., 2023), and to center human and more-than-human relations in learning from and with the land (Marin & Bang, 2018; Marin, 2020). The two co-authors participated in three multisensory walks le d by community members, youth with their parents, neighbors, and other teachers. Some of the participants wore POV cameras to capture the ir interactions with each other and the more-than-humans with whom they share the land. This participant generated video data was first analysed individually by Tatiana, who organized and selected video data to then collectively watch and analyse with teachers and community membe rs. Our micro-analysis focused on  the relationship between participants’ embodied and discursive inter actions with other members of the community and wit h ecological elements from the land.

Three 60 minute collective design sessions followed  where youth, parents, and teachers drew on their experience of walking together and analysing the walks’ video-data, to reflect on their relation with the land, their land-based literacies, and the ways these could be integrated to the language curricula.

## Findings and implications

Drawing on our collective and individual micro-analysis of participant-generated video-data from two walks, we showcase the assemblages of land, collective memory, and cultural practices that produce land-based literacies in this rural community. We positi on land walking, forest and creek crossing practice s as literacies that require reading and meaning-making from the land, and which allow individuals t o relate to other beings and thrive in the changing landscape of their rural community. We then discuss how syncretic understandings of these literacies are allowing an AL teacher to design and enact land-based curricula in this rural community. This study adds important insights on how syncretic understandings of learning and literacies may inform more relevant and equitable curricula for youth in rural contexts of the Global South, thus, contribut ing to decolonizing literacies and AL education. Furthermo re, the study describes how rural teachers’ make se nse of syncretic learning and literacies to inform pedagogies that draw on the knowledges and literacies from the land.
