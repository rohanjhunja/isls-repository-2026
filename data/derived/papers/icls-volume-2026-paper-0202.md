# Beyond Teacher Professional Development: A Dual Preparation Model for Fostering Dialogic Engagement in East Asian Classrooms

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Dialogic teaching initiatives in East Asian educational contexts often fail to take root, largely due to teacher-centric professional development that overlooks student readiness. This paper introduces a novel Dual Preparation model addressing this gap through systematic, coordinated preparation of both teachers and students as active partners in pedagogical change. The model is built on two synergistic principles: establishing a shared framework through explicit instruction in Academically Produc tive Talk (APT) for dialogic discourse and ICAP (Interactive, Constructive, Active, Passive) for cognitive engagement; and conducting coordinated capacity building by equipping both parties with cognitive strategies including mind mapping, mnemonics, and Group Mind Mapping Mnemonics (GMMM), supported by domain-expert AI tools providing personalised guidance. This paper presents the theoretical rationale for dual preparation, arguing that information alignment between teachers and students enables a more sustainable and effective dialogic classroom culture. The model is under empirical investigation in one East Asian primary school.

Dialogic teaching, which reframes learning as a social process of co-construction, is a powerful pedagogy for fostering deep understanding and critical thinking (Alexander, 2020; Mercer & Howe, 2012). Frameworks such as Academically Productive Talk (APT) p rovide teachers with structured “ talk moves ” to facilitate reasoned, evidence-based classroom discourse (Michaels et al., 2008). Despite its proven benefits, implementing and sustaining these practices remains a significant challenge, particularly in East Asian educational contexts (Lee, 2016; Snell & Lefstein, 2018).

The barriers are both cultural and systemic. Deep-rooted Confucian traditions promote a hierarchical teacher-student relationship where teachers are viewed as the primary authority and students are expected to absorb information passively (Biggs, 1996). This cultural script often discourages questioning and active participation, as students may fear “losing face” by making mistakes or challenging authority (Zheng & Chen, 2025). This passivity is further reinforced by the intense pressure of high-stakes exam ination systems that prioritise rote memorisation and content coverage over the seemingly time-consuming processes of open data from our ongoing work with 395 students in a primary school reveals a critical pattern: most students reported frequently engaging in passive learning behaviours (e.g., copying notes verbatim), while far fewer reported regularly engaging in constructive activities (e.g., adding their own ideas or connecting new content to prior knowledge). This finding reveals a classroom ecosystem ill-prepared for the demands of genuine dialogue, where even well-intentioned reforms struggle to gain traction.

To address this challenge, this paper presents and justifies a novel conceptual model. The roadmap is as follows: first, we will dissect the theoretical gap in existing dialogic implementation research. Second, we will elaborate on the components of the Dual Preparation model and their synergy. Finally, we will conclude with the model’s conceptual contributions and outline future research directions to validate its efficacy. The theoretical gap: The missing piece of coordinated readiness The predominant response to these implementation challenges has been to focus on teacher professional development (PD) (Chen et al., 2020; Sağlam et al., 2022). This approach, while necessary, is insufficient because it is fundamentally teacher-centric. It implicitly treats students as passive objects to be acted upon by the teacher’s new skills, a deep irony for a pedagogy that purports to foster student agency (cf. Snell & Lefstein, 2018). Moreover, even when teachers acquire dialogic facilitation skills,  they often lack expertise in cognitive learning strategies that could help students build the knowledge foundation necessary for confident participation. This focus on teacher practice fails to address the dual challenge: students’ beliefs about learning, their anxieties about participation, their lack of experience in articulating nascent ideas, and their underdeveloped cognitive strategies for mastering content to a level that enables confident discussion (O’Connor & Snow, 2017). 1492 This paper argues that the critical missing piece in dialogic implementation research is the explicit cultivation of coordinated “ dialogic readiness ” for both teachers and students. We define this as a multidimensional construct that, for students, includes willingness (affective), confidence (psychological), and capacity (cognitive) to engage in academically productive talk, and for teachers, includes  not only dialogic facilitation skills but also understanding of cognitive learning strategies and tools that support student preparation. This readiness cannot be assumed; it must be systematically developed in parallel for both parties (Snell & Lefstein, 2018). Without it, teachers are left trying to facilitate a conversation with participants who have not been oriented to the new rules of engagement, leading to frustration and an eventual reversion to traditional, didactic practical gap.

The Dual Preparation model: A principle-based framework We are testing this conceptual model through a quasi-experimental study comparing two conditions. The control condition (Group A) represents standard dialogic implementation: teacher-only APT professional development, with students receiving no explicit preparation. The experimental condition (Group B) implements the full Dual Preparation model described below. This design examines the effects of coordinated preparation on classroom dialogue quality.

The Dual Preparation model is built on two synergistic principles designed to cultivate coordinated dialogic readiness: (1) establishing a shared metacognitive and practical framework for all participants, and (2) conducting coordinated capacity building for both teachers and students. Principle 1: Establishing a shared metacognitive and practical framework This principle addresses the fundamental questions of why dialogue matters and how it should be conducted by creating a common language and shared goals for all classroom participants. Shared Understanding of Dialogic Practice (APT).  Both teachers and students in Group B receive explicit instruction in the Academically Productive Talk (APT) framework (Michaels et al., 2008), which provides specific “talk moves” for orchestrating productive discourse: revoicing (restating student contributions to validate and clarify them), prompting for reasoning (“Why do you think that?”), pressing for evidence (“What makes you say that?”), and creating opportunities for students to build on each other’s ideas (Michaels & O’Connor, 2015).

When students understand the purpose of these moves, they transition from passive recipients to active partners who can anticipate, interpret, and purposefully contribute to co-constructed discourse, transforming APT into a shared practice.

Shared Understanding of Cognitive Engagement (ICAP). Both teachers and students in Group B are also explicitly taught the ICAP framework (Chi & Wylie, 2014), which categorises engagement into four hierarchical modes: Passive (receiving information without overt processing), Active (manipulating materials wi thout generating new content), Constructive (producing outputs that go beyond given information), and Interactive (coconstructing knowledge through collaborative dialogue). The ICAP hypothesis predicts learning outcomes improve hierarchically: Interactive > Constructive > Active > Passive. The ICAP framework provides all participants with a shared, non-judgmental vocabulary to understand different levels of cognitive engagement. It reframes classroom participation from the socially fraught act of “speaking in class ” to the cognitive goal of “engaging constructively or interactively ” (Morris & Chi, 2020). While APT provides the procedural “how” of productive talk, ICAP provides the metacognitive “why” by making visible the cognitive benefits of different engagement modes (Chi et al., 2018). By understanding that interactive dialogue represents the deepest form of cognitive engagement, both teachers and students are given a compelling rationale for prioritising discussion, debate, and co-construction of ideas. Principle 2: Coordinated capacity building This principle equips both teachers and students in Group B with concrete tools to support and master content, thereby lowering the cognitive load of engagement and increasing student confidence. Building Foundational Cognitive Capacity.  Both teachers and students in Group B are taught core cognitive strategies: (1) Mind mapping—a visual technique for organising information hierarchically around a central concept (Buzan & Buzan, 1996). (2) Mnemonics—memory enhancement strategies that create meaningful associations to improve encoding and retrieval (Putnam, 2015). This directly addresses a critical barrier —lack of content mastery. By lowering the cognitive load required to grasp foundational knowledge, students can allocate more resources to higher-order discussion and argumentation. Structured Practice Through GMMM.  Group Mind Mapping Mnemonics (GMMM) provides a structured collaborative activity where students practi se these strategies in small groups, progressing through 1493 individual thinking, group discussion, collaborative mapping, and mnemonic creation. This creates a low-risk environment for applying both cognitive strategies and dialogic skills. AI-Powered Support.  Since most subject teachers lack specialised expertise in mind mapping and mnemonics, and since individual teachers cannot provide detailed guidance to dozens of students simultaneously, these strategies are supported by a suite of domain-expert AI tools:  (1) Memory Method Suggester provides multiple mnemonic strategies when students input content to memorise; (2) Mind Map Feedback Analyser provides structured feedback when students upload photographs of their maps; and (3) Reference Mind Map Generator produces starter templates when students struggle to begin. These tools are informed by recent work integrating foundation models, knowledge graphs, and prompt engineering in educational settings (Hu & Wang, 2024). Critically, the design of  these tools is grounded in scaffolding theory (Wood et al., 1976): each tool addresses a specific, bounded subtask rather than making holistic pedagogical judgments. The higher-order work of connecting cognitive strategies to learning goals, evaluating student understanding, and orchestrating classroom dialogue remains the responsibility of teachers and students, not the AI system. Teacher Mastery and Co-Facilitation. Critically, teachers also master these cognitive strategies and AI tools. This enables them to provide effective support when students encounter difficulties and to coordinate seamlessly during GMMM activities. The researcher personally co-facilitated one  GMMM session with each experimental group teacher, modelling how to orchestrate these collaborative learning sessions effectively. Synergy: An integrated system The model’s power lies in how the synergy between these two principles creates information alignment, a state in which teachers and students share the same conceptual frameworks, cognitive strategies, and engagement expectations. Cognitive strategies lower  the knowledge barrier to participation (Principle 2), while shared frameworks provide common goals and vocabulary for interaction (Principle 1). Because teachers understand the strategies and tools students use, they can offer targeted support; and GMMM activities provide structured arenas where students apply their cognitive and dialogic capacities together. This shared understanding enables a positive feedback loop: more capable student participation makes teachers’ use of APT more effective, which in tu rn encourages deeper student engagement, ultimately transforming classroom culture (see Figure 1). 1494 Conceptual contributions and future directions This paper’s primary theoretical contributions are threefold: (1) It introduces “coordinated dialogic readiness” as a key construct, shifting implementation research from a teacher-centric view to a partnership-based perspective that reconceptualises both teachers and students as essential partners in pedagogical change. (2) It proposes a theoretical synthesis integrating ICAP (metacognitive “why”) with APT (practical “how”), providing a foundation for student engagement that teacher-only approaches cannot achieve. (3) It offers a grounded model for integrating AI as a bounded cognitive scaffold without supplanting the educator’s role (cf. Zawacki-Richter et al., 2019). The primary outcome measure is classroom dialogue quality, operationalised through ICAP coding (Chi & Wylie, 2014) of video-recorded lessons. Specifically, we compare the frequency of Constructive and Interactive engagement episodes between conditions to test whether dual preparation produces higher-quality dialogue than teacher-only preparation. These measures directly align with the model’s theoretical rationale: if coordinated preparation increases dialogic readiness, this should manifest as more frequent higher-order engagement during classroom discourse. Secondary measures include student self-report surveys on dialogic readiness and analysis of student artifacts and AI usage logs. Interaction analysis (Jordan & Henderson, 1995) will further examine qualitative shifts in discourse patterns over time.

We argue that treating both teachers and students as essential partners in pedagogical change offers a more sustainable pathway for transforming classrooms into the dialogic learning environments envisioned by learning scientists (García-Carrión et al., 2020).
