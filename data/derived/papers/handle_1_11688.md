# Towards a “Disciplinary Analytics”: What and with Whom Do Learning Scientists Write When They Write About Codesign?

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Here we introduce a “Disciplinary Analytics” approach to helping learning scientists see and understand research communities and themes in the field. We suggest that such analytics could support (inter)disciplinary collaboration, equitable convening practices, and effective knowledge sharing. Using “codesign” as an initial thematic focus, we present example visualizations based on public conference proceedings, and articulate opportunities for developing disciplinary infrastructures and routines that could help researchers, particularly newcomers, navigate the “big tent” of learning sciences.

## Introduction and theoretical framework

In this poster, we take initial steps in refining D isciplinary Analytics as a form of Learning Analyti cs. Taking up “codesign” as a critical yet complex topic of broad  interest to the field, we explore how Disciplinary  Analytics can be used to combine data, computational methods and disciplinary routines and infrastructures as a sociotechnical system that enhances learning and research in the discipline of the learning sciences. Disciplinary analytics Disciplines are dynamic systems of coordinated human and materi al practices structuring learning, perception, and action in socially meaningful ways (Stevens & Hall, 1998). Disciplines perpetuate practices through situated processes of formal and informal learning, including apprenticeship and learning within communities of practice (Lave & Wenger, 1991). Such processes shape individuals per communal standards of “important” research, “good” practice, and “proper” conduct, disciplining newcomers’ goals and processes of learning and becoming. Learning Analytics  is a growing field of design and research efforts f ocused on “the measurement, collection, analysis, and reporting of data about l earners and their contexts, for purposes of understanding and optimizing learning and the environments in which i t occurs” (Siemens & Baker, 2013). Unlike tradition al research on learning, Learning Analytics aims to be “actionable in the moment,” enabling learners and educators to “do something about [the learning] right now for  the same learners from which the  data came” (Wise, 2021). Humandecision-making and action are integral to the sociotechnical system of Learning Analytics, linking sensemaking processes with good responses in practice (van Harmelen & Workman, 2012; Wise et al., 2021). Building on these ideas, we define Disciplinary Analytics as applying analytics to understand learning and change processes within a discipline. The goal is to use data and computational methods to optimize learning and the disciplinary environment. We are interested in contributing to the development of a Disciplinary Analytics that provides rapid feedback to individuals and communities within the discipline in ways that allow them to better understand, engage with, adjust, and enhance disciplinary goals, routines and infrastructures for improved learning and research.

Codesign Codesign leverages stakeholders’ experiences, persp ectives, and expertise into the design process and,  ideally, the final design product (Tierney et al., 2021; Pit t, et al., 2021). The process of codesign creates a  unique opportunity to make our design processes more inclu sive and responsive to the needs, identities, and h istories of stakeholders. At the same time, codesign has the po ssibility to challenge implicit hierarchical struct ures and inequities embedded in curriculum design and products (Tierney et al., 2024; Iversen, Smith, & Dindler, 2017). Across the learning sciences, codesign not only inc ludes a wide variety of stakeholders, but also diff ers in process and levels of involvement of codesign st akeholders. For instance, when and how stakeholders  are involved in codesign can impact the ways and depth in which their perspectives are included. Involving codesigners only at the end of a design process, fo r instance, positions them as end-users but not necessarily as providing valued perspectives on what is designed. Involving teachers and researchers in curriculum de sign, but not youth, makes a statement about what expertise w e value (as well as impacting what is created). Put  simply, these decisions matter, but we currently do not hav e a deep understanding of what the strands of codes ign are in terms of characteristic goals and methods in the learning sciences. This can be a challenge to collaborative research and the ongoing development of codesign practices.

Using disciplinary analytics to enhance codesign research The varied meanings and multiple strands of codesig n present challenges for researchers —especially newcomers—trying to navigate the learning sciences' “big tent” (Nathan et al., 2010; Sommerhoff et al., 2018) of goals, values, and practices. These difficulties are compounded by the field’s inherent disciplinary diversity, wide range of objectives, and epistemologies. Understand ing how different codesign approaches are taken up by various groups of learning scientists, in different  ways, and to different ends can help groups and in dividuals in the “big tent” learn from and inform each other. Disciplinary Analytics, conceptualized as a sociotechnical system linking data on research collaboration with routine infrastructures for convening, learning in and shaping the field (e.g., processes managed by ISLS), may help researc hers navigate these complexities, illuminate common  areas and leaders of discourse, and identify methodological differences and commonalities, making it easier to connect and collaborate across diverse strands of inquiry and design.

## Method and findings

As a proxy for research activity, we focused on pub lication activity as archived in the ISLS Repositor y, which captures peer reviewed conference proceedings for ISLS since 2006. A search for “codesign” yielded 154 papers. Metadata on this corpus —including coauthors, titles, publication years, and abstracts —was transformed into a CSV file. The team cleaned, restructured and analyzed the data in R, examining social structures of collaboration with the igraph package (Csardi and Nepusz, 2006) and themes and la tent structures in collective research discourse using the topicmodels package (Grün and Hornik, 2011). Preliminary analysis shows significant growth in th e number of co-authorial subnetworks writing about co-design since 2007 (Figure 1). We also identified authoritative actors who are consistently at the center of these co-authorial subnetworks, and bridge actors who link d ifferent subnetworks. Topic modeling of abstract te xts uncovered latent student-focused and teacher-focused discourses on codesign. The study team is r efining and integrating these analyses, and will present compre hensive visualizations of  codesign subnetworks, temporal patterns in latent discourses on codesign, and prop osals for using these analytics to support research ers. By presenting this early work at ISLS, we hope to stim ulate discussion among learning scientists, and gat her initial feedback to inform the design of useful Disciplinary Analytics activities and systems. ## Significance

This early work represents an initial step toward o perationalizing Disciplinary Analytics as a sociote chnical system to enhance collaboration, inclusivity, and k nowledge-sharing within the learning sciences. By leveraging public metadata on codesign research, we can routin ely generate “synoptic” perspectives on the field, and link them with existing and new disciplinary infrastruct ures to help researchers —particularly newcomers —identify and situate themselves among trends, networks, and latent themes within the “big tent” of the learning sciences. We envision embedding these analytics into annual I SLS community activities, such as pre-conference early-career workshops, to scaffold researchers’ entry in to the field. Additionally, these tools could routi nely identify emerging perspectives and highlight underr epresented but important areas of inquiry, informin g more systematic and inclusive processes for recruiting r epresentative participants for symposia and structu red poster sessions, for instance. By revealing and fostering connections across diverse disciplinary strands, these analytics can illuminate prominent (central) perspectives whi le also legitimizing and supporting new, divergent and interdisciplinary subgroups within the field’s diverse community of researchers.
