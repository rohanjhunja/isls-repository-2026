# Active Learning as Joint Achievement in Postsecondary Science Classrooms

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper illustrates with a single case drawn from a sample of postsecondary science instructors, how the sociohistorical framework of mediational tools can help further understand the range of instructional moves that instructors candeploy when attempting to engage their class in Active Learning (AL) on a specific physics topic. The findings of this study contribute insights into an understanding of how instructors plan and implement AL lessons. This understanding also has implications to professional development that aims to support instructors’ AL practices.

Objective Active Learning (AL) is an instructional approach focusing on students’ active construction of knowledge instead of passively listening to lectures. Despite its sig nificant positive impact on student learning outcom e in higher education (Freeman et al., 20 14), adoption by instructors remains spotty. Researchers have proposed different conceptual models to assist practitioners, such as by categorizing AL activities by their cognitive en gagement level (Chi et al., 2018) or disciplinary focus (Lom bardi et al., 2021). However, it is not yet clear h ow instructors prepare AL resources and activities and deploy them in classrooms in the presence of students. The goal of our study is to shed light on how exper ienced college science instructors mediate their knowledge across activities of design, enactment, and reflection to make AL possible in their class. In this paper, we illustrate, with a single case drawn from a sample of postsecondary science instructors, how AL instruction is jointly achieved by the teacher and their students across different phases of teaching activity.

## Theoretical framework

Adopting a sociohistorical perspective broadens the  view of teaching and learning to include the inter play of mediational tools and socially patterned activities  governed by more or less explicit norms in the int eracting activity systems of the teacher and  the students (Mehan, 1997; Wertsch, 1997). In that context we view AL as a joint achievement of teacher and students, situated  in the affordances designed by the instructor and allowed by the students’ capacity to engage in appropriate ways. This perspective governs our methods (see next section) and it also provides a blueprint for scaffolding teacher professional learning.

## Methods

Taking the above perspective concretely leads to a methodological commitment to collect and analyze ri ch data on a wider time span to capture how teachers think and talk about the mediational tools to put into place, to what extent these tools  become instantiated and appropriated in a real clas sroom and how teachers reflect on these processes and their outcomes. This allows us to understand the relations and gaps between curriculum as intention and as reality and to reveal opportunities for teac her learning (Voogt et al., 2019). Here we report o n a single teacher case drawn from a dissertation study which focuses on six postsecondary science instructors participating in an AL professional community established for a d ecade in a major city in Eas tern Canada. To capture the complexity of teaching activities, we conducted pre-and post-class interviews with all six instructors to understand their intended and accomplished teaching activities in a typical AL class we observed. We transcribed the video recordings of the obse rved class and interviews verbatim and conducted qu alitative analyses (i.e., content and interactional analysis) on transcript d ata. Across the cases, we expect to see variations in the relationship between the intended and the enacted a ctivities, with som e cases revealing more alignment than others. Alignment indicates congruency within teach ing, and misalignment can signal surprises and tensions leading to opportunities to expand the teaching activity system. Given the page limit, this paper illustrates the case of Aspen, a physics teacher with eight years of teaching experience, teaching an introduct ory mechanics course with a regular science student group.

## Results

The analysis of Aspen’s class and interviews shows a good alignment between his intended use of the instructional resources and the accomplished learning activities he orchestrated in the presence of students.  Specifically, Aspen explicitly identified in the interviews the learnin g goal he anticipated students to achieve in the cl ass – to understand better a specific topic. To achieve this  goal, he further explained how he purposefully sel ected problems targeting students’ learning difficulties based on his knowledge of common alternative ideas and of the types of materials that can easily elicit such stud ent intuitive learning resources. By confronting students with their attempts to rely on their intuitions and the resulting failure to solve the problem, Aspen expected students to understand why and how the normative disciplinary way of thinking affords to better solve the conceptual problem at hand. It is noteworthy that Aspen’s instructiona l design prior to the class, seemingly a solo activity, actually relies heavily on his knowledge of student learning accumulated through years of teaching, hence a joint product of Aspen and the marks his students left in his professional life. The analysis of the class data revealed even more s trongly how he and his students co-construct the AL activities by taking turns to contribute to the five moment-to-moment class episodes with their respective material resources and verbal interactions (see Figure 1). W e illustrate this point by zooming in on the use of  student reflective writings (RW). Prior to the class, stude nts were asked to read a short text, produce a short reflective writing, and answer a few conceptual questions related to the reading. In class, Aspen orchestrated these materials into five teaching episodes, within which he consta ntly brought exemplars from student reflective writ ings back into the classroom discourse space to connect with the key topics they just discussed in the conceptual problem sessions. In the post-class reflection interview, Aspen expressed a sense  of satisfaction with how the class went and acknowledged students’ participation before and during the class. As illustrated above, the success of AL is continge nt on good alignment between the instructor’s plan, his activities, and students’ activities. In our study, we expected and observed cases that vary in terms of alignment between the instructors’ attempted AL activities and what the class achieved. For example, students in  another physics instructor, Andy, had not completed an assi gned pre-class worksheet, so Andy had to resort to a full lecture activity during the class instead of lettin g students take the lead on the review session of t he worksheet task as planned.

## Scholarly significance

The study contributes to the field by illustrating how a sociohistorical framework and its key concepts such as mediation can help shed light on the complex, situated, and mediated nature of teacher AL practice and knowledge using a college science teacher’s case. In Aspen’s class, the instructor and students jointly accomplished th e AL class, highlight ing the required competence for proper participation in  the shared enterprise by all  agents. Furthermore, we illustrate how following teachers’ thinking and doing across phases offers lenses into the diverse ways of knowing and resources deployed to support students’ agentic participation in the classroom context, and into opportunities for teachers’ pedagogical learning when misalignment occurs.
