# Investigating Students’ Memory for Feedback in a Naturalistic Classroom

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
The purpose of the present study was to examine students’ memory for academic feedback in a naturalistic classroom setting. We investigated whether introductory applied psychology students had differential recall for four types of feedback. Students completed an activity, were given authentic feedback on their assignment, and recalled that feedback one week later. Findings suggest that students had the lowest recall for the two types of feedback that theoretically stand to benefit them most. Overall, students’ recall across all feedback categories was very low compared to the amount of feedback units given by the instructor. We also saw a pattern of students “transforming” the more generalizable feedback they originally received to more task-specific feedback at recall. Practical implications of these findings are discussed.

## Introduction

Feedback is an integral part of the academic process. Its purpose is to provide students with information that can propel them forward from their current state of kno wledge/skills to a superior goal state (Hattie & Ti mperley, 2007). Literature in this area has highlighted the factors that may impact the cognitive and affective responses to different types of feedback and, consequently, the uptake and implementation of that feedback (e.g., Fyfe et al., 2015; Reeve et al., 2014). Even though such studies  on feedback are plentiful, little is known about s tudents’ recall of the feedback they receive. This is surprising, especially as learners’ memory of their feedback co uld influence whether the feedback is ultimately implemented, modified, or ignored (see Winne & Butler, 1994). In their systematic review of the literature, Hatti e and Timperley (2007) identified four main types o f feedback students can receive: Task Feedback (FT), Process Feedback (FP), Self-Regulation Feedback (FR), and Praise/Self Feedback (FS) (See Table 1 for defining features and examples). Because Process Feedback is more generalizable and encourages deeper learning, it is seen as more beneficial and longer-term than Task Feedback. Arguably, the most beneficial type of feedback is Self-Regulation Feedback because it instructs students on how to self-assess their work and develop the skills necessary to become better self-regulators. Praise/Self Feedback can have ironic consequences for future motivation because it does not encourage students to invest further effort. Thus, it tends to be the least effective at improving academic performance. Overall, Hattie and Timperley (2007) conclude that feedback’s efficacy depends heavily on the level at which it was aimed. In the current study, we use this framework to categorize the feedback that students receive and recall receiving on a real classroom assignment. Much of the previous research on feedback has bee n done in laboratory settings, where feedback is manufactured and unrelated to the actual performance of the student on a given task (e.g., randomly providing positive/negative feedback to participants without considering their actual performance). The current study, which follows up on preliminary results from one lab section, aimed to fill these gaps in the literature by examining how much feedback students remember from an actual classroom assignment and whether students’ memory differed as a function of feedback type.

## Participants

The present study was conducted in a first-year introductory applied psychology college course. The class had an enrollment of 102 students, 76 of whom consented to  participate in the study (Mage = 18.98, SD = 1.36). The students were split into four different lab sections, where they completed weekly projects in groups of 3-4. This IRB-approved study was designed to blend into the course with minimal disruptions to its natural flow. Students completed an applied activity on the  human body’s response to stress in their groups. A fter submitting their assignment, the course instructor and four teaching assistants (TA) met to discuss gr ading procedures, ensuring consistency in grading and mak ing sure the same detail of feedback was provided a cross labs. All assignments were graded before the following laboratory session. At the beginning of that lab session, students were given 10 minutes to individually read through their group’s assignment feedback. ## Results

The average number of feedback units for each feedback category were calculated at the classroom level for both TA comments and student recall (see Table 2). As st ated above, the course instructor and TAs met to di scuss grading procedures, ensuring consistency in the qua lity and focus of the feedback. TAs in all lab sect ions gave feedback that represented all four feedback types outlined by Hattie and Timperley (2007). However, the amount of feedback provided differed significantly across the lab sections, therefore we could not collapse t he data. On average, TAs gave FT and FP most often and an approximately equal number of feedback units in both categories (although this varied slightly by TA), whereas FS was given least often. In line with this, FT and FP were recalled most frequently by students, on average. However, students were more than twice as likely to recall FT than FP, despite being given both types in almost equal meas ure. FR was recalled least often by students, on av erage. Notably, students’ recall (across all feedback categories) was very low compared to the amount of feedback units received from the TA. Overall, findings suggest that students have differential recall for the four types of feedback.

## Discussion

This study demonstrates a noteworthy discrepancy between the types of feedback that educators give and the way students understand, classify, and remember that feedback. We observed that students recalled l ittle of the feedback they received, and when they did, it was p rimarily the marginally beneficial task-based feedb ack. Theoretically, it would be most beneficial for students to remember FP and FR feedback, as these types have the greatest ability to generalize and be useful on future, novel learning tasks. Findings suggest that even if students are receiving the types of feedback that have the potential to be most impactful, they may not be drawing out the key elements to understand and remember their feedback in the ways that stand to benefit them most long-term. We saw examples of this “transformation process,” where students received process-based feedback but actually remembered it in a specific, task-based way. Due to space constraints of the current paper, we have provided only two examples (see Table 3).

Prior research demonstrates that critical constructive feedback, which scaffolds students to improve on tasks, often remains untapped (Tärning et al., 2020). Our findings begin to shed light on why this might be. Students may not be purposefully ignoring or not im plementing feedback; instead, it might be a product  of not understanding it in the same way that educators int end for them to, altering how they store and recall  feedback. Additionally, Máñez and colleagues (2019) observed that sixth-grade students spent more time attending  to feedback after failure than after success. Therefor e, future analysis of our data could explore whethe r students were differentially remembering positive versus negative feedback. Practically, these results beg the question: how can we train students to better locate, remember, and use self-regulatory feedback (or at least prevent stude nts from transforming useful, generalizable process-based feedback into more specific task-based feedback in their long-term memory)? If we successfully accomplish these aims, we stand to better prepare students for future learning and improve course performance outcomes.
