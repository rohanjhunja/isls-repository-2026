# When Collaborative Viewing of Instructional Videos Fails

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study examines to what extent the collaborative viewing of instructional videos supported by a collaboration script affects the acquisition of grammar knowledge and collaborative and digitalization-related skills. A pretest-posttest control group design with viewing mode (collaborative vs. individual) as the independent variable was implemented. No statistically significant effects of collaborative viewing on learning outcomes were found. Several correlations of learning activities and learning outcomes contradicted expectations. The results emphasize the need for further research.

## Theoretical background and prior research

Instructional videos combine the presentation of information as oral text and visuals simultaneously to teach declarative or procedural knowledge (Fiorella, 2021). A possible implementation in a classroom setting could be collaborative viewing. However, this setting still has not been examined systematically (Fiorella, 2021). Consider two pupils collaboratively watching a video on the morphology and use of a future tense in an English-as-a-foreign-language classroom who engage in an exchange by relating their verbal contributions to their partner’s contributions. This is known as transactivity, which is considered beneficial for learning (Vogel et al., 2023). To achieve a high level of transactivity, pupils can employ several collaborative learning activities (King,

2007) such as explaining, asking questions, elaborating on the information presented in the instructional video or

arguing about a certain topic. Thus, they externalize their current understanding, expand on their knowledge by relating it to prior knowledge or reconfigure their understanding of the knowledge presented (King, 2007; Weinberger et al., 2007). During collaboration, learners alsoneed to jointly regulate and adapt in order to maintain a shared group goal (Järvela & Hadwin, 2013). Collaborative regulation may foster the engagement with the instructional video content by rewinding, pausing, etc., thereby benefiting the acquisition of content knowledge, such as grammar knowledge (Fiorella, 2021). It may also foster the internalization of collaboration skills (Fischer et al, 2013; Vogel et al., 2017). Furthermore, interaction with the functionalities of a video player may enhance learners’ efficiency in navigating the video and dealing with the transient nature of the medium (Fiorella, 2021) and thereby foster digitalization-related skills. However, research indicates that effective collaboration often does not happen spontaneously, but may be supported by collaboration scripts to prompt beneficial learning activities and thereby support learning (e.g. Fischer et al.; 2013; Kollar et al., 2006). These considerations lead to the following research questions: (1) What is the effect of collaborative viewing of instructional videos supported by a collaboration script compared to individual viewing on the acquisition of (a) grammar knowledge, (b) collaboration skills, and (c) digitalization-related skills? (2) Which collaborative learning activities are beneficial for learning when pupils watch instructional videos collaboratively supported by a collaboration script, and how are these learning activities carried out by the pupils?

## Instructional unit and procedure

The 70-minute instructional unit was taught by the first author using two instructional videos from a major video platform: one on the future tense and one on London’s sights. After an introduction to the video player’s functions, the pupils watched the first video on a tablet and completed a worksheet on the morphology and use of the future tense and then transfer tasks regarding the use of the future tense. In the second half, they watched the second video, extracted information on the sights and engaged in a dialogue about what they planned to visit on a trip to London. The pre-test was conducted approximately five days prior, and the post-test immediately afterwards.

## Design and participants

A pretest-posttest control group design with viewing mode of instructional videos (collaborative vs. individual) as the independent variable was implemented. A convenience sample of 44 pupils (18 female, 24 male; age M = 11.74, SD =.885) from three 6th grade secondary school English-as-a-foreign language classes participated. Whole classes were assigned to conditions at random, resulting in 24 pupils in the experimental group (two classes) and 20 pupils in the control group (one class). Manipulation of the independent variable In the collaborative viewing of instructional videos condition, the pupils were randomly grouped into pairs (or triads in two cases) to solve tasks collaboratively and were supported by a collaboration script embedded in the worksheets encouraging them to ask questions to their partners and explain, elaborate and argue. In the control condition, the pupils watched the videos alone and solved all tasks individually. Measurement of variables In the pretest and the posttest, grammar knowledge was assessed with 12 and 10 cloze test items, respectively, asking the pupils to complete sentences with the correct form of a given word (Cronbach’s α =.745 /.632). Collaboration skills were assessed with identical items in both tests. The participants were required to describe strategies for collaborative situations (Cohen’s κ =.651 -.733). Digitalization-related skills were assessed with identical items in both tests. The participants had to annotate which elements of a video player to use in order to achieve certain goals and to describe how to extract information from an instructional video (Cohen’s κ =.559 – 1). For the collaborative learning activities, utterances were coded as explaining, elaborating, asking questions or arguing based on transcribed conversations. Explaining and elaborating were later combined to explaining and elaborating. The proportion of time spent on each activity was calculated. Concerning, RQ1, analyses of covariance with post-test scores as the dependent variable, viewing mode as the independent variable, and pre-test score as a covariate were performed. For grammar knowledge, post-test scores were higher in the condition with individual viewing of instructional videos. However, this difference was not statistically significant, F(1,38) =.011, p <.918. For collaboration skills, the pupils from the condition with individual viewing of instructional videos performed slightly better in the post-test. This difference was also not statistically significant, F(1,38) =.253, p <.607. For digitalization-related skills, the post-test scores of the pupils in the condition with collaborative viewing of instructional videos were slightly higher than in the condition with individual viewing of instructional videos. This difference was not statistically significant, F(1,38) =.253, p <.618.

Concerning RQ2, Kendall rank correlations between learning activities and the three learning outcomes were computed on the dyad level. Given the small number of dyads, in an exploratory way only the descriptive values of the correlations are considered in the following. Surprisingly, the correlations of explaining and elaboration with digitalization-related skills (τ = -.16, n.s.) and of asking questions with both collaboration skills (τ = -.58, n.s.) and digitalization-related skills (τ = -.82, p <.008) were negative and of moderate to large size, while the correlations between these two learning activities and the remaining learning outcomes were negligible. Only arguing consistently exhibited positive correlations of moderate size with the three learning outcomes within this sample (with grammar knowledge: τ =.47, with collaboration skills; τ =.44, digitalization-related skills: τ =.35, all n.s.).

While the correlations between arguing and the learning outcomes were in line with the notion that highly transactive learning activities collaboration skills are beneficial for learning (Vogel et al., 2017), the correlations of the other two types of learning activities and the three learning outcomes were not. An explanation in line with prior research is that pupils who asked many questions may not have received explanations of sufficient quality (Webb & Palinscar, 1996). Similarly, the negative correlation of explaining and elaborating and asking questions and digitalization-related skills suggests that collaboration may hinder interaction with the instructional video resulting in fewer possibilities for practicing the use of the digital technology. Additionally, the script may not have prompted beneficial learning activities appropriately. These circumstances may explain why no effect of scripted collaboration on learning outcomes was found.

The study’s quasi-experimental design with only three classes and a small convenience sample constitutes a limitation. Besides, the independent variable involved the simultaneous manipulation of collaboration and collaboration script. Third, the learning activity analysis considers neither the speakers’ individual utterances nor their quality. Finally, the brief instructional unit may have limited the knowledge and skill acquisition time.

These shortcomings as well as the lack of a beneficial effect of collaborative viewing of instructional videos with collaboration support on learning outcomes despite typically positive effects of (supported) collaboration (Chen et al., 2018; Vogel, 2017) certainly necessitate further research. In today’s educational landscape, collaborative virtual learning environments have become pivotal platforms for fostering identity development among adolescents. Adolescence is a critical period for exploring identity, where social contexts and personal aspirations shape the evolving self-concept. Within these settings, identity-based motivation (IBM) theory offers a framework for understanding how adolescents interpret and respond to challenges in learning. IBM theory posits that identities are dynamically constructed and that when tasks align with a student’s identity, they are perceived as meaningful and worth the effort (Oyserman & Destin, 2010). Conversely, when tasks feel incongruent with one’s identity, challenges are more likely to be interpreted as insurmountable, undermining motivation and engagement.

The design of collaborative virtual learning environments plays a critical role in supporting identity exploration and fostering self-regulation among students. Virtual platforms offer unique opportunities to bridge identity and motivation, providing spaces where students can see their future selves reflected in academic goals and collaborative successes. This study leverages a global, virtual, collaborative afterschool program as a case study to examine how participation influences students’ self-perceptions over time. By conducting pre-and postassessment interviews, we explore how students’ thinking about themselves shifts through their engagement in this environment. Insights from these assessments highlight how culturally responsive practices and identitycongruent tasks encourage students to reinterpret difficulties as signals of importance rather than impossibility (Oyserman, Fryberg, & Yoder, 2007).

This study examines data from pre-and post-assessment interviews from students to capture shifts in their thinking around challenges (Espino et al, 2024). Through these interviews, we assess the transformative potential of such programs in fostering adolescent identity development and promoting academic success. The context for the paper involves students who engage in an informal, virtual, international, project-based science, technology, engineering, and mathematics (STEM) learning and media making community. Students participate in global, synchronous online sessions on Zoom between adolescent from multiple countries weekly during the school year. Pre and post assessment interviews were individually conducted with each student before and after their participation of one year in the project environment. The data analyzed in this study consists of individual pre and post assessment interviews taken of 29 adolescent students from the US and Brazil, Ghana, Kenya, and Mexico in September 2023 and June 2024. From this group, 14 students were from the US, and the remaining 15 from outside the US. A codebook was developed through an iterative, grounded analysis of the data, with 11 salient constructs from the data: Aspirational Orientation, Collaborative Openness, Deficiency Awareness, Encountering Challenges, Math Orientation, Perseverance, Personality Attributes, Science Orientation, Self-efficacy, Social Attributes, Social Awareness. Each interview was transcribed, reviewed by two researchers, then coded independently by two raters to determine the presence or absence of each construct in the codebook for each response. After coding, a process of social moderation to reach final consensus on the coded data (Herrenkohl & Cornelius, 2013). Afterwards, the data was uploaded to the epistemic network analysis (ENA) webtool to create a visualization to support the analysis. ENA is a technique of quantitative ethnography to produce visualizations that help model the discourse of the data by identifying the co-occurrences between the constructs (Shaffer, 2017). The strength of co-occurrences between two constructs is indicated by thicker lines, while the opposite is true for a thinner line, indicating a weaker connection between constructs. These visualizations allow us to examine comparisons between groups. Each student represented the unit of analysis, and each interview defined as the conversations in which connections were limited.
