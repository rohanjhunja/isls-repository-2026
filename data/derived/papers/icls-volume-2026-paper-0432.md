# Leveraging Play as an Entryway for Tangible Computer Science Learning Experiences in Early Childhood Settings

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In this work, we explore how three types of free play toys (blocks, clay, and stuffed animals) can serve as tangible manipulatives for computer science learning. We propose that such toys act as entry points to engagement and can then be scaffolded and guided by teachers to help transition children from free play to guided playful learning. Drawing on three cases, we suggest that allowing natural exploration and play can allow children to develop authentic, personal connections to their learning. Once these connections are established, subsequent scaffolding and support candeepen their understanding of new concepts.

As computer science (CS) and engineering education become more prevalent in early childhood spaces, there is a need to make technical knowledge accessible and developmentally appropriate for young children (Blake-West & Bers, 2023; Peppler et al., 2019). F acilitating play-based learning with tangible manipulatives has been shown to be a developmentally appropriate modality for young children (González -González et al., 2019; Silvis et al., 2021). We offer three ways in which common objects of play—blocks, clay, and stuffed animals —can become a medium for generating interest and engaging in CS and engineering concepts, creating coding playgrounds (Bers, 2020). Our case analyses are motivated by the following research questions: 1) To what extent did these play objects serve as an entryway for CS concepts? and 2) To what extent did our curricular and teacher-directed scaffolds bridge the gap between experiences of play and higher-order CS representations that serve CS learning goals? We posit that, with scaffolding, play can be a vehicle for a more concrete and authentic engagement with abstract concepts.

## Literature review

There is a robust corpus of evidence demonstrating play-based learning as an effective pedagogical approach in early childhood education (Reimer & Smith, 2023, Taylor & Boyer, 2019; Vygotsky, 1967).  Views of play-based learning can be mapped along a continuum with child-led play at one end and teacher-led playful learning at the other (Pyle & Danniels, 2017). Three common forms of free  play included building (e.g., blocks), playing with toys (e.g., stuffed animals), and sensory play (e.g., clay). With teach er-led playful learning, teachers can leverage these forms of play while employing specific scaffolds like modeling and designing goal-oriented activities that guide play towards learning goals (Tabak & Reiser, 2022). In this work, we explore how free play serves as an interest entryway (Kolodner et al., 2017) to support engagement and strengthen engagement with subsequent teacher scaffolds. The case studies (Merriam, 1998) presented in this paper draw on data from three separate studies examining playful computer science (CS) learning interventions in early childhood settings (Pre-K and Kindergarten). We draw on video recordings and notes from participant observation from each of the cases. All video data was qualitatively analyzed to identify moments of play and learning.

Case studies

## Case study 1: ScratchJr Blocks

Description In a kindergarten classroom, children encountered ScratchJr Blocks, a technology that uses block-play as a means to explore CS concepts. In the second lesson, children were given one instruction: to explore. Immediately, children jumped into working with the blocks, snapping them together. They talked little about what the program might do, 2725 only that they wanted to build the longest code. Two lessons later, the teacher guided the children in recognizing the parallels between the physical and digital blocks. Now, children were no longer trying to build the world’s longest code but rather the world’s best code for a dance.

These brief vignettes illustrate a transition from free play to guided playful learning. In the first instance, the blocks served as an invitation to engage in free play. After further scaffolding, children understood that the objects hold computational meaning. As the groups planned their dance steps, they engaged with the idea of symbolic representation and algorithmic thinking. While child-led play may not have immedia tely led to specific learning objectives, it provided children with authentic experienc es that the teacher gradually leveraged towards targeted playful learning.

## Case study 2: Early childhood maker literacies with squishy circuits

Description On the first day of a weeklong workshop, five children explored engineering and circuitry concepts using conductive clay. While a facilitator got their battery packs ready, children were prompted to play with their clay. Gesturing to the battery pack in front of him, a child exclaimed, “Why do we need Play-Dough if we are going to do this?” After exploring, all five children successfully created a circuit. Later, children were prompted to create whatever they wanted if their creation lit a lightbulb. They created plates of spaghetti with light-up meatballs and airplanes with motorizedpropellers. We observed that the clay enabled the children to deeply engage with circuitry concepts. They experimented with novel configurations, created short circuits, and caused the lights to dim and brighten in the process of bringing their imaginations to life. Their ability to engage with complex topics may have been due to the ways in which the activity initially drew upon their authentic interests and then offered opportunities for building confidence.

## Case study 3: Smart toys

Description Preschoolers interacted with technologically augmented stuffed animals (“stuffies”). After a structured activity, they had unstructured free time and were invited to explore on their own. During this time, they engaged deeply with the stuffies but only as stuffed animals. They had their stuffies talk to each other and go on adventures. In later unstructured playtime, teachers intervened and prompted children to create a sequence of sounds and colors. The two children began to work together, with one acting as the stomper and the other acting as the sequence monitor. Despite being oriented to the functional capabilities of the toys, the children chose to engage in imaginative play. While their choice was perceived initially as a distraction, the children were easily able to shift their focus to the sequencing task with teacher guidance. Inviting the children to engage through free play brought to light a natural collaboration which was capitalized on later as a social support for deepening their engagement with CS concepts. Across each case, we observed children utilizing these objects as they do in their typical play. After further scaffolding, the objects began to serve a dual purpose as both toys and a means of understanding CS concepts. We suggest that there is benefit to natural exploration and free play that gives children an opportunity to find their own, authentic way of connecting to their learning.

Allowing children to play according to their natural play practices is an effective preparation method for future learning. While free play serves as an effective invitation to learning, our work also highlights the necessity of guidance and scaffolding to help children move along the play-based continuum. Modeling, designing goal-oriented activities, and answering learners’ questions serve as effective scaffolding strategies. Free play is a critical entry point which can be expanded upon through guidance to create authentic learning experiences. 2726 Bers, M. U. (2020).
