# What Makes Actions Matter in Virtual Reality? Examining the Effects of Conceptual Congruence and Sensorimotor Engagement on Geometric Reasoning

**Conference:** ISLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This dissertation examines how physical actions in virtual reality influence geometric reasoning through two complementary studies. Study 1 synthesizes nine experiments (N=774) using integrative data analysis to establish baseline mechanisms of embodied geometric reasoning. Study 2 employs a 2×2 experimental design (Target N=200), manipulating conceptual congruence and sensorimotor engagement of physical actions in VR environments to test how these action characteristics affect geometric reasoning. Participants engage with virtual geometric shapes via hand tracking, with multimodal data capturing verbal reasoning, gestures, and VR interactions. This work advances theoretical understanding of embodied learning and evidence-based design principles for immersive learning environments. This dissertation, guided by embodied learning theories, aims to understand how body movement influences mathematics learning in XR. It specifically investigates the effects of physical actions on geometric reasoning through two research questions: (RQ1) How do physical actions and gestures impact geometric reasoning in non-VR settings? and (RQ2) How do interactions with geometric shapes, with different levels of conceptual congruence and sensorimotor engagement, influence geometric reasoning in a VR environment? ## Motivation and research questions

Extended reality (XR) technologies hold promises for engaging and intuitive mathematics learning, yet significant research gaps remain. Current studies often lack a solid theoretical foundation, focus more on whether XR is effective rather than why, under-explore the unique potential of body movement, and devote less attention to mathematics learning than other STEM fields.

This dissertation, guided by embodied learning theories, aims to understand how body movement influences mathematics learning in XR. It specifically investigates the effects of physical actions on geometric reasoning through two research questions: (RQ1) How do physical actions and gestures impact geometric reasoning in non-VR settings? and (RQ2) How do interactions with geometric shapes, with different levels of conceptual congruence and sensorimotor engagement, influence geometric reasoning in a VR environment?

## Theoretical background

The intersection of embodied learning theory and XR technology motivates this investigation, with geometry providing an ideal context where physical actions naturally support mathematical reasoning. The Grounded and Embodied Mathematical Cognition (GEMC) framework (Nathan & Walkington, 2017) demonstrates that taskrelevant actions that are designed to highlight key mathematical properties facilitate geometric reasoning. In an experiment, Walkington et al. (2022) found that the cognitive relevance of actions matters more than movement itself: task-relevant actions significantly improved reasoning, particularly when accompanied by explanatory gestures, while irrelevant actions showed no benefit despite similar motor engagement. Virtual reality presents unique opportunities and challenges for embodied mathematics learning. Johnson-Glenberg's (2018) embodied learning framework for VR identifies two critical dimensions that this dissertation directly examines: conceptual congruence, which is the alignment between body movement and learning content, and level of sensorimotor engagement, the degree of body involvement. This framework hypothesizes that higher congruency and greater body involvement lead to better learning outcomes. Skulmowski and Rey's (2018) taxonomy of immersive learning further suggests that physical manipulations that are more aligned and integrated with the task create stronger memory traces through the generation of motor codes, though they caution that excessive interactivity without conceptual relevance may increase extraneous cognitive load. These theoretical perspectives converge on key predictions for VR geometry learning: (1) The conceptual congruence of virtual manipulations should matter more than general interactivity, and (2) active manipulation should outperform passive observation, but only when actions align with mathematical content. Study 1 synthesizes raw data from 9 independent randomized controlled experiments (N=774). Taking an Integrative Data Analysis approach (Curran & Hussong, 2009), it employs Item Response Theory to harmonize measures across heterogeneous datasets, then uses structural equation modeling within a multiple-indicator multiple-cause (MIMIC) framework to test pathways and estimate effects of task-relevant/irrelevant actions on geometric reasoning outcomes via latent embodied cognitive mechanisms. Study 2 is a 2×2 between-subjects experiment in which participants (Target N=200) are randomly assigned to conditions, manipulating two key attributes of actions they are directed to perform in VR: Conceptual Congruence (CC; High vs. Low) and Sensorimotor Engagement (SE; High vs. Low). The study involves two phases. During the baseline phase, participants are asked to prove or disprove six geometric conjectures outside VR. In the intervention phase, participants evaluate the truth of six new conjectures. They don a Meta Quest 3 headset that, through an app called CurioXR, displays custom-designed virtual geometric shapes in a VR environment. Participants interact with each shape either by direct manipulation (SE High) or by watching a video (SE Low), with the interaction being either relevant (CC High) or irrelevant (CC Low) to the conjecture that immediately follows. After each interaction, the shape disappears, and participants evaluate the truth of a conjecture about the shape's concepts or properties.

For example, the triangle inequality states, “In any triangle, the sum of the lengths of any two sides is always greater than the length of the third side.” Participants are first presented with a virtual triangle (Figure 1a), unaware that the triangle inequality conjecture will follow. Then, depending on their condition assignment, they are asked to do one of the following: (1) Move the top vertex closer to the bottom side of the triangle (CC High, SE High; Figures 1b, 1c), (2) watch a video clip of someone else moving the top vertex closer to the bottom side (CC High, SE Low), (3) move the whole triangle downward (CC Low, SE High), or (4) watch a video clip of someone else moving the triangle downward (CC Low, SE Low). After completing the interactions, the triangle disappears, and participants read and either prove or disprove the conjecture (Figure 1d). The condition-specific intervention unit is repeated across six conjectures, with the order counterbalanced. ## Preliminary findings and data gathered

Study 1 preliminary analysis of five experiments (N=425) revealed that task-relevant actions show positive indirect effects on deductive geometric reasoning (β* =.062, p =.001), while task-irrelevant actions impair geometric reasoning (β* = -.047, p =.001), supporting the hypothesis that cognitive benefits of embodied interactions depend on conceptual congruence with mathematical content rather than general motor activation. For Study 2, multiple data streams will capture the embodied geometric reasoning process: video recordings of verbal geometric justifications, co-speech gestures, and screen recordings documenting VR hand interactions and shape manipulations (See Figure 1c). Geometric reasoning will be assessed using the qualitative coding rubric developed by Nathan and colleagues (2021) regarding generalizable, logical, and operational reasoning. Expected contributions This dissertation makes several contributions to the learning sciences and mathematics education. First, it systematically investigates what makes physical actions effective in VR by isolating conceptual congruence and sensorimotor engagement as design dimensions, providing evidence-based principles for VR learning environments rather than simply comparing different media. Second, it demonstrates how integrative data analysis can promote cumulative science in the learning sciences while advancing methods for studying embodied cognition through multimodal VR data collection. Finally, this research addresses the notable paucity of experimentally controlled, theoretically motivated studies on thinking and learning with immersive technology. Curran, P. J., & Hussong, A. M. Measuring and Supporting Active Listening Skills in Collaborative Problem Solving through Multimodal AI Xiaomeng Huang, New York University, xiaomeng.huang@nyu.edu Abstract: Collaborative learning is central to education, yet it often involves unequal participation—some voices dominate while others remain unheard. Such imbalance constrains both individual learning and group outcomes. Prior research has primarily focused on regulating speaking while overlooking active listening, which remains difficult to assess and support in practice. This dissertation addresses this gap by positioning active listening as a measurable and learnable collaborative skill. I develop the Theory–Measurement–Evaluation (TME) framework, which operationalizes active listening constructs into multimodal behavioral indicators and builds interpretable multimodal learner models. Grounded in these explainable evaluations and pedagogical principles, I design a dialogic feedback system that translates analytic insights into actionable guidance to support reflection and skill development. A pre– post, between-subjects experiment will evaluate its effectiveness. Collaborative learning is central to education, not only as a way to build understanding across perspectives, but also as preparation for civic life, where collaboration is essential. Yet participation in collaboration is rarely equal. Studies consistently show that some students dominate while others are sidelined, which limits both individual learning and group outcomes (Barron, 2003). Most interventions to date have focused on efforts to design equitable conditions—structuring tasks, scripting activities, or assigning group roles (Cress et al., 2021). Far less attention, however, has been given to students ’own capacity to notice and regulate unequal participation within their groups. A promising but underdeveloped pathway for building such capacity is active listening. The National Communication Association defines active listening as “the process of receiving, constructing meaning from, and responding to spoken and/or nonverbal messages” (Worthington & Bodie, 2017, p. 214). This definition highlights core skills such as hearing, understanding, remembering, interpreting, evaluating, and responding (HURIER model, Brownell, 2023). In collaborative learning settings, active listening requires interpreting and responding to peers ’contributions in ways that make space for diverse voices and foster shared understanding. Despite its importance, active listening is rarely taught in schools. Teachers often lack the time and resources to observe subtle interactional dynamics and provide feedback, and even research has largely emphasized regulating speaking (e.g., turn-taking) over listening. As a result, one of the most critical levers for improving equitable collaboration—students ’ability to listen actively—has remained underdeveloped. This dissertation addresses this gap by pursuing three interrelated research inquiries: RQ1. What are the theoretical and practical considerations for effective analytics-supported feedback systems that aim to develop collaboration skills, and what does the current state of research reveal about such interventions? RQ2. How can active listening be characterized and measured in small-group collaboration using multimodal learning analytics? RQ3. Based on these measurements, how can we design and evaluate pedagogically grounded, AI-generated dialogic feedback that helps students improve their active listening skills? To answer RQ1, I proposed the Theory–Analytics–Pedagogical Feedback (TAP) theoretical framework (Huang & Ochoa, 2025), extending prior learning analytics models to integrate theory, measurement, and feedback for the design and evaluation of systems that foster collaboration skill development. Using TAP as an analytical lens, I conducted a semi-systematic review of existing collaboration feedback systems and identified four major gaps in current research and practice. These findings underscore the need for systems that move beyond raising awareness to actively supporting the development of collaboration skills. To answer RQ2, I developed the Theory–Measurement–Evaluation (TME) framework (Huang et al., 2026b) (Figure 1). TME transforms multimodal data (audio and video) into interpretable learner models by linking a theory model that operationalizes active listening constructs into behavioral indicators, a measurement model that automatically extracts contextualized multimodal traces using Idea Tracing Analysis (ITA; Huang et al., 2026a), and an evaluation model based on uncertainty-aware probabilistic modeling. The framework was initially validated using data from an exploratory lab study with 30 college students collaborating in nine groups on an o pen-ended design challenge. Multimodal interaction data were captured using a 360-degree conferencing system and smart pens, and audio was transcribed and speaker-diarized to support verbal data analysis. Figure 1 Bridging Explainable Modeling and Actionable Feedback To address RQ3, Building on the multimodal learner model produced by TME, feedback is delivered through a LLM-mediated conversational interface structured by the PEARLS framework and its advocacy– inquiry method (Eppich & Cheng, 2015) (Figure 1). The system presents construct estimates and exemplary moments, and provides personalized actionable guidance. In this design, the LLM operates within predefined pedagogical constraints rather than as an open-ended generative agent. A randomized between-and withinsubjects, two-session experiment with 90 college students is planned. Feedback will be randomized at the group level, between two sessions. Outcomes will assess pre–post changes between conditions in self-awareness, motivation, and behavioral change measured through TME across sessions. Expected contributions This dissertation makes theoretical, methodological, and practical contributions to CSCL research. Theoretically, it expands our understanding of collaborative processes by reconceptualizing active listening as a visible, measurable, and learnable process that shapes inclusion and knowledge co-construction. Methodologically, the proposed TME framework enables theory-grounded multimodal measurement of collaboration skills. ITA advances collaborative discourse analysis by treating ideas not only as outcomes of interaction but also as an analytic lens for interpreting verbal and nonverbal behaviors across multimodal data. Pedagogically, this work demonstrates how explainable assessments can be translated into actionable, dialogic interventions that support self-regulated collaboration skill development, amplify diverse voices, and promote more equitable participation in CSCL environments.
