# Middle School Student Science Practices in a Justice-Oriented Wildfire Science Unit

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
The growing impacts of climate change-driven disasters and environmental issues on communities create a compelling case for using these issues as anchoring phenomena to support both science learning and disaster resilience. This poster shares results from a co-produced middle school wildfire science unit. Results include 1) How self-situation in localized data sets shaped student conclusions and 2) How students incorporated knowledge of their community when analyzing data and constructing evidence-based arg uments about wildfire resilience. Poster proposal Climate change-driven disasters and environmental issues (CCDDEI’s) such as wildfires, hurricanes, and extreme weather are increasingly disrupting the lives of both students and their communities (Benvolenza & DeRignez, 2019; Lambrou et al., 2023). Moreover, the impacts of these issues, especially wildfire, are likely to become more severe and unjust in the coming decades (Davies et al., 2018; Méndez et al, 2020; Qui et al., 2025, Smith et al., 2020). Youth are vulnerable to trauma from increasingly destructive wildfires (Brown et al., 2019) and missed school days from school closures due to hazardous air quality (Santana et al., 2021). However, youth can play needed roles in addressing wildfire-related (Codreanu et al., 2014; Cox et al., 2019) and other environmental justice issues through participatory action learning (Ballard et al., 2017; Morales-Doyle & Frausto, 2019). There is potential for using CCDDEI’s as both anchoring phenomena for science learning and pathways to support youth and community resilience to these issues. However, how instructional materials incorporate such phenomena shapes whether the materials help equitably support student learning (Penuel et al., 2022) and resilience to these disruptive issues (Condreau et al., 2014; Park et al., 2024) or reinforce existing inequities in both education (National Academies, 2025) and disaster resilience (Lambrou et al., 2023). (2022) to explore how analysis of the spatial aspects of wildfire risk can be used to support students’ engagement in justice-oriented science learning. This paper shares results from a co-produced curriculum pilot that blends western scientific knowledge of wildfire and justice-oriented data sources that center students' perspectives on the impact of wildfire on their community. Research question: How do middle school students construct evidence-based arguments and use data science practices in a justice-oriented wildfire science unit? This study took place at a small public middle school in a high fire risk area of western Nevada. 56% of students identified as White, 43% as Hispanic, and 27% were from low-income families. In the prior fall, a nearby town experienced a wildfire with 14 structures lost. We partnered with an experienced teacher to co-develop eight wildfire themed lessons. The unit combined concepts about wildfire science  with analyses of ways to support community members’ wildfire resilience. In the final project of this unit students created a community-specific poster that used the Claim Evidence Reasoning format to 1) describe the problem of wildfire in their town and 2) propose a solution to increase the wildfire resilience based on students' analysis of their community's specific needs related to wildfire. Students were encouraged to incorporate their own knowledge of their community and local geography alongside the provided data sources when making claims. Data was collected through recordings of planning sessions, video and audio recordings of the class, interviews with 11 students, and artifacts of student work. Data analysis primarily focused on classroom recordings, student interviews, and artifacts of student work to foreground student argumentation and data evidence-based arguments in student posters and interviews. Artifacts of earlier student work and classroom recordings were then deductively coded using a frame developed from the initial codes to explore the context in which students' data analysis practices shaped the conclusions made  about wildfire resilience in their posters. Where possible, progressions of student learning were traced from worksheets and classroom recordings in the first few classes through to the arguments made in their final poster presentation. This poster will share two examples of student work and excerpts from class recordings to support discussion on this topic. Theme 1: Localized data sets supported data science practices drawing on students’ knowledge of their community and expanding students’ understanding of their community. The lessons used publicly available census-tract level data on wildfire risk and populations with special considerations for wildfire risk as data sets in this unit. When looking at maps of wildfire risk of their community students, without prompting, identified where their home was located, supporting deeper engagement with the data set. Additionally, when students created evidence-based arguments about spatial patterns in wildfire risk some of them combined fire behavior concepts shared earlier and their knowledge of tree density in their community. Analysis of census data also led to challenging perceptions of their community. One of the census tracts contained a culturally significant street with several expensive homes  and the greatest number of people living in poverty. A group of students who did not live in that neighborhood initially challenged the information shown in the data believing that only wealthy people lived in that area, but after closer inspection came away with a more nuanced understanding of their community. Theme 2: Centering students’ knowledge of their community elevated both justice-oriented concepts and stereotypes on populations with vulnerabilities to wildfire. During interviews some students shared how they used knowledge of family members with a disability or over the age of 65 to design wildfire risk reduction actions tailored to the specific needs of that individual. Figure 1 depicts an example where knowledge of the mobility challenges of people over 65 is used to design a solution to the overlap of  areas with high risk of wildfire and people over the age of 65 in this community. Instructional efforts to encourage students to incorporate knowledge of their community led to minimal critical analyses of this knowledge and a range of asset versus defici t-based approaches. For example, a group of four male Hispanic students focused on relationships between wildfire and Hispanic populations in that community with an asset-based perspective “Hispanic people help you for anything. Like, if there's like a fire, they'll try to help the best, they're hard workers”. Another group who focused on people with disabilities used stereotypical depictions of disability and deficit-based perspectives when designing solutions to support these people’s wildfire preparedness. The impacts of climate change-driven issues (Benvolenza & DeRignez, 2019; Newsome & Newsome, 2023) add complexities to the recommendations in the Equity in K-12 STEM Education report (National Academies, 2025) for supporting equitable student learning in an era of anthropogenic climate change. Localized data can support students more deeply engaging with data sets and leveraging prior knowledge when making evidence-based arguments. However, this study also highlights that careful scaffolding should be used to help s tudents’ use knowledge of their community around impactful issues in asse t-based ways rather thandrawing on stereotypes.
