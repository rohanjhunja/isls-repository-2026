# Seeds of Algebraic Thinking When Programming a Motorized Swing

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This case study examines the seeds of algebraic thinking that a fifth grader used in a programming task. By reviewing video data and interviews, we showcase how the student used two seeds of algebraic thinking to program the movement of a swing: closing-in and more x, more y. Our findings illustrate how programming tasks can elicit early algebraic thinking and provide meaningful contexts for developing foundational mathematical concepts.

## Introduction

Over the past two decades, there have been great strides in exploring children’s capacity for algebr aic thinking, including unpacking the ways children can reason about core algebraic concepts such as variables (Brizuela et al., 2015), relational thinking (Fujii & Stephens, 2001), mathematical structure (Blanton et al., 2019), and algebraic thinking as generalized arithmetic (Carpenter et al., 2003). Research on school algebra has often involved breaking down the subject into strands. The two strands most commonly studied view algebra as either a generalization of arithmetic (Carpenter et al., 2003) or as the study  of functions (Kaput, 2008).  We agree with other researchers (e.g., Carraher & Schliemann, 2007; Stephens et al., 2017) that functional thinking is a key entry point into algebra because it allows students to generalize and repres ent relationships between co-varying quantities in mul tiple forms (Stephens et al., 2017).  Past works have stu died how young students reasoning about functional relationships in mathematics classroom settings (e. g., Blanton et al., 2015; Brizuela et al., 2015; Stephens e t al., 2017). What remains unexplored, however, is how students might reason about functional relationships in contexts beyond the traditional classroom setting. For examp le, little is known about how young learners engage  in this type of thinking in interdisciplinary settings such  as programming. Reviews on computational thinking, programmable robots, and K-12 mathematics (e.g., Lv et al., 2022; Seckel et al., 2023) reveal that most research focuses on primary education, with the content prim arily centered around geometry and arithmetic. Howe ver, studies exploring the use of programmable robots in K-6 education to address algebraic concepts remain limited. Early algebraic thinking in such contexts requires examining how students draw on early, intuitive know ledge to make sense of relationships between quantities. Wal koe and Levin (2020) provide a useful framework for  this type of exploration through the framework seeds of algebraic thinking. The authors define seeds of algebraic thinking as small, context-sensitive knowledge pieces that emerge from student s’ interactions with their environment and support their understanding of futu re algebraic concepts. These seeds are rooted in ea rly experiences, distinct from formal school algebra, and inherently neutral, neither correct nor incorrect, depending on the context in which they are applied (Walkoe & Levin, 2020). Therefore, we asked ourselves the fol lowing research question: What seeds of algebraic thinking related to functio nal thinking does a student use when programming a motorized swing?  In answering the research question, we illustrate h ow interdisciplinary tasks, such as programming, can elicit precursor ideas to functional thinking. Moreover, we showcase how the seeds framework can help us understand students’ mathemat ical thinking across various situations due to the seeds’ context sensitivity.

## Method

This paper reports on one part of a larger project regarding students’ use of a training machine learning technology, Smart Motors, to design engineering projects within  broad thematic areas (Xu et al., 2024). Adopting a  designbased paradigm (Cobb et al.,  2003), Xu and collaborators designed and facilitate d a school-break Science, Technology, Engineering, and Mathematics (STEM) cam p for elementary students in a private school in Massachusetts. The summer camp ran for five days, w ith three-hour daily sessions, and involved 12 young participants. In this camp, the students used Smart Motors to design a Community Garden, a Future City, a Maze, and an Escape Room. The summer camp sessions were recorded, and students were interviewed. In the interviews, students were asked to explain their design. We reviewed vid eos to identify seeds of algebraic thinking  that students used when constructing their designs. Afte r multiple reviews, we identified seeds of algebrai c thinking when we observed evidence in students' utterances t hat aligned with the three key attributes of seeds:  rooted in early experiences, sub-conceptual, and applicable across different context s. We inferred a student's use of a seed based on their verbal expressions. To evaluate this, we asked ourselves, “Is this way of thinking root ed in early experiences, not yet a formal math concept, and app licable to other contexts?”. We coded video and interview data by identifying moments when students expressed  reasoning aligned with Levin and Walkoe’s (2022) s eeds of algebraic thinking. Codes were assigned when students used seeds such as closing-in or more x, more y (Levin & Walkoe, 2022). Here, we report how a fifth grader, Jacob, used see ds of algebraic thinking when making a swing move using a Smart Motor User Interface (see Figure 1, middle) in the C ommunity Garden project (see Figure 1, left). With this interface, students can connect the sensor to the motor by entering values into a table. Each value pair is plotted automatically, and the motor moves according to the inputs, causing the swing to move. In the results section, we illustrate how Jacob use d two seeds of algebraic thinking, namely closing-in and more x, more y,  while explaining how he used the interface to progr am the motorized swing. Jacob serves as one illustrative case, and in the poster presentation, we will share additional examples from other students to highlight the range of ways these seeds manifested in the project further.

## Conclusion and implications

In addressing the research question, we showcased h ow a student used two seeds of algebraic thinking, closingin, and a covariation seed  when programming a motorized swing. As identified b y Levin and Walkoe (2022), closing-in is a strategy where students progressively refine their estimates or reasoning to converge on a solution. Here, Jacob used this iterative strategy to adjust the smoothness of the swing. While doing this, he elicited a seed of covariation: more x, more y. This seed is an idea that invol ves understanding how an increase in one quantity results in an increase in another (Levin & Walkoe, 2022). Although Jacob did not relate the values in the sensor with the values in the motor, he established a rela tionship between the values in the sensor and the m ovement of the swing; the more values in the sensor, the more the swing will move. This idea about two covarying quantities is a precursor to functional thinking, as described in early algebra literature (Blanton et al., 2015; Stephens et al., 2017). Furthermore, a strategy that might initially seem procedural, such as closing-in, can lay the foundation for conceptual understandings of change, particularly c ovariation, as emphasized by Levin (2018). This hig hlights the value of exploring the interplay between goal-oriented seeds, like closing-in, which are directed towards achieving specific outcomes, and seeds that emerge without a particular goal. Such interactions may show how procedural strategies contribute to developing foun dational concepts i n early algebra and enrich early algebra instruction.
