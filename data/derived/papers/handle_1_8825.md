# The Learning Must Go On: Experience of Keeping Participants Engaged in Online Robotics Competition During COVID-19 Lockdown

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Participant retention in online learning has often been discussed as a challenge in educational research. During 2020-2021, the existing challenges were burdened by the COVID19 pandemic. This study explores certain engagement techniques with participants through an online project-based learning mode provided as a part of a nationwide robotics competition during the pandemic. 54 teams (176 participants) from 42 institutions participated in the competition to build a sophisticated Field Programmable Gate Array (FPGA) architecture. The study aims to understand participant engagement, the effect due to COVID-19 pandemic and lockdown, personal development due to competition, and the effect of scaffoldings and guided efforts. Despite the challenges due to the pandemic, additional scaffolding and guided efforts lead to approximately 24% of teams successfully building the architecture. This is similar to an average of 26% in pre-pandemic editions of the competition without these additional scaffolds. These efforts and mode of learning are thus found to successfully engage participants during pandemic.

## Introduction

With technological advancements, online learning gained popularity (Hung & Chou, 2015; Peltier et al.,  2007) and provided opportunities to learn from renowned f aculty and universities in a distance mode (Phatak,  2015). However, participation, satisfaction, and retention  in online learning has been a challenge that is co ntinuously researched by the educational community (Alqurashi,  2018; Panigrahi et al., 2018). COVID-19 followed b y lockdown forced educational institutions across the globe to adapt to online learning in 2020 (Patricia AguileraHermida, 2020). The pandemic and lockdown led to se veral new challenges for online learning. It tested  the readiness of teachers to adapt to online learning (Scherer et al., 2021), student’s preparedness for t he same (Alshurafat et al., 2021), and innovativeness in the educational systems (Pokhrel & Chhetri, 2021) to face the new challenges in changing times. The closure of universities for the physical presence of students and teachers forced researchers to explore new ways of engagement, espe cially for implementing pedagogies such as project-based learning in an online mode (Charania et al., 2021).  Thus, participant retention and providing an apt l earning experience became even more challenging. Adequately planned and designed instructions are needed for online education in such crises to achieve learning goals (Adedoyin & Soykan, 2020). Due to pandemic situations, learning has also been affected due to lifestyle changes (Lee et al., 2021), fear in the community due to pandemic (Hall et al.,  2008) causing increased stress and anxiety (Hasan & Bao, 2020; Rehman et al., 2021), etc. Attempts are being  made continuously to provide as much engaging lear ning experience as possible in the online mode. These attempts are not only to make the learning experience similar to the in-person mode of learning but also to support and motivate students through conversations and collaborations (Yates et al., 2020). These conversations are found  to foster knowledge construction among participant s, especially through discussion forums leading to col laborative learning (Sun et al., 2018). This collaboration among peers and with instructors is also found to i nfluence learner retention by keeping participants engaged (Hone & el Said, 2016; Peltier et al., 2007). To fu rther keep participants engaged, a well-orchestrate d learnercentric course that understands and addresses learn er needs and enables them to learn by doing also re sults in better engagement (Murthy et al., 2018). Apart from  engagement, the learning experience design also pl ays a crucial role in learner retention. Studies have fou nd course design as an important factor in guiding learner retention as well as in achieving learning goals (de Freitas et al., 2015; Lai & Bower, 2020; Rayyan et al., 2016). Thus, various factors affect learner engagement in online learning. However, the existing literature does not discuss the effect of these factors in pandemic  situations in massive project-based learning condu cted in an online mode. This paper explores the effect of various scaffolding and guided efforts on participant’s experience in such a learning experience. We study the experie nce of participants in the e-Yantra Robotics Compet ition (eYRC). eYRC is a massive online robotics competition with typically 10000+ participant conducted in a distance mode. This competition is conducted annually during September to March by e-Yantra (a project funded by the Ministry of Education, Govt. of India) at the India n Institute of Technology Bombay since 2012. In eYR C, ICLS2022 Proceedings 448 © ISLS students from undergraduate engineering, polytechnic, and science colleges participate as a team of fo ur. These teams are allotted a problem statement called a “th eme” which is an abstraction of a real-world proble m. Each theme requires teams to learn and implement complex  robotics and embedded systems tasks to win the competition. Participants learn these skills at a distance in a project-based learning mode (Krithivasan et al., 2015). The competition is conducted in two stages wherein the first stage provides all the participants with the basic knowledge and trains them in the skills required fo r solving the theme. Typically, 25% of the top-perf orming teams in the first stage are qualified for the second stage of the competition. In the second stage, teams are given all the necessary hardware to implement the learnings to solve the theme problem. They are provided with more resources, tips, guidance, and doubt clearing through discussion forums and online sessions to build a  robot for solving the theme problem statement. They are provided with a document called “Rulebook” that describes this problem statement and the evaluation criteria in de tail. The overall problem is divided into three sub-problems. These sub-problems are given as tasks.

In this study, we attempt to answer the following research questions pertaining to the second stage of a theme called “SankatMochan Bot” in the competition conducted during COVID-19 lockdown (December 2020 to March 2021): (RQ 1): What was the level of engagement of participants as they complete the competition? (RQ 2): What are the self-rated effects of COVID-19 and lockdown on the performance of participants? (RQ 3): How do participants report the impact of competition on personal development? (RQ 4): How do participants perceive scaffolding and guided efforts? SankatMochan Bot Theme Play: Problem “SankatMochan” is an Indian word in Hindi language that can be split into sankat (danger) and mochan (saviour) to resemble someone who saves a person in danger. “ SankatMochan Bot” is an abstraction of an Industry 4.0 problem where teams build a robot to identify poten tial dangers/threats and act before any crisis. Fig ure 1 (a). shows a 6ft x 6ft arena that represents the various  units of an industrial plant. These units are call ed Warehouse (W), Main Processing Unit (MPU), Packaging Unit (PU), Safety Services Unit (SSU). On the arena, the units are represented as circles, and are connected by a black-lined path for the robot to navigate. The path is divided into sections identified by numbers as shown in the figure. There is an elevated path called “BRIDGE” that connects MPU and SSU directly. Colored patches are placed in W, MPU, and PU on the arena to indicate industrial faults. For example, fire, chemical spill, and circuit trip  are indicated by red, blue, and green color patche s. Their remedies are the same color patches present in the SSU. The teams had to build an autonomous robot that begins at the “START” and navigates through PU, MPU, and W  checking the safety status at each unit using a co lor sensor. As soon as the robot identifies a fault, it indicates the same by glowing a corresponding color on the RedGreen-Blue (RGB) LED and communicating to a compute r system through wireless communication. It then reports to SSU, identifies remedies for the faults found, and turns OFF the corresponding LED. Finally, it wirelessly communicates the fault resolution to the computer system. Theme Play: Tasks Participating teams were guided through three tasks to solve the theme problem statement in the final task. The first task had two parts which were printing the ar ena on the flex sheet and testing the hardware. The  teams received all the major hardware along with their da tasheets and manuals to build the robot. Some of th e major components included a Field Programmable Gate Array  (FPGA) board, color sensor, line following sensor, ZigBee module, motors, motor drivers, wheels, battery, etc. All these components were tested by the participants as per the instructions provided in the first task.  In the second task, participants had to build a ro bot using the provided components to autonomously follow the line and encircle one of the units on the arena. The robot also had to identify a color patch and establish wireles s communication with the computer system. In the th ird task, participants had to make the robot traverse autonom ously through two units. The robot was required to use the bridge and identify the faults as it encountered them and communicate wirelessly to the computer system. In this task, teams integrated all the modules which they had developed in previous tasks. This task was a mini version of the final problem statement and aimed to prepare  participants for the final task. In the final task, participants were given a configuration (placement of color patches, a combination of unavailable paths, etc.) of the problem statement to be solved. Figure 1 (b). provides a snapshot of the theme run of one of the teams showing the robot traversing the arena and the messages displayed on the computer system. ICLS2022 Proceedings 449 © ISLS Figure 1 Arena and theme run on arena Scaffolding and guided efforts We aimed to keep the participants engaged in the competition and to ensure that maximum teams complete and submit the final task. For this, teams were provide d with various scaffolds and were guided through va rious activities during the tasks. One of the scaffolds was the structuring and problematization of the final problem. In the literature, these are found to be effective sca ffolds to change the task so that it helps learners  accomplish it, which otherwise would be out of the learner’s reach (Reiser, 2004). The competition was structured and the entire implementation for the final problem statement was broken into different tasks. Some major technical challenges were to design a robot with FPGA (acting as a brain), wireless communication, sensor interfacing, path planning, bridge traversal & algorithm design using concurrent programming. These challenges were divided into different tasks as described earlier.

A thorough understanding of the problem statement in the first place is necessary to solve the problem. This problem statement with rules and scoring formu la was described in the rulebook provided to the te ams. A quiz was provided to the participants that gave the m an opportunity to test their understanding of rul es through taking this quiz enabling them to learn by doing (Murthy et al., 2018). In this quiz, they were provided necessary hints and were directed to relevant sections of the  rulebook for incorrect answers. They could attempt  the quiz multiple times until they have thoroughly understood the problem statement and rules. Apart from scaffolding, instructor-student engageme nt plays a significant role in ensuring continuous participation and apt learning experience in online  learning mode (Hone & el Said, 2016; Peltier et al., 2007; Petronzi et al., 2016). We used Piazza (https://piazza.com/) to facilitate interaction among students and instructors. This platform was available for students to clear their doubts 24x7. Piazza also provided an opportunity for peerto-peer interaction where other participants can help to clear the query. Being a competition, they were instructed to help each other but not to publish or share the solution. Apart from query resolution, each week on  Monday and Thursday, instructors posted hints and tips called Monday Mantras and Thursday Tips. These posts aimed to help participants get different perspectives on the  problem and develop various approaches to test and  debug solutions and guide them to be informed designers (Crismond & Adams, 2012). To further assist teams, instructors conducted three online doubt clearing sessions through video conferencing during the competition. These sessions were intended to resolve doubts and queries that we re difficult to be addressed on the discussion foru m. Furthermore, one-to-one interaction was also conduc ted where instructors guided teams on their approac h to solving the problem, clarified their doubts, and motivated them to complete the competition.

In the second stage of the competition, 54 teams (1 76 students) had participated. These participants w ere undergraduate engineering students from 42 institut ions and various engineering streams. This included  146 participants from Electrical, Electronics or Electr onics & Communication Engineering, 15 participants from Computer Science and Information Technology and 15 participants from other streams such as Mechanical (8), Mechatronics (4), and Robotics and Automation (3). Among the 176 participants, 56 were in the final year of their engineering, 95 in the third year, and 25 participa nts in the second year of engineering. There were 1 20 male participants and 56 female participants.

## Data Collection

Online feedback was taken after submission of the final task to understand the experience of participants through a questionnaire. These questions were based on various aspects of online learning and pandemic situations found in literature, as discussed earlier. Participants w ere asked to rate theme design, competition structu re, doubt clearing through the discussion forum, live interaction, rulebook quiz, weekly tips, hints, etc., and the perceived impact of lockdown. They were also asked if they ha d COVID-19 cases at their home which affected their participation. Informed consent was taken from part icipants to use their feedback and demographic deta ils for research purposes. Total 143 participants filled the feedback which is considered in this study.

## Discussion

The competition discussed in this paper is conducted annually since 2013 and has received an immense response from students (Sabanwar & Arya, 2019). However, during 2020-2021, in a pandemic situation, many factors were different. The team members were not necessarily at the same place. This hindered the participants from effective meetings and discussions necessary for collaborative learning (Kolodner et al., 2009). This effect could be implied from the gradual decrease in the submission rate in  the tasks. As the tasks become difficult, more col laboration and brainstorming among participants becomes necess ary. Although participants had various online modes  of collaboration such as video conferencing, hardware availability became another obstruction in particip ant retention. The hardware kit to solve the theme problem was available to only one team member. As a result, only the team member having access to hardware could und ertake robot building, testing, and all other tasks  which also leads to increased load, resulting in dropout (Bawa, 2016). Thus, among the participants completing the final task, we see the majority of the team members had access to hardware (47 of 50). Studies have found the pandemic situation impacting  learner engagement due to changes in lifestyle, increased stress, and anxiety (Hasan & Bao, 2020; L ee et al., 2021). This paper also shows a similar i mpact. Almost 50% of the participants that did not complete the final task reported their participation was affected due to COVID-19 cases in their home during competition. Participants also reported COVID-19 situations to influence their experience. Their feedback on perceived experience in non-COVID-19 situations highlights the aspects of non-course or learning-related factors influencing learner retention (Henderikx et al., 2018). Understanding the challenges due to COVID-19 situat ions, various efforts were taken for participant retention. These efforts are found to help participants in continued participation. These findings are in line with previous studies to engage learners in online mode (de Freitas et al., 2015; Hone & el Said, 2016; Pel tier et al., 2007). Studies have reported the pandemic situation  tonegatively affect learner engagement, satisfact ion, and retention (Wang et al., 2021; Wester et al., 2021). However, the extra efforts to scaffold the learning and motivate participants in this edition of the competition res ulted in continuous participation and similar reten tion rates as during pre-pandemic editions of the competition.

Participants reported a positive impact of competition on their personal skills. Although all members did not get the opportunity of working on hardware, the  collaborative activity helped them contribute at t heir own level leading to learning (Alibali & Nathan, 2018; Lave & Wenger, 1991). Despite the issues in collaboration due to online mode, participants preferred to work in teams rather than individually. Thus, even in these challenges, the competition achieved various project-based learning goals (Krajcik & Blumenfeld, 2005).

## Conclusion

This study discussed various efforts for participant retention in a massive online robotics competition conducted during COVID-19 lockdown. Our study shows that the pandemic situation has impacted the participants performance. Apart from the impact on performance, pandemic also impacted their learning and focus leading to increased stress. Scaffolds such as a gradual increase in task difficulty, doubt clearing, and continuous engagement through the discussion forum and online interactions through video conferencing helped in keeping participants engaged in the competition. As a result of these efforts, despite the pandemic, the completion rate was 24% which is similar to that in the pre-pandemic situation. The study is based on participant feedback through an online selfreported survey. This limits the generalization of findings in this scope. Further studies with more w ays of data ICLS2022 Proceedings 453 © ISLS collection and application of the scaffolding effor ts in other domains (other than engineering educati on) can confirm the findings discussed to be generally applicable.
