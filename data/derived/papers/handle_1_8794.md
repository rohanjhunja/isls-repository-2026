# Learning in Practice Space Games

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Classroom-based assessment is essential to good teaching and learning. Nevertheless, pre-service and in-service teacher’s assessment professional development (PD) has been slow to change and the research supporting good assessment PD is still nascent. In this paper, we report the investigation of a relatively new approach to assessment PD, a game-based intervention, MetaRubric, a game designed to provide teachers with a playful experience that supports critical examination of rubrics, including their design and use. Games-and in particular practice space games-have been suggested to be potentially useful for teacher professional development, however little has been written about their design, practical application to teacher professional development, especially in the context of assessment, or mechanisms of action. The data presented here provide preliminary empirical support for and detail the design of one such intervention.

Rubrics for Improving Teacher Assessment Literacy Teacher’s assessment practices are widely acknowledged to be an important influence on student learning. Black and Wiliam (1998), for example, in their widely circulated meta-analysis, reported that classroom assessment can improve student performance on large-scale assessments with effect sizes between 0.4 and 0.7, which are “amongst the largest ever reported for educational interventions” (p.61). Despite its importance in education, assessment has a decades-long history of being a challenging area of teacher preparation (Pastore & Andrade, 2019). In the early 1990s, top education associations in the U.S. collaborated to publish the Standards for Teacher Competence in the Educational Assessment of Students (American Federation of Teachers et al., 1990). A decade after the Standards publication, assessment continued to be a critical issue, with authors citing “unacceptably low levels of assessment literacy among practicing teachers and administrators in our schools” (Stiggins, 1991) and surveys suggesting that pre-service and in-service teachers held inadequate levels of assessment literacy across the same period (Campbell et al., 2002; Plake, 1993). Despite the longstanding support of classroom assessment for improving learning, professional development programs have not significantly improved in preparing teachers to practice in-class assessment (DeLuca et al., 2016). Rubrics, or scoring tools typically presented as a matrix split into levels that describe expectations for an assignment’s evaluation, are one type of in-class assessment tool that may be useful for addressing this need; they are a tool that is both effective and that can be reasonably introduced through typical teacher professional development. Research supporting classroom-based rubric use has grown massively over the past two decades-from 100 papers in 1997 to 1000 in 2005, to 5000 in 2013 (Dawson, 2015) - making a review of rubrics literature beyond the scope of this paper. Two points are worth noting from other reviews of the literature, however. First, empirical evidence supporting the benefits of rubric use has grown while evidence showing negative effects of their use is not supported (Brookhart & Chen, 2015; Dawson, 2015; Jonsson & Svingby, 2007; Panadero & Jonsson, 2013, 2020). Such studies have shown that rubrics can be useful in assessing higher order cognitive skills, performance skills and complex skills (Arter & Chappuis, 2006; Mertler, 2004), in supporting student self-and peer-assessment, and for communicating with learners how they can improve their work (Andrade, 2005). In addition to improving assessment and learning, rubrics are used by teachers to communicate expectations for an assignment, provide focused feedback on works in progress, and grade final products (Andrade, 1997, 2000; Popham, 1997).

Second, because effective rubric design and use is a professional, on-going process, training for both students and teachers is recommended (Brookhart & Chen, 2015; Reddy & Andrade, 2010). Rubrics by themselves are not entirely self-explanatory (e.g. Andrade, 2005) and require interpretation by the students in order to be used effectively, particularly with open-ended authentic tasks. Additionally, while there is strong evidence that rubrics can be used as a regulatory device that ensures consistency, rubrics alone do not necessarily lead to better assessment validity (Jonsson & Svingby, 2007). Thus, how a rubric is designed and used matters, and to be effective, professional development programs may need to help teachers shift rubrics away from a focus on products or content and toward what rubrics do best-make expectations and criteria explicit (Jonsson & Svingby, 2007). This paper and project focused on this latter point of professional ICLS2022 Proceedings 235 © ISLS development. Specifically, it explored the design and use of a game intended to improve how teachers thought about and used rubrics in their classrooms. Based on teachers’ use of the game, MetaRubric, we suggest that one way to create games for teacher professional development is to create artifacts that support 1) in-game, playful opportunities for players to connect prior professional experience and knowledge with local practices, problems, and solutions, and that 2) encourage opportunities for reflection and sharing amongst their colleagues. Games for Teacher Professional Development Games have been seen as a useful means of professio nal development (PD) in diverse domains, such as architecture (Agapiou, 2006), engineering (Baker et  al., 2005; Deshpande & Huang, 2011), construction management (Pariafsai, 2016), entrepreneurship (Kri z & Auchter, 2016), nursing (Tan et al., 2017), med ical surgery (Sabri et al., 2010), and journalism (Hatfi eld & Shaffer, 2006). However, game-based learning applications are still nascent for teacher PD (Pozz i et al., 2016). More attention devoted toward teac her professional development for effective game use (e. g., Annetta & Shapiro, 2019; Foster & Shah, 2020; S tieler‐ Hunt & Jones, 2019) than toward using games as tool s for teacher professional development (Meredith, 2 016; though see Markauskaite & Goodyear, 2017; Vrasidas & Solomou, 2013). This new field of games for teach er professional development has only recently begun to organize and theorize how such games should be designed, how their designs tend to lead to particular learning outcomes, and how such games should best be used. Given the variety of contexts and learning goals of teacher professional development, an educational PD game’s design could vary widely, making it paramount at the outset to be clear about the game’s intended effects, its relationship to other aspects of PD, and theori zed mechanisms for learning (Gaydos, 2015). For exa mple, a teacher PD game may be designed to provide a context in which players may develop an improved understanding of subject matter content (e.g., statistics; Dayal & Sharma, 2020) or more directly to help players understand and practice particular ways of knowing and being that are based on teacher professional standards and practices (e.g. lesson design; Markauskaite & Goodyear, 2017). In t his paper, we detail the game MetaRubric’s design and intended learning goals, present data from its use,  and then situate our findings and the game in the context of epistemic games in order to theorize the role of games in teacher PD. MetaRubric, a Teacher Professional Development Game-Based Activity MetaRubric is a card game-based activity designed to be played  by groups of 3-5 players in education professional development settings. The game consists of a set of numbered cards, each containing instructions for the players to follow. To play MetaRubric, one player flips through the deck of cards, reading  aloud the instructions on each card for all of the players to  complete. Cards are flipped over and read until th e deck is complete. The game is divided into two rounds. Cards #1-12 are around 1, and cards #13-20 are round two. There is a recommended break in between the rounds, which are expected to take about one hour each. During the first round, players are instructed to create a movie poster based on a film all players are familiar with, create a rubric to evaluate that poster, and then evaluate one another’s’ posters using the rubric. During the second phase, players are instructed to create a rubric to evaluate the rubrics that they created during the first phase-a “meta-rubric”. The players then evaluate each other’s rubrics using the meta-rubric. The broad aim of the activity is to provide players  with a playful, creative experience that they can evaluate with a rubric and a meta-rubric that they use to evaluate the rubric they just created. In this completing the meta-rubric creation process, the hope is that participants will reflect on the advantages and disadvantages of rubrics, especially for open-ended, creative activities like the one they just completed. The activity is creative and game-like (e.g., it includes scoring) though it may not be a “game” per se, as it does not tend to sup port typical game activities. For example, the design relies on the emotional affordances that are provoked by the purposeful ordering of activities –the movie poster is deliberately created before the rubric used to evaluate it. Re-doing the activity or doing the events out of order would presumably provoke different reflections from the players, making MetaRubric’s re-playability more like narrative-based games that  are typically played only once, and less like games that support strategizing and replayability. Given the familial nature of games and difficulty i n clearly defined borders (e.g., Gee, 2011) MetaRubric’s design sought to integrate design principles from previous research on teacher professional development. In particular, three design principles that were primary in this work were:

1)  Teacher professional learning as social-Research on teacher professional development has demonstrated

the value of teachers learning from each other as they take on new instructional reforms (e.g. Grossman, Wineburg & Woolworth, 2001). MetaRubric is played in a group of colleagues or other profes sionals (e.g., rather than as a single player game at a PC).

2)  Teacher professional learning should de-privatize practice-In general, this collective work of t eacher

learning serves to de-privatize the teachers’ practice (Bryk et al, 2001; Wardrip & Herman, 2018). Too often, teachers’ work has been characterized as isolated without allowing colleagues to see each other’s work (Lortie, 2002). In MetaRubric, rubric creation and interpretation – an often-iso lated activity – is carried out in real-time and discussed in groups.

3)  Teacher professional development should supports sense making-MetaRubric creates opportunities for

teachers’ sense-making of what new forms of instruction might look like,  particularly through providing a concrete example (the rubric creation process) (Rosebery & Puttick, 1998). Teachers can observe other professionals’ sense-making processes and through the guided discussion, share with others what they’re thinking (e.g., their justifications and rationale). These discussions create opportunities for teache rs to learn new practices (Horn et al, 2015) Prior to this study, MetaRubric had been used as an educational experience at conferences and as a part of teacher PD workshops. Participants, through self-report, were generally positive about the game exp erience reporting a range of educational take-aways (Kim & Rosenheck, 2020). MetaRubric is a teacher professional practice space game (Reich et al., 2018), meaning t hat it is designed to provide an opportunity for te achers to rehearse and reflect on their instructional practic es, particularly through playful activities that he lp to surface problems they might encounter during design and ena ctment. Teacher professional practice space games a re theorized to compel teachers to change their use of rubrics due to greater empathy with how students experience the rubrics and a deeper recognition of the tensions faced during rubric design and use (Reich et al., 2018). This study sought to gather more direct data supporting this theorized process, connecting the particularit ies of its design to teacher learning and professional develop ment. To do so, this study focused on educators’ me aning making through examining their discussion before, during, and after game play (Horn & Kane, 2015), seeking to draw connections between how it is that teachers talked about the game and their teaching practices.

## Methods

Eight high school teachers were recruited to participate in this study. All were from the same high school where one of the author’s worked as a principal. The recruited teachers had been teaching for 2-12 years, taught at least one English class in general, advanced, or special education classes for students from 9-12 grade. The teachers were asked to bring rubric materials that they currently or previously used in their classes for discussion. At the start of the first session, all of the teachers wer e briefly told about the game, its design, and the structure of the research. Then, they were split into two groups of four based on their professional learning community  and colleagues they work with on a regular basis. In th ese groups, they were 1) interviewed, 2) played MetaRubric, and 3) were interviewed again, with each group led by one of the paper authors. The following prompts were used to guide the interviews, which lasted between 30 mi nutes and 1 hour: What kinds of formative assessmen ts do you currently use in your teaching? What are your views on rubrics and how have you used them? How do you think about designing assessments to support studen t-centered learning? Can you share some examples of assessment tools that you use? Researchers were present in each group during game play to answer questions and provide additional moderation, such as keeping trac k of time and explaining unclear instructions. Teac hers’ interviews and their discussions during game play w ere audio recorded and transcribed. Additional rese archer field notes from the observations were also included in the corpus of data. Analysis To understand teachers’ views of rubrics going into game play, two researchers independently reviewed the preinterview, game, and post-interview transcripts, and created descriptive codes to answer the following  research questions: 1) “How do teachers view rubrics?” 2) “H ow do teachers talk about the game?” and 3) “How do teachers talk about rubrics relative to the game experience?” Working independently, the descriptive codes were then grouped into themes. To maximize coverage and coherence of the research question results, the researchers then shared their generated themes in order to create a combined theme set. They then reviewed their descriptive codes to either combine codes, generate new themes, or to reassess whether a new code might need to be applied to the original data.

Utterances used to answer the first two research questions were found by looking for explicit references to the game or to rubrics in the transcripts. Utterances used to answer the third question (making sense of the game in terms of their professional experience related t o rubrics) were inherently less clear, as such reas oning would likely be internal rather than explicit. For exampl e, ideal data would include a teacher saying someth ing like, “Based on the game, I think that rubrics...” or “Ba sed on my experience using rubrics, I think that th e game...” ICLS2022 Proceedings 237 © ISLS Therefore, in order to answer this question, we looked for utterances about rubrics that occurred during game play. This data allows us to characterize the game-based, rubric-oriented discussion. We sought credibility in our analysis through sever al means (Lincoln & Guba, 1985). Specifically, we sought to maintain methodological consistency through our data collection and analysis (Morse et al., 2002). By this, we mean that our data and analysis were aligned with our research questions and the theoretical framework guiding the study. This was not meant to constrain our process of analysis. Rather, this alignment was intended to ensure “trustworthiness” (Lincoln, 1995) in that our point of inquiry, our analytic approach and ou r analyses were carried out systematically and as we designed. Also, we conducted regular critical discussions of our analysis among the research team. This permitted us to compa re each other’s interpretations, challenge one anot her’s analyses and refine our own coding definitions to m aintain a common understanding among the group. Whe n consensus was not reached, additional examples from  the data were brought to the discussion and the co ding definitions were refined until consensus was reache d. Finally, having a principal as part of the resea rch team allowed the team a built-in way for the researchers to check interpretations.

## Results

How did the teachers view rubrics?

With regards to student learning, the teachers described rubrics as providing a backdrop against which they and their students could evaluate work, “setting the tone” for feedback on how the student could improve. For example, one teacher described rubrics as a tool that provides a space within which students’ work could fall (e.g., a 3 out of 4 for clarity), and teachers, students themselve s, or peers could perform such an assessment. In ad dition to being used by students for formatively evaluating t heir work, the teachers saw rubrics as a means for communicating with students, helping them students to internalize the values along which their work is  judged (e.g., good papers are clearly written). Ideally, the teachers expressed, rubrics provided both a scale and descriptive text that students would use repeatedly as they edited and improved their work. Another function of rubrics the teachers discussed was as tools to communicate their evaluations to students, to other teachers, to parents, and to adm inistrators. Two communication examples were emphas ized, communication with colleagues, so that different te achers within department were “on the same page” wi th regards to evaluation, and communication with admin istration and parents, so that teachers could expla in (e.g., during parent-teacher conferences) why a student paper received a particular score. The rubric, in this way, served as both a means for professional communication amon gst teachers, and as an assessment tool that provid ed transparent and justifiable rationale for student grades. The teachers also cited challenges to using rubrics to help students learn. They expressed concerns that the students might not understand what a rubric’s component means (e.g., what does it mean for a paper to show originality?), what the difference between rubric s cores means (a 3 or a 4 on clarity), or how a stude nt might change their work, given the feedback they get through the rubric (e.g., how can a sentence be changed to become clearer?). They pointed out that creating longer, more verbose versions of rubrics could provide more examples, hopefully communicating the evaluation metric in more detail, but were concerned about students not reading the rubrics, especially if the rubric was long or had a lot of dense text. Applying a rubric can also be difficult because interpreting student work relative to a rubric is inherently subjective, and different teachers within the department can use the same rubric but end up with a different  score for the student’s work, a problem that is am plified the more diverse or creative student submissions are. A dditionally, different parts of student writing may  fall into different levels of quality, leading to problems li ke “circling between the [levels]” for an overall e valuation. Finally, teachers noted that rubrics are not often used in real-world settings, and so while students may find them useful during school, the skill needed to use a rubric well might not have application once students graduate. How did teachers talk about the game?

During the post interview, teachers described posit ive and problematic aspects of the game, MetaRubric. They pointed out that the game highlighted the way that rubrics don’t always support a level of creativity in student work, rather, rubrics can limit what students create from the onset. MetaRubric, they said, helped to highlight this difference-between trying to come up with creative solutions versus more narrowly aiming to fulfill assignment criteria.

Reflecting on MetaRubric as a professional development tool, the teachers p ointed out that the game very quickly helped them to talk about professional practices while also avoiding personal aspects of professional work that can get “dicey,” such as when two teacher s might critically discuss the merits of a rubric t hat one of them had developed. By providing a playful, neutral activity, MetaRubric provided an experience where teachers ICLS2022 Proceedings 238 © ISLS felt they could more easily talk amongst one another about the professional challenges of rubric use, “getting on the same page” especially with colleagues who don’t normally work closely together. With regards to drawbacks to MetaRubric, they pointed out that the game did not seem to te ach them anything particular (e.g., a significantly new way to view rubrics, a new method for rubric creation or use). This was especially notable for these teachers when they encountered and reflected on the parting words of the game, which states that players should have gained “a new perspective on what is and isn’t helpful in evaluating students’ open-ended work.” How do teachers talk about rubrics in terms of the game experience? We categorized how teachers talked about rubrics in terms of the rubrics generated through the game as well as how teachers talked about rubrics relative to the game and in their professional work. With regards to the gamebased rubrics, the teachers’ discussions were based around the use of the rubrics, such as “what [scor e] did you give that? I gave that an 8.” As they used the rubrics in the game to grade one another’s movie posters, they also asked one another for clarifications (e.g., “What did you mean by that?”). With regards to talking about the game-based rubrics and their own rubrics, the teachers pointed out that they had a different definition of “rubric” than the game seemed to use. Three of the teachers said that, within the past year they had taken a professional development  course that was explicitly oriented toward creatin g good rubrics, and that based on that course, they understood rubrics to be different from what the game was focused on. As a result of that prior PD, they’d consider the game-generated rubric to be more akin to a checklist than a rubric, as it did not go into sufficient detail regarding the assignment’s grading criteria. Prior to this study, informal observations of how MetaRubric was played suggested that the target experience was being achieved, with participants making and thinking about rubrics more deeply, reflec ting on their own prior understanding of rubrics, and their use of rubrics in classrooms. Results from this st udy support that, showing first-hand how MetaRubric provokes rich discussion around the problems that teachers face, including their understanding of what rubrics are a nd what role rubrics play within their profession. These discussions, including the teachers views and the p roblems they face with rubrics, were extremely cont extdependent, relating to both the teachers’ personal experiences and training with rubrics, the goals for teaching and learning they have in their classrooms, and the school environment in which they work.

## Discussion

Design and Use of MetaRubric – Is surfacing problems enough? MetaRubric was originally designed as a professional practice  space game that could provide a play-based experience through which teachers create rubrics, a nd then reflect on the rubrics and their own rubric-based practices (Kim & Rosenheck, 2020; Reich et al., 201 8). Observations from this study suggests that MetaRubric was successful in this regard – it created a collaborative playful experience that was a short enough to play during an hours-long PD workshops, it simplified rubric creation, and it served as a shared experience that was reflected on relative to professional notions of rubric design and use. MetaRubric and other similar professional practice space game s, by design, use the games’ rules and mechanics to allow for teachers to playfully engage with these professional practices, surfacing problems rather scaffolding particular teaching practices (Reich et al., 2018). That is, the game does not embed, for example, “how to make rubrics better” in its core design or in the post-game reflection. Instead, the game’s intended experience ends once an issue is raised, and at this point, relies on teachers’ prior professional knowledge and experience to make the experience educational and beneficial moving forward. As a result, players may walk away from a game play session not necessarily knowing how to improve their own rubrics.  In this study, for example, teachers raised the challenge of creating rubrics that provide verb ose descriptions that attempt to provide students w ith clarity against the risk of creating rubrics that were too long and that students did not read at all. The pro blem was one that many had struggled with and none new how to re solve effectively. So, while the teachers were gene rally positive about the PD experience and the way that the game created a space where they could quickly and easily discuss rubric-related issues, they also expressed that the artifacts generated through game play were  neither rubrics (by their understanding) nor did the experience significantly change their view of rubric design and use. Further design work is therefore needed if the game  experience is to achieve its intended goal of help ing participants impact their own rubric design and use.

Learning in a practice space game The work of teaching has long been characterized as taking place in isolation (Lortie, 2002), however educational reformers have begun to emphasize the importance of  professional practice learning opportunities that ICLS2022 Proceedings 239 © ISLS intentionally bring teachers together to reflect upon and shift teaching practice (Wardrip et al., 2015). As the data from this study suggests, PD games like MetaRubric may be particularly useful tools for this community-oriented reflection, as the game tends to achieve its intended design goal of helping participant raise issues of practice that they faced. In order to move forward with MetaRubric’s design and differentiate between individual and community-oriented learning that can occur beyond raising issues of practice, we believe it’s necessary to further specify what and how learning might occur, especially relative to the particularities of the game’s design. To do, we turn to Markauskaite, Goodyear, and Bachf ischer (2014)’s typology of epistemic games, which differentiates between epistemic games that are formal, in which disciplinary epistemologies are conveyed to the player and those that are functional, pertaining to the epistemic knowledge used to make sense of the world, especially in professional contexts. In formal epis temic games, the epistemologies adopted by players are new and knowledge is associated with a particular disci pline. In functional epistemic games, players are c oncerned with determining how disciplinary knowledge is situated and applied to their professional actions. Markauskaite, Goodyear, and Bachfischer (2014) go on to categorize different epistemic game types based on 1) their review of five different fields’ professional development course material and 2) interviews with relevant faculty. The game types they describe are: propositional games, situated problem-solving games, meta-professional games and transprofessional games, translational public discourse games, and weaving games. MetaRubric, though it was not intended to be designed with the taxonomy in mind, can be interpreted as a (functional) meta-professional game, or a game that does not aim to convey new disciplinary knowledge to the players, rather is d esigned with the intention to “enhance joint knowledgeable action” (p. 1291) especially through re-describing the activity from a shared professional view with intentions of applyin g disciplinary knowledge to local professional cont exts. Identifying MetaRubric as a functional meta-professional game is helpful for specifying design aims, identifying shortcomings in achieving those aims, and for guiding the development of supplementary and supporting learning activities to address such shortcomings.

For example, teacher professional development practice space games like MetaRubric are characterized by at least two parts, a playful experience that mo dels a component of teaching and the post-game refl ection activity (Reich et al., 2018). The playful experience is used as a foil for reflection; the reflection is presumably to help teachers relate their game experience to their own actual professional practices (though its role is not clearly specified in prior literature). In MetaRubric, in particular, five reflection questions are provided: Did this activity make you think differently about rubrics? Next time you make a rubric for a class, what will you keep in mind? How did you feel about the format of this rubric? Did it work well or was it constraining? How would you modify it to better meet your needs? Revisiting the design intention of the game, we can see how these questions might change – from questions that are intended to provoke individual conceptual and practical change, toward a change in the local professional community through sharing of expertise, connecting with one another, and finding local solutions.

Advancing Games for Teacher Professional Development Practice space games are a relatively new approach to teacher PD, and more work is needed to both unde rstand how such games can be designed and used effectively, especially across different audiences and environ ments. Given the complex landscape of teacher PD, differen t models of game and play-based learning will need to be explored. For example, games designed for professio nal environments with participating experts might b e designed with less focus less on directly affecting individuals’ practice through novel understanding and more on helping teachers sharing expertise with one another. Such games could better support players through d ialog-talking about current practices, the professional p roblems the face, and then coming up with solutions  to solve these problems. Learning in such a game activity is  less like knowledge acquisition from game play, and more like participation in a professional learning community; the role of the game is to support professional community development. Nevertheless, given the rapidly changing landscape of education technology and the way th at inclass assessment skills and associated professional development have long been critiqued (e.g., Brookhart, 2001; Campbell et al. 2002), practice space PD games rese arch is especially timely (Timmis et al., 2016; Wil l et al., 2019).
