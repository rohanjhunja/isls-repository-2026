# Parents as Design Partners in Fostering Curiosity and Connection

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study employs a longitudinal diary approach to explore how adult caregivers at home characterized and supported children’s learning with technology during and after the pandemic. By enabling in-the-moment, multimodal storytelling, the approach provides unique insights into how families navigate tensions, foster connections, and adapt digital tools to their needs. Implications include advancing remote, scalable methods for studying family learning practices and sharing stories as inspiration for design. Initial findings suggest caregiver attention to technology opportunities that support authentic inquiry, transparency in joining in collective exploration and learning, and colocated experiences.

## Introduction

Children’s screen time jumped dramatically during t he 2020 pandemic, with an international meta-analysis estimating an average daily increase of 84 minutes (Madigan et al., 2022). In the U.S., screen time ha s remained relatively stable since then, with ear ly elementary school children averaging about 3.5 h ours a day (Common Sense Media, 2025). The narrative around screen time and use is complex and often conflicting, with widespread concerns about social media and mental health along side enthusiasm about to ols that foster creativity and connection. As conversations around technology and young children continue to evolve, learning science s research has an important role in providing insight s about everyday learning with technology relevant to caregivers, educ ators, and designers. This paper examines the role of parents and other caregivers at home as pivotal agents in recognizing and fostering learnin g experiences that integrate digital tools to build  children’s confidence, curiosity, and interests. Aligned with the ISLS 2025 theme of “Educating for World-Making,” we explore new methods that allow us to surface caregi vers’ roles in shaping transformative learning ecol ogies through their documentation of home-based activities. Through this lens, we aim to insp ire research and design that bridges systemic support for caregivers with methodologies for understanding and amplifying their impact.

In this paper we describe methods and report findin gs from a longitudinal remote diary study that captured a diverse set of families’ experiences during the 2020 transition to remote learning and in the four years since with the goal of understanding the ways in which families adapt. In this analysis we consider the questions:

(1) What were caregivers’ hopes and concerns for th eir child’s learning and how was technology conside red in

these views? (2) What kinds of interest-driven learning experiences were happening at home that integrated technology? (3) What did caregivers notice when doc umenting moments that invite curiosity, support sha red enjoyment, and inspire a desire to continue learning over time? To answer these questions, we analyze responses from caregivers to a series of daily prompts about learn ing at home and present quantitative and qualitativ e summaries, including case representations of two families to showcase partners in context. Theoretical framing This work considers learning and development as part of ecological and sociocultural processes, happening across spaces in which young people spend their time and f acilitated by interactions with the people and reso urces in those spaces. Digitally anchored  activities shared with parents, siblings, grandpare nts, and extended family can generate questions, explanations, and extended conv ersations that build connections to real-world experiences and support the development of language and literac y (e.g. Dore et al., 2021). Digital tools also allow for the cocreation of artifacts that offer opportunities to r eflect together on original content. Ethnographic s tudies have shown that these forms of joint media engagement (J ME) can be a powerful mechanism for foste ring cultural knowledge, coordinating activities between home and  school, and co-developing interests that evolve overtime (Takuchi & Stevens, 2011; Barron & Levinson, 2017). JME is an emerging area in Human-Computer Interaction (HCI), with a recent meta-analysis emphasizing the importance of considering i nterrelated components of participants, goals, roles, media properties, conte xt, and engagement dynamics to design media experie nces that effectively support meaningful outcomes for familie s (Yu et al., 20 24). Parents are also recognized as broader vital learning partners who scaffold their children ’s understanding, act as learning brokers, and enga ge in collaborative activities, including learning from their child, that foster critical thinking, creativity, and real-world connections (Barron et al., 2009), frequently enric hing learning experience by drawing on their unique  funds of knowledge—including cultural, experiential, and technical expertise (Moll et al., 1992).

## Methods

To document the ways in which families adapted to r emote learning and continued their learning as scho ols reopened, our team conducted a remote diary study—a form of experience sampling (Larson & Csikszentmihalyi,

1983) allowing researchers to collect part icipant reflections on moments as they occur. In sp ring of 2020, after

schools began closing, we collected daily documenta tion from 109 families using dscout, a smartphone-based platform designed for user experience research. Thi s allowed our team to inter act with families and collect rich qualitative data without face-to-face contact, affording broad geographic reach and pandemic-safe practices (Takeuchi, Martin & Barron, 2021). This method uniq uely foregrounds parent perspectives by positioning  them as collaborators in data collection and analysis. They c hoose what to document and what meanings to assign, sharing stories in their own voices and on their own schedules.

## Participants

dscout maintains a panel of 100K+ participants roughly representative of the US smartphone owning population. Over 1.25K of them expressed interest in participat ing in this study by completing an initial screener  and 264 fit our criteria of having a child between ages of 5-10 at home whose school had moved to remote instruc tion. To ensure diverse economic perspectives, qualified app licants were sorted into three household income gro ups and 37 participants were randomly selected from each. In total, 109 families across 28 U.S. states completed the 2020 study, of which 45% reported household incomes belo w the 2020 median for American families ($75K). We followed up with a subset of 23 families in 2022 and 2024 (focusing learners in K-2 grades in 2020). Multimedia diary entries In 2020, data collection was organized around theme s of: school-provided resources and connections  prepandemic and during remote learning; examples of daily learning moments; and reflections on learning and family life during this time. In 2022 and 2024, participants we re asked to share updates regarding school support and connections as well as examples of daily learning moments  and reflections on learning using technology. Participants responded to a series of prompts for each theme. Daily learning moment entries included a 2-minute video of the caregiver describing and reflecting on  a learning moment they observed that day, a photo of the activity, and multiple-choice items noting social partners (e.g., siblings, friends, parents), origins (e.g., school, parent-initiated), content area (e.g., art, writing, math), and resources (e.g., books, devices). Caregivers also rated how much fun their child was having during the activity and how much their child was learning (on a 0-10 scale). Follow-up questions were asked through direct messaging. Data was exported from dscout as.csv files and media artifacts. SPSS was used for quantitative analysis,  organizing data files with families as the unit of  analysis (N =

109) and also by diary entries (N = 926). Qualitative data was explored through rounds of open coding in Dedoose

with research team discussion and by concurrently d eveloping visual case representations of families i ncluding media-rich diary entries with audio and photos to see family stories over time.

## Findings

Competing and multidimensional narratives around learning and technology In 2020, caregivers recognized the value of technology for learning in entirely new ways. When asked to indicate challenges posed by remote learning in the first fe w months of the pandemic, academic worries about th eir child falling behind were far less common (25.0%) than so cial isolation, the most frequent concern across fa milies (77.8%), with no differences related to household income. Parents whose schools offered synchronous video class time emphasized the social and emotional benefits of routine connection to teachers and peers (Pozos et al., 2021). When asked about benefits of remote learning, three  quarters (78.5%) cited greater awareness about wha t and how their child was learning, supported as parents listened in on video sessions and explored acce ss to personalized metrics of learning progress in digital apps and platforms (Barron et al., 2021). In 2024 when asked what they were most concerned about with respect to their child’s learning and wellbeing, most common (65.2%) was making sure their child is inspired by what they are learning at school followed by concerns about screen time (60.9%). Parents also recognized how technology can  inspire learning. In ranking the ways technology has been important to their chi ld’s learning from a list of 10 items, pursuing hobbies and interests was top-ranked across families (M = 8.8 using a weighted average ranking system). Joint engagement and social connections fostering emergent interests Daily learning moments shared by caregivers across our dataset include rich examples of these inspirat ional and interest-driven moments, including young people and families pursuing hobbies and interests at home. In the 2020 data, most of the 668 learning moments originated f rom school (75.6%), but caregivers rated parent-and childinspired activities (23.5%) significantly higher in  terms of how much fun their child was having (M = 8.3) compared to those from school (M = 6.7), F(1,654) =  41.79, p <.001. Of the 258 technology-supported learning moments shared across 2022 and 2024, caregivers cit ed children themselves as the catalyst for 40.0% of  the activities while school assignments accounted for 33.7% and caregivers at home encouraged 15.9%. While Josh appreciated the structure their school provided during remote learning, much of it leveraging technology-enabled supports like Zoom classes and personalized learning platforms, he shared concerns about social isolation and worried that Carter was spending too much time on screens. The transition to digital persisted beyond 2020 and Josh noted new challenges for parental involvement. In 2022 he struggled to n avigate online learning platforms Carter used for s chool assignments, “I can't see the questions that he's a nswering. He fea red students might become overly dependent on digital sources instead of reasoning, what he calls a “big brain drain.” His vision of AI included a “dark, dark future” where “truth will only be defined by what AI responds.” Josh also recognized how technology has supplemente d learning in ways that allowed his son to thrive and pursue interest-driven learning and social engagement. When Carter' s school went remote in 2020, Josh reported searching for online activities for his son to keep him engaged and connected. The “only re ason” Josh signed Carter up for chess club was that it was the sole remote extracurricular school offering that year. Chess has since “grown into a passion” for his son. While the  club was remote it utilized Chesskids.com, where k ids could learn and practice remotely with other players. In 2024 Jocelyn was frustrated with the ext reme school district focus on  standardized testing, reflecting, “[it] seems like she’s learning, but it's just the way she's learning. ## Discussion

Designing systems of support that connect children’ s diverse learning ecologies is recognized as an im portant direction in fostering transformative experiences that address the whole child, promote equity, and support agency in learning (Darling-Hammond et al., 2020; Pinkard, 2019). To ensure meaningful design and uptake of emergent technologies, the unique experiences and reflection s of caregivers are essential. As lifelong learning  partners, caregivers offer critical insights into what foster s engagement,  builds connections, and meets their children’s needs. By documenting learning moments as they occur, remote multimodal diary studies foreground caregivers’ voices and capture the evolving contexts, purposes, and social dynamics of technology use. In our study, themes raised in case narratives high light the ways in which caregivers both embrace and resist learning with technology (see also Barron & Levinson, 2017), making value decisions during opportunities for observing and joining in the learning. Josh reports “learning a lot” watching Carter use ChessKids and Jocelyn shares how the entire family uses Alexa for joint discovery and questioning. Parents also note integrating digital and physical routines, such as Carter’s chess team experiences and when Alexa becomes a regular part of Stella’s family Scrabble games. As both Carter and Stella learn through digital exploration, they bring new knowledge to their in-person social worlds. Moments of authentic inquiry and connection  are picked up and extended in both family stories—noticing interest-driven learning often influenced parental decisions  to allow more online time and autonomy. These longitudinal narratives offer unique insights into how families are addressing digital learning tensions, contributing to theoretical understanding of how parental attitudes and practices leveraging technologymediated learning evolve. Given the rapid change in technologies making their way into the home space, there is a need to support caregivers as learners and co-designers of learning at home and brokers of cross-setting learning (Barron et al., in press). Our team is exploring wa ys to share these parent stories of learning cataly sts and generative technologies with other parents and teac hers as multimedia artifac ts that can be filtered by particular challenges or goals. Continued methods for foregrou nding caregiver voice and inviting them as design p artners are important next steps in shaping transformative boundary-crossing systems.
