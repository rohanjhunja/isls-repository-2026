# Examining Socio-Cognitive and Affective Dynamics of Team Functioning and Leadership in Medical Simulations

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
T eamwork and leadership are critical in promoting team success and positive outcomes in high intensity contexts. Given the urgency during pediatric medical emergencies, it is crucial to understand what support s or impede s successful team dynamics, including cognitive, metacognitive, affective, motivational, and social (CAMMS) processes. Thus, this study explores the dynamics of first -, second -, and third-year medical residents’ (N = 21) CAMMS processes and their influence on individual and team performance in three team-based pediatric emergency simulated scenarios. Results indicate a significant correlation between motivation and cognitive and social processes, as well as a significant influence of team performance on CAMMS dynamics. Our findings support the need to further examine the impact of individual level processes on medical team functionality and performance. Using multimodal data, future studies will continue to ex plore relationships involving CAMMS dynamics and medical team functioning and performance.

Medical simulation as a learning modality allows medical trainees to safely practice interprofessional and interpersonal skills, such as teamwork and leadership, in strenuous, high-stakes environments (Murphy et al., 2016). Efficient teamwork is pivotal to team success and positive outcomes, especially in high intensity contexts such as acute pediatric medical environments  (Schmutz et al., 2019). To understand what facilitates or impedes efficient teamwork, it is essential to consider the (dis)engagement of  various cognitive, affective, metacognitive, motivational, and social processes (CAMMS; Azevedo et al., 2018)  of each team member and how they may either enhance or hinder individual and team performance  (Giannakos et al., 20 19). Despite the collaborative nature of healthcare teams, previous research in medical education  has emphasized individual performance and SRL enactment; however, there remains a lack of understanding regarding how each team members’ CAMMS processes play a role in their SSRL and the overall performance of healthcare teams (Bransen et al., 2021). Socially shared regulated learning (SSRL) requires the  collaborative activation and regulation of CAMMS processes between multiple individuals working together towards a common goal (Järvela et al., 2023). Effective SSRL is highly contextual (Järvenoja et al., 2015), requiring shared mental models (Kazemitabar et al.,

2022) of both individual and joint task goals, defining expectations in accomplishing shared objectives and

managing team dynamics (Hadwin et al.,  2018). While SRL persists within the paramete rs of a designated team role, these efforts feed a collective objective, requiring the recognition and co-regulation of team-level behaviors (Hadwin et al., 2018; Bransen et al., 2021). As such, medical learners (i.e., students and residents) must carefully balance both their individual roles and expectations and their role as team members in the context of effective team management and collaborative learning (Bransen et al., 2021). Given the criticality of teamwork on team performance, there remains a need to investigate and distinguish the impact of each member’s CAMMS processes on overall team functioning and performance (Schmutz et al., 2019).  This study intends to identify the relevance of individual CAMMS processes and their influence on individual and team performance within team-based acute emergency medical simulations using low-fidelity manikins.

## Participants and simulated scenarios

414 First-, second-, and third-year medical residents (N = 21; Mage = 31.5) specializing in pediatrics and emergency medicine were recruited from an American  university teaching hospital to complete three simulated acute pediatric emergency scenarios (e.g., newborn with Meconium Aspiration Syndrome, Congenital Heart Defect, and Supraventricular Tachycardia) in a children’s hospital simulation suite using low-fidelity medical manikins. Each simulated scenario was facilitated and evaluated by subject matter exper ts by providing standardized information about the simulated patient and necessary lab results when prompted.

## Procedure

Once consent was provided, participants completed pre-self-report measures (see Table 1) and then were randomly placed into teams of four, comprising of three medical residents and one confederate nurse. Participants completed the three simulated scenarios with their respective team s, but alternated roles (team lead er, primary assessment, and airway management) at the beginning of each scenario, assuming one role at a time. The team leader in each simulated scenario was instrumented for collection with multimodal data (i.e., portable eye tracking glasses, electrodermal activity [EDA]) to capture fluctuations in CAMMS processes in real time and video/audio recordings captured entire team performances from multiple angles. Each scenario trial followed an identical paradigm: (1) roles were assigned and the team leader was instrumented; (2) the team entered the simulation room and instructed to stand in designated areas based on the role; (3) a pre-recorded verbal medical brief was presented to participants; (4) the simulation began  and each team was given a maximum of seven minutes to complete each case. The scenario was considered complete when medical intervention was successful, indicated by the facilitators or seven minutes had elapsed. Each simulation room contained a low-fidelity manikin connected to monitors that accurately represented the patient’s condition throughout the scenario. Following the completion of each simulation scenario, pa rticipants were given three minutes to decompress while the team leader was de-instrumented, new roles were assigned, and the new team leader was instrumented. This process was repeated until each participant assumed each role one time, and the three scenarios were simulated. Following the simulation of all three scenarios, participants completed post-self-report questionnaires (see Table 1), followed by a debrief led by medical experts.

Coding and scoring Self-report measures were administered before and after the simulated scenarios (see Table 1). To evaluate changes in participant’s CAMMS processes from pre-to post-simulation, difference scores were calculated and used in subsequent analyses.

Leadership performance was assessed using the Concise Assessment of Leadership Management instrument (CALM; Nadkarni et al., 2018). The CALM consists of 15 items rated on a 4-point Likert scale and evaluates four elements of leadership: (1) role/style; (2) communication; (3) team management; and (4) medical management. SMEs reviewed video recordings of each simulated scenario post-hoc and rated the team lead er’s performance, ranging from 16-60.

RQ3: How do the changes in self-reported CAMMS measures influence the structure of participant clusters?

Given the significant differences between clusters in team performance, we then examined how each of the variables contributed to the structure of participant clusters. To address the highly correlated variables defined in Research Question 1, we conducted  a principal component analysis (PCA) on changes in confidence and 416 interprofessional collaboration competencies from pre-to post-simulation. PCA revealed two components that accounted for 67% of the total variance (PC1: 49.93%; PC2: 17.43%).

Using the identified PC’s and the remainder of the self-reports, hierarchical cluster analysis with Euclideandistance identified two distinguishable clusters. Cluster 1 (N = 11) exhibited larger, positive changes in affect (e.g., MES subscales; positive and negative activating and deactivating; see Table 3a)  and smaller, negative changes in social, (meta)cognitive, and motivational components (e.g., PCs and cognitive reappraisal subscale from ERQ; see Table 3b). Cluster 2 (N = 10) exhibited smaller changes in affect (e.g., MES subscales; positive and negative activating and deactivating) and larger, positive changes in social, (meta)cognitive, and motivational components (e.g., PCs and cognitive reappraisal subscale from ERQ). To explore CAMMS processes in the context of emergency medical simulations, we examined the relationships between changes in participant’s CAMMS processes from pre-to post-simulation. Our results indica te a strong inverse relationship between participants reported confidence and teamwork. We also examined if distinct participant profiles could be defined based on changes in reported CAMMS processes from pre-to post-simulation, and if they differed in both individual and team performance. Cluster 1 exhibited a significant decrease in confidence and a slight increase in teamwork competencies, and Cluster 2 exhibited an increase in confidence and a predominate decrease in teamwork competencies. While there was no difference in leadership performance between clusters, Cluster 2 solved the acute medical simulation scenarios significantly faster than those in Cluster

1. We interpret these findings  as a reflection of how team performance influence d participants’ reported

confidence and teamwork competences. Given the distinc t participant profiles and their differences in team performance, we aimed to determine which CAMMS processes contributed most to cluster structure and distinction. However, no specific variable was greatly influencing the defined clusters. We interpret these results as the interaction between CAMMS processes is more influential on cluster distinction, rather than viewing them individually. Our findings further push the narrative of highlighting the interaction between individual CAMMS processes and how they contribute to the overall functioning and performance of a healthcare team.

The study of socio-cognitive and affective dynamics in team-based medical simulations is an area of research that significantly advances the intersection of healthcare education and learning sciences (Huang et al., 2025). By embedding research on medical teams within the CSCL tradition of regulated collaborative learning, we can bring theoretical depth to simulation-based education and push the boundaries of CSCL theory itself. Future research should further investigate the dynamic interplay between individual-level CAMMS processes and collective team dynamics in high-stakes medical simulations.
