# The Impact of Responsive-Teaching-Focused Co-design on Teacher Practice

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
The Next Generation Science Standards promote students’ engagement in authentic science practices, including theory-building practices such as modeling and explanation. The role of teachers in NGSS-aligned classrooms includes devising strategies for identifying, understanding, and connecting students’ ideas. In other words, teachers are required to respond to student ideas. Yet, the demands of responsive teaching pose challenges for teachers. To address challenges and support teachers in implementing responsive teaching, we co-design classroom lessons and strategies with teachers. This paper presents our co-design approach to supporting teachers in developing responsive teaching strategies. We analyze classroom video of one teacher’s classroom from two consecutive implementations of co-designed lessons. Responsive teaching was made an explicit focus of co-design before the second implementation. We investigate the instructional moves of the teacher and present evidence that making responsive teaching the focus of teacher-researcher co-design causes a shift in the instructional moves of a teacher.

## Introduction

Building on the idea that learners construct new knowledge on the foundation of their pre-existing knowledge, the National Research Council (2012) advocates engag ing students in activities that reflect the  authentic knowledge-building practices of  science, including theory-building practices such as modeling and explanation (NGSS Lead States, 2013). For students to successfu lly engage in these practices, they need support in articulating, evaluating, and refining their pre-existing ideas. This support can be given by  teachers when they create a context where students feel encouraged to share their id eas, and when they provide resources and adopt strategies to elicit students’ prior knowledge and relate it with new knowledge (Baviskar et al., 2009). In this approach to teaching – known as responsive teaching – teachers attend to students’ ideas, seek to understand their perspectives, and make connections between their thinking and scientific knowledge (Robertson et al., 2015). This approach has proven to have a positive impa ct on helping students attain normative scientific understandings (Robertson et al.; 201 5) and  facilitating students’ engagement in  theory-building practices (Swanson et al., 2023). However, teachers’ development of comfort and expertise with responsive teaching is contingent on time and professional support (Jacobs et al., 2010). One of o ur teacher partner s specifically requested support and resources for implementing re sponsive teaching, as she mentioned the non-existence of teacher training on responsive practices: …I did not know how to elicit this. We don’t have a lo t of structures being taught to us, especially as science educators, for how to get the  kids to get their thoughts out there, how to organize them, … And we are never trained on this… To support our teacher partners in implementing responsive teaching strategies, our research team made developing these strategies  a focus of our co-design approach. Through the co-design process, researchers recognize teachers as partners in the design of need-based interventions and innovations, making space for flexibly iterating the outcome of the process (Roschelle et al., 2006). This partnership  increases teachers’ agency to develop and adapt strategies and increase s their comfort with implementing re search-based innovations in teaching (Severance et al, 2016). Co-design also positively impacts the development of teachers’ practices when it is the emphasis of research practice partnerships (Coburn and Penuel, 2016). This paper presents evidence that making responsive teaching the focus of teacher-researcher co-design causes shifts in a teacher’s instructional moves.

Theoretical foundations Responsive teaching is an instructional style that encourages student participation through the design of tasks and activities to elicit students’ prior knowledge, with the recognition that their ideas are resources for the construction of more formal knowledge (Smith, diSessa & Roschelle, 1993). Responsive teaching enriches the experiences of students, positioning them as responsible and relev ant agents in their knowledge construction (Hammer et al., 2012). While it has benefits, responsive teaching poses challenges to teachers. Some of these include addressing students’ expectations of being given correct answers in a typical teacher-student role in a science classroom, the institutional pressure of attaining success in high-stakes tests (Levin, 2008), and teachers’ lack of k nowledge of how to respond to students’ thinking (Jacobs et al., 2010).

## Study design

The data for this study  is from a larger multi-year design-based research project  with middle school teachers focused on engaging middle school students in scien tific theory-building practices, including computational modeling (Swanson et al., 2023). This paper focuses on one teacher, Ms. K, a National Board-Certified 8th-grade science teacher with about 20 years of  experience. Ms. K has partnered with our research team since 20 20, participated in three summer workshops, and implemented two co-designed lesson units. Preceding the first (Yr.

1) implementation in the Fall of 2022, Ms. K partic ipated in two 4-week summer online co-design workshops

(2020, 2022). Across these  workshops, Ms. K was introduced to theory building in science classroom s and codesigned a week-long unit on energy transformation. After Yr. 1’s  implementation of the energy unit, Ms. K participated in another 8-week in-person summer co-design workshop (2023). The goal of th is third workshop was to iteratively refine the unit based on findings and reflections of Yr. 1’s implem entation. Through a process of collaborative goal setting at the start of the w orkshop, the project goal shifted to address a n identified need to include responsive teaching  strategies. We helped our teacher partners devise strategies fo r eliciting and responding to students’ ideas. As a result of this, Ms. K rede signed class activities in the energy transformation unit and implemented them in the Fall of 2023 (Yr. 2). In this work, we investigate the impact of the iterative codesign on Ms. K’s teaching strategies by comparing the two-classroom implementations. Co-designed activity The co-designed energy transformation lesson featured two main activities. The first acti vity was the  “C an Crusher”: crushing an aluminum can with a stainless-steel water bottle dropped from different heights t hrough a hollow tube. Students varied the amount of water in the bottle and the height from w hich it was dropped. Yr. 2’s extended activity required students to test, identify, and document the system  that would crush the can with the least amount of energy. The second activity was the “Marble on a Ramp” (Yr. 1) or the “Car on a Ramp” (Yr. 2), with each object (marble or car)  rolling down and colliding with a block at the base  of the setup. Students were expected to  vary the mass of the marble (or car) and the angle of inclination of the ramp  and record their observations. Also, Yr. 2’s extended objective included prompti ng students to identify the system with the least amount of energy that would cause a half-meter movement of the block upon collision.  Students created energy models of the “Can Crusher” and the “Marble (Car) on the Ramp” systems, compared their observations to identify patterns, used graphic organizers to examine relati onships, compared their models in small groups, and  engaged in extended discussions about energy transformation  in the systems. These activities facilitated a critiquing of their reasoning for the constructed models, enriching the scientific practices of theory building. Due to the iteration of Yr. 1’s lesson implementation through the co-design workshops, the redesigned lesson was  built for more responsive teaching practices – providing more opportunities to elicit and respond to students’ ideas.

## Data collection and analysis

The Yr. 1 implementation spanned 5 days in Ms. K’s 8th-grade classroom with 32 students, while  the Yr. 2 implementation spanned 7 days in a classroom of 26 students. Audio and videotaped data and observation s were collected each day and transcribed (using Otter AI). To analyze the transcripts, we examined the pattern of Ms. K’s utterances during each lesson’s whole-class discussions and represented and quantified them using a method of verbal analysis (Chi, 1997). We took a grounded approach and used an ope n-coding strategy (Corbin and Strauss, 2008) to identify responsive teaching moves where Ms. K elicited and responded to students’ ideas. The refined coding scheme  was divided into two major categories: eliciting moves and responding moves. The descriptions and examples of these moves are presented in Tables 1 & 2 respectively. Intercoder reliability was conducted with two researchers on three of the transcripts with a Cohen’s Kappa of 0.91 (O’Conner et al., 2020). ## Findings

To account for the variation in the number of implementation days and minutes (Yr. 1: 5 days/225 mins, Yr. 2: 7 days/320 mins), the proportion of move type/minute was used to compute the percentage of occurrence of each coded move. Ms. K’s eliciting moves increased from 17% in Yr. 1 (n = 38) to 43% in Yr. 2 (n = 139). A similar trend was observed in her responding moves with an increase from 26% in Yr. 1 (n = 58) to 61% in Yr. 2 (n = 196). ## Discussion

We leveraged our co-design approach to support our teacher partners in developing and implementing responsive teaching strategies in a high-stakes context. One characteristic feature of co-design, as stated by Roschelle and Penuel (2006), is its significance in addressing an educational ne ed which, in this context, is the ability to  help students articulate and refine their thinking toward knowledge construction while engaging in scientific practices.

In this paper, we presented data about the shift in  instructional practices of a teacher after she par ticipated in codesign workshops. Teacher-researcher co-design is therefore one way to support teachers in developing skills to implement responsive teaching  practices. Building on our previous work on the co-design of computational modeling lessons with responsive practice as an implicit goal (Swanson et al., 2023), the present paper makes the argument for the central role of co-design in research practice partnership interventio ns to develop responsive instructional practices. We intend to analyze lesson implementations of our other teacher partners using our coding scheme and seek to build a more nuanced model of responsive teaching in a theory-building context.
