# Joint Visual Attention Predicts Learning in 1-On-1 Remote Teaching: A Dual Eye-Tracking Study

**Conference:** ISLS 2023

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Because of the pandemic, more learning experiences are now taking place online. In this paper, we aim to understand how remote interactions involving physical material can be enhanced with technologically mediated systems. We look at the data from a previous study where pairs of participants programmed a microcontroller. One participant was trained as an expert and acted as an instructor to teach the other participant remotely. Each session was divided into an “instruction” phase led by the expert and a “practice” phase led by the learner. Both participants wore a mobile eye-tracker. We analyze the eye-tracking data from 75 sessions to understand what drove variations in learning. We found that joint visual attention correlated with learning gains during the instruction task, and with the instructor’s prediction of the learner’s gain during a practice task. Cognitive load (estimated from pupil dilation) was also a significant predictor for learning. We discuss the implications of these results for studying and shaping online interactions.

## Introduction & literature review

While the COVID-19 pandemic has had many negative impacts on our daily lives, it has also opened new opportunities for using virtual spaces in flexible ways. Many interactions that used to take place in person are now done through computer-mediated systems, such as video conferencing, virtual worlds, or chat-based systems. In some cases, these systems have improved interactions and significantly simpl ify our lives, and in some other cases, they have caused frustration and decreased the quality of interac tion. The same applies to education. As more and more learning environments are transitioned online, there is a nee d to identify the affordances and limitations of remote instruction on students’ learning experiences and outcomes.

In this paper, we want to better understand how remote interactions that involve physical material can be enhanced by technologically mediated systems. Many STEM (Science, Technology, Engineer ing, Mathematics) domains require the use of physical tools such as electrical circuits, micro-controllers, magnets, and chemicals, and thus have been traditionally conducted in person. Given that remote and/or virtual hands-on experiences bear the potential to increase the reach of effective STEM education, even be fore the pandemic, there have been attempts to explore the efficacy of remote hands-on experiences like virtu al laboratories, demos, and hands-on sessions. These remote experiences frequently focus on simulations and/or vi rtual environments and less frequently on experiences that involve real, physical instruments (for reviews, see Heradio et al., 2016 and Reeves & Crippen, 2021). It has been argued that while careful designs can help virtual and remote labs achieve their intended learning goals, it would be hard for students to acquire familiarity w ith equipment and materials in this way due to the lack of direct interactions with them (Gamage et al., 2020). Here, we explore how technology can support remote, synchronous hands-on learning experiences where physical learning instruments are available for direct manipulation. Specifically, we focused on peer instructions due to its freque nt use in hands-on STEM learning through peer tutoring and teaching-assistant-led lab sections (Porter et al., 2013; Darcy et al., 2016). We consider three types of online interactions for the scope of this paper. Communication can take place through: 1) a traditional webcam-based video feed (e.g., Zoom, FaceTime, Skype) where participants mostly see each other’s faces, 2) a first person video feed, from a Head-Mounted Camera (HMC) where the participants see the field of view of another person (e.g., GoPro videos), 3) a HMC video feed of another person augmented with the other person’s gaze location (e.g., Shared Gaze Visualizations; SGVs, D’ Angelo & Schneider, 2021). There is prior work on all three forms of computer-mediated communication. For the  webcam condition, studies have looked at the effect of “instructor presence” (i.e., using a traditional we bcam to show a front-facing view of the instructor). Even though this is the most used form of online communication, intere stingly, systematic reviews have found mixed or null effects on students’ learning (e.g., Henderson & Schroeder, 2021). For the HMC perspective, there is evidence that experiencing events through another’s eyes better supports learning compared to a traditional third-person view of the same event (Lewis et al., 2019). F inally, reviews on Shared Gaze Visualizations (e.g., D’Angelo & Schneider, 2021) suggest that showing pre-recorded or real-time eye-tracking data can enhance communication and learning. In a prior study (Sung, Feng & Schneider, 2021), we compared CSCL 2023 Proceedings  © ISLS 84 these three forms of computer-mediated communication and found that SGVs gene rally outperformed other conditions in both learning gains and instructor tracking of learner performance.

In this paper, we conduct a secondary data analysis of the eye-tracking data to  better understand how SGVs in remote peer hands-on instruction may result in these identified benefits. Prior work suggests that various eye-tracking features – for example cognitive load (estimated from pupil dilation; Beatty & Lucero-W agoner, 2000; Schneider & Pea, 2013) and joint visual attention (Jermann et al., 2011; Schneider  et al., 2018; Sharma et al., 2013) tend to be correlated with collaboration quality among dyads of similar expertise. W e are interested in replicating these results for a new context (i.e., teaching session involving physical material). The contributions of this paper are as follows. We apply dual eye-tracking methodologies used in peer collaboration to one-on-one remote teaching that involves physical instruments. We also adapt these methods to take into account the more rapid and asymmetric interaction that takes place between a learner and a teacher. We correlate these measures with learning gains, and also with the instructor’s abi lity to predict learning gains from the learner. Finally, we compare these metrics across our three experimental conditions. General description of the study & research questions The data analyzed in this paper comes from a study where pairs of parti cipants programmed a micro-controller (GoGo board; Sipitakiat et al., 2004). One participant was trained as an expert and acted as an instructor to teach the other participant. They each wore a pair of glasses equipped with a camera and a mobile eye-tracker during the session. The interaction was mediated in three different ways: 1) w ith a traditional webcam, 2) with a first person Head-Mounted Camera (HMC) video feed; 3) with the gaze of the  other participant displayed on top of the HMC view of the eye-tracking feed (Figure 1). The learner completed a pre/post-test, the difference of which is used as the measure for learning gain during the session. Additionally, the instructor tried to predict the learner’s responses on the post-test based on observations and interactions with the learner during the learning session. For the scope of this paper, we are interested in analyzing the eye-tracking data to better understand what drove higher learning scores of learners and more accurate predictions of instructors. We look at time spent on different areas of interest, pupil dilation (which can be used as proxy for cognitive load; Bea tty & Lucero-Wagoner, 2000), and measures of joint visual attention (Jermann et al., 2011). Our research questions (RQs) are as follows:

• RQ1: Do eye-tracking metrics (e.g., time spent looking at Areas of Interest, pupil dilation metrics, joint

visual attention) correlate with learning gains and predictions?

• RQ2: Do eye-tracking metrics correlate with self-reports of learners’ levels of engagement,

understanding, and perceived levels of instructor responsiveness / effectiveness?

• RQ3: Do eye-tracking metrics differ between our three experimental conditions?

## Methods

Description of the study Experimental setup The full description of the study can be found in Sung, Feng & Schneider (2021); we pr ovide a summary of the experimental manipulation and findings here. Eighteen instructors with prior experie nce with programming and microcontrollers (mean age 26.5, 10 id entified as female) were recruited from a master’s program in educ ation. After an initial hourlong session of training and one practice session, each ins tructor conducted three to nine CSCL 2023 Proceedings  © ISLS 85 teaching sessions to different learners. All instructors taught in all thre e experimental setups, and the number of sessions conducted in each setup is balanced for the same instructor. A total of 75 English-speaking adults (mean age 26, 43 identified as female) were recruited from the community to ser ve as learners in the study sessions. During each session, the instructor taught the learner to use and program the GoGo Board using an instructional manual available for both the learner and the instructor. All learning sessions  required learners to physically engage with the GoGo Board to assemble the circuitry and program the microc ontroller. The setup of the instruction manual naturally led to an initial instruction-focused portion (“instruction phase”) and a later problemsolving focused portion (“practice phase”) in the sessions. During these different phases, the dynamic between the instructor and the learner naturally shifts, so we manually located the timestamps of the phase changes so that we can analyze the phases separately. The pre-and post-tests contained 10 questions each and were carefully designed to match in format and content (see Sung, Feng & Schneider, 2021). The dif ference between the posttest and the pre-test is used as measurement for the learners’ learning gains. After each session, the instructor was asked to predict the learner’s performance on each of the questions on the post-test. Prediction accuracy was calculated from the difference between the actual post-test score and the s ummed instructor predictions for each learner.  Additionally, the learner and the instructor filled out surveys about their learning and teaching processes and tool usage. A subset of learners and instructors were interviewed about their experience. Eye-tracking preprocessing Eye-tracking data was collected from two Tobii Glasses (Figure 2). By default, the data collection system captures a video from the perspective of each participant (through a camera located on the bridge of the glasses), and a log file describing the full eye-tracking data. This text file contains timesta mps (synchronized with the video feed) and various eye-tracking metrics (e.g., locations of the participant’s gaze on the video, pupil dilation, etc.) Figure 2 Homography procedure to remap gaze data from each participant (top) onto a referenc e image (bottom) using fiducial markers (in red). The original and remapped gaze points can be seen as blue and green ellipses. Analyzing mobile eye-tracking data is challenging because the x and y location of the gaze is relative to the perspective of the participant. To detect what is looked at, we disseminated fiducial markers in the environment (similar to QR codes, as seen on Figure 2). Since we also want to compute measures of joint visual attention, we remapped the two participants’ gazes onto a refer ence image. We used the methodology described by Schneider

(2020) to do so. The bottom of Figure 2 (left side) shows an image that combines all the Areas of Interest (AOIs)

CSCL 2023 Proceedings  © ISLS 86 of the study: the various instructions sheets used by the learner and instructor, the computer display used for programming the micro-controller, and a laptop used by the two participants to comm unicate. Each AOI is surrounded by fiducial markers, that are then detected using a computer vision algor ithm (Chilitags; Bonnard et al., 2013). When enough common markers are detected between the participant’s perspe ctive and the reference image (shown as red line in Figure 2), we use a homography to remap the gaze for each frame o f the video from the top to the bottom view into the reference image. Because the homography remaps both participants’ gazes onto the same 2D plane, we can compute measures of joint visual attention between them. Given the complexity of the process and the crucial role that the results play in subsequent ana lysis, we applied several sanity checking methods before moving forward. Firstly, we genera ted a short video for each session to make sure that the homography worked as expected. We also created heatmaps and line graphs (Figure

3) and manually inspected them to ensure that the processing results were a s expected. Finally, we generated

metrics for each session (e.g., the number of gaze points, number of succes sful homographies) to identify problematic sessions (i.e., where metrics were +/-2 standard deviation fr om the sample mean). 8 sessions had to be removed due to missing data or issues with the homography. Left side: heatmap of the down sampled eye-tracking data (red = instructor, blue = learner). Right side: number of gaze points that were categorized as joint visual attention. Eye-tracking analysis For our eye-tracking analysis, we defined three types of AOIs: 1) the instr uction sheets, 2) the computer display used for programming the micro-controller, and 3) the laptop screen used for  communicating with the other participant. We used these AOIs for our analyses below. We generated three types of metrics:

1) Time on various AOIs: for the learner and the instructor, we measured how many gaze points were on

each AOI. We divided this number by the total number of gaze points for each participant to mitigate the fact that there is a varying number of missing data points for each person (depending on the quality of the calibration and fit of the eye-tracking glasses). The final metric represents the percentage of gaze points on each AOI.

2) Pupil dilation: pupil dilation can be used as proxy for cognitive load (Beatty &  Lucero-Wagoner,

2000). While this measure has received a significant amount of empirical suppor t, it can be influenced by individual differences and environmental factors (e.g., ambient light). To take into account individual differences in pupil size, we normalized this value between 0 and 1 using a min-max normali zation. We also controlled ambient light by running the study in rooms with constant light (i.e., with no windows). We then computed the average size and standard deviation of the normalized pupil size for the learner and instructor. This measure should be taken with a grain of salt, however, because pupil dilation measures can be noisy.

3) Joint visual attention (JVA): JVA has been used extensively in collaborative learning settings and has

been consistently associated with collaboration quality (Schneider et al., 2018). In prio r work, the distance between two gazes to be considered as JVA was defined based on the task, and  the lag between two gaze points is usually +/-2sec (Richardson & Dale, 2005). We explored different values for  these parameters in our analysis to allow for the more rapid and asymmetric interaction that takes place between a learner and an instructor in this setup. We also had to adjust how JVA is computed for different conditions: in  the Gaze condition for example, whenever one participant was looking at the laptop screen, we looked at whether there was JVA on the entire field of view of the other participants (since this is what was displayed on the laptop screen). ## Results

RQ1: Do eye-tracking metrics (e.g., time spent looking at AOIs, pupil  dilation metrics, joint visual attention) correlate with learning gains and predictions? Results are summarized on Figure 4. We found no significant correlation between the amount of time spent looking at the three types of AOI (i.e., instructions sheets, computer display, and laptop screen). For both the instruction and the practice phas e, we found that the average pupil dilation of the learner was significantly correlated with learning gains (r(51) = 0.39, p < 0.01, and r(51) = 0.32, p < 0.01, respectively). This suggests that higher levels of cognitive load were associated with higher scores on the learning tests. For the instruction phase, we also found that the standard deviation of the instructor’s pupil dilation was negatively corr elated with the learner’s learning gains (r(51) = -0.35, p < 0.05) and positively correlated with the instructor’s ability to predict the learner’s perform ance on the tests (r(51) = 0.46, p < 0.01). This suggests that more variation in the instructor’s cognitive load might be detrimental to tea ching but might indicate a better ability to predict student’s learning. We computed measures of joint visual attention and varied the distance (in pixel on the common image) between gazes for them to count as JVA, and the time lag between them. We  found that while the distance did not affect the results, the time lag did. Most results became non-sig nificant when considering a lag of more than a second. For the results below, we only considered simultaneous moments of joint visual attention with no time lag. We found that joint visual attention was correlated with learning gains during the instruction phase (r(51) = 0.39, p < 0.01) and with the instructor’s ability to predict learning during the practice phase (r(51) =  0.31, p < 0.05).

RQ2: Do eye-tracking metrics correlate with self-reports of learners’ levels of engagement, understanding, and perceived levels of instructor effectiveness? After the study, learners had to fill a survey asking them about their experience. In this section we analyze their self-r eported levels of engagement, understanding of the content taught, and perceived level of instructor effectivenes s. We explore whether any of the eye-tracking metrics correlate with them Understanding: learners with larger levels of normalized pupil dilation during the instruction phase reported better understanding of the material (r(51) = 0.3, p < 0.05). However, the more they had moments of joint visual attention on the laptop during the practice phase, the less they fe lt like they understood the material (r(51) = -0.31, p < 0.05).

Engagement: learners with larger levels of normalized pupil dilation during the instruction phase reported being more engaged (r(51) = 0.32, p < 0.05). During the practice phase, this was also associated with the instructor spending more time monitoring the laptop screen (r(51) = 0.33, p < 0.05). Instructor effectiveness: during the instruction phase, the more the instructor looked at the laptop screen the more he was found to be effective (r(51) = 0.3, p < 0.05). During the practice phase, this was negatively CSCL 2023 Proceedings  © ISLS 88 associated with the time spent by the learner looking at the laptop screen (r(51) = -0.44, p < 0.01) and the standard deviation of the instructor’s pupil size (r(51) = -0.31, p < 0.05) RQ3: Do eye-tracking metrics differ between the three exper imental conditions? We ran an ANOVA to check for differences between the three experimental conditions. For the instruction task, we did not find any significant differences on any of the eye-tracking metrics (F < 1). For the practice task, we found a significant difference for joint visual attention: F(2, 49) = 3.5, p < 0.05. Post hoc comparisons using the Tukey HSD test indicated that that participants in the webcam condition had more joint visual attention than in the HMC condition (with no gaze). The pairs’ actions revealed more details that were hard to observe from the eye-tracking data alone. First, we found that joint visual attention played a major role in facilitating communication in both sessions. The instructor and the learner needed to know that they were looking at the same content, to ground their exchanges. This was evident when instructor s said “you see it? Great”, or “yeah, that should be it”. How joint visual attention was achieved, however, looked very diff erent across conditions. In the gaze group, we observed that the instructor merely said “this” or “here” a nd looked at a sheet or an  object and used their gaze or finger as a deictic (i.e., pointing) gesture to attract the learner’s attention. This minimized communication compared to the webcam condition. When instructors used the webcam, they had to provide more CSCL 2023 Proceedings  © ISLS 89 verbose explanations (e.g., “look at page number XXX and at table YYY”) or pr esent material to the webcam which occurred more physical effort. Because grounding was more effortful, instructors tended to talk for longer periods of time before checking on the learner to make sure that they were  on the same page (for example in the excerpts above, they were 15 exchanges in 92 seconds in the gaze condition, compa red to 4 exchanges in 157 seconds in the webcam condition). In contrast, instructors in the gaze conditions tended to have faster and shorter interactions, that were punctuated with learners’ positive interjections (“I s ee”, “yes”, “alright”, “okay”, “perfect”). It was also easier for the instructor to check on the learner, by quickly looking at the laptop screen and making sure that the right material was being looked at (in the excerpt above, a fter almost every instruction, the instructor of the gaze condition looked at the point of view of the learner displayed on the laptop screen).

## Discussion

In this paper, we conducted a secondary data analysis of the eye-tracking data collected during a study on remote teaching (Sung, Feng & Schneider, 2021). We found that the time spent looking at various AOIs did not correlate with learning scores, but measures of cognitive load and joint visual attention did. More specifically, we found that joint visual attention was correlated with learning gains during the instruction phase and with the instructor’s ability to predict learning during the practice phase. This suggests that visua l coordination is an important grounding and learning mechanism when an instructor explains concepts to a learner. Hig h levels of JVA might also indicated that instructors were following the learner’s thought process during the practice task, which helped them identify moments of (mis)understanding. Interestingly, the fact that we had to decrease (and ultimate remove) the time lag suggests that teaching interactions are faster than inte ractions between peers analyzed in collaborative learning studies (e.g., Schneider et al., 2018). Additionally, we found that when learners had larger pupil sizes, they tended to learn more – which is not surprising, since this can be seen as a proxy for their cognitive load; this measure was also correlated with their self-reported level of unde rstanding and engagement. In short, we can identify students who are “trying hard” by looking at their pupil dilation. During the instruction phase, the standard deviation of the instructor’s pupil dilation was negatively correlated with learners’ gains on the test. Because this measure was also correlated with learners’ evaluation of in structor effectiveness, we hypothesize that this reflects the instructors’ ability to teach the material in a one-on-one setting, by handling questions and adapting to unforeseen situations. Interestingly, this measure was also correla ted with the instructors’ ability to predict learning scores during the instruction phase. This might indicate a w illingness to engage with learners about points of confusion (whereas other instructors might have waited until the practice phase). Surprisingly, we did not find any difference between our three experimental conditions  on any of the eye-tracking measures. This suggests that for remote one-on-one teaching, lea rning is not related to the number of gaze-based interactions (e.g., the number of times that they achieved joint visual attention), but rather their quality. By qualitative analyzing two sessions, one from the gaze and one from the webcam conditions, we found that the learner-instructor interactions were notably different. In the gaze condition, the instructor tended to have shorter, more dynamic interactions with the learner, punctuated with a large number  of pointing gestures, with their finger or gaze. This suggests that building a common ground was more effe ctively done in the Gaze and HMC conditions. In the webcam condition, the instructor spent less effort grounding their discussion in physical material. Instead, the discussion was more high-level and the instructor made less attempts at checking learners’ understanding. This could explain why we found that learners tended to learn more in th e gaze condition, based on our prior analysis (Sung, Feng & Schneider, 2021).

As a first attempt to study the gaze patterns of learner-instructor pa irs in remote instruction involving physical artifacts, the study is limited in scope. While one-on-one instruction is a crucial part of in-person STEM instruction that involves physical materials, in remote learning settings, due to the  limited access to specialized physical materials, it is unlikely that individual learners who are not a part of an organized learning and teaching environment (as is the case during the COVID-19 pandemic) can obtain the same physical mater ials as a peer or an instructor. It is possible, however, that an expert might be able to provide remote instruction to students within an organized learning environment (e.g., a classroom) in addition to existing instructional support. In this case, it would be beneficial to study how the same technology can support one-to-many instructions or even asynchronous interactions. For future work, we want to better understand the quality of gaze-facilitated learning interactions through qualitative analysis of the interactions so that we can uncover how the technol ogy can be leveraged in other instructional setups.

## Conclusion

In this paper we studied remote one-on-one interactions between and instructor a nd a learner involving physical material. We conducted a secondary data analysis of the eye-tracking data and found several metrics to be correlated with learning, the instruct or’s ability to predict learning gains, and self-reports. We also conducted a CSCL 2023 Proceedings  © ISLS 90 preliminary qualitative analysis of participants’ interactions using a webcam or HMC view augmented with eyetracking data; we found that grounding generated more effort in the webcam condition, which caused participants to check on each other less often. These results have implications for remote teaching, especially in 1on1 settings: learners and instructors need effective tools to facilitate grounding, and a tradi tional webcam view is likely not enough. Our study suggests that eye-tracking and HMC views might facilitate more effective communication compared to a traditional webcam video feed. Our findings replicate prior res ults showing that Shared Gaze Visualizations (SGVs; D’Angelo & Schneider, 2021) can support social interactions in  remote educational settings. As eye-tracking technology becomes more affordable and commonplace, we can hope to see more SGVs in online learning settings. An interesting line of work is to see how it can enhance learning in non-traditional online teaching, as we have done here with STEM content involving physical objects.
