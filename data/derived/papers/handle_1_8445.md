# From Queer Feminism to Anti-Racism: A Sociocultural Analysis of Activist Learning Across the Lifespan

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Learning scientists have begun to spotlight social movements as important sites of learning. In this paper, we build on this growing literature by analyzing how a White lesbian activist came to learn anti-racism through her participation in the queer feminist movement. Although researchers recognize the longitudinal nature of activist learning, due to resource constraints ethnographic studies often last no longer than a couple of years. Here we respond to this challenge by presenting an analytical approach for tracing the learning of anti-racism over extended timescales (e.g., decades). Specifically, we suggest the potential of memoirs to illuminate learning processes across social settings and across the lifespan.

## Introduction

Scholarship in the learning sciences has shown that  racism, particularly White supremacy, remains a mo dern societal problem (Nasir, 2011). After George Floyd’s public murder in the United States in 2020, peopl e across the globe engaged in new forms of anti-racist activism. However, few studies have investigated anti-racism as a learned phenomenon. This is significant because empirically grounded theories of anti-racism learning have the potential to make educational efforts more effective, thereby bringing us closer to a racially just world.

In this paper, we argue for a lens on this problem  that examines learning across settings and across the lifespan. To that end, we analyze the anti-racism learning processes of a grassroots organizer named Mab Segrest (hereafter referred to as Mab), as narrated in her book Memoir of a Race Traitor (1994). Mab’s story is compelling because after growing up with a racist family in deeply segregated Alabama, this White lesbian woman became active in queer feminist movements and eventually came to identify as a “race traitor,” resisting White supremacy and committing herself to deep forms of anti-racist activism. Drawing on situated and sociocultural perspectives on learning (Lave & Wenger, 1991; Wertsch, 1998), we explore the following research question: What aspects of anti-racism did Mab learn, and how can that learnin g be described in terms of the relative availabilit y of sociocultural artifacts related to race, identity, and activism across her lifespan? With this study we aim to make two main contributi ons to research on learning in social movements (Curnow & Jurow, 2021). First, we offer the beginni ngs of an analytical approach designed to trace act ivist learning across extended timescales. Specifically, we explore the unique affordances of memoirs as a data source for analyzing learning. Second, our findings reveal novel insights about activist learning, particularly how some activists might learn to accept the risks associated with grassroots activism. Prior Research In recent years, researchers have begun to conceptualize social movements as important sites of learning (Curnow & Jurow, 2021). Regarding anti-racist activism spec ifically, several studies have drawn on sociocultur al perspectives to document anti-racism learning proce sses in a range of settings (e.g., environmental ju stice movements—see Curnow et al., 2019). Methodologicall y, learning scientists in this area have recognized  that learning is distributed across both space (i.e., social setting) and time (Jurow & Shea, 2015). Unders tanding the full complexity of how, where, and when people learn to be activists requires an analytical approach that traverses what Erickson (2021) calls the “fuzzy boundaries” o f people’s lives: how learning travels between peop le’s personal and professional lives, as well as between childhood and adulthood. With respect to time, Erickson (2021) has argued for researchers to employ a “diachronic approach to ethnography—ethnography in time depth” (p. 156). Whereas many prior studies have taken a longitudinal approach, due to resource constraints they can often only trace people’s learning over a couple years at  most. What is needed is a methodology that can cap ture the longitudinal, nuanced quality of activist learning.

## Conceptual Framework

Anti-racism entered mainstream discourse in Europe in the wake of World War II. Since then, the field has neither settled on a single definition of anti-racism, nor does it seem wise to seek one given the adaptable nature of racism across geographic locales and social settings (Gill born, 2004). Still, there is consensus that anti-ra cism goes ICLS2022 Proceedings 1201 © ISLS beyond awareness to involve concerted actions (Paradies, 2016), and that anti-racism is intersectional with efforts to resist related oppressions (e.g., ableism, homophobia) (Dei, 1996). To theorize anti-racism as a learned phenomenon, we draw on situated perspectives that c haracterize learning as shifts in participation in social practices (Lave & Wenger, 1991), as well as Vygotskian sociocultural theory, which emphasizes the cultural artifacts peo ple deploy to accomplish goals and thereby mediate human activity (Wertsch, 1998). Artifacts come in materia l forms (e.g., a book that changes one’s understand ing of racism) or non-material forms (e.g., relationships with others that facilitate participation in anti-racist practices). A key idea in the forthcoming analysis is the notion of availability. That is, people’s thoughts and actions are mediated by those artifacts that are available to them within their lifeworld. Imagine a child who was raised in a certain type of White supremacist household: t he artifacts primarily available to them would incl ude racist narratives about White superiority, family members spouting those narratives, and racist signs like th e Confederate flag. Even if the child rejects White supremacy later in life (i.e., as anti-racist artifacts become more available), we can speculate that legacy racist artifacts from their past may still exert a gravitational pull on their identities and how they live their lives. Thus, as we conceptualize anti-racism learning in terms of s hifts in the relative availability of artifacts, we ask: how do various artifacts available in a person’s life—acro ss both time and setting—create the conditions for learning anti-racism? A Brief Chronology of Mab’s Life Mab Segrest was born in 1949 in Alabama, a place with deep roots in slavery and a history of violent commitment to White supremacy. During her teenage years in the 1960s, Mab’s own town became a national flashpoint of the Civil Rights movement as her school was being deseg regated. Mab’s discomfort with the treatment of Bla ck people during this time led to tensions with her pr o-segregation family. And while she did not identif y then as queer, Mab’s sexual identity further contributed to her feeling like an “outsider.” After completing graduate school in North Carolina, Mab decided to join a Southern queer feminist group that published a journal called Feminary. In part, this decision was driven by the break-up of her relationship with a woman named Pat, which led Mab to seek community with other lesbians and greater acceptance of her sexual identity. This period was crucial to Mab’s eventual  uptake of anti-racist organizing as a member of a Black-led group called North Carolinians Against Racist and Religious Violence (NCARRV).

## Methods

We follow Jurow and Shea (2015) in using ontogeneti c analysis to trace individual development over lon ger timescales. To that end, we explored the value of memoirs as data for making claims about learning. Not only do memoirs zoom in on micro-interactions (e.g., conversations between key actors), but they also often narrate events connected across many years. For this study, we searched for memoirs written by U.S.-based anti-racist activists since the turn of the 20th century. We chose Mab’s memoir because of the critical role White people must play in resisting White supremacy. Additionally, Mab’s intersectional identities as a lesbian woman in the Sou th made her story particularly compelling.

Our analysis focuses on her time with Feminary (1976-1983; p. 37-47) because this was a significa nt pivot in Mab’s life, as she learned ideas and practices related to queer feminist activism that set the stage for her work with NCARRV. We began by segmenting this memoi r excerpt into episodes and identified each by interactions or activities that Mab was engaged in (e.g., leading an anti-racist workshop, attending a protest). We then tagged segments and clustered them thematicall y (e.g., intersectionality, coalition building). Fi nally, we independently made lists of material and non-materi al artifacts relevant to the activity in each segme nt, wrote memos and made visual representations, and met seve ral times to compare and reconcile. Our identificat ion of artifacts was informed in part by previous schemes for identifying sociocultural artifacts (e.g., Nasir, 2011). We note that our positionalities—Shah (straight Bro wn man) and Hou (queer Han Taiwanese)— informed our highlighting of timescales, given the longitudinal nature of our anti-racism learning since childhood.

## Findings

From a situated perspective, we found evidence of s everal shifts in the quality of Mab’s anti-racism d uring this time (e.g., uptake of intersectionality; coalition-building across multiple social marker groups). Here we focus on one: Mab’s shift towards high-risk, on-the-ground organizing. Specifically, the form of Mab’s activism changed from a mostly ideological form of activism to a more grassroots form focused on direct action: With Feminary, our battle had been largely interior, a psychic confrontation with the lethal forces of the culture as we had internalized them. It was an intense, revealing, but sometimes ICLS2022 Proceedings 1202 © ISLS insular process…By 1983, I had hit the limits of this internal work…Guided by the people who eventually incorporated North Carolinians Against Racist and Religious Violence, I set to work to learn to organize. (p. 52) Outside her ideological training and writing at Fem inary, Mab had begun to participate in several prot ests and civil disobedience actions. But what this quote highlights is the centering of grassroots organizing in her activism, which then precipitated in her move to NCARRV. Still, how did Mab learn this aspect of anti-racism? Anti-racist organizing in the 1980s, in one of the  historical hearts of Klan activity (North Carolina), was dangerous work. Sustainable activism requires a high risk-tolerance and sophisticated organizing skills. Next, we analyze how changes in the relative availability of certain artifacts mediated Mab’s security in her sexual identity and her understanding of activism itself. In turn, we argue that these shifts facilitated Mab’s learning of this aspect of anti-racism.

Self-acceptance affords greater risk tolerance In 1976, Mab was at a low point in her personal lif e. For years, the position of “outsider” was a cultural artifact that took up considerable space in Mab’s lifeworld (e.g., family tensions over race; uncertainty over her sexual identity). As a young adult, this narrative of bein g an “outsider” was temporarily assuaged after fall ing in love with Pat, her first serious lesbian partner. But in  the loneliness and vulnerability that opened after  Pat left, Mab redeployed the “outsider” narrative as an identity marker. It was under these conditions Mab joined Feminary. Several new artifacts related to queer identity become available to Mab through various experiences in Feminary. In addition to material artifacts in the form of written histories of feminist and queer feminist movements, Mab was suddenly surrounded by lesbian women committed to empowering their communities. We can infer that these new relationships afforded Mab  a model of queer people more firmly rooted in thei r sexual identities. About these friends and colleagues Mab wrote: “Their feminism was the language of my freedom, the necessary antidote to all my years of loneliness, self-hatred, and fear” (p. 44). Alongside her growing acceptance of her sexual identity, Mab was also re-establishing a sense of safety and belonging in her personal life. Moving in with Carl and Allan—who she refers to as “gay brothers”— and with her friend and lover, Barb, helped Mab re-esta blish a sense of home that was lost when Pat left. This “alternative family,” as she calls it, brought more stability into her life. Overall, we argue that the stability Mab found through her queer feminist activism and home life laid a foundation for Mab to participate in higher-risk forms of activist practice. Of course, being a queer person in that time and place already took tremendous courage. However, anti-racist organizing represented a new level of risk. Our argument is this: when you feel like you belong, you are more likely to feel capable of participat ing in the collective work of high-risk organizing. The artifa ct of “outsider” became less available, or as Mab p uts it: “Lesbian-feminism was my door into the possibility of belonging and self-acceptance…it was not long before the other issue basic to my identity—race—began to surface” (p. 46). New artifacts shape Mab’s perspective on the forms of activist work In parallel with changes in Mab’s feelings of personal acceptance and community belonging, new artifacts were also emerging that changed how she understood the work of activism itself. One formative experience in Mab’s professional life was an anti-war protest she attended with thousands of women at the Pentagon in Washington, D.C. During the Pentagon protest, some of her fello w organizers were arrested, and Mab connects this t o the desegregation battles during her childhood in Alabama: The armed guards at the Pentagon felt like the arme d force that George Wallace had sent to surround my school. Its stone walls radiated the sa me coldness as the marble of the Alabama capitol. As I waited that night outside the jail in  support of the last of our crew to emerge, I knew I was finally deciding to put my power against the power of the state.” (p. 49) There are several important sociocultural dynamics in play here. First, in recognizing “the power of the state,” Mab is re-mediating how she understands her  own activism and what it should be. The experience  of watching her friends jailed at the Pentagon—the ult imate symbol of U.S. militarism—made available a ne w artifact in the form of a narrative about the high-stakes nature of anti-oppressive work. Second, the lines between Mab’s past and present appear to blur, as she analogizes State-sponsored resistance at the Pentagon protest to her childhood experience of watching her community in A labama resist desegregation. The common thread is t he realization that activism transcends local conflicts—that both feminist and anti-racist activism must contend with State-level power.

Typically, these workshops consisted of participants personally reflecting on race in their lives and engaging in role plays, which affor ded them opportunities to practice intervening on r acism in authentic situations. These roleplays were intended to culminate in discussions of activism, but as Mab noticed, this rarely happened: “We found that participants b ecame so absorbed in the interpersonal issues that we never got to the activism. Were we starting at the wrong end of the process?” (p. 49). In suggesting that gr assroots activism might require higher priority, Mab was re-evaluating the nature of her own work. It appears that a greater urgency for direct action was becoming centered in Mab’s activism, and NCARRV would offer Mab an opportunity to realize that urgency.

## Discussion & Future Considerations

This paper contributes to research on learning in social movements by offering the beginnings of a methodological approach that attends to learning at both micro and  macro timescales. In response to Erickson’s (2021)  call for greater “time depth” in such work, we have argued h ere for the potential of memoirs as data, which to our knowledge have not been used to analyze learning. Like all data, memoirs have limitations, but we believe they resonate with more established ethnographic approaches to studying learning. In Mab’s case, our analysis reveals that her learn ing did not hinge on a single epiphany about racism or a particular moment of clarity, but that instead it  unfolded over long stretches of time in nonlinear fashion. We showed how fluctuations in the availability of different artifacts over many years help explain her eventual move to anti-racist organizing. The foregrounding of new artifacts (and backgrounding of others) created the conditions for new and deeper forms of learning. More broadly, we suggest that this study highlights the value in applying learning theory to questions of anti-racism.

We close with two questions for future consideration. With respect to research, if people’s motivations for and learning of anti-oppressive activism is med iated by forces across the lifespan, then how can w e design research that surfaces the historical nature of the se learning processes? And relatedly, with respect to practice, how should anti-racism education initiatives account for the long timescales of anti-racism learning?
