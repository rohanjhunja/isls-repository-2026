# Words that Welcome Tools: Partnering with Purpose in Learning

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Learning unfolds through partnerships between people, tools, and the words that connect them. This paper explores how language shapes access to tools in classrooms where science, technology, and inquiry meet. Drawing on Critical Language Awareness, Ecolinguistics, and Posthumanist perspectives, we view language as an active partner that opens or closes participation. Words like precision or control may exclude, while terms like observe, trace, or unfold invite curiosity and belonging. Through classroom vignettes using the Foldscope, we show how linguistic framing transforms a tool from an object of mastery into a companion in discovery. Language and materiality are entangled: the Foldscope's toy-like physicality enables invitational language, whils t that language reinterprets materiality as a pedagogical affordance. We propose an ecological praxis of language that treats communication as inclusion, sustaining engagement, agency, and care. In keeping with ISLS 2026’s theme, Partnering with Purpose, we focus on words that welcome tools into shared inquiry.

In keeping with ISLS 2026’s theme, Partnering with Purpose, we focus on words that welcome tools into shared inquiry. Learning rarely happens in silence. It hums in words spoken, gestures shared, and the whirr of a low-cost microscope coming into focus. Classrooms, when we listen closely, are alive with these murmurs. They are not only spaces where knowledge is delivered but where language and materials converse, shaping how we think, what we notice, and what we come to care about.

This paper begins with that hum. It asks what happens when we see language and tools not as background supports to learning but as partners in it, co-actors that build meaning with us (Keune, 2024). Our interest lies in how language mediates access to tools: how words make instruments approachable or intimidating, and how discourse around science and technology invites or restricts participation (González -Howard et al., 2023; Lee et al., 2019). When we recognise materials as active contributors to learning rather than passive objects, we begin to see how language itself shapes these human-material collaborations. This inquiry emerges from three teaching contexts in Mumbai, India: a business classroom, a literature classroom, and a community-based intervention. Across these settings, students engaged with the Foldscope, a frugal paper microscope (Cybulski et al., 2014), through different linguistic framings. We trace how shifts in language shaped how learners approached, interpreted, and worked with the tool. In different classrooms ranging from school to tertiary education across sessions in different disciplinary domains examining different objects, students shared, 'It's a whole world.' The statement carried wonder and belonging. The microscope gathered light, but language gathered attention. Together, they made a small act of seeing and caring. Such moments remind us that education for sustainability is not only about acquiring environmental facts but about nurturing relational awareness of how language, perception, and material engagement shape our sense of place (Keune, 2024). When we teach or design learning experiences, the way we speak about tools can either extend or limit participation. Terms like precision, calibration, or efficiency may sound neutral, yet they candistance learners; words like play, explore, or unfold can invite them in (Canfield et al., 2020; Lee et al., 2019). This paper invites educators and learners to notice these weavings: how metaphors and descriptions make tools accessible or intimidating; how the design of a tool carries its own discourse; and how every sentence, gesture, and material encounter leaves a t race on the planet we inhabit and on us. To partner with purpose, we suggest, is to recognise that meaning itself is an ecological act, shaped by the words that welcome tools into our hands and sustained by the tools that extend those words into the world (Keune et al., 2019). We engage with one guiding question: How does the language that accompanies a tool shape its accessibility, disciplinary mobility, and capacity for shared inquiry?

Why this matters: The invisible barrier As the learning sciences community increasingly emphasises making science and technology education accessible, significant resources flow towards affordable tools, open-source platforms, and inclusive design (Canfield et al., 2020; Judd et al., 2021). Frugal science innovations such as the Foldscope exemplify this commitment: low-cost instruments designed to democratise scientific inquiry across diverse educational contexts 991 (Clinton, 2023; Cybulski et al., 2014; Ganesan et al., 2022). The Foldscope, a low-cost origami-based paper microscope, provides optical quality comparable to conventional microscopes whilst being portable, durable, and accessible(Cybulski et al., 2014; Ga nesan et al., 2022). Yet a powerful barrier remains: the language we use to introduce, describe, and facilitate engagement with these tools (Judd et al., 2021; Lee et al., 2019). When educators speak of 'calibration', 'precision', and 'control', they may inadvertently construct walls around the very instruments designed to democratise access (Bae et al., 2021; González-Howard et al., 2023). Research in science education demonstrates that linguistic practices shape who participates in scientific discourse and who remains at its margins (Bae et al., 2021; González-Howard et al., 2023). Studies of multilingual and urban classrooms show that when students' everyday language and funds of knowledge are excluded, participation becomes constrained and learners are often positioned as deficient (González- Howard et al., 2023; Svoboda, 2025). Drawing on posthumanist and material perspectives, it is seen that how we speak about tools alters the relationships learners develop with them (Keune, 2024; Wohlwend et al., 2017). When language invites learners to explore alongside tools, it opens possibilities for shared meaning-making (Keune, 2024; Keune et al., 2019; Wohlwend et al., 2017). The problem extends beyond vocabulary: it lies in tone and framing (Canfield et al., 2020; Judd et al., 2021). Language that positions instruments as objects to be controlled rather than companions in exploration limits engagement and recognition of emerging expertise (Keune et al., 2019; Wohlwend et al., 2017).

This paper argues that linguistic framing is not decorative but determinative: it decides who feels capable of inquiry and who hesitates at the threshold (González-Howard et al., 2023). Partnering with purpose, therefore, requires attention not only to what tools we provide but to the words that welcome them into learners' hands (Canfield et al., 2020; Cybulski et al., 2014). Partnership extends beyond human collaboration to include the alignment of language, tools, and learners, making language infrastructure for participation (Keune, 2024; Kelly et al., 2012). This paper positions linguistic awareness as a partnership practice that crosses boundaries between educators, researchers, tool designers, and learners, and that recognises tools themselves as partn ers in the collaborative production of knowledge (Keune, 2024; Keune et al., 2019). By attending to how discourse shapes access to tools, we open pathways towards more equitable and inclusive scientific engagement, where the words we choose sustain curiosity, agency, and care (Canfield et al., 2020; Lee et al., 2019; Wohlwend et al., 2017).

## Theoretical framework: Partnerships across perspectives

This paper draws on three interconnected theoretical traditions: Critical Language Awareness, Ecolinguistics, and Posthumanist perspectives. Together, these frameworks position language as an active partner shaping how tools become accessible and how they travel across disciplines. Following Lev Vygotsky (1978), language itself can be understood as a psychological tool that mediates human action. Here we extend this view to consider language both as a tool and a mediator of engagement with material tools. This dual role allows us to examine how language does not merely accompany tools but actively shapes their meaning and use. Critical language awareness: Language as framing power Critical Language Awareness (CLA), developed by Norman Fairclough (1989, 1992) and Catherine Wallace (1992, 1999), demonstrates that language frames authority, inclusion, and belonging. Terms such as objectivity, control, or precision carry disciplinary histories and signal who a tool is ‘for’. When a microscope is introduced through scientific protocols, it becomes marked as a science tool, limiting its accessibility across domains (Lee et al., 2019; Svoboda, 2025).

Building on this, Edward Haig (2003) proposed Ecological Critical Language Awareness (Eco-CLA), linking language to ecological relationships. We extend this perspective to tools, asking how linguistic framing shapes not only access but the pedagogical identity of tools. Linguistic awareness here becomes a collaborative practice, enabling educators to recognise how discourse positions both learners and tools, and to open possibilities for cross-disciplinary engagement.

Importantly, linguistic awareness becomes collaboration between teacher reflexivity and student agency (González-Howard et al., 2023). When educators notice how their language positions learners in relation to tools and notice how that language disciplines both learner and tool, they create conditions for a partnership that crosses boundaries. This transforms CLA into a partnership practice that can unbind tools from their disciplinary origins. Ecolinguistics: Language as living system Ecolinguistics and ecological literacy treat language as part of living systems (Halliday, 1990; Orr, 1992; Capra, 2004). Expressions such as natural resources or waste disposal shape how we value the world; so too do the ways we talk about instruments and  what they might reveal (Stibbe, 2015, 2021). Technical discourse inscribes tools within disciplinary practices, limiting how they are used and understood. When we describe tools using the 992 language of extraction and control, we reproduce anthropocentric framings that position nature as a resource rather than a relation (Stibbe, 2021). We also reproduce disciplinary territoriality, suggesting that tools belong to those who command specialised  vocabularies, constraining both who can use them and what they can become. A microscope introduced through scientific precision remains a science tool; introduced through wonder and pattern, it can travel across disciplines.

Conversely, relational language such as “what the tool shows us” or “worlds within worlds” positions tools as participants in shared inquiry (Keune, 2024). If students learn only the disciplinary vocabulary and not the exploratory metaphor, they inherit tools that cannot travel. Here, metaphor is not decorative but epistemic: it enables ways of seeing to move across contexts allowing tools to travel beyond disciplinary boundaries. The language of specialised protocols must be accompanied by words that connect wonder, pattern-seeking, and meaning-making across contexts (Keune, 2024). In this sense, language functions as part of the ecosystem of learning, shaping not only who participates but what becomes pedagogically imaginable with any given tool.

## Posthumanism: Tools as co-participants

Posthumanist and relational thinkers such as Barad (2007), Haraway (2016), and Braidotti (2013) extend this view by dissolving the boundary between humans and tools. A microscope or algorithm is not simply an aid to cognition but part of the apparatus that  performs knowledge (Barad, 2007; Keune, 2024). This performative capacity emerges through the entanglement of material, language, learner, and context. When a student peers through a Foldscope and names what they see, both language and material act togeth er and what they produce depends profoundly on the discursive frame that welcomed the tool into that moment (Wohlwend et al., 2017). We name this explicitly as a multispecies partnership that includes human, linguistic, and material actors (Keune, 2024; Keune et al., 2019). The concept of intra-action, central to Barad's (2007) agential realism, reframes materiality from design affordan ce to a cycling interplay produced by the physicality, fluidity, and messiness of entangled bodies, things, and places. In this understanding, the learner and the tool do not simply interact as separate entities with predetermined roles; they intra-act, mu tually constituting one another through their engagement (Wohlwend et al., 2017). A Foldscope is not inherently a 'science tool' or a 'literature tool'; it becomes what language, purpose, and material allow it to be (Keune, 2024). This perspective raises a provocative question: if tools are not predefined instruments but co-participants whose identities emerge through use, what role does language play in liberating tools from disciplinary capture? How educators speak about tools, whether as instruments bound to specific protocols or as companions open to diverse inquiries, shapes not only the values that accompany learning but the very ontology of the tool itself (Keune et al., 2019). Language here is constitutive, shaping relationships between learner, tool, and world, thus determining what disciplinary borders the tool can cross (Wohlwend et al., 2017). Ecological critical language awareness as bridge Eco-CLA provides not a master theory but a bridging framework that allows us to see how power (CLA), ecology (Ecolinguistics), and material-semiotic practice (Posthumanism) converge in classroom discourse (Haig, 2003; Stibbe, 2015). When a teacher says 'calibrate carefully and record your observations precisely,' three things happen simultaneously: authority is positioned (who has expertise), relationship to the instrument is framed (as scientific apparatus requiring disciplinary knowledge), and participation is mediated (who feels capable and what purposes feel legitimate). Language becomes the infrastructure through which partnerships between learners and tools either flourish or fail, and through which tools are either confined to or freed from their disc iplinary origins. Most significantly, linguistic framing determines the pedagogical mobility of tools. A Foldscope introduced as a 'paper microscope for science education' remains tethered to laboratories and biology curricula. The same tool introduced as 'a way of seeing h idden patterns' or 'a window into worlds within worlds' becomes available for literary analysis, cultural studies, business education, or philosophical inquiry. This insight reveals a gap in current research: whilst we know that language shapes access to t ools, we know far less about how language shapes the disciplinary flexibility of tools, how words might unbind pedagogical materials from their intended contexts and invite them into unexpected partnerships. Eco-CLA offers researchers and teachers a vocabulary for asking not only what students learn with a tool but how language makes that tool approachable, meaningful, and disciplinarily mobile (Stibbe, 2015). When language is relational rather than technical,  it opens entry into shared inquiry that crosses traditional boundaries; when it is codified within disciplinary vocabularies, it narrows both belonging and pedagogical imagination (Bae et al., 2021). By attending to linguistic choices as ecological acts, ones that cultivate or constrain relationships between learners, tools, and disciplines, educators can consciously design discourse that sustains engagement, agency, and care whilst expanding what becomes pedagogically possible (Canfield et al., 2020). 993 Toward ecological praxis: The central argument An ecological praxis of language treats communication as an act of inclusion and liberation. It invites educators to notice how words position learners in relation to tools, to listen for the tone of access or distance in instruction, and to design experiences where language frees tools from disciplinary boundaries (González-Howard et al., 2023; Keune, 2024). In that attention lies the possibility of sustainability: of curiosity, wonder, and intellectual boundary-crossing.

This paper's central argument synthesises these three theoretical traditions: language operates as an ecological gateway that mediates learners' relationships with tools, determining who can access instruments and what pedagogical identities those instruments can assume (Keune, 2024; Keune et al., 2019). Tools are not objects with fixed disciplinary affiliations but partners whose collaborative potential emerges through linguistic framing (Wohlwend et al., 2017). When educators shift from directive, discipl ine-bound language (calibrate, measure, control) to relational, exploratory language (observe, discover, trace patterns), they transform instruments from gatekeeping devices into flexible companions supporting inquiry across unexpected contexts. This paper contributes to learning sciences by demonstrating that linguistic framing not only shapes access to tools but also their disciplinary mobility, positioning language as infrastructure for human-material partnership. By disciplinary mobility, we refer to the capacity of a tool to move across domains of knowledge without being constrained by its original disciplinary framing. Partnering with purpose requires recognising that partnerships exist not only among human collaborators but also among words, materials, and learners and that these partnerships can transcend and reshape disciplinary boundaries (Kelly et al., 2012). This m ultispecies partnership practice demands attention to the linguistic infrastructures that either confine tools within disciplines or enable their pedagogical mobility (Keune, 2024). The question this raises is whether linguistic reframing can enable a sing le tool to serve multiple disciplines, not by compromising disciplinary depth but by revealing the fundamental acts of inquiry that underlie diverse knowledge practices. Reimagining discourse to shape access to and the pedagogical flexibility of tools, we open pathways towards more equitable and imaginative educational engagement, where the words we choose sustain curiosity, agency, and care (Canfield et al., 2020; Keune et al., 2019). Methodological note: Reflective practice as research This inquiry emerges from reflective practice, a mode of research generating grounded insights about teaching and learning (Schön, 1983). This research represents a collaboration among four practitioner-researchers. The first author, a specialist in Literature and Language and Management Studies, served as instructor for the courses Linguistic and Stylistic Analysis of Text and Business Perspectives in the Global Context. The second author, a science educator with expertise in cognition and learning, brought perspectives on how tools mediate conceptual development. The third author, trained in biology with years of Foldscope experience, and the fourth author, a mindfulness practitioner, co-facilitated an out-of-school intervention. The inquiry draws on three distinct educational settings in Mumbai, India. The first was Business Perspectives in the Global Context,  a management course having 60 Master's students (40 male, 20 female) at a suburban college. The second was Linguistic and Stylistic Analysis of Text, a language and literature course having 40 Master's students (28 female, 12 male) at a different suburban college. The third was an out-of-school weekend intervention in Mumbai's largest slum, engaging approximately 20 students (some in school, others in college) in exploring connections between mindfulness and scientific observation. In each context, the Foldscope was introduced through different linguistic framings. Data sources included reflective field notes, student verbal responses, linguistic artefacts (instructions, prompts, framing statements), and observational notes on engagement patterns. We approached analysis through an interactional and interpretive lens, consistent with design-based reflection traditions in learning sciences, tracing how language, tool use, and learner response co-evolved during classroom activity. Analysis focused on moments when linguistic shifts altered student-tool relationships. We acknowledge important limitations. This is exploratory, generative work that opens questions rather than testing hypotheses. Findings emerge from practitioner contexts and are intended to generate theoretical insights about language-tool relationships that inform future investigation. Illustrative contexts: Language making tools accessible These ideas grew from teaching experiments across three distinct contexts: two formal Master's courses (management and literature) and one out-of-school intervention. These illustrative cases reveal how linguistic framing shapes tool accessibility across radically different pedagogical purposes. All three contexts used the Foldscope, a low-cost paper microscope (Cybulski et al., 2014), yet each setting transformed the tool through linguistic framing. In business education, it became a lens for organisational 994 culture. In literary studies, it was used to explore the depth and layering of texts. In mental health work with marginalised youth, it functioned as both an instrument and a metaphor for invisible psychological experiences. None traditionally incorporates scientific instruments, making linguistic framing essential for access. The business classroom: Seeing organisational culture In Business Perspectives in the Global Context (60 Master's students), the initial introduction followed scientific protocols: 'Calibrate the microscope carefully. Adjust the lens until clear. Record observations precisely.' Students handled the paper instrument tentatively, treating it as fragile equipment requiring expertise they lacked. The instructor reframed: 'Unfold it slowly, let the light find its way through, turn it until the image becomes clear, notice what emerges.' Students relaxed immediately, more willing to experiment. Each group observed dust particles from the classroom floor, connecting observations to a case study about Gillette's India market adaptation.

After reframing, observations became metaphorical. 'It looks like movement, like something alive,' one student whispered. Another added, 'It's like layers of a story, you see one thing first and then something else emerges underneath.' In the discussion, one student remarked on the parallels between what the Foldscope revealed and the opacity of initial market research, 'When Gillette entered India, they only saw the surface. They missed the invisible cultural layers.' Another noted, 'Organisational culture is like this dust particle. From far away it looks simple, but when you really look closely, there's complexity you couldn't see.' The language of observation replaced measurement, enabling business thinking to be more attentive to complexity and humility about what remains unseen.

The literature classroom: Reading through material In Linguistic and Stylistic Analysis of Text  (40 Master's students), students initially received directive scientific language: 'Focus carefully. Maintain steady hand. Observe the specimen systematically.' The term 'specimen' particularly distanced students.

The instructor shifted to exploratory language mirroring literary analysis: 'Pick up something from outside, a dried leaf perhaps. Look through the Foldscope and describe what you see. Think about texture, depth, patterns.' Students treated the Foldscope as they might a poem: dwelling, noticing layers, seeing what emerges through sustained attention. One student described a dried leaf: 'It's like a map of veins, like rivers branching through landscape.' Another said, 'A world folded in, you keep seeing more the longer you look.' A third connected directly to course content: 'This is like reading that Frost poem,’ The Road Not Taken’. On the surface it's simple, just a leaf. But when you really look, there's architecture, structure, meaning in how everything connects. What seems simple holds so much complexity.' Students drew parallels between microscopic observation and textual analysis. 'When we read superficially, we miss the stylistic choices beneath the surface,' one noted. Another added, 'The Foldscope makes visible what was invisible. Close reading does the  same with language.' One student who had found the literary leaf. The meaning was always there, you just need the right way of looking.' The mental health intervention: Making the invisible visible The third context occurred at an organisation providing care to marginalised communities affected by HIV/AIDS, tuberculosis, and substance abuse. Both facilitators had grown up in the same community and returned with an intimate understanding of the mental  health challenges young people face. The linguistic framing was carefully constructed to transform the Foldscope from a scientific instrument to a metaphorical tool. Children (aged 8 to

21) first observed everyday objects through the microscope using disc overy-oriented language: 'Notice how

different it looks. See the complexity in something that seemed ordinary. Pay attention to what you couldn't see before.' After students became comfortable with observation, facilitators extended the metaphor explicitly: 'Mental health is like the microscopic world. It's there, real, complex, but we can't see it directly. Just as the Foldscope focuses light to help us see tiny structures, mindfulness helps us notice what's happening in our minds and feelings. The lens doesn't create what you see; it reveals what was always there.' After microscopic observation, facilitators guided children through SIFT (Sensations, Images, Feelings, Thoughts), making the parallel explicit: 'SIFT is a tool like Foldscope. The Foldscope uses a lens to make the invisible world visible; in SIFT, awareness is that lens. Through it, we can see the hidden landscape of our inner world. Each emotion is like a slide: joy, fear, anger, surprise, something to place under the lens and examine closely.' The concretisation worked. A 10-year-old wrote, 'I felt joyous, I am feeling surprised and felt disgusted too!' A 16-year-old wrote, 'I felt afraid when something appeared suddenly in front of my eyes. But I was also surprised. I wanted to keep looking at it. The ant looked huge, and at first I was scared, but then I felt happy.' 995 Students appropriated the metaphor independently, using Foldscope language to discuss their own mental health: 'focusing on feelings', 'bringing thoughts into clarity', 'zooming in on what matters.' The linguistic framing had successfully transformed a scientific instrument into a shared vocabulary for psychological experiences previously difficult to name.

What the partnership revealed These cases reveal language, tools, and learners as partners in knowledge production (Keune, 2024; Wohlwend et al., 2017). From CLA, linguistic shifts redistributed epistemic authority (Fairclough, 1992). From ecolinguistics, the invitational language mode lled relational rather than extractive material engagement (Stibbe, 2021). From posthumanism, the Foldscope emerged as a co-participant whose pedagogical identity was constituted through language-tool-learner intra-actions (Barad, 2007). The cases demonstrate that disciplinary boundaries around tools are linguistically constructed and therefore linguistically permeable. A microscope designed for science can become a tool for organisational analysis, literary interpretation, or emotional awareness when language frames it differently. This challenges assumptions about tool appropriateness: perhaps no tool belongs inherently to one discipline. Rather, linguistic framing determines what pedagogical work tools cando and who feels invited to use them.

Crucially, however, linguistic framing alone does not fully account for the Foldscope's accessibility. The tool's materiality itself plays an essential role in enabling these transformations (Keune, 2024; Wohlwend et al., 2017). The second author's experience facilitating over 100 workshops reveals a recurring pattern: participants consistently perceive the Foldscope as 'toy-like' rather than as a 'serious science tool', with researchers frequently asking, 'This is good for kids, but can we do research with this?' despite substantial published research using the instrument (Cybulski et al., 2014; Ganesan et al., 2022). Yet this same materiality (paper construction, origami assembly, bright colours, lightweight portability) that undermines research credibility simultaneously makes the tool approachable and accessible. Unlike a compound microscope, which is bulky, delicate, and intimidating, the Foldscope invites touch and experimentation without fear. Language and materiality are entangled in constituting pedagogical identity (Barad, 2007). The Foldscope's toy-like materiality makes invitational language credible in ways a metal microscope could not, whilst invitational language reframes toy-like qualities from limitations into affordances. The same materiality that makes researchers question legitimacy enables disciplinary mobility. Tools and words co-constitute accessibility: the Foldscope crosses boundaries not through linguistic framing alone nor material design alone, but because its specific materiality enables certain framings, whilst those framings reinterpret what the materiality means.

The shift from 'calibrate' to 'unfold' is not merely semantic. It operates through four interconnected mechanisms. Cognitively, invitational language reduces mental distance between learner and tool (González- Howard et al., 2023). Affectively, it creates emotional safety that enables experimental engagement (Keune et al., 2019). Socially, it positions learners as co-participants rather than apprentices following protocols (Fairclough, 1992). Material-semiotically, it transforms the tool itself from a discip linary gatekeeper to a collaborator in meaning996 making (Barad, 2007; Keune, 2024). This in turn affects what pedagogical collaborations can emerge (Keune, 2024; Wohlwend et al., 2017). These mechanisms reveal that access to education is not only material but linguistic. Teachers candevelop awareness through reflective practice: recording oneself teaching, noting the verbs used to introduce tools, and experimenting deliberately with refr aming. This ecological praxis treats communication as an act of inclusion, inviting educators to notice how words posit ion learners and to design experiences where language and tools work together to invite participation (Stibbe, 2021). For tool designers, this research suggests embedding invitational language in interfaces, manuals, and materials. For teacher educators, it points to making linguistic mediation visible in professional development. This partnership practice crosses sectors.

We acknowledge important limitations. This exploratory work emerged from three contexts facilitated by practitioner-researchers aware of the inquiry's focus, which may have influenced pedagogical choices. The small scale and Mumbai setting limit immediate generalisability. Cultural and linguistic specificity (English-medium instruction) requires investigation across diverse language contexts. Future research should examine how linguistic framing of digital tools (AI, coding platforms) shapes access; what happens when students are explicitly taught to notice linguistic mediation; how linguistic practices vary across cultural contexts; whether we candevelop measures of 'invitational language' in education; and how marginalised students experience linguistic barriers differently. Cross-sector partnerships among linguists, learning scientists, and tool designers could substantially advance this work.

This inquiry moved from classroom observation to theoretical insight to practical implications, revealing that language operates as an ecological gateway mediating learners' relationships with tools. The Foldscope crossed disciplinary boundaries through entanglement of material design and linguistic framing. Its toy-like materiality enabled invitational language to reframe scientific instruments as companions in discovery, making the microscope accessible for organisational analysis, literary interpretation, and mental health work. If students learn only the metric and not the metaphor, they inherit a narrow version of truth (Orr, 1992). The work of teaching, then, is to keep both alive: the precision of the tool and the openness of the word. Partnership begins there, in the quiet moments when language welcomes learning into being. Partnership here extends beyond human collaboration to include language and material tools as co-participants in learning. To partner with purpose is to recognise that every learning encounter is assembled from multiple actors: human, linguistic, material. The Foldscope taught us that tools carry worlds within them, but only when words welcome them into being. As the learning sciences community deepens its commitment to cross-sector partnerships, we must also attend to the partnerships that happen in classrooms every day: between teacher and student, yes, but also between language and tool, word and world. These small partnerships are where transformation begins, not in grand initiatives but in the quiet shift from command to invitation, from mastery to companionship, from knowing to caring. Partnership, after all, begins with how we speak.
