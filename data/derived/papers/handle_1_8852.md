# Supporting Students’ Critical Action Within a Cultural Context: A Role for Arts-Based Pedagogy in a Community of Learners

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Transformative learning requires students to actively identify issues, question the narratives that surround them, and develop strategies for change (Freire, 1978). However, for such learning to be truly impactful it must be contextual and culturally relevant to learners. In this paper, we report on the design, implementation and outcomes of a performative learning workshop organized at a secondary school in Bengaluru, India. We highlight the importance of the co-design process and its influence on joint inquiry and increased community engagement. Data from student interactions, discourse and artifacts was analyzed to understand how a culturally relevant artsbased activity can engage students in critical action, collaboration, and embodied learning about local issues. The findings suggest that knowledge and action are both deeply embedded in and influenced by cultural experiences as represented in the students’ collective creations. We discuss the implications of this research in the broader context of the learning sciences.

## Introduction

This paper reports on a co-designed, culturally responsive learning experience that engaged students in arts-based critical action within a secondary school in Bengal uru, India. The activity took the form of a Rangoli Utsav (or Rangoli festival), as part of the school's Diwali celebrations. Rangoli is a traditional Indian art form that consists of multicolored drawings (typically circular patterns) designed on the floor using materials such as colored sand, powdered limestone, flower petals, etc. Over the past year, we have established a professional learning community for teachers in Southern India, comprising three workshops that focused on topics of critical action and culturally responsive pedagogies. This paper studies one of th e curriculum activities produced by teachers, in wh ich the Utsav provided a context for a performative learning workshop. Students were invited to use Rangolis to express their personal and cultural identities or address socio-environmental problems that concern them, as an exercise of critical action.  This project is situated within a broader program of research and critical action called CALE: The Critical Action Learning Exchange (Carvalho et al., 2021). This research responds to a growing need in the learning sciences to nurture culturally responsive pedagogies in which students develop their own sense of local purpose (Enyedy et al., 2011, Paris & Alim, 2017) situated in authentic cultural contexts and belief systems (Hod et al., 2018).

The following research question guided our work: How can critical action pedagogy support students in developing understanding and expression about global issues within their local cultural context?

## Theoretical Framework-Critical Action and Arts-based Pedagogy

In Freire's words, "Only beings who can reflect upo n the fact that they are determined are capable of freeing themselves. Their reflectiveness results in the exercise of a profoundly transforming action upon the determining reality. Consciousness of and action upon reality a re, therefore, inseparable constituents of the transforming act by which men become beings of relation."  Critical action is here understood as the behavioural component in the Freirean concept of conscientização, which is the process of acquisition or development of critical consciousness. Critical consciousness is composed of three mutuall y reinforcing elements, namely critical reﬂection, critical motivation, and critical action (Diemer et al., 2015; Watts & Hipolito-Delgado, 2015). Critical reﬂection refers to the process of learning to distinguish culture from nature, or in other words, of acquiring a perspective that allows for social constructs and structures to be perceive d as questionable and malleable, instead of being r egarded as inescapable, naturally imposed realities. Critical motivation is an individual's perceived capability and commitment to contribute to social advancement through the reinvention of social structures that enabl e unjust, oppressive, harmful, or unhealthy conditions. Finally, critical action refers to the engagement in action dedicated to producing socio-political changes perceived as c onducive to more just and healthy conditions (Freir e, 1978; Watts et al., 2011; Jemal & Bussey, 2018). Conscientização, thus, can be understood in the words of Paulo Freire as the process of “learning to perceive social, pol itical, and economic contradictions, and to take ac tion against the oppressive elements of reality” (2000, p. 35) ICLS2022 Proceedings 647 © ISLS Culturally Responsive Teaching with Art One form of critical action is art-making, given its capacity of encompassing the conceptual components of critical consciousness. Art is a powerful way to address glo bal issues across cultural contexts. Teachers play a crucial role in this endeavour, enabling students to connec t their lived experience to issues and ideas, cultu re and community. According to Stephen K. Levine: By creating an alternative world of the imagination, the artwork shows possibilities that are absent or dormant within our everyday awareness. Moreover, the work takes us away from this everyday world within which we experience ourselves  as unable to act; we could say that it “decenters” us from this world.  In so doing it fre es us up for new possibilities that were not apparent within our limited horizon of understandin g. […] Artmaking is itself a sensoryaffective experience that gives participants an exp erience of their own capacities for action. Because it affects us through the body and the emot ions, artmaking can provide experiences that restore us to a feeling of being fully alive. And in making the work, by acting within the limited frame of the materials and the time and space available, we recover our capacity to be effective in the world, something that we have lost  in the helpless situation in which we find ourselves. (2011, p. 28) In this sense, the process of artmaking awakens our  "dormant" ability for critical reflection, triggers our critical motivation by helping us to perceive ourselves as " able to act", and "recovers our capacity" for criti cal action. There is a long history of using art education to confront issues of race, cultural discrimination or various forms of othering. According to Ladson-Billings’ (1995) f ramework, arts-based pedagogy should respond to students' cultural knowledge, prior experiences, frame of references, and diverse performance styles (Lai, 2012; Lee, 2012). The present study addresses cultural responsiveness through designing learning activities in what Lai describes as culturally familiar and creative ways (Lai, 2012) Moreover, the arts have repeatedly demonstrated a capacity of catalyzing social change. Art has the power to r aise our consciousness, empower, advocate, and prov oke (Estrella, 2011, p. 42).

Communities of Learners A final theoretical element for this work is that o f learning communities, which can support collabora tion and progressive discourse (Brown & Campione, 1994) a de ep focus on ideas (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2005), a nd collective forms of inquiry in which the community creates a knowledge base that serves as a resource for individuals and small groups (Slotta, Quintana & Moher, 2018).  Critical action that occurs within the context of a community of one’s peers can add meaning and purpose to the community, democratize learning, and support individual members as they develop an identity within the community context. This study was conducted in the context of a parti cular professional learning community called the Critical Action Learning Exchange (CALE), which hel ps teachers develop curriculum lessons that empower students in the face of social, economic and politi cal issues in a rapidly changing world (Carvalho et  al., 2021). CALE supports teachers in the design of culturally responsive learning activities that encourage stude nts to connect to global issues through their own personal  and cultural lens moving from knowledge to action as explained in Figure 1.

## Methods

Co-design is a participatory approach that brings t ogether researcher’s knowledge and lived experience s of the participants (Roschelle & Penuel, 2006). Our workshop was designed in partnership with a teacher-leade r from the school, five classroom teachers and administrat ors. Identified priorities and indicators of succes s of the workshop were negotiated and tailored to the needs of the school in their local, temporal context. The timing of the workshop around the Hindu festival of Diwali was important to the school, since the festival itself represents the victory of good over bad, light over darkness and the need to act against the evils of society. Indian teachers chose the traditional art form of Rangoli for its f amiliarity to students and cultural appropriateness. The school had previously organized similar Rangoli festivals (see Figure 2), celebrating a folk-art rooted in 50 00 years of tradition. But it had never been done as a source of critical action. Figure 2 Rangolis drawn by students in the school in previous years As a CALE curriculum, the Rangoli activity was designed to link to the six components of the framework shown in Figure 1. Students set out to investigate social issues by participating in a school-wide brainstorming activity during the weekly assembly and continued building knowledge around these issues (Knowledge). They then came together as groups, bringing in their individual ideas and visions for change, and reflecting on their positioning. They also investigated the forces that impact their chosen issue (Criticality). Working together, they determined what should be depicted within the Rangoli and how it would hopefully affect an audience of the wider school community (Action). Each student was given the oppo rtunity to bring in their unique competencies and a rtistic skills (Individual), engaging in peer exchanges of ideas within their group. A learning community approach was adapted to move students from a competitive mindset to a collaborative one (Community). Finally, the Rangolis were on display for the school and shared with the research group in Canada. Pictures of the Rangolis serve as discussion artifacts showcased on social media with  the goal of increasing the engagement of educators, researchers and students around the world (Globe).

## Participants

Six teachers (including one teacher-leader who coordinates teacher professional development) from a secondary school in Bengaluru, India were engaged in the design of this curriculum, along with six members of the CALE research team in Canada. Participation in the Rangoli Utsav was open to all students in Grades 6-10. Intereste d students submitted their names and themes of intere st, individually, or in pairs to their respective t eacher coordinators. A total of 82 students, 63 of whom identified as girls and 19 as boys, signed up to participate in the workshop. These participants were surveyed verbally  on whether they have engaged in critical action-or iented activities or Rangoli art before. While 72% of participants indicated some experience with Rangoli, none of the participants indicated any previous participation i n critical action-oriented activities. The teachers  then collectively deliberated on group formations based on student requests, interests, experience, synergy  and motivations. 12 groups were formed of 6-7 students each; 4 students excused themselves from the groups  and opted to be community helpers through the planning and execution of the workshop.

## Procedure and Data Sources

While the brainstorming for the issues of significance to the community took place as a collective whole-school activity, interested participants gathered in their small groups to share their ideas on specific them es of interest. Students work ed in their small groups on design ideas over a wee kend, sharing their hand drawings, topic research, artist ic ideas, design recommendations and individual mot ivations with each other on Monday. ## Analysis and Findings

To understand the ways in which our critical action  workshop supported students in expressing their understandings of global issues that mattered to them, we performed an interpretive analysis (Sgier, 2012) on the data collected through artist statements, student a rtifacts, teacher and student discourse, workshop p ictures and videos. Throughout the analysis, we sought to identify patterns of coherence, as well as anomalies to these patterns. Trustworthiness (Shenton, 2004) was established through a debriefing with the participants, iterative questioning, member checks, peer-scrutiny and thick descriptions.

Co-design Analysis The co-design method had many benefits, including g rounding our workshop in the local context of the s chool community, and enabling enthusiastic participation. As the teacher-leader noted, in her reflection, “…they (the participants) have been so enthusiastic about the whole event …. they were so engrossed in doing it and immersed in the experience they didn’t even want to have a break”. In this project, co-design of the activities with teachers at the school achieved informal-mutual learning thr ough (i) sharing past practices of community activi ties (ii) building common understanding around critical actio n; (iii) construction of shared meanings on arts-ba sed pedagogy; and (iv) idea-generation.

Critical Visual Analysis of Artifacts Instead of looking at the formal artistic elements such as line, value, shapes, forms, spaces, colour and texture, we analyze students’ artistic creation using a Critical Visual Analysis (Aiello, 2006) in which the primary concern is students’ perception of the critical issues, the id eology behind their creations, the iconographies students employed, and the use of social semiotics in illust rating the problems and solutions. Students’ creations show a range of evidence of their perception of global iss ues: five rangolis included a representation of the  globe; four ICLS2022 Proceedings 650 © ISLS included a representation of a Hindu deity, demonstrating that students are employing icons from their traditional culture; eight out of 12 groups also expresses stud ents’ ideologies through the implementation of text s, such as “Her tears will not correct this imbalance, actions  will save the girl child”. Among the 12 pieces, th e Woman Empowerment (see Figure 3) is a good example of students creati ng meaning through redefining the social semiotics of an Indian goddess as a determined woma n.  Students’ embodied, collective Rangoli art popu lated their physical space with critical messages that connected global issues with their local culture. Table 2 presents a summary of these findings. The main finding that emerged was that all groups (n = 12) appeared to understand what their issue was, the challenges of addressing the same, and the types of knowledge needed to effectively address them. Six of the 12 groups included short text descriptio ns within their Rangolis, to help the user interpre t the issues. Also, four out of the 12 groups used their content knowledge to improve their artist statement and explain “their action”. Interestingly, all four of those groups (C ovid Warriors, Save Water, Save the Trees, One Plan et One Chance) addressed the issues through a scientific l ens. The “Good over evil” Rangoli, which showed an evil mythological villain with multiple heads, was unique in that, while it artistically portrayed a range of the issues, calling for action, there was no treatment of how these issues might be addressed. Individual interpretations of the ICLS2022 Proceedings 652 © ISLS issue varied and were deeply rooted in students’ own personal contexts. For example, while one student working on the “Remove your Obstacles'' Rangoli (which addressed the issues around mental wellbeing) reflected on the symbolism of the elephant (big ears so we can listen more, big belly to laugh etc.), another student visualized the elephant as “path makers in a forest that lead the way”, and another interpreted the same from a religious viewpoint, “you pray to Ganesh…”. Groups also built on each other’s calls to action, “education is the way to bring people out of slums and out of poverty”. The most common theme that was found in the analysis of their artist statements and reflection was a call to the collective, “We Indians need to…”, “it is necessary for every person in the country.” etc. Finally, while the actions called for were not  always critical in nature, they informed the commu nity of its role in tackling these issues.

## Discussion

The CALE framework encouraged students to bring cri ticality and voice to their artistic designs throug h knowledge sharing. This was evident in the significant divergence of the Rangoli activities that occurred during this year’s Diwali festival from those of years past. The framework provided pedagogical support for increasing both the scope and depth of students' critical inqu iry. It helped them expand the range of their ideas, building ICLS2022 Proceedings 653 © ISLS connections beyond the individual level, to include  their broader community (e.g., farmers, health car e professionals, teachers, the police) and the whole world (e.g., global warming). It also supported students in going progressively deeper in their ideas, helping them b uild knowledge about their local issues, engaging t hem in critical reflection, and engaging in critical action through art making. Thus, arts-based critical making enhanced students’ expression of their ideas. Through collec tive inquiry on critical issues, connected to local  cultural contexts, students perceived a sense of relevance, participation, and empowerment during this process.  This experiential learning activity became a practice of meaning-making by designing, implementing, and sharing with others in their own community.

## Conclusion

During the ICLS 2020 symposium on “Reconceptualizing Learning: A Critical Task for Knowledge-Building and Teaching” (de Royston et al., 2020) highlighted the importance of understanding participation in cultural practices as being fundamental to the study of learning, call ing upon the community to engage in and with such “foundational work” at this critical time. The pres ent research demonstrates some of the challenges and opportunities of situating learning in and within the cultural practices of students in India. Yet this is just the start. More work is still needed on how to extend our learnings to other contexts and develop guidelines for culturally responsive curriculum designs that go beyond the traditional definitions of learning by supporting student agency, knowledge building and growth as empowered citizens.
