# Teacher-Developed Design Principles for Assessing Critical Thinking

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
We describe how nine classroom teachers designed and reflected on assessment practices for measuring critical thinking as part of a larger district Portrait of a Graduate program. Instead of developing a single assessment approach, teachers created a set of design principles that could be used flexibly by other classroom teachers seeking to assess students’ critical thinking. Our findings indicate the importance of grounding assessment of complex, transferable skills in classroom practices and student artifacts.

A Portrait of a Graduate (PoG) is a shared vision developed by community stakeholders that defines what every graduate should know, understand, and be able to do (Safir & Dugan, 2021). The framework extends traditional academic metrics to include essential  skills like critical thinking, communication, and collaboration, preparing students for success in K-12 education, life, careers, and college (Atwell & Tucker, 2024; Anderson, 2024). As of early 2025, approximately 20 states and hundreds of districts had adopted portraits to guide education policy and practice, reflecting the increasing recognition that education must equip students with the skills needed to thrive in an ever-changing world  (FairTest, 2025). More than an academic framework, PoG acknowledg es the whole learner, valuing students’ social, emotional, and intellectual development alongside traditional academic skills. Within PoG, critical thinking often emerges as a highly valued yet challenging competency to define and assess, raising important questions about how schools can measure this essential skill (Mills et al., 2024). For decades, scholars have debated whether critical thinking should be understood as a generalizable set of skills or as a domain-specific practice tied to disciplinary knowledge. Proponents of a generalist view argue that many reasoning tasks share commonalities across domains, suggesting that critical thinking skills can transfer broadly (Halpern, 1998; Davies, 2013). By contrast, others emphasize that critical thinking is domain and subjectspecific (McPeck, 1990; Moore, 2011). To make matters more compl ex, some scholarship recognizes that while there are common skills that transcend domains, meaningful critical thinking also requires substantial domain knowledge (Bailin et al., 1999; Smith, 2002). Willingham (2007, 2020) found that while explicit reasoni ng strategies can be taught, students struggle to apply them in novel situations unless they also know the relevant content. Taken together, these perspectives highlight why critical thinking, despite being widely recognized as essential, is difficult to measure using traditional assessments. What is measurable is not always the same as what is valuable (Safir & Dugan, 2021). Because a PoG reflects the whole learner, evidence of its competencies should be visible throughout the system—in classrooms, in hallways, in student dialogue, and in public exhibitions of learning. Safir and Dugan (2021) recommend “streetlevel checks,” such as listening to students explain their work, observing how competencies show up in classrooms, and curating authentic exhibitions for families. They also advocate for authentic performance-based assessment systems that allow students to demonstrate and reflect on their growth in PoG competencies. Performance tasks, reflections, and exhibitions provide rich, authentic evidence of student learning, but they are also hard to standardize and quantify. This tension underscores both the promise and the challenge of developing assessment systems that honor the PoG and center the human experience. Due to the complexity of assessing critical thinking, it is critical to engage educators as co-designers of an assessment system rather than as implementers of an externally developed tool. Co-design creates space for educators and researchers to collaboratively develop and test formative assessment practices in classrooms (Goertzen et al., 2023). Studies of preservice and in-service educators have shown that iterative co-design supports both teacher learning and the development of assessments that are usable in practice (Amin et al., 2024). Co-design can move teachers past treating assessment tasks as a checkbox and instead fully engaging with the “spirit” of formative assessment (DeLuca et al., 2019, p. 275).

The study presented here is part of a larger research practice partnership (RPP) seeking to understand how school districts can notice, recognize, and measure student growth in durable skills. The practice-side partner, a district leader focused on implementing the district’s Portrait of a Graduate, brought a problem of practice to the researcher related to assessing student critical thinking. Together, the researcher and district leader identified the need to support classroom teachers in designing assessment practices of students’ critical thinking skills. This 1158 resulted in the creation of an Internal Assessment Cohort (IAC), a group of teachers interested in developing tools and resources to support assessing student critical thinking. In our work with the IAC, we sought to answer the research question: When asked to assess student critical thinking, what challenges and opportunities do teachers identify?

This study used a design-based research (DBR) approach (Flick, 2022), in which iterative cycles of practice, reflection, and redesign guided the development of the PoG assessment tools. DBR was selected because it combines theory-building with practice, en abling teachers to test ideas in real-world contexts while collaboratively creating knowledge (Braun & Clarke, 2006). Nine educators participated in an IAC which included two elementary educators, one middle school mathematics educator, two high school Int ernational Baccalaureate educators, one high school English/Advanced Placement (AP) Language educator, and three high school educators supporting the district’s program designed for students who thrive in nontraditional school environments. All meetings we re co-facilitated by the researcher and district leader and centered on co-design with the classroom teachers.

Data sources included a range of educator-and student-generated artifacts. At the first meeting, teachers developed posters outlining their current assessment practices, strengths, and challenges in measuring critical thinking. Across subsequent sessions, educators produced assessment design workbooks documenting prototypes of formative critical thinking assessments, as well as rubrics, assignment guidelines, and surveys. Student artifacts from these prototypes were collected, including a critical thinking survey, reflective essays, and portfolios. Additional data sources included exit tickets from team meetings, facilitator notes, final slide decks summarizing assessment approaches, and both the draft and final design principles. The analysis was iterative, as initial coding cycles in February focused on documenting existing practices, March and April on prototyping and reflection, and May and June on developing and synthesizing the PoG assessment design principles. We also used thematic to assessing critical thinking. Both the research-side and practice-side partners independently reviewed data sources and identified emerging themes. Together  we narrowed our analysis to five codes that cut across the different data types and meeting phases: student reflection, alignment to district definitions of critical thinking, evidence of shifts in teacher’s understanding of critical thinking, and types a nd characteristics of formative assessments. We used our discussion resolving codes to inform our development of the findings presented below. Three major findings surfaced in our work with the teachers in the IAC that related to assessing critical thinking within the PoG. These findings highlight both the complexity of assessing critical thinking and the possibilities for co-designed approaches that balance flexibility with coherence. Our findings represent multiple grain sizes of this work: necessary components of assessments of critical thinking, formative assessment development as professional learning, and the need for critical thinking assessment guidance, not constraints, at a systems level.. Self-reflection is powerful, but it requires structure Student self-reflection surfaced as a critical component of PoG assessment, with educators’ experiences highlighting both its promise and its challenges. As one teacher said in her exit ticket, “Students need a place to reflect on their own learning and their own growth.” One high school math teacher created a self-reflection survey that resulted in 180+ open-ended responses about students’ ability to interpret data as part of critical thinking. While the reflections revealed personalized insights, the data proved overwhelming to analyze and difficult to use instructionally. The three teachers working in an alternative setting included reflections in capstone portfolios, but found many responses to be shallow and disconnected from critical thinking. For instance, one student admitted they could have managed their time better, but did not connect this to the quality of their work or suggest strategies for improvement related to critical thinking. In contrast, when reflection was tied to a specific task and guided by clear expectations, responses were richer. In a third-grade classroom, educators asked students to reflect on a specific critical thinking task. Additionally, the educators provided students with “I can” statements to support students in understanding when they interpreted, analyzed, synthesized, and evaluated information during the critical thinking task. Sentence stems further supported students in reflecting on feedback they received during the task and considering how they would revise their work based on the feedback (Figure 1, left). Likewise, in an AP Language course, a teacher developed a mock trial rubric aligned to the district’s critical thinking definition and grade-level expectations. Students were assessed using the rubric and then completed written reflections guided by their rubric scores. Student artifacts from both settings suggest that the structured reflection opportunities combined with the clear 1159 task guidelines offered students a useful process for reflection on their critical thinking skills. For educators, these reflections offered meaningful insights into the students’ thought processes.  Across the cohort, teachers agreed that students engaged  more deeply in the self-reflection process when it was embedded in a specific task and grounded in clear criteria.

Defining success criteria revealed differences and fostered professional growth Educators agreed that clear success criteria were essential, but found what “counts” as critical thinking varied across grade levels, subjects, and individual perspectives. One educator reflected after a meeting, “I still feel like we do not have a super clear vision on what our product is.” The process of defining criteria proved to be valuable professional learning. The team that created a Grades 3 –5 rubric (Figure 1, right) deepened their understanding of interpretation, analysis, synthesis, evaluation, and reflection as dimensions of critical thinking. One educator shared “Looking at the critical thinking map helped center our rubric.” Another explained that she felt more confident teaching critical thinking because the process clarified what critical th inking looked like in her classroom. In this way, developing success criteria as a part of designing assessment tools supported teachers’ sensemaking and professional growth, even as interpretations of the construct continued to evolve. Figure 1 Critical Thinking Assessment Artifacts from Internal Assessment Cohort Design principles offered flexibility where one assessment could not The cohort initially set out to design a critical thinking formative assessment that could be applied broadly across disciplines and grade levels. However, through design cycles of experimentation and reflection, we quickly recognized that a single assessm ent could not capture the complexity of the competency across contexts. While teachers agreed on the importance of assessing critical thinking, their perspectives on what it looks like in practice varied significantly, as the educators recognized that diff erent approaches to assessing critical thinking (e.g., rubrics, surveys, portfolios, etc.) each contributed unique and essential insights. Rather than forcing consensus on a single assessment type, the cohort shifted to developing a set of design principles that could guide assessment across contexts and allow for flexibility and local adaptation. Small groups brainstormed initial design principles and shared with the larger group. As common ideas emerged, we developed a common list of principles that all members reviewed, revised, and reached agreement on. These principles included: embedding assessment in authentic learning tasks, using student-friendly success criteria, supporting student reflection and ownership, capturing growth over time, and using common rubrics and language where appropriate. Educators saw these principles not as prescriptive rules but as a framework for coherence that respected the variability of how critical thinking looks in practice. This pivot ultimately honored both the shared commitment to measuring critical thinking and the reality that it shows up differently depending on the content 1160 and setting. The design principles provided a framework for coherence without constraining teachers to one tool, capturing the balance between districtwide direction and classroom and building-level flexibility. Figure 2 Teacher-Designed Design Principles for Critical Thinking Assessment Tools These findings indicate that schools interested in defining and assessing critical thinking would be well-served to use design principles, not prescribed assessments, to co-design assessment with teachers that are aligned to shared definitions of critical thinking skills and ask students to self-reflect on their use of these skills in relation to a specific task. This recommendation illustrates both the complexity of measuring critical thinking and highlights the potential for co-designed assessments that honor teacher expertise and lived experiences (DeLuca et al., 2019). Grounded in student responses, the co-designed assessments developed by the IAC align with the holistic vision of the PoG – developing students who can self-reflect on their own learning with precision and teachers that have a shared understanding of how critical thinking is evidenced. By leveraging an RPP, engaging educators as codesigners, and developing assessment design principles rather than prescribing externally developed assessment tools, the assessment practices were contextually grounded and deepened teachers’ own understanding of critical thinking. This process reflects Safir and Dugan’s (2021) concept of street data, which calls for attending to how competencies surface in student work, dialogue, and reflections, and using this data to inform system design. While limited in scope, this study offers implications for classroom teachers, school and district leaders, and assessment developers seeking to assess skills commonly found in Portraits of a Graduate. Beginning with design principles that provide a framework for assessments, can lead to formative assessments that are authentic, contextually relevant, and aligned with district priorities. An important direction for future research might be to explore how teacher-developed design principles can support the assessment of other Portrait of a Graduate competencies, such as communication and collaboration, to see if similar assessment tensions and opportunities arise across different skills. Additionally, future research that examines how these design principles function when adopted and scaled in specific subject areas (e.g., math, ELA, science), schools, or districts would offer important insights into how districts could approach this work systematically.
