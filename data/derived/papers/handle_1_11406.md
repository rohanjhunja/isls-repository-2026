# How Do Researchers and Teachers Co-Design Computational Thinking Education? A Synergistic Approach for Professional Learning

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper examines the dynamics of a researcher-teacher partnership in co-designing computational thinking (CT) learning experiences for young children. While CT integration in early childhood education has gained increasing attention, the gap between theoretical frameworks and practical implementation remains a significant challenge. Through the lens of co-design in professional learning, we analyze the synergistic effort between researchers and a teacher during a five-day summer program involving 14 children (ages 4-12) in eastern China. Our findings reveal distinct patterns of engagement among different stakeholders, highlighting the complementary nature of theoretical and practical expertise in the co-design practices. The insights contribute to our understanding of effective professional learning experiences for CT education and offer practical implications for scaling similar initiatives in diverse cultural contexts.

## Introduction

The integration of computational thinking (CT) into  early childhood education has emerged as an import ant educational initiative worldwide, reflecting the gr owing recognition of CT as an important thinking sk ill that is essential for children ’s development (Liu et al, 2025). However, implementing CT education presents uniq ue challenges, particularly in contexts where teachers  may have limited exposure to CT concepts and pedag ogies (Liu et al, 2024). While researcher-developed programs offer theoretical foundations for CT education, they often struggle to account for the complexities of local educational contexts and the practical constraints teachers face in implementation (Liu, 2023).

The challenge of bridging theoretical frameworks wi th practical implementation is particularly evident in bringing new pedagogical concepts into practice (Gomoll et al., 2022). Teachers often find themselves caught between adhering to researcher-designed programs and adapting these programs based  on their pedagogical intuitions and understanding of their local context (Kelter et al., 20 21). This adaptation process, while necessary, can sometimes lead to departures from the theoretical foundations that initially informed the program design (e.g., Mak & Pun, 2015).

The notion of co-design offers a promising approach to addressing this theory-practice gap (Davis, 2023). Through the co-design effort, researchers and teachers can work together to develop CT learning experiences that are both theoretically reliable and practically viable. Participatory design principles suggest that such collaboration can enhance both the quality of educational innovat ions and teachers ’ professional growth (Cober et al., 2015). To that end, our project launched a five-day CT learning program aimed to enhance young chil dren’s sense of community identity while developing their CT compet encies. Rather than implementing a pre-designed curriculum, researchers and teachers engaged in dai ly co-design sessions, adapting and refining activities b ased on ongoing observations and emerging needs.

This paper focuses specifically on understanding the dynamics of these co-design discussions, examining how different stakeholders contribute to and engage with the design enactment. Through analyzing the interactions among participants, we seek to understand: (1) how the researcher-teacher partnership community engaged in codesign discussions, and (2)  to what extent different roles (i.e., researchers, researcher-teachers, and teachers) impacted co-design engagement. By examining these questions thr ough the lenses of co-design, we aim to contribute to the growing body of knowledge about e ffective approaches to CT education implementation and teacher professional learning in early childhood settings.

## Theoretical perspectives

The co-design discussion can be a powerful approach for br idging theory and practice among researchers and teachers and establishing an active learning environment that explores effective pedagogies and empiri callyinformed theoretical frameworks (Penuel et al., 2007; Gomoll et al., 2022). The past  research highlights that the co-design discussion facilitates a more nuanced unders tanding of the complexities involved in teaching, a nd promotes a process of “welldoing” where participant s actively engage in the design, reflection, and implementation (Davis et al., 2023). From the researcher’s perspective, this collaborative approach fosters deeper engagement with educational content and promotes th e alignment of theoretical frameworks with practical applications tailored to specific learning environm ents. From the teacher ’s perspective, in the co-design discussion, they actively engage in facilitating th e development of knowledge rather than merely consu ming it, which helps them develop their professional vision (Gomoll et al., 2022). This synergistic relationshi p enhances the relevance of research findings to practical tea ching scenarios, ensuring that theoretical insights  are grounded in the realities of classroom practice.

Previous studies on the co-design approach have mainly focused on its effect o n teachers’ professional learning and classroom enactment. For instance, one  research has delved into influential factors in de veloping teachers’ professional vision during co-design discussions and established one three-part co-design cycle related to their specific contexts (Gomoll et al., 2022). O ther research focuses on investigating the relation ship between curriculum and professional learning, and acquiring implications from co-design styles for instructional practice (Kelter et al., 2021). However, a notable gap exist s in previous research, which has mainly focused on  teachers’ perspectives and gains in the co-design process, lacking a grounded understanding of how researchers and teachers impact each other over time (V oogt et al., 2015; Kelter et al., 2021).

## Context

This study is a part of a larger research project c onducted in a learning community for young children in eastern China over the course of five days as an after-school program. The teachers and the researchers wo rked collaboratively to create a learning program that a imed at helping to establish a better community for  young learners through children’s perspectives to observe the neighborhood surroundin gs when conducting their daily life routine. The overarching goal of the learning program is to enhance children’s sense of identity development and integrate CT into children ’s way of thinking in their daily lives. Fourteen ch ildren, aged between 4-12, participated in the project, engaging in two hours of activities each day. In this study, we focus on the professional learning experience through co-designing dynamically across researchers and teachers. Therefore, the participan ts are two researchers from universities and one te acher from the children’s community. Among them, one of the researchers, Su san, took up the role of the teaching assistant and acted as a researcher-teacher during the implementation. All participants, including the children mentioned in the conversations, are pseudonymous.

## Data analysis

After each daily learning activity, the researchers and the teacher participant engaged in a 1.5-hour co-planning section over the course of the project. Co-planning activities involved  debriefing, discussion, and co-design components. These sections were audio-taped, and transcribed in both Chinese Mandarin and  English by one researcher. To validate the accuracy of the English  transcripts  and interpretation, we underwent a rigorous verification process involving cross-checking by multiple researchers and participant-checking to achieve consensus.

To better understand the co-design engagement and the roles of different participants, the research team conducted an exploratory qualitative case study (St ake, 1995, 2010). The case presented in this study is bounded by the unique nature of the co-designing activities and the specific engagement pa tterns observed. For data analysis, we employed open coding and thematic analysis to derive the emergent engagement model, specifically utilizing a holistic coding approach during the fir st cycle of coding (Saldaña, 201 4). ## Findings

RQ1: How did the researcher-teacher partnership community engage in co-design discussions?

We had three co-design participants in this discussion with specifi c references to Evelyn (teacher), Logan (researcher), and Susan (researcher-teacher). Their specific engagement patterns were t raced as a spiraling co-design cycle to describe the continual and iterative procedure (see Figure 1). In the inner cycle, the three categories

(1) noticing, (2) guiding, and (3) reflecting occur red in sequential order and continued throughout th e process of

co-design (see example quotes in Table 1). In these three categories, the teacher and resear chers stated their observations, findings, and thoughts from their per spectives. The teacher focused on creation and deli very of pedagogical content, while researchers paid more at tention to the feasibility of the curriculum and th e potential bridge between theory and practice.

Outside of the inner cycle of co-design conversation, the category describes the design enactment which is grounded in noticing, guiding, and reflecting. I n Figure 1, the design enactment is depicted as the  outer cycle as the context of the co-design engagement. Specifically, noticing, guiding, and reflecting were all situated in the context of teaching enactment. For example, through  the co-design discussions, researchers and teachers collaboratively adjusted learning objectives, pedag ogical strategies, and learning activities together, ensuring the appropriate application of pedagogical theory in au thentic learning contexts. Design enactment highlig hted the essential role of contexts and iterative co-design processes in translating educational theory into effective learning activities.

Researchers demonstrated a leadership role in occur rences of guiding. Based on the limitation of the activity design they observed, the researcher share d related learning theories to guide the learning design and teaching practices, while the teacher shared practical instructional strategies to address students ’ specific needs. For example, Logan (researcher) proposed three stag es that could happen in the curriculum design. The first was to increase hands-on experiences, the second was to reflect on the application of existing computa tional devices, and the third was to engage children in their expression grounded i n usage and reflection. Based on the sharing, Evelyn (teacher) reviewed and reflected on her implementation of learning activities. In the reflecting part, both researchers and the te acher shared their perspectives on the challenges o f bridging theories and practices. The teacher inquired about the possibility of tea ching CT to younger children, while the researcher discussed the potential limitations of the existing curriculum design and application of CT in early childhood education. For example, Evelyn (tea cher) questioned how CT could be learned in the way that considers young children’s needs and development. Logan (researcher) built on Evelyn’s question and discussed the limitations of existing programming courses for young children. In the design enactment phase, the participants integrated the observations, perspectives, and reflections shared through noticing, guiding, and reflecting, to redes ign the learning activities. For design enactment, the researchers and teachers engaged in collaborative b rainstorming, with no evident leadership role drivi ng the conversations. Everyone concentrated on redesigning the activities to facilitate children’s learning and prepare for future design. For example, after the participants reflected on how they had underestimated the children’s learning abilities and simplified the activity settings, they collaboratively redesigned the activity to be both in alignment with children’s life scenarios and their prior teaching and personal experiences. Overall, the dynamic shift in roles fostered a productive and synergistic co-design conversation, encouraging the sharing of diverse perspectives and emphasizing the active participation of all stakeholders.

## Discussion and scholarly importance

Our analysis reveals a dynamic four-part co-design cycle that extends the current understanding  of research-practice partnerships in computational thinking education. Unlike previous models highlighting the pro gression of the co-design process (e.g., Gomoll et al., 2022), our fin dings focused on how co-design conversation contributed to design enactment through an iterativ e spiral where noticing, guiding, reflecting, and d esign enactment continuously inform each other. In partic ular, the role dynamics observed in our study highl ight a unique pattern of shifting leadership that enhances  the co-design process. Duri ng noticing phases, teachers ’ practical expertise led the discussion, grounding d iscussions in classroom realities. Although such pr actical considerations are often tense with theoretical ide ation, resolving the tension can lead to more pract ical and impactful changes (Kelter et al., 2021; Mak & Pun, 2015). In guiding phases, research ers’ theoretical knowledge came to the fore, helping to frame observed phenome na within broader theoretical frameworks. In the ob served co-design discussion, we posit that adopting a post ho c theoretical lens based on practical consideration s is a productive way to lead to the synergistic reflectio n phase. During the reflection phase, both groups c ontributed equally to understanding challenges and opportunities. In particular, the dual role of researcher-teacher served as a vital bridge between theoretical and practical pe rspectives, facilitating deeper understanding acros s both domains (cf. Juuti et al., 2021; Kyza & Agesilaou, 2022). This fluid exchange of leadership aligns with principles in participatory design (Cober et al., 2015).

The findings reveal important insights into bringing CT education in early childhood settings, particularly in contexts where teachers may be unfamiliar with C T concepts (Liu et al, 202 5). Our co-design approach demonstrated that effective CT integration requires more than just theoretical frameworks or practical experience alone (Liu, 2023). The synergistic process enabled teachers to gradually develop their understanding of CT while simultaneously helping researchers refine their the oretical frameworks based on practical implementati on challenges. This was particularly evident in how  the team adapted CT-related pedagogies to align with young children’s developmental needs and local cultural contexts. These findings have significant implications for th e field of early childhood CT education and suggest several promising directions for future research. T here is a need for in-depth studies examining how co-design relationships evolve over time and their i mpact on sustained CT implementation (Ketelhut et a l., 2020). In addition, the role of dual-role stakeholders (e.g., researcher-teacher) needs further investigation, particularly in terms of their potential to facilitate theory-practice integration in educational innovation. The se directions could contribute to developing more effective models for teacher professional learning in diverse settings.
