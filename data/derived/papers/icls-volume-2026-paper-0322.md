# Exploring Reflection Quality: Divergent Self-Efficacy Outcomes in Makerspaces

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Makerspaces are often promoted as environments that build student self-efficacy (Morado et al., 2021), yet not all learners experience gains. This exploratory mixed-methods study examines the relationship between reflection quality and changes in self-efficacy within a semester-long makerspace course. Thirteen graduate students were categorized into gain (n = 7) and loss (n = 6) groups based on pre/post survey scores. Two early reflections per student were coded using ten categories and analyzed for length, breadth, depth, and structure using epistemic network analysis (ENA). Qualitative examples illustrated how these patterns manifested in reflective writing. While both groups produced reflections of similar length and breadth, they diverged in depth and structure. The gain group developed integrative structures linking challenges to evaluation, learning, and future planning, while the loss group provided descriptive accounts of challenges and problem-solving. Findings suggest that reflection quality, not quantity, distinguishes self-efficacy trajectories and that deliberately scaffolding evaluative, forward-looking reflection could potentially support self-efficacy growth in makerspaces.

Makerspaces have become increasingly prominent in education as environments that support design, real-world problem-solving, and creativity through hands-on activities (Andrews et al., 2021). Yet for these environments to be genuinely beneficial, they must also support students’ self-efficacy, defined as their confidence in their ability to succeed (Bandura, 1997). Self-efficacy is a dynamic construct; it can strengthen when learners interpret setbacks as opportunities for mastery or erode when they view them as signs of inadequacy (Bandura, 1997). Because makerspaces involve frequent technical failures and open-ended challenges (Yulis San Juan & Murai, 2021), students’ self-efficacy is especially vulnerable in these settings. Vongkulluksn and colleagues (2018) documented declines in self-efficacy and interest over the course of a makerspace semester, suggesting that not all students benefit equally from these experiences. Since self-efficacy is linked to persistence and long-term engagement in STEM (Lent et al., 1984; Vongkulluksn et al., 2018), understanding why some learners gain self-efficacy while others lose it is crucial. This underscores the need to examine the mechanisms behind divergent self-efficacy trajectories and to identify ways to support its growth. Reflection is a central mechanism through which self-efficacy can either be strengthened or diminished (Yong & Roberts, 2025). According to social cognitive theory, self-efficacy is shaped not only by experiences themselves but also by how individuals interpret them, drawing on sources such as mastery experiences, vicarious experiences, verbal persuasion, and emotional states (Bandura, 2001). Reflection supports this interpretive process by turning experiences into judgments of competence (Zimmerman, 2002). In makerspaces, it plays a pivotal role in self-efficacy development by helping students interpret setbacks and successes, gauge their skills, and anticipate future performance (Bandura, 1997; Mavri et al., 2025; Turakhia et al., 2022). Yet reflection does not automatically strengthen self-efficacy-what matters is how students reflect. Schön (1983) distinguishes between surface description and deeper reflection-on-action that generates transferable insights, and Flavell (1979) differentiates between metacognitive monitoring of learning processes and active regulation o f those processes. These frameworks underscore that the quality of reflection matters in self-efficacy development. Recent research has shifted from asking whether students reflect to examining how reflection quality shapes learning and self-efficacy. Structured written reflections significantly increased students' self-efficacy compared to non-structured ones in a service learning course (Sanders et al., 2016). In clinical education, reflection was found to have either positive or negative effects on self-efficacy depending on the depth of engagement and students' orientation toward reflective practice (Yong & Roberts, 2025). In STEM education, structured reflection on problem-solving and design processes has been shown to strengthen metacognitive engagement and confidence in learning (Donald et al., 2020; Singh & Diefes-Dux, 2022). Brubaker and colleagues (2020) found that an introductory makerspace course emphasizing structured reflection and portfolio building was associated with particularly strong self-efficacy gains among certain student subgroups. Taken together, these findings suggest 2101 that self-efficacy develops not from reflection per se, but from reflection that promotes insight, agency, and selfregulated action. Yet most prior work has examined reflection in professional and clinical education, with limited research in makerspaces where frequent technical failure and iteration are central to the learning process. Moreover, while existing studies demonstrate that reflection quality matters for self-efficacy development, they have not examined how reflection patterns distinguish learners who gain self-efficacy from those who lose it, leaving the mechanisms behind divergent trajectories unclear. To address this gap, the present study examines how the structure of reflection, or how reflective elements are connected, differentiates students who gain self-efficacy from those who lose it in a makerspace course. By linking these patterns to outcomes, the study identifies how reflection organization supports or undermines self-efficacy development and informs strategies for scaffolding more productive reflective practice in makerspaces.

## Participants and data coding

Nineteen graduate students enrolled in a semester-long makerspace course participated in the study. Self-efficacy was measured at the beginning and end of the semester using the General Self-Efficacy Scale (Schwarzer & Jerusalem, 1995). Based on relative change scores, students were grouped into gain (n = 7) and loss (n = 6); those with no change were excluded to focus on divergent trajectories. Each student submitted weekly reflections documenting their design process, challenges, and learning; for this st udy, we analyzed two early reflections per student (n = 26) to capture initial reflection patterns. Reflections were segmented into sentences and coded using a 10-category scheme developed through a hybrid (deductive-inductive) approach, including Description (factual recounting of events), Challenge (obstacles encountered), Problem-solving (strategies or adaptations used to overcome difficulties), Reasoning (justification of design or process choices), Evaluation (assessment of effectiveness or workflow), Learning (insights or takeaways), Future Plans (intentions for improvement or next steps), Affect (emotional responses), Social Interaction (collaboration or feedback), and Agency (initiative expressed by the student). Two trained researchers independently coded the reflections; a subset was double-coded for reliability (Krippendorff’s α >.80).

We examined reflections along four complementary dimensions. Length captured the overall quantity of reflection, operationalized as the number of sentences and coded segments. Breadth represented the range of ideas expressed, measured by the number of unique codes per reflection. Depth reflected the relative emphasis on different aspects of reflection, calculated as the proportion of code types within each reflection. Given the small sample, we did not conduct inferential tests; instead, we focus on descrip tive contrasts to illustrate meaningful differences in reflection patterns. Structure  was analyzed using epistemic network analysis (ENA), which statistically tests group differences while mapping the co-occurrence and sequential links among codes. Each reflection was treated as the unit of analysis, and a mean-subtracted network was used to visualize differential connections between groups. Finally, to contextualize these quantitative analyses, we conducted a qualitative reflections through different code emphases. This provided interpretive depth by linking coded categories to concrete examples of reflective discourse.

Length Both groups wrote similar amounts. Students in the gain group produced an average of 24.5 sentences per reflection (SD = 6.58) and 32.5 coded segments (SD = 10.65). Students in the loss group wrote an average of 22.9 sentences (SD = 11.82) and 26.8 coded segments (SD = 14.44). These differences were minimal, suggesting that outcomes were not driven simply by how much students wrote (Figure 1, Panel A). Breadth Students in both groups drew on a range of codes, but the gain group demonstrated slightly broader coverage, averaging 7.4 unique categories per reflection (SD = 1.83), compared to 6.6 in the loss group (SD = 1.83). Variety alone did not fully distinguish the groups; rather, the difference lay in how categories were mobilized (Figure 1, Panel B).

2102 Depth Clear distinctions emerged in the proportional use of codes (Figure 1, Panel C). Gain group reflections allocated more weight to higher-order categories such as Evaluation (10.8% vs. 5.9%), Reasoning (11.9% vs. 7.7%), Learning (6.1% vs. 2.0%), and Future Plans (4.0% vs. 0.5%), showing greater attention to interpretation, takeaways, and forward-looking thinking. Smaller but consistent differences also favored the gain group in Affect (7.2% vs. 5.2%), Agency (1.8% vs. 0.6%), and Social Interaction (4.3% vs. 3.3%). By contrast, loss group reflections were dominated by Description (41.2% vs. 31.1%), Challenge (19.2% vs. 11.9%), and Problem-solving (14.5% vs. 10.8%), emphasizing recounting steps and obstacles. Overall, the gain group distributed their reflections across evaluative, interpretive, and growth-oriented dimensions, while the loss group leaned heavily on descriptive and problem-focused categories.

Structure Descriptive contrasts in length, breadth, and depth were complemented by ENA, which provided rigorous statistical evidence of structural differences between the two groups (t(10.94) = 4.22, p <.001, Cohen’s d = 2.33). The subtracted network (Figure 1, Panel D) highlights these contrasts. Students in the gain group (blue) showed stronger links between Learning and Affect (6-8; numbers correspond to coded categories in the order listed in and a pattern of connections among Evaluation, Learning, Future Plans, and Affect (7-8, 5-7, 6-7). These patterns suggest a growth-oriented cycle where emotions were tied to learning, and evaluation was tied to both challenges and future planning. By contrast, the loss group (red) was anchored in Description-Challenge-Problem-solving (1-2, 2-3, 1-3), with additional ties to Social Interaction (2-9, 1-9). This structure reflects a narrower, problem-focused loop centered on obstacles and strategies, with less movement toward evaluation or future-oriented insights.

Figure 1 Qualitative examples Excerpts illustrate how gain and loss groups differed in their reflective connections. A gain-group student wrote: “Overall, I am happy with how the name tag turned out and learned a lot from this first assignment… If I weren’t as constrained for time, I would have liked to challenge myself… I’m already feeling inspired… to continue creating and trying new techniques.”  Others wrote, “Lessons learned… helped me redesign more confidently… a supportive atmosphere allowed me to seek feedback.”  and “There was a learning curve this week… I already feel my laser cutting and design skills progressing… I am looking forward tonew challenges and to learning how to incorporate more complex features into my design work.”  These reflections link evaluation, learning, affect, and future aspirations, illustrating a growth-oriented cycle. In contrast, a loss-group student reflected: “Then, another idea popped up in my mind… I didn’t really like the setup, so I moved on… A struggle I had was how I can make part of the lines engraved and part cutouts… I was not able to, so I went to office hours and the TA helped me.” Another noted, “It took longer than expected to draw all the symbols… I checked how others were doing and picked the way that looked good.”  These reflections focus on descriptive challenges and reliance on external help, without extending toward broader evaluation or future planning. Overall, gain-group students tended to reframe challenges into learning and motivation, whereas loss-group students remained anchored in immediate difficulties and troubleshooting.

2103 Quality over quantity in reflection These findings suggest that self-efficacy growth in makerspaces is associated with reflective quality rather than quantity. While both groups wrote similar amounts and used comparable varieties of reflective categories, they diverged in depth and structure. Students who gained self-efficacy distributed their reflections more evenly across higher-order categories and touched more on affect, agency, and social interaction. They displayed integrative structures linking challenges to evaluation, learning, and future planning. They also connected emotions to insights, reframing difficulties as lessons. These patterns align with productive failure (Kapur, 2016) and self-regulated learning theory (Zimmerman, 2002). Prior studies similarly show that reflective capac ity, including deliberate analysis and insight extraction, predicts self-efficacy (Barkhordari-Sharifabad et al., 2025), and that high-performing groups demonstrate stronger connections between affect and higher-order reflection stages (Tang et al., 2023).  Our results suggest that evaluative, forward-looking reflection serves as an internal scaffold that helps students reframe obstacles as learning experiences. In contrast, students who lost self-efficacy  remained in a Description-Challenge-Problem-solving loop, focusing on obstacles without evaluating their implications or extracting transferable principles. Description alone fosters shallow reflection unless integrated with analysis and evaluation (Jung et al., 2022). This pattern reflects metacognitive monitoring without regulation (Flavell, 1979) and mirrors findings that engineering students engaged primarily in low and medium levels of planning and monitori ng with little evaluation or transfer (Singh & Diefes-Dux, 2022). Without progression toward meaning-making, reflection may inadvertently reinforce inadequacy by foregrounding difficulties without resolution (Clifford, 1984). Early detection and pedagogical intervention These structural differences emerged in the first two reflections, suggesting that reflection structure may provide an early signal of students at risk of declining self-efficacy. Reflections that remain in descriptive loops, such as repeatedly recounting challenges without evaluation or forward planning, may point to the need for timely instructional support. This is particularly important in makerspaces where technical frustrations are common (Yulis San Juan & Murai, 2021). Studies on guided reflection provide evidence-based strategies for moving students beyond description. Davis (2003) showed that directed prompts asking students to explain, evaluate, and plan led to higher reflection quality than generic ones, while Hübner and colleagues (2010) found that structured question sequences benefited students who struggled with open-ended reflection. Worked examples that contrast descriptive reflections with evaluative, forward-looking ones can also help students internalize quality standards. Effective prompts should address multiple dimensions, including description, analysis, reasoning, emotional awareness, evaluation, future intention, social interaction, and agency (Nückles et al., 2020). For example, prompts might ask: “What specific challenge did you face, and what made it difficult?”, “Why did you choose that approach, and what reasoning guided your decision?”, “How did you feel during the process, and how did those emotions influence your actions?”, “What worked well or did not, and what  did you learn from it?”, “How will you apply these lessons next time?”, “Who supported or collaborated with you, and how did their feedback shape your thinking?”, and “What steps did you take on your own initiative to overcome obstacles or improve your work?” Such multidimensional questioning might foster forward-looking reflection that supports sustained self-efficacy growth.

Contributions and future directions This exploratory study offers three key contributions. Methodologically, it demonstrates how combining sentencelevel coding with ENA can uncover structural differences in reflection that remain invisible in surface-level measures such as length or breadth (Shaffer et al., 2016). Practically, it highlights reflection as a central mechanism linking makerspace experiences to self-efficacy development and as a promising site for timely instructional intervention. Computationally, it points toward the potential  of automated analytics to detect and support reflection quality at scale. Future research could integrate ENA with natural language processing (NLP) to identify reflection structures in real time, allowing instructors to detect at-risk students and provide just-in-time scaffolds.

Subsequent studies should test these findings with larger samples, extend analyses across diverse makerspace contexts, and examine whether targeted reflection scaffolds can mitigate self-efficacy loss. If these while longitudinal studies could explore whether early reflection depth predicts long-term persistence and identity development in maker education.
