# Summary Writing Support by Structural Organization With Kit-Build Concept Map for EFL Readers

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
The activities learners do in making summaries facilitate them to think more carefully than only reading. Although summary writing can be helpful both for fostering and assessing performance in reading, it remains a challenge for most learners, especially in languages learners are not familiar with. Moreover, summary writing can be a problem for students to master because some learners have diﬀiculties identifying the main ideas from detailed information in the text. This study focuses on the issue of improving the performance of English as a foreign language (EFL) learners’ summary writing skills by utilizing the kit-build concept map tool and text structure strategy. A web-based system has been developed, and an online cross-experiment has been conducted, with 75 Indonesian students at the intermediate level of language proficiency involved. Students’ written summaries were collected and evaluated based on two indicators. The result indicates the effect of system use on improving the quality of summary is positive. It supports learners by helping them clarify important ideas and write summaries with organized information.

## 1 Introduction

Summary writing is a bridge that connects writing a nd reading. The writing of a summary plays a dual r ole in reading comprehension. It can both boost and measur e reading comprehension: when learners are involved  in writing a summary of a reading text, they need to a ttend more closely to what they are reading to comm unicate the text in their own words; on the other hand, they are supposed to demonstrate how they comprehend the given source text through writing (Mokeddem & Houcine, 20 16). Ferris and Hedgcock claimed that learners summarizing what they are reading get involved in t he process of reproduction which includes: (a) thor ough comprehension of the source; (b) selection of the text’s most salient information; (c) deletion of less-than-essential information; (d) comprehension and integration of t he selected information; and (e) arrangement of sel ected material in a way that reflects the rhetorical stru cture of the original. When they start to write a s ummary, there are two main tasks. One is to sift through material, extracting the main ideas. The other is to integr ate those important ideas or information, weaving them into a consistent expression of words. They are supposed to review the source text during this process, weighing each piece of information presented. Such activities mea n that making summaries while reading will think more care fully than only reading. Therefore, summary writing  is a conducive way to better performance in reading. By doing this, readers are required to concentrate on the overall comprehension of the source text compared to those who just focus on following the idea at the word or sentence level.

2 Problem and research goal Although summary writing can be useful for fosterin g and assessing performance in reading, it remains a challenge for most learners. The situation could be even worse when it comes to a language that they are not quite familiar with, such as English as a foreign languag e (EFL) learner. Previous studies shed light on thi s issue. Researchers claimed that summary writing appears to be the most difficult to master (Lin & Maarof, 2013). It is “a highly complex, recursive reading-writing activi ty involving constraints that can impose an overwhe lming cognitive load on students. (Kirkland & Saunders, 1991)” Othman (2009) stated that the result of her study based on the reports of 60 secondary school teachers revealed that learners have difficulties in differentiating between main ideas and supporting details from source texts. As mentioned before, the process of summarization can be mainly divided into two parts. Whether learners can properly pick the main ideas should be given great  weight, and these choices significantly affect their summar ization performances. Learners can never make a goo d summary if they cannot decide what kind of ideas should be given more weight. However, problems are far more than this. In light of other studies, students generally follow a strategy of copying sentences from the source text; have difficulty writing summaries with their own words (Kirkland & Saunders, 1991)(Othman, 2009). Hutchins (1987) has claimed that summarization inv olves the understanding of texts. Kintsch and Vandijk (1978)(1983) that discourse comprehension necessarily involves recognizing text structures and the ability to summarize. When it comes to the structure of a text, two notions should be made clear: the microstructure and ICLS2022 Proceedings 440 © ISLS the macro-structure. There is a basic distinction b etween these two terms. The micro-structure represe nts the relation between the sequence of sentences in the a ctual text. The macro-structure represents relations between blocks of sentences and the global organization of texts. These relations, at both levels, may be them atic and semantic. Thematic relations include anaphora, back ward and forward reference, and topicalization. Sem antic relations include cause-effect, time, and logical relations (Hutchins, 1987). Hutchins (1987) also stated that there is no unique summary for a text because summaries a re expressions of the macro-structure of the source  as interpreted by an individual in the light of background knowledge. This study aims to try to tackle one or some of the problems discussed above to help EFL learners reach better performance in summary writing. The criteria applied to evaluate a summary focus on two perspectives. In terms of the content, whether learners ’	summaries contain main ideas (important information) from the original text as many as possible and to what extent the red undancy is. On the other hand, from the perspective  of the organization of information, whether they can arrange	their	ideas	in	an	organized	way	or	whether	their	ideas are	logically	connected/integrated	to	follow	the	macro-structure	of	the	source.

## 3 Method

The structure strategy teaches readers to identify the overall top-level structure of the expository text and to use that structure to organize their reading comprehension. Comprehension is defined as the synthesis of n ew ideas with existing memory structures using the framework  of the structure strategy (Meyer & Wijekumar, 2007). Generally speaking, the top-level structure has fiv e patterns: cause and effect, problem and solution,  sequence, description, and comparison. The power of teaching students the structure strategy includes helping them follow the logical structure of text to understand how an author organized and emphasized ideas” and “help le arners organize their writing such as written summaries” (Meyer & Ray, 2011). The structure strategy’s main i dea patterns give them even more help in identifying and strategically using the organization of the text.” (Wijekumar et al., 2012) A concept map or conceptual diagram i s a diagram that depicts suggested relationships be tween concepts (Hager & Corbin, 1997). Tajeddin and Tabat abaei (2016) also have stated the advantages of usi ng concept mapping include: “allowing students to thin k deeply about concepts and to store information mo re effectively in their memory” and “helping students organize different concepts and visualize the relat ionships between the main concepts in a meaningful way.” Chin et al. (2010) state the effectiveness of interactive feedback and automated assessment of concept maps. Kit-build concept map (Hirashima et al., 2015) is a digital tool derived from the common concept map and supports a concept map strategy (Pailai et al., 2017). The ability of Kit-build concept map includes a construction tool where users can construct concept maps and an automatic concept map assessment where the system can report diagnosis reports.

4. Function design

Dealing with summary writing as a whole would be a challenge for learners. Summary writing requires learners to have the ability to understand what is important and how the important ideas should be logically organized or related. To address this issue, an alternate is to decompose the task into certain sub-tasks. Based on this, learning activities carried by the system can be defined as follows:

1) Kit-building on paragraph level: In this step, learners can construct concept maps from given kits for

each paragraph and get diagnosis feedback.

2) Clarification of important information: following the instruction of structure strategy. In this step,

learners can pick up propositions, choose a structure for the proposition and clarify the roles of concepts. Feedback is also available.

3) Integration of information: identifying as a structure. In this step, learners can merge/integrate concepts.

They can also split merged nodes in case of mistaken operation. 4.1 Kit-building on the paragraph level To reach a summary with good quality, one premise i s that learners need to understand each paragraph o f the original text. By utilizing the kit-build concept map, learners can check the reflection of their understandings and see the difference between their comprehension and the instructor’s comprehension. They can choose to modify their maps according to the feedback they get. Figu re 1 shows an example of a kit decomposed from a co ncept map made by a teacher. In Kit-building, learners make propositions with the provided nodes and links for making a concept map of the reading text. This phase aims to make the same concept map as the teacher to unde rstand what is written in the text correctly. The system p rovides feedback to learners on the difference betw een the learner’s and the teacher’s concept map, as shown i n Fig.2. That is, information that describes one of the five relationships mentioned above should be viewed as important information (This study only focuses on two structures which are problem/solution and ca use/effect). In this phase, learners should identif y whether the idea presented fits one certain structure. As shown in table 1 and table 2, there is a signifi cant difference between results without and using t he system. Parameter Main	Points	denotes the number of main ideas a learner mentione d in his/her summary. It evaluates their ability to clarify important ideas from the s ource. Redundancy	refers to the number of information that is less essential. Integrated	ideas	show the number of pairs of ideas from different positions or paragraphs a leaner integrated into his/her summary. These tables show that their scores of main points and integrated ideas increased while their redundancy decreased with the use of th e system. An increase in scores of main points mean s that students’ summaries focus more on the important information. A decreasing redundancy indicates the conciseness of their summaries has been improved. With the use of the system, learners’ ability to identify what k ind of information is salient and the ability to get rid o f less-useful information has been improved. An inc rease in integrated ideas implies that learners can better see global connections among ideas by using the system. Global connections point to the global organization of the text. It is a sign of a better arrangement of information. Then we checked the average map scores and top-level structure (TLS) map scores and the qualities of their summaries between the classes. ## 6 Concluding Remarks

According to the results, we can now answer two research questions remained. First, the use of the system has a significant positive effect on the quality of learn ers’ summaries. With the support of the system, the  content of learners’ summaries contains more important ideas and is more concise than those without using the system. On the other hand, students tend to easily see connect ions between ideas. The way they arrange information within their summaries has also been improved with the sup port of the system. The three learning activities i ntegrated into the system each contribute to each step of sum mary writing. The use of kit-build concept mapping and its instant feedback positively affects learners’ summary writing. The application of structure strategy helps learners clarify essential information; TLS mapping is conducive to seeing the connections between ideas and organizing ideas.
