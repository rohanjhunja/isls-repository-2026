# Toward Liberatory Praxis: Facilitative Moves and Tensions in Youth Organizing

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Building on pedagogies of liberation through youth participatory action research (YPAR), this work explicates the role of adult facilitators as they navigate tensions and make sense of their roles and responsibilities within an emerging youth council. The cases highlight the complexity of intergenerational relationships within youth-centered research, specifically in the context of organizing. This work demonstrates a need for further work that examines the interactional micro-moments between youth and adults that embody a praxis of liberatory education.

## Introduction

Youth participatory action research (YPAR) grows fr om a lineage of radical social movements and libera tory pedagogies (Freire, 1970). YPAR contest s deficit imaginings of youth, critically document s conditions of structural inequality, and creates spaces for collective resistance. At a high level, this paper argues that organizing spaces are critical sites for intergenerational lea rning that center ethical relations and engage  praxis that move toward pedagogies of liberation. To this end, I examine ho w a youth council in its early stage opens a space for learning as participants make sense of their roles and navigate moments of tension with each other and with adults. I present three cases based on adults' facilitative moves and sensemaking to exami ne how participants on the council engaged relational practices that support critical inquiry, reflection, and action (Malorni et. al., 2022). I conclude with a discussion and call for further research that illuminates the micro-moments of intergenerational learning and joint work that facilitate and deepen relationships, which are central to YPAR and libera tory education.

## Background

In youth-centered research, conceptualizations of ‘youth’ often run the risk of perpetuating neoli beral, capitalist ideals that flatten identities of both youth and ad ults (Sukarieh & Tannock, 2014). Youth are framed i n binaries as either unemployed delinquents or “assets” to invest in; either empowered agents of change or disempowered vessels that need to be saved. YPAR is one approach  to conducting research that centers ethical relati onships. However, despite being touted as a powerful approach to research and action, facilitation within YPAR is often a “black box” that obfuscates how relational power dy namics unfold during a project. Furthermore, unders tanding how relationships are set up and facilitated within YPAR projects matters for how projects oriented toward critical consciousness and liberatory education are designed and facilitated. This discussion offers a look at the relational labor and contexts that precede YPAR projects and that are vital in organizing spaces. I draw on intergenerational learning to notice where adult/youth binaries are reinforced or challenged  (Vossoughi et  al., 2021) and illustrate how identities —for both youth and adults —encompass shifting and evolving roles and responsibilities. I use organizers Mariame Kaba and Kelly Haye’s words to define ‘organizing’ as “a more specific set of practices” and “a craft that requires us to cultivate a variety of skills, such as intentional relationship building and power anal ysis” (Hayes & Kaba, 2023, p. 14). Organizers try to “move other people, [and] create collective movement in a  very conscious, deliberate and strategic way, informed by a larger social agenda” (Hayes & Kaba, 2023, p. 14). Existing work on YPAR focus on its implementation in formal educational settings. Yet, YPAR principles can – and often are – adopted in informal learning settings such as social movement spaces. By examining the principles of YPAR and organizing  in the overlapping context of a youth council, I seek to elucidate  how facilitative moves (with its craft, skills, and intentionality) unfold across sites for learning. Moreover, I aim to push YPAR beyond formalist conceptions and institutions, in order to better theorize how intergenerational learning unfolds in organizing spaces. Finally, this work responds to prior calls for youth-centered work to address adultism while attending to the ways facilitation happens. I hope to sharpen theories of learning and identity through a critical examination of adultism within youth organizing spaces and YPAR projects. Drawing on Malorni et al.’s (2022) practices in YPAR facilitation and scholarship on intergenerational learning (Bang  et. al., 2020), I discuss how tensions that surfaced within a youth council bring into focus th e micro-moments of facilitation and sensemaking of adults. These moments demonstrate how organizing can be a site for rich intergenerational learning  while holding the inevitable relational complexities that surface through organizing. Finally, it contributes toneeded discussion on the social identities and experiences of adult faci litators (Malorni et al., 2022, p. 5). I consider: What moves do participants make toward critical inquiry, reflecti on, and action? How do adults facilitate and navigate moments of tension within a youth organizing space?

## Research context

The council  is an organizing group established by two high school students, Zia and Marcus,  during 2021 municipal elections in Ridgeview West, a neighborhood in a large midwestern city. All youth members of the council were in high school and had prior training and experience organizing with vario us citywide and local groups. Two adult facilitators —Sarah (author) and Azha—were connected to each other through the municipal campaign prior to being connected with the youth council. Both Azha and Sarah grew up in Ridgeview West and have immigrant backgrounds from Pakistan and Korea respectively. Azha was involved in the election campaign and had experience working with the Rohingya community center in the neighborhood in addition to his day job. Sarah is a graduate researcher examining education across formal and informal spaces and has been working the past six years with refugee and immigrant youth  through a non-profit organization. A majority of the refugee youth that participate in the programs also live in Ridgeview West. The council met in the election office once a week to plan programs for youth in the neighborhood and build a sense of community. Council members engaged  in some campaign activities such as door knocking and phone banking. At the meetings, youth facilitated icebreaker activities for members to get to know one another in addition to using the time to plan events. For exam ple, the council organized a “Senior Night” to intr oduce high schoolers to the college application process and how to apply for financial aid. I draw on field notes, memos, and recordings of council meetings as a participant obs erver. Pseudonyms are used for all participants and  locations, except for the author, Sarah.

## Analysis and findings

In a review of YPAR projects, Malorni et al. (2022) identify three relationship practices that are key to achieving transformative outcomes and unpacking relational dy namics that are central to youth organizing: 1) fac ilitating critical dialogue, 2) sharing power, and 3) building a collective identity. Below, I use these relational practices as an analytical framework to conceptualize the cases.

Facilitating critical dialogue A confrontation between two youth members on the co uncil opened dialogue about the process for accepting or rejecting members and the council’s communication  practices. Zia, co-founder and a key leader of the council, had been texting a member, Kaleb, for his ETA to join the meeting at the office. Kaleb continued to delay for two hours sending messages about “bad traffic” and later sent a photo as evidence. Using an internet search tool,  Zia traced the photo back to a generic stock image. Zia expressed deep dismay at Kaleb’s dishonesty and called him, asking him to “just be honest” and “stop lying” (Field Notes, 11/2022). A second incident brought things to a head. As part  of their campaign work, council members engaged in door knocking and phone banking. One member, Dav on, reported seeing Kaleb at the opposing candidate ’s office phone banking for them and, on another day, holding the opponent’s campaign signs outside of th e local library. At the start of the next meeting, with Kaleb tuned in through a video call (camera off), Zia asked Kaleb whether these reports were true. He denied it repeatedly. Finally, Zia told Kaleb that he needed to “take a break” and return when he was “ready to be more honest” (Field Notes, 11/2022). Sarah, an adult who was present at this meeting, conside red stepping in to mediate between Zia and Kaleb, but hesitated since she felt that she neither had enough facts about the situation nor the relational context to confidently intervene. Unaware that Zia had decided to confront  Kaleb at the start of the next meeting, Sarah felt caught off-guard. She continued to observe and listen to the interaction trying to make sense of how the youth handled conflict amongst themselves.  After Zia ’s confrontation, Kaleb abruptly left the meeting.  Sarah then initiated a conversation with the group about the p rocess of how people joined, how the council handle d this confrontation, and how they might handle future conflicts within the council. Sharing power Sharing power involves a fundamental shift in assum ptions of youth capacity and adult privilege (Malor ni et al., 2022). After one of his first phone call meetings w ith the council, Azha told Sarah that he was “not impressed” (Field Notes, 12/09/2022) with how the meeting went. Sarah, on the other hand, had shared that she was “very impressed” with the youth who had come to the meeti ng with an agenda, an icebreaker activity, and clea r action items (Field Notes, 12/09/2022). Azha was frustrated and expressed how he fe lt like the mission of the council was not clear: “What are their whys?” (Azha, Field Notes, 12/09/2022). Later, Azha would share his desire to get more “context” for the council, especially after ex pressing feelings that he was “thrust into somethin g that was already happening” (Field Notes, 01/10/2022).

The youth on the council  had prior relationships and experiences organizing together before the adult facilitators joined. This pre-existing context  created some frustration  for Azha, who tried to facilitate a conversation with the group to move toward collaborative goal setting (Malorni et al., 2022). Azha’s sensemaking around existing relationships, and attempts to build relationships, galvanized around his request to hear “whys”. Because the shared mission of the council was not clear or articulated in a statement, Azha’a assumptions about their capacity shaped his dismissive view of youth practices to run meetings. To work toward shared power, the council would need clear and meaningful goals linke d to collaboration, participation, values, and expe ctations (Malorni et al., 2022, p.6). Without an articulated  mission statement, Azha struggled to make sense of his role in the council. He stopped attending meetings soon after.

Building a collective identity Developing a collective identity “supports the forg ing of solidarity across social struggles and the p ractice of sharing power” (Malorni et al., 2022, p.8). The same conversation that Azha attempted to open with the group around their “whys” was also a move to build a  collective identity. In one of his first meetings with the council, Azha asked: “So I’m curious to hear what your whys are–why are you doing this work?” (Field Notes, 12/09/2022). Azha’s interest in hearing youth share their “whys” was an invitation for youth to begin articulating a collective mission as a council. This move to facilitate discussion around “whys” demonstrates one of the ways Azha tried to make sense of, and support, the council’s move toward a collective identity. In a phone call later, Azha told Sarah how frustrated he was with the meetings because the  council failed to respond with a clear, collective “why” and there was no written mission statement  (Field Notes, 09/2022). For Azha, the connection between “whys” and the materialization of a mission statement felt necessary for establishing a collective identity. A written statement was one of the ways  Azha made sense of the  council’s collective identity. In the same conversation, both Sarah and Azha  wondered about their roles and wanted to ask the yo uth directly what they “would like” from them (Field Notes, 08/09/2022).

## Discussion

The cases presented illustrate the relational labor and processes that precede, and are essential to, YPAR. More specifically, this work seeks to illuminate the sensemaking around facilita tion for adults. First, building relationships with youth while trying to facilitate critical dialogue, share power, and develop a collective identity are deeply interconnected and mutually dependent. This labor resists linear timelines and agendas and does not engage the same structures that might be found in YPAR project s established in formal settings such as schools. Azha’s sensemaking around his role on the council was m arked by his feelings that he had  “entered something that was already in motion”. His attempt to make sense of relationships and solid arities within the council led to frustration, which he tried to work through by facilitating a critical discussion toward a mission statement  and collective identity.

Second, simultaneously building and reading relationships engendered moments of hesitation and uncertainty that made adult intervention unclear. This was salient when Zia confronted Kaleb about honesty and communication. After Zia’s confrontation, Sarah attempted to facilitate a dialogue about expectatio ns around communication for the group. However, she still felt uncertain about her role as a facilitator and cited a desire for more relational context. She felt that because youth members knew each other from school and other  organizing spaces, they might hold more  trust during moments of critical intervention. Given youths’ prior experiences organizing with each other, future work might examine youth to youth relationships and how youth facilitate. Third, not only did the adult sensemaking about relationships to youth differ, but each adult engaged in different facilitative moves in response to tension s. Azha read the youth meetings as “unimpressive” a nd his frustration with the “whys” and the absence of a mission statement led him to stop attending meetings. Sarah read the same practices as youth as modeling key skills necessary for building relationships such as icebre akers and check-in questions. The different sensemaking that unfolded shaped  how they subsequently chose to engage or disengage from the group. A case like this requires further analysis to consider how race, gender, class, and ability shape facilitative moves. As Malorni et al. (2022) share, “…all youth development settings are not the same, and facilitators should pay attention to what makes each domain unique” (p. 9).

## Conclusions and implications

The council is a space for youth to build on their prior organizing experience  while attending to local issues in Ridgeview West. YPAR is a promising approach to bring organizing,  research, and policy together for social transformation. This paper offers three cases that demonstrate how relational labor and processes in an organizing group are necessary for carrying out YPAR even before an “official” YPAR project is articulated. Malorni et al.’s

(2022) identification of three relational practices is a u seful framework for conceptualizing relational labor and

facilitation in the council. What becomes evident is  not only how adults wrestled with their roles, but when and how they attempt to facilitate critical dialogue, build relationships with youth, make sense of collective identity, and share power. Facilitative moves in organizing spaces, whether engaged by adults or youth, require closer and careful attention.

The relational practices surfaced in council interactions resulted in varying consequences reflected in the dis/engagement of youth and adults (e.g. Azha and Kaleb did not re turn). It also demonstrates how youth and adults respond to opportunities  to co-construct meaning and relationships. In other words, it  highlights how learning happens organically as the work is unfolding—what Kirshner (2015) calls “just in time learning”.  What gets taken up by the group, when, and how are all part of opportunistic learning. In their conversation, Sarah and Azha wondered about their roles and wanted to ask the yo uth directly what they “would like ” from them (Field Notes, 08/09/2022). This explicates some of the sensemaking that both a dults engaged and the kinds of supports they envisioned the council needed. Whether materia l (mission statement) or more facilitative, they decided to directly ask the youth, suggesting an attempt to share, rather than assume, power. This work sits in the gray area s of relationship building and facilitation that not only shape broader relational dynamics but can serve to expand or restrict collective orientations and learning in organizing sp aces. Youth organizing through the lens of YPAR highlight s the complex facilitative moments  that surface when engaging in vital relational work. Existing scholarship has demonstrated the numerous benefits of YPAR on youth development and learning, yet the theories of facilitating YPAR within political organizing is underdeveloped: particularly when it comes to facilitative moves by  adults and youth. I attempt to expand these litera tures and sharpen theories that contribute to liberatory education. Lastly, t his work should provoke further discussion around how scholars migh t attend to relational tensions as moments to better understand organizing spaces with youth as an important form of intergenerational learning and joint work (Vossoughi et al., 2021). Consequently, this holds implications for how YPAR is designed, and designed in the context of organizing with youth, and broader scholarship on liberatory pedagogies.
