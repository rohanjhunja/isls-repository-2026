# Mathematics Teachers Framing of Relational Interactions

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Research on teacher noticing has theorized how teachers attend to student thinking, but less on how teachers attend to relational interactions. This article draws on frameworks from equitable mathematics instruction (Battey & Neal, 2018) and teacher noticing (Louie et al., 2021) to infer teachers’ framing of relational classroom interactions and its connection to teachers’ attention to, interpretations of, and responses to students. We analyze noticing interview data from 11 secondary mathematics teachers who participated in a research collaborative aimed at theorizing noticing for equity. We infer teachers’ framing by capturing how they interpret and make connections to broader principles of teaching and learning when attending to various relational interactions. Findings suggest that teachers can attend to similar relational events, but interpret and characterize their responses in ways that suggest different types of framing.

## Introduction

The historical, political, and racialized nature of  schooling shapes mathematics teachers' noticing (L ouie, 2018; Louie et al., 2021). However, teacher noticing has largely been studied from a cognitive perspective (Santagata et al., 2021) and heavily prio ritized teachers’ attention to students’ mathematic al thinking (König et al., 2022). Broadly speaking, research on teacher noticing has only recently begun to theorize the relational dime nsion of classroom interactions (Kang, 2022 Mendez & van Es, under review). Thus, we build on this work and incorporate sociopolitcal perspectives of teacher noticing to a cknowledge and center the importance of framing tha t shapes teachers’ noticing. Specifically, we situate this study within the teacher noticing literature to begin theorizing how teachers’ framing of relational interactions relates to teachers’ attention to, interpretation of, and characterizations of instructional responses. The following question guides this study: How is framing connected to mathematics teachers’ noticing of relational interactions?

Theoretical framing We ground this study by bringing together two lines  of research. First, we consider math education res earch that emphasizes the importance of relational interaction s in a classroom. Second, we draw on sociopolitical perspectives of teacher noticing literat ure to theorize how teachers’ framing shapes how th ey attend to and interpret relational interactions.

The relational dimension of mathematics classrooms Mathematics education research has become increasin gly interested in the relational aspects of the cla ssroom (Eligio, 2017; Roth & Walshaw, 2019). Historically, relational dimensions of mathematics classrooms have been conceptualized from psychological perspectives (Martínez-Sierra & García-González, 2017) to support students’ academic achievement (Hamre & Pianta, 2007). More r ecently, critical and equity-minded scholars have begun conceptualizing relational dimensions of the classroom from sociopolitical lenses to promote joy (Gutiérrez, 2017) well-being (Kokka, 2019) and mathematical identities (Gutiérrez, 2018) for racially and culturally diverse students (Bartell, 2011; Battey & Neal, 2017). However, this  is not to dismiss the importance of academic rigo r.  A case study of a white female teacher in a classroom with  mostly Latinx students found that a third of relat ional interactions were positive (Battey, 2013). Notably,  this teacher maintained a rigorous mathematics cur riculum while attending to relational interactions. Thus, relational interactions are not meant to replace academic content, but rather to theorize novel approaches to equitable mathematics instruction. Research on equitable instructional practices in ma thematics also includes how students experience the classroom as a supportive, inclusive space (Nasir et al., 2021). For example, research advocates for teach ers to create learning opportunities that draw  on students’ experiences, positions them as capable, and draws on their cultural worlds (Bartell et al., 2017; Rubel, 2017). Connecting to students’ cultural identities can h elp teachers promote positive relationships and enable students to feel that they  are valued contributors of mathematics. For example, Newcomer (2018) documents Latinx students’ increased sense of safety and support when their teachers understood the process of learning a second languag e. These findings suggest that teachers’ awareness  and interpretations of students’ emotions and identitie s can shape how teachers enact practices to promote  positive relational interactions.

To incorporate relational elements into equitable m athematics instruction, Kokka (2022) theorized teachers’ affective pedagogical goals for social justice mathematics. Through the proposed framework of affective pedagogical goals, Kokka (2022) outlines ways of “c entering relationships, healing, and student and co mmunity wellness” (pg. 136) that considers the structural o ppression imposed by schools and frames students’  negative emotional responses as normal responses to existing  harmful environments. This theory draws on caring with awareness (Bartell, 2011), which alludes to the tea cher noticing literature by considering the role of  teahcer’s thoughts and attention to students’ whole selves. O verall, this body of research in mathematics educat ion with a growing emphasis towards theorizing the importance of relational interactions has coincided with the sociopolitical shift in teacher noticing research.

Teacher noticing of the relational dimension We turn to the teacher noticing literature to theorize the relationship between framing and how teachers attend to, interpret, and characterize their instructional practices related to the relational dimensions of classrooms. First, we acknowledge initial constructs put forth by the teacher noticing research, attending and interpreting (van Es & Sherin, 2002; 2008). Attending refers to what teachers observe (student behavior, body language, loud talking, etc.). Research suggests th at relational interactions are a significant compon ent of what teachers attend to (Kang, 2022). The most common of these events include: communicating classroom norms and acceptable behaviors, reassuring progress to motiva te students, and incorporating students’ interest i n the work. Interpreting refers to how teachers make sense of what they attend to. How teachers interpret is also consequential for the types of events that they attend to (Crespo, 2000). To illustrate how these two constructs  appear in practice, we draw on a case documenting a mathematics teacher s’ noticing towards relational interactions (van Es  et al., 2022). Raymond, a math teacher, attends to how stud ents feel during playful interactions that he initi ates. He interprets that thes e interactions can help create a more family-like classroom and that learning about students’ interests can help him redirect student behavior. W e consider these dimensions of noticing important f or how teachers interpret relational interactions.

Second, we theorize the importance of teachers’ making connections between specific events and broader principles of teaching and learning  (van Es & Sherin, 2008). For example, teachers can learn to recognize interesting moments within their teaching that are representative of issues they are committed to inve stigating. For instance, van Es & Sherin (2008) share how a novice teacher, Daniel, highlighted to a researcher videotaping his classroom that “This is a great clip for the vi deo club meeting”. This t eacher’s call out of a specific event in his class points to his understanding of what const itutes noteworthy classroom interactions that can b e leveraged for group discussions. Thus, we consider how teachers’ narration of their classroom practices can help illuminate their noticing patterns.

Third, we incorporate the sociopolitical perspectiv e of noticing research that considers how framing shapes noticing. We draw on Hand and colleagues (2013) who discuss how a “[frame] sets the scene, defining the characters, what counts as ‘rational’ action, and even the means by which people might accompli sh particular purposes” (pg. 253). To illustrate this point, we c an consider a well known scene (a birthday party) t hat entails certain acts (giving presents), actors (a celebrate d person and loved ones), that culminates in an exp ected action (singing a celebratory song). However, even well-known scenes have significant cultural differences. For instance, a Mexican tradition after singing a happy birthday song involves guests chanting for the birthday person to take a bite from the cake. Then, as the birthday person ta kes a bite of the cake, one or more guests might at tempt to shove the birthday person’s face into the cake (Val e, 2003). While normalized in Mexico, this behavior  might rupture the frame of ‘ra tional’ action for a birthday celebration anywhere else. We utilize this example to raise questions about relational interactions in mathematics classrooms and teachers’ affective framing (Sherin & Russ, 2014). For example, how and when are student conver sations during mathematical activities considered ‘rationale’? How do teachers interpret such convers ations? During interactions, actors bring their own  frame to the event (Hand et al., 2013). For instance, a teac her might have a classroom management frame that le ads their noticing to expect students to be quiet. Another teacher might hold a different frame, that which views classrooms as spaces for collaborative learning, thus leading to different expectations. We then take framing to mean the process by which teachers apply frames to specific classroom interactions. Specifically, we are concer ned with inferring teachers’ framing from their patterns of noticing (Russ & Luna, 2013). Framing is an important addition to noticing as it brings attention to the sociopolitical and relational nature of classrooms. These four constructs are summarized in the Framing-Attending-Interpreting-Responding (FAIR) framework (Louie et al., 2021). However, we concept ualize teachers’ responses as separate from their n oticing, and have instead theorized the importance of teachers’ making connections to broader principles of teaching and learning by focusing on teachers’ narration of their instruc tional practices. Nonetheless, the FAIR framework i s useful for bringing attention to how framing is heavily tied to the dominant narratives and that these are embedded within schooling as an institution and (Rubin & van Es, 2023; Shah, 2017; Zavala & Hand, 2019). This research brings attention to how narratives influence which students are assigned mathematical competence, how competence is characterized, and documents the perv asive racialized nat ure of dominant ideologies in schools. Therefore, we theorize that teachers’ noticing patt erns, as documented through noticing interviews, ca n help illuminate their framing. Specifically, we focus on the teachers’ framing of relational interactions.

## Study context

This study draws on data from a collaborative profession al development program situated in the Western Unit ed States consisting of university-based researchers, secondary mathematics teachers, and community-based youth leaders to theorize teacher noticing for equity. We  use data collected from two cohorts of teacher par ticipants. The first cohort consisted of four mathematics teachers and the second cohort consisted of nine teachers. However, two teachers participated in both cohorts for a total of 11 teachers (all of whom are given pseudonyms). The first cohort of teachers were identified as advancing equitable instruction by their school district leaders.

## Participants and data sources

Researchers from this collaborative observed and vi deo recorded each teachers’ classroom between three  to six times. Within a week after the observation, the res earcher who conducted the observation then conducte d a noticing interview, which seeks to elicit reasoning processes that dr ive observable actions in the classroom episodes (Hunting, 1997). For these interviews, the  researchers selected segments for the teachers to watch and discuss. These segments were chosen by the research ers based on where they saw teachers’ enacting affirm ing and equitable instructional practices (Bartell et a l., 2017). Most of these video segments were about one minute long, but some extended to be a few minutes. Due to  time constraints, teachers typically  responded to 2-6 segments. However, researchers also welcomed teache rs to identify and discuss moments of teaching that  they found noteworthy. After showing a clip from the tea chers’ classrooms, researchers asked open-ended questions such as the following: What kinds of things were you paying attention to d uring this interaction? What stood out to you? Why was that important to you? What were yo u feeling in that moment? Given the open-ended nature of the interview questions, teachers often made severa l comments about each video segment. All interviews  lasted about one hour, during which teachers made an avera ge of 9.5 comments per interview. Cohort 1 teachers participated in up to six noticing interviews; these were cond ucted in teachers’ classrooms or at the researchers ’ offices. For Cohort 2 teachers participated in three noticing interviews; these interviews were conducted and video recorded using Zoom.

## Analytic approach

Data analysis was qualitative in nature and followe d several phases. The first phase of analysis drew on two dimensions of noticing-attending and interpreting-to generate a list of what Cohort 1 teachers notice d in response to each clip and how they interpreted what they saw. We also included a dimension of teachers’ narration of their instructional response, to examine how tea chers’ tied specific events to broader issues of te aching and learning (van es & Sherin, 2008). For this first ph ase, we utilized three noticing interviews for each  of the four teachers. Our unit of analysis were the comments ma de by teachers about a particular topic, student, o r instructional practice after watching video clips from their classroom instruction (Jacobs et al., 1997). Each video segment elicited between one and six comments. From  this initial coding, we created a coding framework  of six relational dimensions. This included the following six dimensions: addressing behavior, framing mathem atics ability, acknowledging student contributions, atten ding to culture and language, and setting the emoti onal tone, teachers’ attention to their own emotions (Battey & Neal, 2018). The second phase of data analysis involved coding t hree additional interviews from Cohort 1 teachers using the six dimensions of relational interactions. The first author coded all independent comments u sing the relational interactions coding framework described in the previous phase and then created data matrice s to summarize and categorize teachers’ noticing by the three noticing dimensions outlined above. We assign ed one of these six codes to each comment if they discussed relational interactions in either the attending or interpreting aspects of their noticing. Comments that were not related to one of these six dimensions were excluded from this analysis. This resulted in dropping between 20 and 50% of comments across Cohort 1 teachers. Phase three involved coding an additional interview  from each teacher in Cohort 2 and writing analytic memos about teachers’ overlapping and distinct noti cing practices related to relational interactions (Miles et al., 2014). Using these analytic memos, the first author  created six themes related to teachers’ attention to relational events. To ensure that emergent themes were grounded in the data, we then returned to the data to connect different teachers’ comments onto our five themes (Nowell et al., 2017). Finally, the first author returned to the data matrix and analytic memo for each teacher and inferred a teachers’ most common noticing pattern, that is, similarities in noticing throughout interview(s).

## Discussion

This study builds upon a growing body of literature that theorizes teacher noticing from a sociopolitcal lens (Louie, 2018; Louie et al., 2021) and calls on teacher noticing research to consider the relational dimensions of classrooms. While we list six such categories of relational events that teachers at tended to in Table 1, this is by no means an exhaustive list of all that teachers can and do att end to. Similar to Kang (2021) that found a science  teacher was attentive to the relational dimension of their classrooms in 67% classroom events, teachers in this study attended to relational events in every noticing interview and at similar percentages. Our findings recognize th at despite attending to similar relational events, teachers ca n make different interpretations. Based on these ca ses and the broader sociopolitcal noticing literature, we argue  that teachers’ framing can shape how teachers inte rpret such relational events.

We approach this work through a noticing lens, and contribute that teachers’ noticing is tied to the framing of teachers’ expectations (Sherrin & Russ, 2014). Work on teachers’ aspirations to improve cla ssroom interactions suggests that teachers understand  the importance of providing emotional support, but can lack the knowledge to support students (Solheim, 2019). The current study found that teachers not only understa nd the need to support students’ emotionally, but also hav e a deep understanding of how t raditional mathematics classrooms affect students, students’ relational hi stories and how their own dispositions towards emot ions all shape how they interact with students. Along with a  desire to maintain positive relationships with stu dents, our findings suggest that teachers opted to re-interpret behavior that might otherwise be consider ed disruptive and could lead to escalating conflict and negative emot ions. This work expands research on teacher noticin g by describing how teacher’s recognize relational interactions as a necessary component for cultivati ng classroom spaces where students can engage and learn.

We note the impact that teachers’ noticing can have on students. For instance, Leslie’s decision to leave a part of who she is outside of the classroom reifi es the dominant narrative that frames her interpret ations and leaves less opportunities for studen ts to witness their teacher bring in their full sel ves into the classrooms. This can have implications for students wishing to expre ss their non-mathematical identities, but worry it may not be appropriate.

Additionally, the findings of our study have implic ations for frameworks that attend to the relational interactions between teachers and students. The dat a from our study suggest that a teacher's own emoti ons and well-being is strongly tied to their noticing. Yet, existing frameworks of equitable mathematics instruction do not capture this dimension. The adage “You can’t pour f rom an empty cup” reflects the necessity of attendi ng to teachers' well-being in cultivating positive interactions with stu dents. Indeed, the literature points to the heavy emotional toll of mathematics teaching (Chang, 2009; Wang et al., 2019). Thus, incorporating teacher w ellbeing into frameworks of relational interactions could sh ed light as to how and why student-teacher relationsh ips develop and for which students.

## Limitations

The teachers in our sample were never explicitly as ked to share their views on dominant narratives abo ut mathematics. We infer their framing based on their local patterns of noticing across multiple intervie ws (Russ & Luna, 2013). Bringing in our teacher-participants’ perspectives could further our understanding of their framing. Additionally, there remains the question of how tea chers’ beliefs and implicit biases shape their noti cing. However, these constructs were not  within the scope of this study given the aim of inc orporating a more sociopolitical lens, which considers the role of framing. Future directions This study’s methodological approach (noticing inte rviews) allowed us deep insights into teachers’ att ending, interpreting, and narration of instructional practi ces to infer framing. Future research can integrate  classroom observation data to nuance how te achers’ noticing is related to their instructional practices, thereby allowing a further examination of the relationship between noticing and instructional practice.
