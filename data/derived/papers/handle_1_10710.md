# Everyday Entry Points to Visual-spatial Thinking: Implications from a Thai Farmer’s Reservoir Planning

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Visual-spatial skills underlay reasoning in a range of domains, making their development of continued interest. While a range of computer-based spatial reasoning platforms have been created recently, they remain inaccessible to many learners in the Global South and may supplant the real-world problem-solving work through which spatial reasoning was historically developed. We present a narrative vignette of a Thai farmer who developed paper-and-pencil representations to solve a spatial reasoning task in her village. Drawing on theories of how representation guides cognition, we document how this farmer’s representations enabled her consequential work and shaped it in interesting ways. This example could inform educational design and facilitation efforts to offer life-relevant activities to develop visual-spatial skills.

## Motivation and background

Visual-spatial skills underlay an array of professional practices across STEAM fields (e.g., Palmiero & Srinivasan, 2015; Wai et al., 2009). Research also indicates th at spatial reasoning skills are trainable and trans ferable (Uttal et al., 2013). Thus, recent decades have seen growt h in interest, curricula, and techniques to support  learners in developing their visual-spatial skills. Some current approaches to improvin g visual-spatial skills embrace computational platforms (e.g., Hohenwarter & Hohenw arter, 2002; Platonic Games, 2017, 3D modeling (e.g., Sketchup, 2000; Tinkercad, 2011) or virtual/augmented reality technologies (e.g., Baumgartner et al., 2022; Supli & Yan, 2023). While these designs enable learners to manipulate objects, explore their spatial properties, and even be immersed within them, they remain out of reach f or many of the world’s learners, over 30% of whom s till do not have consistent internet access (ITU, 2022). A technocentric approach to developing visual-spatial thinking also overloo ks the real-world problem-solving contexts through which visual-spatial thinking was historically developed.

We share an example of how a Thai farmer developed visual-spatial representations to address challenges in her everyday life, in this case, estimating reservoir volumes for a community water management project. Using artifact analysis of her written representations, process description, and member-checking, we document how she represented practical problems in ways she could so lve mathematically. We also document how those representations shaped her thinking about those pro blems, at times in conflict with normative mathematics. We suggest this emergent example contributes to the repertoire of approachable, life-relevant visual-spatial reasoning problems.

We base the analysis of our focal farmer’s visualization and calculation process in theories of distributed cognition (1). Based on the ideas of Lev Vygotsky (1978) and the sociocultural tradition, Hutchins (20 00) and colleagues developed theories of distributed cognit ion, which maintain that thinking occurs not just i n the individual mind but in partnership with other people and external objects. We suggest that the trend within spatial reasoning toward high-tech tools and platforms acknowledges this persp ective, if tacitly. Within work on distributed cognition, Kirsh (2010) especially emph asizes the roles of external representations-words, numbers, pictures, graphics-in assisting, expanding, and shaping cognition. Acc ording to Kirsh, working with exter nal representations reduces the cost of cognition, enab ling us to do more in less time than we could other wise do ‘in our heads.’ Among other functions, external represe ntations “facilitate re-representation; … they facilitate the computation of more explicit encoding of informatio n; … they help coordinate thought” (2010, p. 441). Guided by these constructs, we explore: How does an adult novice represent and reason about  a spatial reasoning problem arising from her everyday life?

## Methods

## Participant and local context

In 1997, Saijai, a self-described “illiterate villager” then in her mid-30s, joined the constructionist educational efforts of the Suksapattana Foundation (SF). With remarkable grit and mentorship tailored to her everyday needs (from basic financial literacy to long-term planning), Saijai transformed herself over the course of 20 years from a housewife farmer at the whims of weather to a com munity leader marshaling efforts for sustainable wa ter management.

Saijai lives in Tonya village in the Buriram provin ce of Thailand, about a 4-hour drive northeast of Bangkok in a region where poor soil quality and sparse irrigation make farming a challenge. Most Buriram farmers migrate to cities during the non-growing season to support their families (World Ban k Group, 2022). In community discussions, Tonya villagers reflected on their situation, realizing “that our main problem was we lack water resources for consumption and agriculture” (Saijai, 2017). After two years of local research, Saijai learned there was actually enough water during the rainy se ason but no way to save it for the dry season. Thus  began the 15+ year-long “80 Pansa Klong” water management project (“kl ong” is Thai for canal), an effort to construct dams, canals, and reservoirs to achieve year-round water access. This water management project is the context for our focal volume calculation.

## Data collection and analysis

Data was collected as part of a larger research project on Thai Constructionism (Blikstein & Fields, 2021) during research visits to Tonya village in 2017, 2018, and 2019. We interviewed Saijai during each visit, and during our third interview, we inquired more deeply about practical math and science issues  related to her leadership of the water management project. Asked specifically about how she estimates reservoir volume, Saijai retrieve d a notebook where she had recorded journal entries, diagrams, and calculations, which we photographed. After some months of studying her broader learning trajectory through both artifact analysis and thematic coding of her interview transcripts, we returned to this volume calculation. Sipitakiat, who is a native Thai speaker, spoke with Saijai by phone to verify our understanding of her approach as a form of member checking. This microge netic and triangulated artifact analysis yielded the vign ette presented below, which draws on interview tran scripts, photographs, multiple rounds of analysis captured in researcher memos, member-checking, and comparisons with standard mathematical volume calculations.

## Findings

In illustrating Saijai’s approach to volume calcula tion, we aim to demonstrate how her budding represe ntational skills enabled her spatial reasoning and shaped tha t reasoning in identifiable ways. As Tonya village’ s water management project grew, klong construction exceeded villagers’ capabilities with hand tools and small-scale machinery. To effectively negotiate contracts with professional excavators, Saijai needed to estimate the excavated volume of dirt on which the costs were ba sed. The surface of e ach klong is rectangular, but the sides slope down at an angle to reduce erosion (Figure 1). Saijai needed to account for this slope when calc ulating the volume of dirt to be removed.

Truncated pyramid diagram (bottom right) for reference Figure 1, left, shows Saijai’s cross-sectional diagram of the klong, with a top surface length of 40m and a bottom length of 28m. She used a 1:2 slope ratio, meaning that the slope’s run (its horizontal distance) is 6m on each side. Using the slope of 1:2, a run of 6m gives a depth of 3m (not in her notes). Saijai then multiplied 40m x 28m to give 1120m 2, the surface area without two of the sloped region s, which we interpret as her conception of the dig area. She multiplied this result by the 3m depth to get the 3360m 2 written below (we note her use of m 2 instead of m 3). At a dig price of $18 THB/m 3, the total cost was $60,480 THB (approx. US$ 1800 in 2023 currency.) Figure 1, right, shows another approach to the same calculation. Starting with a s imilar diagram (the slope’s run indicated but not labeled), Saijai used the 1,6 00m2 (40m x 40m) top surface area as a reference unit. Then she wrote “minus slope 480,” which we infer she calculated as 40m (the surface length) x 6m (the slope run) x 4 (the number of sides) x 0.5 (the slope). The 1, 600m2 surface area minus this 480m 2 slope area is 1120m 2 “remaining soil” to be dug. Multiplying this dig ar ea by the 3m depth gave a dig volume of 3360m 2, as in h er approach on the left.

Describing where she learned her techniques, Saijai said: I learned from a backhoe driver. … For example, if my reservoir is 40 x 40 or 40 x 20,  what will the bottom area be? I noted down what he taught me to calculate in cubic meters. … From here I will get the dimension for this. This one is  28 meters. [pointing to the calculations] This is 40 meters. Then you need to deduct the slope out. And you will get the dimension of the bottom then divide by 2. But I cannot calculate the number now… I noted down in the notebook. By her own description, Saijai was not entirely com fortable performing the calculation (“I cannot calc ulate the number now”) and relied on her written notes (“I no ted down in the notebook”). We infer that Saijai’s diagrams enabled visualization, representation, and mathemat ical operation on reservoirs in a way otherwise out side her grasp.

We also note how Saijai’s diagrams shape d her conceptualization of reservoirs in interesting ways. Her 2-dimensional drawings depict the reservoir’s inverted, truncated pyramid shape i n cross-section (Figure 1), clearly capturing the surface and bottom dimensions as well as the sl ope and its run. Her diagrams don’t capture as well the geometry of the reservoir’s corners. Perhaps as a result, her calculation of the slope dig area (Figure 1, right) accounted for the sloped sides (480m2 = 40m x 6m x 4m x 0.5m) but double counted each corner (see Figure 2). Accordingly, her result is slightly less than t hat given by the canonical mathematical formula for  the volume of a truncated pyramid (3,504m 3 for a reservoir of those dimensions), an error of o nly-4.1%. As Saijai used this technique tonegotiate dozens of contracts, it clea rly worked well enough for her purposes. It enabled  the construction of dozens of reservoirs, securing both year-round water for Tonya village and Saijai’s leadersh ip position in the water  management project, an especially remarkable role given the typically gendered responsibility for water in Buriram (Andajani et al., 2015). ## Discussion and implications

Our vignette of a rural Thai farmer’s volume calculation illustrates how representations-here, equations, a crosssectional diagram, and annotations-enabled her depiction of a reservoir’s features in ways that allowed her to operate upon them and shape d her visualization of those features in mathematic ally relevant ways. Through artifact analysis triangulated with interviews, research memos, and member-checking, we documented how Saijai represented and organized the reservoir’s dimension s-its surface and bottom lengths, the run of its slopes, and its depth-to estimate its volume. Consistent with research on  external representations (e.g. Kirsh, 2010), Saija i relied on these inscriptions and stated that she could not perform this work without them. At the same time, again consistent with the literature, these representations pushed back and shaped Saijai’s thinking in meaningful ways; her 2-dimensional diagrams obscured the corners’ precise spatial configuration. Though the repercussions for Saijai’s contract negotiations are minimal, we argu e this dynamic is a helpful reminder for designers of visualspatial tools that the particulars of any representation can reflect back into learners’ thinking about the represented concept in unanticipated ways.

We also argue that Saijai’s reservoir volume calcul ation serves as a useful example to think with in formulating meaningful opportunities for visual-spatial reasoning, regardless of learners’ access t o computing technology. Using only pen and paper, Saijai  successfully represented and reasoned about a compl ex spatial reasoning task that both draws from and contributes back to her everyday context. In contrast to common reactions to school mathematics (e.g., math phobia, low math self-efficacy), Saijai’s relationship with acquiring and using her mathematical knowledge was one of pride; becaus e her geometry knowledge solved a real problem, the elaborate mathematical inscriptions in her notebook  were a useful and prized possession. Echoing other researchers’ claims for the value of everyday activity as a founda tion for mathematical learning (e.g., Nunes & Bryant, 1996), our findings suggest that offering s uch meaningful mathematical contexts for adult lear ners and children alike could provide similarly productiv e entry points to challenging mathematics skills su ch as visual-spatial reasoning.
