# Sources of Difficulties in Reading Comprehension in Adults With ADHD

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Academic success is highly dependent on students’ ability to comprehend written texts. Students with Attention Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD) often struggle with reading comprehension, but the source of their difficulties is unclear. The current study compares reading comprehension, as well as linguistic and cognitive skills in adults with and without ADHD. Results show worse reading comprehension in adults with ADHD and an association between reading comprehension and sustained attention. Implications for reading comprehension instruction are discussed.

## Introduction

Academic success is highly dependent on students’ ability to read and comprehend written texts. Individuals with Attention Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD) oft en struggle academically (Barkley, 2000), and readi ng comprehension deficits, although not considered a p rimary deficit in ADHD, may contribute to these aca demic difficulties. It is currently unclear whether individuals with ADHD have reading comprehension difficulties and what underlies these difficulties if they exist. So me studies found that children and adults with ADHD  have reading comprehension difficulties (Brock & Knapp, 1996; Samuelsson et al., 2004; Stern & Shalev, 2013), while others presented inconclusive findings (Ghelani et al., 2004). These inconsistencies may be related to individual differences in readers with ADHD, as well as to the  tools that assess reading comprehension. Reading comprehension may vary as a function of cognitive and linguistic skills. In addition, the texts that assess reading comprehension differ in length, complexity, the deg ree to which they are meaningful and engaging, and their attentional demands, thus posing differential challenges to individuals with attention deficits. The type of reading comprehension questions can also affect the findings.

Most studies on reading comprehension in ADHD focu s on identification of difficulties in reading comprehension. Little is known, however, about the nature of these difficulties. Reading comprehension  is an active process in which the reader simultaneously e xtracts and constructs meaning from the written tex t, thus creating connections between the ideas presented in the text, and forming a coherent mental representation of the general message (Graesser et al., 1994). This process involves linguistic and cognitive abilities (e.g., vocabulary, memory, and attention), motivation (e.g., purpose o f reading and interest in the content), and knowled ge (e.g., topic knowledge and knowledge of comprehension strategies; Snow, 2002). Other critical components of reading comprehension are meta-comprehension, a special cas e of metacognition, and self-regulation. Metacomprehension is the knowledge that readers have about their own comprehension (Flavell, 1979; Poissant, 2005). Self-regulation is the experiences, feelings, and thoughts that occur during reading, or any other ongoing cognitive activity. These experiences give individuals an int ernal feedback about their comprehension and allow them to use strategies to solve comprehension problems. Readers who are less confident in their comprehension may use more strategies to compensate for their poor understanding (Temelman-Yogey et al., 2020). Individuals with ADHD have deficits in executive f unctioning (Pennington et al., 1993), and these deficits can affect each of the skills necessary for reading comprehension. For example, students with ADHD can struggle with remembering the text, with maintainin g constant level of focus (i.e., sustained attentio n) during reading (Stern & Shalev, 2013), or with monitoring comprehension. Indeed, reading abilities of student s with ADHD are often technical, with no deep semantic processing, and students may read an entire chapter but have no idea about its content (Levine, 1987). Students with ADHD may also be less motivated than students without ADHD, due to their earlier negative experience with  reading comprehension. Understanding the cognitive mechanisms underlying reading comprehension will he lp create better assessment tools, and will help re fine research-based instruction methods to promote the development of proficient comprehenders (Snow, 2002). The current study aimed to compare reading comprehension in students with and without ADHD and to characterize the source of reading comprehension difficulties in students with ADHD, if they exist.

## Participants

Fifty-nine students from the Open University of Israel took part in an on-line study in exchange for course credit. Twenty-five participants (19 women, M age = 28.04 years, SD = 4.70) comprised the ADHD group, and 34 participants (19 women; M age = 27.10 years, SD = 4.51) comprised the control group. All participants were born ICLS2022 Proceedings 921 © ISLS in Israel, they were native Hebrew speakers, and they reported no developmental or learning disabilities besides ADHD. The groups differed neither in age, t (57) =.78, p =.442, nor in years of education, t (57) =.22, p =.813. Tasks Neuropsychological testing Participants answered some basic demographic questi ons and then completed a continuous conjunctive performance test (CCPT) to measure their sustained attention. In the CCPT, participants were presented with 320 geometric shapes and were asked to press the space bar when the target (a red square) appeared. The st andard deviation of reaction times was used as a measure o f the ability to keep a constant level of performan ce during the task, so that large SDs reflect poor sustained attention (Tsal et al., 2005). After completing the  CCPT, participants performed the reading task, followed b y an immediate recall task (Segal et al., 2019), as  well as a Hebrew vocabulary test (Kavé et al., 2022). In the immediate recall task, participants named 12 pictures and were then asked to recall the names of the pictures. The memory score is the percent of items recalled correctly. In the vocabulary task, participants were asked to choose the correct meaning of 12 infrequent Hebrew words o ut of four alternatives. The vocabulary score is the percent of correct responses. Next, participants provided some more background information regarding their development and their language history, and then they rated the level to which seven ADHD symptoms characterized their behav ior on a 1 (never) to 7 (very often) scale (Konfort es, 2010).

Reading tasks Each participant read one of two informational text s: a text about the Taj Mahal (417 words) or a text  about Babylon (426 words). Participants were asked to rea d the text and then to rate their comprehension ("h ow well did you understand the text on a scale of 1 (did no t understand) to 4 (understood perfectly), as well as to judge their learning ("what are the chances that you will  answer comprehension questions correctly on a scal e of 25100%?"). Then, they were presented with seven multiple-choice questions about the text. The accuracy score is the percent of correct answers to these seven questions.

## Discussion

The present study explored whether adults with ADHD show difficulties in reading comprehension compared to age-matched students without ADHD, as well as the cognitive underpinnings of these difficulties. Participants with ADHD reported higher levels of AD HD symptoms, they had significantly lower sustained attention, and their reading comprehensio n was worse relative to aged-matched controls. Part icipants with ADHD also rated the texts that they read as more difficult, and demonstrated lower confidence in their ability to answer comprehension questions thandid participants without ADHD. The two groups did not differ in memory or in vocabulary.

These findings support previous reports of reading comprehension difficulties in adolescents with ADHD (Stern & Shalev, 2013), and suggest that reading co mprehension difficulties persist into adulthood. These difficulties can also explain why students with ADHD often struggle academically (Barkley, 2000). Participants with ADHD have most likely dealt with reading compr ehension difficulties since childhood, thus develop ing decreased confidence and increased awareness of their comprehension abilities over time. Nevertheless, they were probably unable to implement strategies to improve their reading comprehension. Importantly, accuracy scores were not related to t he level of ADHD symptoms, but to sustained attention, suggesting that inability to keep a constant level of focus impairs reading comprehension. Lapses of attention may impede the creation of connections between the ideas presented in the text, thus limiting the ability to understand the general message.

In the current study, there were no associations be tween memory and vocabulary scores and reading comprehension. This does not mean that memory and v ocabulary are not important for reading comprehension. Instead, the findings suggest that these abilities do not suffice for reading comprehension and that s ustained attention also plays a critical role in the process. Assessing sustained attention in poor comprehends will allow us to construct a more personalized educational plan f or each reader according to the reader’s specific c ognitive functions, and to prevent frustration and increase confidence in reading and academic success.
