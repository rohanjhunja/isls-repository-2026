# Unplugged Activities as Opportunities to Enhance Mixed Reality Educational Technology through Personal Connections

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper explores how unplugged activities can enhance learning in mixed-reality (MR) science environments by supporting students in connecting classroom modeling with their lived experiences. Drawing on funds of knowledge (FoK) and embodied modeling learning theories, we analyze a case study of fourth-grade students engaged in a multi-modal ecosystem simulation. Through storytelling and design activities, students integrated personal experiences—such as caring for pets—into the modeling process, shaping how they modified the MR environment and reasoned about ecological systems. We find that unplugged activities can boost engagement by “doubling down” on the immersive, affective strengths of MR or counterbalance its limitations by rooting inquiry in students’ everyday knowledge. However, we also identify tensions when students’ emotional attachments to modeled agents override broader system-level reasoning. This work highlights the importance of designing unplugged activities as intentional mediational tools in socio-technical learning systems.

## Introduction

Classroom technologies do not operate in isolation; instead, they are part of a complex and interconnected sociotechnical system. Each technology carries unique af fordances, enhancing some aspects of the learning environment while leaving other areas relatively unaffected. Effective curriculum and research design, therefore, requires thoughtful consideration of how to capitalize on the strengths of a given technology while also addressing its limitations. In this paper, an implementation of GEM-STEP (Generalized Embodied Modeling Science through Technology Enhanced Play), a mixed reality (MR) technology that allows student s to play-act scientific phenomena, serves as a case study illustrating this  important but often underspecified part of Design-Based Research (T. Anderson & Shattuck, 2012).

One of the key strengths of MR technology, such as GEM-STEP, is its ability to blend first-and thirdperson perspectives. In MR environments, students c an experience scientific phenomena both as active participants (first-person) and as external observers (third-person) (Ayalon et al., 2023; Enyedy et al., 2015). This dual perspective enriches their understanding by gr ounding abstract concepts in lived experience. For example, when simulating an ecosystem—as is the case for this case study—students take on the role of animals who must do certain things in the simulation to “stay alive,” making decisions from their character’s point of view. Students also step back from the action and discuss the rule s of the system, taking an observer’s third-person perspective as well. To take advantage of the blended perspective that results from engaging in these two perspectives, in our unplugged activities towards the end of the unit on  food webs, we asked learners to introduce a species of their own to a MR ecosystem model. This activity was part of an unplugged station called “Notice & Wonder,” where students reflected on their first-person experience within the MR and connected it to their third-person perspective on the scientific phenomena in ecosystems. This act ivity aimed to ‘double down’ on the personal engagemen t gained from the first-person MR experience. By encouraging students to th ink creatively and make meaningful connections between the MR model and their own embodied experience, we sought to deepen their investment in the ecosystem model and reinforce their understanding of complex ecological relationships. However, our MR technology also has limitations. On e significant challenge is providing robust pathways for students to draw upon outside resource s and their Funds of knowledge (FoK) during inquiry  (Moll et al., 2005). While these MR models are highly imm ersive, they often prioritize exploration of the ga mified scientific phenomena, leading students to work out the rules and dynamics of the system, interacting w ith it as a game but not necessarily connecting its embedded sc ience content back to the real wor ld. In addition, without strong experiential knowledge of the modeled phenomena, students may struggle to use their prior experiences to guide their questions and hypotheses.

In our case study, the ecosystem simulation was a s tylized garden involving hawks, snakes, mice, and caterpillars—organisms with which students generally had limited  direct experiences. To address this gap, we introduced an unplugged storytelling station design ed to facilitate the integration of students’ FoK i nto the learning experience. At this station, students crea ted and shared narratives that connected their prio r knowledge and lived experiences with the m odel's ecosystem dynamics. By engaging in storytell ing, students could bring external insights into their inquiry, bridging the gap between the virtual and real worlds and thereby  enriching their learning with the simulation.

This paper highlights the importance of unplugged c urriculum design to complement educational technologies and enhance specific learning goals by  building on the technologies’ affordances and limi tations. Our guiding research question, then, is: How do unplugged activities provide opportunities f or students’ lived experiences to enhance sensemaking and engagement in a mixed-reality simulation of a garden ecosystem?

## Theoretical framework

Perspective-taking in modeling Children and scientists naturally embody the perspe ctives of entities/phenomena they explore and study  (Keifert & Stevens, 2019; Ochs et al., 1996). However, techn ological advances have enabled augmented or MR environments to directly support this kind of perspective-taking. Such environments have demonstrated success in facilitating the learning of science concepts by enabling learners t o merge sensorimotor perceptions with representations and scaffolds in digital displays a nd by fostering collaborative learning opportunitie s (Colella et al., 1998; Enyedy et al., 2015; Lindgren et al., 20 16; Lindgren & Johnson-Glenberg, 2013). When physical and symbolic worlds merge, they create an “in-between” space, or liminal blend, where learners ca n concretize abstract concepts by engaging in embodied reasoning and perspective-taking (Enyedy et al., 2015). Simulations that enable learners to simultaneously embody first-person (character/agent) viewpoints and observe third-person (observer/aggregate) viewpoints are especially helpful in supporting learning about complex systems dynamics, which are notoriously  difficult for students to understand (Jacobson & Wi lensky, 2006). Embodying the role of a component in a complex syst em supports learners in understanding that componen t’s function in relation to other components (Lindgren & DeLeima, 2022). For example, in one study, young children learned about changes in states of matter by embodying water particles in a MR environment in which a projected simulation showed water particle avatars of the chi ldren alongside a “state-meter” of the macro-level state of water. Children experienced holding hands and walking sl owly in a circle as particles and saw that this mad e liquid at the aggregate macro-level represented in the simulation (Keifert et al., 2017). Through these embodied experiences, the children constructed rules for ind ividual and collective actions that were the basis for reasoning about micro-to-macro relations. The blend of first-and third-person perspectives in the environment thus supported an understanding of complex system dynamics.

First-person perspectives in embodied simulations can als o amplify affective experiences of scientific phenomena. For example, in a museum exhibit, first-person perspective-taking enhanced visitors’ experiences and understanding of climate change (Lyons et  al., 2012, 2013). In the exhibit, visitors took the  perspective of polar bears, wearing polar bear gloves with hidden Wiimotes and circling their arms to swim. They felt the increased effort a polar bear must exert to find food in a landscape altered by climate change, where ice floes are farther apart. In another example, in a participato ry simulation of a disease outbreak, students’ feel ings of being the infected population compelled their scientific inquiry (Colella, 2000). Students expressed pride a t b eing healthy (e.g., “I’m the man … that’s right, I’m a c lean head again … You all want to be like me”) and dismay at being sick (e.g., “You don’t feel good when you have the virus” … “It’s a hard thing to deal with”) (Colella, 2000, p. 492). Additionally, young children playing as be es and flowers in an MR simulation of pollination u sed their affective experiences as children playing with their friends to understand the reciprocal relationships in the system (Jen et al., 2021; C. Lee et al., Under Review). While children playing as flowers that were successfully pollinated reveled in their growth, a child playing as a flowe r that could not be pollinated because it was the o nly one of its kind lamented the flower’s death. Furthermore, students who played as bees expressed concern for this lone dying flower, and this concern prompted a further inquiry  into the dynamics of pollination. First-person perspectivetaking may thus facilitate heightened emotional experiences of scientific phenomena, spur empathy for the entities being embodied, and support scientific inquiry.

Funds of knowledge amd storytelling in science Funds of knowledge (FoK) (Moll et al., 2005) was a helpful framework for designing our science curricu lum to support students in building authentic connections to their learning experiences. FoK “refers to different strategies and ways of knowing needed for a household to funct ion effectively. It is based on the notion that eve ryday practices… are sites of knowledge construction and that these resources can be brought into the classr oom” (García & Kleifgen, 2018, p. 164). While early FoK research was done with teacher educators visiting multilingual families in their homes, science education has cons idered FoK as a tool for making scientific phenomen a more personally relevant and engaging (Barton & Tan, 200 9; O. Lee & Luykx, 2006). Research has also shown t hat teachers and researchers play a crucial r ole in recruiting students’ FoK in critical science  pedagogy (Upadhyay, 2006). In this approach to science education, FoK i s not just for shepherding students from non-dominant backgrounds toward canonical science understanding but for valuing their cultur al expertise as legitimate knowledge production resources.

We argue that storytelling is a particularly effect ive practice for recruiting FoK toward critical sci ence pedagogy. Storytelling synergizes with translanguag ing pedagogy by encouraging multimodal forms of sen semaking, including modeling practices (García, 2009;  Pierson et al., 2021). Storytelling can also span across disciplines and contexts: literacy and art, home and school, just to name a few examples (J. Anderson et al., 2018). Storytelling elicits students’ creative thinking, e ncouraging students to think of new ways that their expertise in other domains might connect to the topics they learn about in science.

## Study context

We examine the participation of fourth-grade students in a food webs and photosynthesis cu rriculum taught in two science classes in a Southeastern elementary school (4.LS2: Ecosystems: Interactions, Energy, and Dynamics; National Research Council, 2012). Co-designed by the authors of this paper and the parti cipating teacher (Ms. Sarah), the nine-week curriculum layered embodiment, computational modeling, and storytelling throughout nine lesson sets (Anton et al., 2025). Using photos from the teacher’s family garden, the anchoring phenomenon invited students to explore and wonder about who (i.e., ani mals) visits backyard gardens and why they might ch oose to visit or inhabit those spaces.

Each lesson set invited students to engage with mul tiple types of models of the garden ecosystems to explore core conceptual ideas, including food chain s, food webs, roles of organisms, energy transfer, adding or removing organisms in an ecosystem, and photosynthesis. Lesson sets entailed dividing students into three groups to cycle through 20-minute station activities (GEM-STEP Technology, Notice and Wonder, and Storytelling), with whole-class discussions at the beginning and end of lesson sets. The Technology station featured a sequence of colle ctive embodied models centered on a garden ecosystem developed using GEM-STEP (Danish et al., 2022). Wearing trackable tags, students interacted with MR models—Food Webs, Energy Transfer, and Photosynthesis —by physically moving through the space and controlling virtual characters. In the initial Food  Webs model, students embodied mice, navigating sur vival in a garden ecosystem alongside predators (hawks) and pr oducers (vegetable gardens). Students modified the models over the first three lesson sets by introducing new organisms and adjusting parameters such as location, speed, size, energy levels, and population size. Guided by their group’s inquiries and interests, students built increasingly complex food webs, incorporating predators (hawks, snakes), competitors (robins, caterpillars), produc ers (vegetable gardens, clovers), and decomposers (eart hworms). Students deepened their understanding of the Energy Transfer model by embodying energy, experime nting with how it flows through the ecosystem, and reordering organisms to optimize the system’s balance. The curriculum concluded with the Photosynthesis model, where students play-acted as molecules—sugar, water, oxygen, and carbon dioxide —collaboratively simulating the process of photosynthesis to grow a virtual plant.

The Notice and Wonder and Storytelling stations pro vided complimentary, unplugged, paper-based activities that encouraged students to reflect, syn thesize, and expand on ideas explored in the techno logy station. The Notice and Wonder station included computationa l modeling and inquiry-based activities where students shared reflections and posed questions about the ga rden ecosystem and their GEM-STEP experiences. Activities at this station involved scripting (modifying code)  to unpack their interactions within the models, pr edict outcomes, and plan future modifications before and after their MR experiences. The Storytelling statio n invited students to creatively express their knowledge, int erests, and prior experiences related to garden eco systems through drawing, writing, puppetry, and play-acting. Each lesson set includes 3 to 4 storytelling prompts designed to explore or extend the themes of the lessons. For example, students wrote stories about backyard gardens, what happens when an organism leaves an ecosystem, and how gardeners use pesticides to protect plants from insects. Focal activity This paper focuses on the culminating Newly Introduced Species  activity, conducted after the Food Webs and Energy Transfer models. In this activity, small gro ups of students (3 –4 per group) designed species interactions and introduced them into the modeled garden ecosystem. The lesson set began with an informational video and a whole-class discussion about invasive species. Students t hen collaborated within their small groups to selec t an organism to add to the ecosystem, completing a work sheet designed to scaffold their process. The works heet guided students in expressing prior real-world knowledge (e.g., drawing the organism, descri bing its native ecosystem), connecting this knowledge to food web c oncepts (e.g., defining the organism’s role, creati ng a food web), and identifying model parameters (e.g., specifying quantity, location, and type of organisms). Students also developed stories about their introduced species to support sensemaking of their food web. Based on each group’s worksheet, researchers create d a new version of the Food Webs model, incorporating their organism art and selected param eters. Groups observed their model on a computer, a nalyzing interactions and proposing iterative parameter adju stments. The lesson set concluded with students emb odying their invasive species in the MR technology and participating in a debrief discussion to reflect on the experience. We hoped that by encouraging students to care for their newly created/introduced organism and engage in sensemaking through bringing in their FoK about their organism, we could (a) further enhance the care students felt by embodying the first-person perspective of an organism in the MR technol ogy, and (b) narrow the gap between their personal experiences with real-world ecosystems and the abstract, third-person perspective represented in the simulation by leveraging their first-person embodiment of an organism they were familiar  with in real life. Ultimately, this connection was intended to deepen their understanding of real-world ecosystems (see Figure 1). Figure 1 GEM-STEP-the MR Embodied Simulation Environment

## Participants

A total of 44 students participated in the curricul um across two classes taught by Ms. Sarah. Students  across classes shared similar demographics. Students were between the ages of 9 –10:  18 girls, 16 boys, 2 nonbinary, and 14 unreported. A total of 36 students reported languages spoken at home: Spanish (19), English (12), and other languages (5).

We selected one group of students for our case study for several reasons. First, they were one of the four groups (of the 12 groups in total) in which all students had consented for us to share their video data in publications and presentations. Second, it  was the only group composed of multiple genders wit h high and low-achieving students, as captured in gains from pre-to post-assessment. Third, the group selected an organism for their newly introduced species with which each student had experience (puppies), creating a unique opportunity to understand how students brought outside knowledge to help them design and iterate on their new ecosystem. The case study was also fairly representative of th e other groups. First, the students showed a simila r range of achievement to other groups based on gains from pre-to post-assessment. Two students in the group had high gains and two had smaller gains (Anton et al., 2025). Moreover, in examining the distribution of these gains, we found that these students represented a spectrum  of achievement: from low to low, low to high, medi um to high, and high to high scores from pre-to post-assessment. Finally, during the lessons, the group displayed a similar sense of care for their organism as other groups did.

## Data analysis

In terms of the trustworthiness of our analysis, we  iteratively analyzed our video data using interact ion analysis (Jordan & Henderson, 1995). First, we watched and content-logged the 3 days of the focal group's Newly Introduced Organism lesson set. Driven by our resea rch questions, we identified episodes where notable  events occurred in the unplugged activities (i.e., discove ries, debates, moments of inquiry, proposed conjec tures, etc.). Select clips were shared with a larger research gro up, allowing alte rnative theories and interpretations to be introduced during the back-and-forth process of refining our hypotheses with video (Engle et al., 2007). In addition to refining our analysis, these sessions led to the  development of our emergent broad categories  of FoK and a sense of care.  These were not intended to be quant ifiable codes; rather, they acted as labels to iden tify rich episodes in which student engagement could be used to refine and ground our theories.

## Findings

GEM-STEP, like all educational technologies, comes with both affordances and limitations. Traditionally, science concepts are taught abstractly, making it challengi ng for students to adopt the perspectives of the or ganisms and phenomena they study. Typically, they engage with these concepts from a detached, third-person, bird’s-eye view. A significant strength of GEM-STEP technology lies in its ability to immerse students in a first-person perspective, allowing them to become the organisms they are studying. This immersive experience enables us to tap into students’ affect and sense of care, enrich ing their engagement in the learning and sense-making process. However, a challenge inherent to MR game-like models is ensuring that the gamified environme nt remai ns meaningfully connected to real-world ecosystems. This section details and compares our unplugged design goals to what happened in and away from the technology we developed. Anchoring phenomenon and eliciting funds of knowledge Our design aimed to tie students’ personal connections closely with the curricular content. On the first day of the curriculum, our partner teacher, Ms. Sarah, introduced this setting with a story and pictures of her family’s garden and its surrounding land: “When I visit my family, sometimes I’ll help in the garden. Sometimes, I just pick lots of vegetables… Hanging around this garden, I starte d noticing some animals that were hanging around.” Ms. Sarah shared her observations and connections to he r family  garden, setting the stage for the anchoring phenomenon and inviting students to share their own  experiences. She displayed photos of various plant s and animals that live in her garden, including a rabbit, deer, and a snake, asking students if they recognized the animals. Students immediately responded with their connectio ns with the images, interrupting her talk with exci ted outbursts, saying, “What is that?”, “Aww!”, and “De er are everywhere,” reactions that reveal the stude nts’ connections to these animals and local ecosystems, laying the foundation for meaningful engagement in subsequent discussions.

For the final image, Ms. Sarah builds suspense, announcing, “You’re about to meet… Dolly Oscar.” She revealed a photo of her family cat, Dolly Oscar, pr ompting an immediate wave of happy reactions from m any students, saying, “Aww!” This introduction further strengthened the connections between the ecological dynamics visible in the images and students’ FoK, particularly regarding the role pets might play in ecosystems. Ms. Sarah explained that Dolly Oscar is an outdoor cat and then asked, “Do you know what he’s brought up before?” Taylor Swift (a self-selected pseudonym) eagerly responded, “Dead birds,  dead mice… My cat got a chipmunk.” Ms. Sarah agreed and added, “He’s actually brought up a mole before.” Taylor Swift chimed in, “Ooh, yeah! My dog brought a mole, and so did my cat.” This exchange h ighlights how students’ lived experiences inform th eir understanding of ecosystem dynamics presented in th e curriculum. In this case, Taylor Swift connected her observations of her pets’ behavior to the broader f ood web in the garden ecosystem. Her insights contribut ed to building a class-wide understanding of ecological relationships, lay ing the groundwork for scientific modeling practices.

## Leveraging first-person perspectives to foster a sense of care

Next, the Puppy Squad simulated on a computer to see the digital version of their n ewly introduced species. As the model began, the puppy agent moved randomly abo ut the screen, and the children's excitement transf ormed into distress. "Noooo… Puppy! No, Puppy!" they crie d out. “The puppy  died.” They immediately began brainstorming ways to change the model to keep the puppy alive. EL suggested changing the predator's behavior: “We should slow down the hawk. I want to slow down the hawk so the puppy dog stays alive.” The group decided on two puppies, Rosey and Lila, so at least one would survive. They gathered around the simulation again, excited to see if their adjustments would help Rosey and Li la survive. Leaning in with anticipation, Da Paw’s voice echoed, “No no no,” as EL tried to boost morale, sa ying, “You cando  this, puppy dogs.” Taylor Swift pulled at her face, visibly tense, while Rose kept cheering, “You cando this,” hoping for a positive turn. But soon, Rose realized that the hawk remained a si gnificant threat, and her concern spilled out with an anguished, “Puppy dogs.” As the puppies continued t o struggle, Taylor Swift suggested a new idea —changing a characteristic of the puppy: “We should make the pu ppy dogs faster.” This worked somewhat, but the age nt’s random movement that was coded into the simulation meant that the puppy agent often ran out of energy before it found food. EL tried to help, pointing to the sc reen and urging, “Eat something, ” as if willing the puppies to make better choices. Rose returned to thinking abou t predators, suggesting they slow down the snake as  well. Taylor Swift offered another solution: “We need to make the dog faster.” And Rose added, “And the hawk  even slower.” Finally, Da Paw wrapped up their strategic changes, suggesting a new way to think about the interactions in the simulation: “We need to make the dog stronger.” Over the course of this exchange the Puppy Squad abandoned their previous goal of caring for the entire ecosystem and instead was determined to save their puppies. They treated the augmented puppies as if they were their pets. They incorporated their understanding o f how to take care of a real puppy dog, taking a ca regiver's perspective of the puppies in the model. They named the puppies, followed their behavior, and tried to save them, crying out “noo” when the puppies need ed help. The students leaned toward the screen to obse rve the puppy closely while pointing and making anxious faces, in dicating they cared about what happened. Their effo rts to change the puppy’s characteristics and how puppies interacted with the other agents were motivated by this care. Overall, their feeling of responsibility seemed to achieve the same emotional engagement with the simu lation as we had previously seen when students took on the role of the agents in the MR environment. Leveraging funds of knowledge to reason about modeling choices After their time with the simulation ended, the gro up continued to discuss what they should change abo ut the system that could help them take care of their pupp y. Away from the technology, the types of solutions  they suggested began to be guided by outside  knowledge rather than focused on computer variables. For example, Taylor Swift suggested, “I think size is… maybe we should make it [the puppy] a little bit bigger so t hat way, it like, [be] stronger. And the ha wk may be a bit smaller.” The researcher int errupted, asking, “So bigger is stronger?” Taylor Swift agreed and explained, “Yeah, because, like, if it’s prey and it is smaller tha n it, then the prey could crush it easier or have a better chance of eating it than something that is bigger than you.” Her reasoning reflected real-world experiences, where larger animals are often s afer from predators because it is harder for the predators to hunt a big animal.

The researcher then posed a thought-provoking question: “Do you think this is how it works in real life, or in the model, or both?” Taylor Swift considered this and replied, “Well, if you have a tiny dog, th e hawk can eat you.” This acknowledges the connec tion between real-life knowledge and the simulation and explains that smaller dogs are more prone to becoming prey. At th is point, EL offered: “What if we delete the hawk, then the dog will be the leader?” The ensuing debate over po ssible changes highlighted the students’ growing understanding of the ecosystem’s hierarchical struc ture but also revealed how GEM-STEP's coding rules and mechanics sometimes resisted students' intuitive re asoning and spurred engagement with the model as a game more so than a representation of the real world. Re cognizing that their puppy wasn’t at the top of the food chain, they began tonegotiate changes, such as slowing do wn the hawk and adjusting the puppy’s size, to help their character survive. This discussion and experimentat ion with changes in the simulation underscored the tension between real-world understandings of predator-prey relationships (a smaller dog is easier for a hawk t o eat) and the simplified GEM-STEP mechanics (a smaller dog agent is less likely cross  paths with a hawk agent and therefore more likely to survive). Furthermore, it demonstrates how the ability to manipulate the rule s in GEMSTEP engaged students in treating GEM-STEP more like a game than a model of the real world (e. g. slowing down the hawk, deleting the hawk, making the puppy bigger). Despite the gaps between the model and reality, the students' emotional connection to their dog characters remained genuine. They demonstrated a thoughtful un derstanding of the difference between real-world ecosystems and how they were modeled in the technology. Storytelling became a valuable tool for bridging these gaps and deepening their understanding, helping to counteract the GEM-STEP’s abstract game-nature by weaving in narratives that grounded students’ learning in relatable and meaningful ways. The need to balance connections with the lesson goals There was, however, a problem that came along with this sense of care for the pets they created for the simulation: the group was no longer focusing on the ecosystem a s a whole. When Puppy Squad play-acted their puppies in the embodied MR environment, they  were singularly focused on the well-being of their puppy characters. Their care and concern were directed almost exclusively a t their beloved puppy, and this care did not transl ate into a holistic understanding or concern for the entire ec osystem’s health. After a few more rounds, when the  puppies kept dying, Rose declared, pointing to the screen, “Delete the hawk!” Echoing her idea, the other kids  happily directed, “Delete it.” “It ruins everything,” Rose added, and the group cheered together “Delete it, delete it.” The researcher asked if the goal was to save the puppy,  and they agreed, saying, “Delete it! Goodbye!” Tay lor Swift led the cheering as the rest of the group said, “Goodbye,” “Goodbye hawk, forever.” In these exchanges, we argue that the third-person perspective is missing, reflecting a gap bet ween individual empathy and broader ecological awareness. The students understood the disruptive n ature of an invasive species. However, their emotio nal attachment to the puppy prevented them from acknowl edging the impact on the whole system and a real-life ecosystem.

## Discussion

In this paper, we asked: How do unplugged activitie s provide opportunities for students’ lived experie nces to enhance sensemaking and engagement in a mixed-reality simulation of a garden ecosystem? Our case study showed that unplugged activities—like storytelling and paper-based modeling—helped students connect personal experiences, such as caring for pets, to their inte ractions with the MR model. These activities deepen ed engagement, fostered care, and supported explanator y reasoning. However, they also revealed tensions w hen students’ emotional attachments to specific species overshadowed system-level ecological thinking. From a design perspective, this underscores the importance of considering when and how unplugged activities are introduced. Initially, we framed them as either amplifying the technology’s strengths (e.g., first-person affect) or addressing its weaknesses (e.g., gamification without real-world grounding). Both strategies proved effective —but in some ca ses, overemphasizing MR’s affective strengths went too far.

We argue that unplugged activities function not sim ply as support tools but as mediational means (Vygotsky, 1980) that shape how students engage with modeling. Rather than compensating for MR’s limitations, these activities provide distinct entry points —especially when they recruit students’ funds of knowl edge (FoK) and personal experiences. In one unplugged lesson o n invasive species, students introduced a puppy out  of affection and care. Their decisions —driven by FoK and emotional attachment —reoriented th e modeling goal toward protecting the puppies, even at the expense of ecological balance. While this led to a scientifically flawed model, the process revealed meaningful engagement w ith system dynamics. Unplugged scripting, in partic ular, served as a bridge between intuitive reasoning and computational thinking. As Vygotsky argued in the context of atypical development (Gindis, 1999), alternative tools should not be seen as compensations but as different means of achieving the same function. In this way, u nplugged activities offered a parallel pathway into  complex, embodied sensemaking.
