# Early Science Hub: Designing Interactive Simulations to Support Preschool Educators' Understanding of Science Core Ideas and Cross Cutting Concepts

**Conference:** ISLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
To support preschool educators’ integration of science in their classrooms, our project is co-designing the Early Science Hub (Hub), a professional learning resource based on promising components identified in prior work, with partner educators and curriculum/media designers. The Hub and its components are being designed to leverage technology and include educative features to make the resource widely accessible across early learning contexts. This paper details the design and iteration of one specific Hub component, the interactive simulations of science core ideas and cross cutting concepts designed to support preschool educators’ understanding of these. The session will invite participants to explore the interactive simulations and engage in a discussion to gather their insights and feedback, which can inform potential future iterations.

Preschool teachers have strengths that uniquely position them to support science and engineering learning, including their experience orchestrating activities that meaningfully promote learning across disciplines and working closely with families to support children’s learning and development. A few recently designed professional development programs have shown evidence of promise and highlighted potential supports or components that may be helpful to preschool teachers as they work to integrate science in their classroom (Gropen et al., 2017; Lange et al., 2021; McWayne et al., 2021). While these findings, together, are encouraging, more research is needed to understand impacts on teachers’ instructional science practice and whether and under what conditions improvements in teacher outcomes translate into improvements in child learning. In addition, while these programs have shown preliminary evidence of promise, they require substantial investments in time and resources, which often pose challenges for adoption and broad impact. Our project aims to co-design the Early Science Hub (Hub), a suite of professional development resources that includes some of the key components that have been identified as promising in prior work, uniquely leveraging technology to make professional resources widely available to preschool educators and embedding educative features to make the resources helpful to coaches and communities of practice existing in the ecology of preschool programs. Our work acknowledges that teachers are learners who develop expertise and build understanding both individually and collectively and that this process unfolds across time and in various contexts (National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine [NASEM], 2021). Thus, our work draws on sociocultural theoretical perspectives that emphasize the important role that social and cultural interactions play in learning and development such as sociocultural cognitive theory (Vygotsky, 1978) and cultural historical activity theory (Engeström, 1999). As part of the Hub development, we are currently engaging with partner preschool teachers to co-design the Hub components and resources. For instance, the Hub video library of science practices and simulations of core ideas and cross cutting concepts will be co-designed to support teachers' individual learning and reflection, as well as their collective learning (in communities of practice and through coaching). Drawing on sociocultural perspectives that bridge home and school learning (Gonzalez et al., 2006), the Hub home school connections toolkit will be designed to help teachers understand learners – to build knowledge of who they are, what their families’ cultural capital and lived experiences are like, and what role their community plays in their lives – and leverage that knowledge to design meaningful science learning experiences. Finally, our work also draws on ecological theories of development (Bronfrenbrenner, 2000) that acknowledge the interconnectedness of early learning. The Hub interactive map of cross-disciplinary connections will be designed to help teachers integrate science meaningfully with other areas of learning, such as mathematics, language and literacy, and engineering.

## Overview of the interactive simulations

The interactive simulations component of the Hub was designed to support and deepen preschool educators’ understanding of developmentally appropriate science core ideas and cross cutting concepts in an engaging and e ducative manner. To co-design the simulations, the project team first reviewed findings from our implementation study of science curricula (Dominguez et al., 2023) to identify science learning goals that were most challenging to preschool educators or where they could benefit from professional learning support. Based on recommendations from science educator advisors, our team linked each of these goals to relevant cross cutting concepts and generated seed idea designs across three disciplinary core ideas (Plants, Light and Shadows, Force and Motion). These seed ideas, described below, were meant to be artifacts to spark design discussions with preschool educators during the second co-design workshop.

1. The Plant Science Magnifier seed idea emerged from prior findings indicating educators benefitted from

support and resources that provide detailed examples of plant parts and their functions. This seed idea aims to allow educators to deepen their understanding of this science learning goal by engaging in a guided walk through with the opportunity to explore additional concepts of interest via a thought bubble feature (see figure 1).

Figure 1 Plant Science Magnifier Wireframe Examples An initial wireframe depicting a thought bubble feature (left) and a subsequent wireframe with updated color visuals (right).

2. For Shadows, the team conceptualized two simulations: Shadow Simulator and Shadow Science. The

Shadow Simulator seed idea was developed because teaching children how a shadow is created when the light shines on a surface and blocks some of the light was easier for teachers to facilitate when using a man-made light source (lamp or flashlight) rather than a natural light source (sun). Thus, this simulation aims to leverage technology in a way that allows teachers to explore this phenomenon in a controlled manner not possible in real life. The Shadow Science seed idea was conceived to support educators’ understanding of how shadows are made while addressing common misconceptions. Similar to the Plant Science Magnifier, this simulation is a guided exploration on how shadows are made yet intentionally incorporates opportunities for educators to isolate and manipulate specific variables that may affect a shadow (see figure 2).

Figure 2 Shadow Science Wireframe Example An initial wireframe addressing a specific light and shadows learning objective.

3. The Ramps Physics Simulator is situated within a miniature golf context where educators engage in a

structured environment that allows them to explore how different variables affect the golf ball to learn more about force and motion (see figure 3).

Figure 3 Ramps Physics Simulator Wireframe Example A second-round wireframe where the user can select different types of objects to test.

Co-design sample and process Our work included close collaborations with preschool partner sites in CA and VA and a curriculum/media design studio with whom we have established relationships through prior early STEM research projects. Starting in Spring 2025, the project team engaged six preschool educators across the two sites and curriculum/media designers in co-design workshops and will continue to do so for the 2025-2026 school year. The overall aim of these workshops was to build and strengthen relationships with each other so we could collaboratively brainstorm, ideate, iterate and reflect on emerging seed ideas for the Hub components. Depending on educators’ availability, the timing of the educator co-design workshops varied from a half-day session to multiple shorter one-hour sessions; however, each workshop included interactive elements and activities designed to address the overarching goal and focused on a specific Hub component. We describe the co-design workshop and related data collection activities below.

Preschool educator survey Prior to the first co-design workshop, the project team administered an online survey to gather preschool educators’ experience implementing science in their classrooms and initial thoughts on the proposed professional learning hub. Survey findings helped the project team better understand and characterize the co-design educator sample and inform the co-design workshop activities. For instance, all educators reported facilitating science learning activities in their classrooms with a majority implementing science “every day” or “a couple of times a week” and only one implementing science “once a week.” Despite this variance in the frequency of facilitating science learning activities, all educators reported feeling “very comfortable” with science facilitation and perceive science as “very valuable” for children’s learning and development, along with other learning domains. Additionally, educators reported on their comfortability with implementing science practices and core ideas (life science, earth science, physical science). Educators primarily expressed feeling “very comfortable” implementing a majority of the science practices with only one or two educators noting feeling only “somewhat comfortable” with the questioning practice and documenting observations and recording data practice, respectively. Although these findings are not surprising given educators’ prior background participating in early STEM research projects, it also revealed promising areas for further exploration during the co-design phase. Preschool educator co-design workshop 1 In addition to orienting co-design educators to the project and providing a refresher on early science, the first codesign workshop focused on diving deeper into the science practices. To prepare for the science practices practices aligned with these multiple sources. As part of this workshop, we explored how a video library could help promote these science practices in early learning classrooms, noting educators’ insights on what they liked, what is missing, and any new ideas. For example, educators suggested including different entry points to the videos and broader video library may vary depending on the educators’ familiarity with early science, thus highlighting an important design consideration as the Hub development continues. To address this and ensure the developed resource is suitable for newer teachers or those with less experience teaching science in preschool, the project team actively recruited additional preschool partners that fit these criteria. Preschool educator co-design workshop 2 For the second educator co-design workshop, the primary objective was to discuss and gather educators’ input on seed ideas of the interactive simulations of science core ideas and cross cutting practices, which is the focus of t he Interactive Tools and Demos session. During the co-design workshop, the project team walked through wireframes of the seed ideas where teachers were encouraged to discuss elements they liked/disliked, potential improvements and/or different tools that could be designed instead or in addition. Future co-design activities Following this second workshop, we synthesized educator and advisor feedback to generate a summary memo to be shared with the designers and help guide design decisions. Based on insights gathered during the second codesign workshop, the designers created another round of wireframes and will have prototypes ready for us to demo with educators in a future meeting. By the end of the co-design phase (Summer 2026), the goal is to have more fully developed simulations ready for this session and a subsequent field study (Fall 2026). Given that co-design workshops continued throughout the 2025-2026 school year, the findings reported in this paper are partial and preliminary. Across both sites, educators generally liked the simulations and rated them highly when asked about the likelihood of using each simulation. While these simulations were described as a teacher-facing resource rather than a child-oriented tool, teachers at one site tended to think about how the simulations could also help their learners, discussing potential ways to utilize the simulations during whole group or small group activities. Thus, some of their design feedback considered the child user experience. To address this at the co-design workshop with the other cohort of teachers, we modified the warm up activity to invite teachers to share about the resources they typically use when planning a science activity, which helped emphasize educators as the target user of the simulations being reviewed. Teachers acknowledged not seeing this type of resource support, indicating its promise, and centered their feedback from the teacher user perspective. Moreover, educators’ feedback highlighted specific design features that they found valuable or worth considering as part of future iterations. For instance, educators liked the thought bubble for numerous reasons. At one site, teachers noted this as a unique feature and liked how the information provided aligns with questions that children may be wondering about. At the other site, teachers liked how the thought bubble was presented as an optional choice and can see how it can be especially valuable for newer teachers or those less familiar with teaching science. Another suggested feature related to audio supports and having an option for a voice to read the text aloud, which can be educative for educators including those who are English learners themselves. As our team analyzes the co-design workshop data, we will look into the potential factors that may be influencing their view of the simulations as an educator resource versus a child tool, including their classroom context and technology experiences as well as the simulation design and features, and how to triangulate this with their design feedback. In turn, this will help the team identify and prioritize future revisions. Interactive demo session During this session, we will share about our co-design process and findings and showcase the latest prototypes of the interactive simulations of the science core ideas and cross cutting concepts. Through hands-on demos, participants will have the opportunity to view and explore the latest drafts of interactive simulations on an iPad from the perspective of a teacher user and share their in-the-moment reflections about feasibility and use via an anonymized Mentimeter link. After they explore and share their insights, we will invite participants to engage in incorporating prior to the Fall 2026 pilot study, and document these using a Miro design board or chart paper and sticky notes.
