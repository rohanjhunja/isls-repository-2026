# Scoping Review of Knowledge-Creation Practices Supported by AI: Identification of Research Strands of Different Communities

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This scoping review examines how AI supports collaborative learning as knowledge creation in varied educational contexts. Unlike previous reviews that discuss learning more broadly, this study focuses on the epistemological and methodological foundations of AI-supported collaboration. In this scoping review, we aimed at exploring AI-supported collaboration with two specific perspectives: (1) the focus on collaborative learning as knowledge creation, and (2) research traditions. Systematic reviews so far have discussed learning in the generic form and have not examined the epistemological perspectives that reviewed studies hold.  From the learning sciences perspective, in particular, the knowledge-creation metaphor of collaborative learning has been developed as a new goal of instruction and has led to the development of CSCL systems. It frames learning as a collaborative process of producing and improving knowledge valuable to a community (Paavola & Hakkarainen, 2005) and emphasizes inquiry, innovation, and collective responsibility for ideas, viewing knowledge as dynamic and evolving through interaction and co-construction rather than static content to be acquired or practiced (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 20 22). This view positions learners as active knowledge builders, engaging in creative and problem-solving processes similar to those found in scientific and professional communities.  A further systematic review o f studies focused on knowledge-creation practices can provide new directions for collaborative learning supported by AIs, aligning with the goal of instruction in the 21 st century.

Besides the specific attention to the epistemological perspective, research traditions (Enyedy & Stevens,

2022) in studies are essential to rigorously provide scientific evidence for a new style of collaborative learning

supported by AIs and to feed insights back into the future design of learning environments. The collaboration-asa-window-onto-thinking tradition reflects a cognitivist epistemology, viewing collaboration as a way to access internal mental processes. Here, dialogue acts much like a “think-aloud” protocol, making interaction largely incidental. The collaboration-for-distal-outcomes tradition adopts an effect-oriented epistemology, investigating how certain discourse patterns (e.g., IRE, exploratory, or accountable talk) are linked to individual learning outcomes such as test performance or motivation. The collaboration-for-proximal-outcomes tradition draws on sociocultural and interactionist epistemologies, focusing on how learning emerges through the interaction itself via intersubjectivity, tool appropriation, or the use of shared referents (Roschelle, 1992; Enyedy, 2003). Finally, the essential methodological approach named as collaboration-as-learning is to rigorously gather scientific evidence of a new style of collaborative learning supported by AIs and feed insights back into the future design of tradition is rooted in distributed and ethnomethodological epistemologies, treating collaboration as both the unit and outcome of learning, and emphasizing the internal organization of activity systems and collective accomplishments (Barron, 2003; S cardamalia & Bereiter, 2022). Each tradition offers a unique perspective on collaboration, shaped by its epistemological stance, and influences what is considered evidence of learning. Methodologically, we not only followed the standard PRISMA protocol but  also examined existing research strands in target studies using Epistemic Network Analysis (ENA)  (Shaffer et al., 2016). Nachtigall et al. (2022) employed ENA to capture the relationships between design features (e.g., collaboration, inquiry-based tasks, real-world contexts) and the intentions of authenticity they served (e.g., emulating professional practices, 329 connecting with communities of practice). This allowed them to visualize and compare patterns of co-occurrence of codes across studies with higher versus lower effectiveness, revealing how particular combinations of design elements were linked to cognitive and motivational outcomes. Their use of ENA demonstrates how network-based frequency-based analyses. We further implemented a new algorithm for the ENA modeling process, specifically the conversation. The approach by Nachtigall et al. (2022), treating each study as a “conversation” in their modeling, could not actually capture a conversation as a dialogue unit among studies. Through bibliographic coupling network analysis, we identified research communities based on shared references  (Hmelo-Silver & Jeong, 2021). These communities served as datasets for calculating code co-occurrences, effectively modeling conversations in ENA. Combining bibliographic coupling analysis with ENA enabled visualization of how different research communities align with particular strands defined by specific epistemological perspectives and methodological traditions.

In summary, the purpose of our scoping review is to examine current research strands in studies of AIsupported collaborative learning and to propose new directions for future research. The following research questions guide this investigation: RQ1:  How are studies on collaborative learning as knowledge creation, supported by AIs, grouped into research communities?

RQ2:  How do the research communities differ in their research strands?

## Method

Article identification through the PRISMA protocol Our search strategy consisted of two groups of terms. The first group targeted various forms of collaborative learning, referring to Mende et al. (2021), while the second group limited the results to studies involving artificial intelligence.  The final search string was: *Topic = (collaborati* OR cooperati* OR group OR peer OR dyad) AND “artificial intelligence” This search yielded 29,208 results. After limiting to English-language publications in Q1-ranked journals in the two categories (“EDUCATION & EDUCATIONAL RESEARCH” and “COMPUTER SCIENCE, INTERDISCIPLINARY APPLICATIONS), 1,298 articles remained. The screening process was carried out in two stages —abstract and full-text screening. At the abstract stage, 10% of the records (130 articles) were independently screened by two reviewers, achieving a Cohen’s kappa of.714. Discrepancies were resolved thro ugh discussion, and the remaining records were divided for single screening. This process resulted in 441 articles meeting the criteria  in Table 1. At the full-text screening stage, 10% of the articles (44) were double-screened, yielding a Cohen’s kappa of.490. Disagreements were resolved by consensus, and the remaining texts were divided equally between the two reviewers. After applying an additional inclusion criterion requiring learner engagement in knowledge-creation processes (e.g., creation and development of material or conceptual artifacts, or conscious knowledge advancement, discovery, and innovation), 72 articles were retained for coding.

Coding of articles for ENA After identifying research communities through bibliographic coupling analysis (Waltman et al., 2010) using VOSviewer (https://www.vosviewer.com/), we coded the selected articles using four dimensions: (1) collaboration context, (2) AI’s role, (3) methodological traditions, and (4) analytical modality of collaboration. We first jointly coded a sample of ten articles and used a social moderation procedure (Frederiksen et al., 1998) to ensure complete agreement on all codes. The remaining articles were then independently coded by the first and second authors. The collaboration context concerns the type of collaboration involving AI. A single learner can use AI for individual learning—a new collaborative possibility enabled by AI, especially generative AI. We refer to this as “Human-AI” collaboration. Alternatively, “Group-AI” collaboration involves AI in activities where multiple learners work together.  The AI’s role examines how learners use AI for collaborative knowledge creation. We developed six codes based on the categories suggested by Sharples (2023): Possibility Engine, Socratic Opponent, Collaboration Coach, Co-designer, Exploratorium, and Storyteller. Since Sharples (2023) does not provide a detailed explanation for Collaboration Coach, we referred to Mikroyannidis and Armakolas (2025), which cites 330 Sharples to generate a similar code. We chose to define Collaboration Coach as an AI use focused on regulating collaboration, rather than generating idea-level epistemic output like Possibility Engine or Socratic Opponent. The methodological traditions  address the methodological approaches used to analyze both “Human-AI” and “Group-AI” collaboration. We referenced Enyedy and Stevens (2022), who identified four methodological traditions to studying collaborative learning: collaboration-as-a-window, colla boration-for-distal-outcomes, collaboration-for-proximal-outcomes, and collaboration-as-learning. Finally, w e referred to the “ Definitional Framework of Modalities in Multimodal Collaboration Analytics Literature ” by Esterhazy et al. (2025). To capture various types of behavior during collaborative activities, they conducted a scoping review and classified the modalities used in multimodal collaboration analytics into five categories: Auditory, Visual, Kinesthetic, Tactile, and Physiological.

Identification of research communities: Bibliographic coupling analysis A bibliographic coupling analysis was conducted to cluster the target articles based on shared references. We identified seven communities; however, because one community contained only a single article (n=1), we excluded it from subsequent analyses.

Studies cited in the six communities present distinct yet complementary perspectives on the evolving role of artificial intelligence (AI) in education. Articles cited in Community 1 (n=16) focus on the micro-level of interactional learning, positioning ChatGPT as a dialogic partner that supports the co-construction of knowledge and reflective feedback, with an emphasis on social presence and authenticity in AI-mediated writing. In contrast, those in Community 2 (n=14) adopt a macro-level, policy-oriented approach, exploring AI’s pedagogical potential alongside ethical, regulatory, and epistemic risks. Articles cited in Community 3 (n=13) bridge these viewpoints, examining feedback ecosystems and collaborative learning where AI analytics enhance metacognitive awareness and shared inquiry. Articles cited in Community 4 (n= 12) shift to an applied, experiential perspective, integrating AI into game-based environments to optimize motivation, flow, and cognitive support through adaptive feedback. Articles cited in Community 5 (n=10) highlight innovation and design thinking, portraying AI as a codesigner blending analytical precision with human creativity and empathy to solve complex educational and organizational problems. Finally, articles cited in Community 6 (n=6) stand out for their synthetic and integrative approach, drawing on diverse empirical studies to depict AI as a comprehensive educational ecosystem that fosters inclusivity, creativity, and self-efficacy across disciplines. While all the cited articles view AI as transformative, they differ in scale—from individual cognition and classroom interaction to institutional governance and systemic innovation—and in emphasis, ranging from practical pedagogy to ethical reflection and creative co-design. Collectively, these distinctions illustrate the multifaceted nature of AI in education: a feedback mechanism, a learning collaborator, a design tool, and a catalyst for broader social and ethical transformation. Research strands of identified communities: Epistemic network analysis We defined each unit of analysis as all data lines associated with a single research community value, subsetted by ID. The resulting networks for each unit (i.e., each article in this case) were aggregated within the model. Network aggregation was performed using binary summation, indicating the presence or absence of co-occurrence between each pair of codes per li ne. The ENA model then normalized the networks for all units before applying dimensional reduction to account for varying numbers of coded lines across units. Dimensional reduction was achieved using singular value decomposition, yielding orthogonal dimens ions that maximized the explained variance (17.5% for SVD1, 12.1% for SVD2). Our model achieved co-registration correlations of 0.95 (Pearson) and 0.96 (Spearman) for SVD1, and 0.93 (Pearson) and 0.94 (Spearman) for SVD2, indicating a strong fit between the visualization and the original model.

To test for differences between the mean networks of communities, we conducted multiple comparisons using Mann-Whitney tests on the location of the centrodes of research communities in the projected ENA space. All clusters were significantly different from one another in either the first or the second dimension. I t suggests that all research communities have their unique research strands. To explore differences in research strands among the communities, we visualized two comparison networks in the ENA space. The comparison networks illustrate how connections between codes differ across communities, highlighting distinct features of each group. Figure 1 (left) shows a comparison between community 3 and community  6, which are the most distant along the first dimension. This dimension represents “research traditions” and “collaboration contexts.” The positive end of the first dimension is prima rily characterized by codes such as collaboration for proximal outcomes, collaboration as learning, and Group-AI. The negative end is marked by Human-AI, with no specific research tradition codes. Figure 1 (right) presents a comparison between 331 community 1 and community  4, which are maximally separated along the second dimension. This dimension reflects “AI’s role” and “analytical modality of collaboration.” Here, the positive side is defined by Co-designer and Collaboration coach, while the opposing side is associated with Possibility engine  and Socratic Opponent, along with the analytical modality, Tactile.

Thus, different communities have developed as unique research strands. A few strands (clusters 4, 5, and

6) were characterized as cohorts of studies focusing on the implementation of AIs without rigorous examinations

of the collaboration process. They touched on the description of the collaboration process through surveys and interviews with learners. The other cohort was articulately grounded on the LS research traditions, such as collaboration for proximal outcome and collaboration as learning, by designing AIs as a Possibility engine and a Socratic Opponent in a Group-AI collaboration context, and rigorously examining the collaboration process. Clusters 2 and 3 were more oriented toward collaboration for proximal outcome s, while cluster 3 was oriented toward collaboration as learning. Across all clusters, m ost studies focused on a specific analytical modality of collaboration, Tactile, because text-based communication has prevailed in the current AI development to support collaborative learning.

Our findings reveal that studies of AI in collaborative learning ha ve been accelerated since 2020, but most strands have not yet examined collaboration processes  in detail. They  implemented AIs into personalized learning (Human-AI context) and primarily focused on distal learning outcomes, such as subject matter knowledge. One identified community is from CSCL and the LS, manifesting specific research traditions  based on the epistemic stance of learning as knowledge-creation. However, the strand is not mature yet in designing AIs in the context of collaborative learning. Besides, most studies examined a specific type of analytical modality, Tactile, even though they implemented multimodal communication using AIs. The future direction of studies will be directed toward the design of Group-AI collaboration supported by AIs’ multimodal communication. Not only can AIs communicate with human learners in multimodal ways, but researchers alsoneed  to develop new theoretical o rientations to design multimodal  AI-supported CSCL environments and to analyze such dynamic communication.
