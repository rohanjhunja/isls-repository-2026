# Lessons from Snails: Learning to Care for Nature

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper explores how gardening supports affective experiences that are critical to fostering long-term care and action for the environment. Using thematic analysis, this case study examines the gardening experiences of four girls who initially saw snails as pests but later wrestled with the ethics of removing them from the garden. The study demonstrates that gardening exposed the students to real-life challenges and ethical questions, which in turn nurtured their sense of care for the environment.

## Introduction

In recent years, Learning Sciences scholars have mo ved towards embracing post-positivist perspectives on research—acknowledging that “In science especially, we’re re ally good at pretending that lived experience doesn’t matter for the sake of whatever we ca ll objectivity in science” (Tzou, 2020, as cited in  Anderson, 2020). This highlights a paradox: we teach science in clas srooms, detached from the very phenomena we study, which are often right outside our doors. Furthermore, as we face climate and environm ental issues, experiencing the outdoors, and developing emotional connections are “the most important influence on human commitment to act in the interests of nature, and to protect it when under threat” (Perkins, 2010, pp. 455). The aim of this paper is to build on these insights and argue for the importanc e of fostering deep, personal care for the world ar ound us. As educators and researchers, it is essential that we design learning opportunities where our students ca n actively drive and live the science, dilemmas, and tensions that make up our world. Learning outside the box Learning is a dynamic, and often messy process. It involves participating, reflecting, embodying, and experiencing real-world phenomena and situations (Keifert et al., 2017; Lave 1996; Solomon et al., 2022). In Anderson’s (2020) interview with Megan Bang and Carrie Tzou, Bang describes how learning often happens in a box; where despite the phenomena we are learning about being right outside our windows we continue to statically  sit inside buildings. If we want to affirm students’ dignities  (Espinoza et al., 2020) as competent thinkers and inquirers, what example are we setting if we do not let students exercise science by moving in the outdoor and natural spaces of our world?

The notion that movement is central to learning is furthered by scholars who argue that the body is entangled with sensemaking, which includes multimodal ways of interacting in learning spaces (Alibali & Nathan, 2011; Flood et al., 2020; Goldin-Meadow, 2014). Especially within the Learning Sciences, we have often argued for the importance of looking at the whole body (e. g. gestures) as part of discourse and interactions.  We see evidence of this when we look at transcripts of interaction analysis that note arms being raised, pointing, or turning whole bodies (see Keifert et al., 2017) as a part o f turn-taking in educational spaces. For instance, Keifert  & Stevens (2018) used interaction analysis to examine how children and families observed the natural world in ways that led to scientific questions about phenomena like fog. In Anderson’s (2020) interview, Megan Bang and Carrie Tzou shared an example of how finding worms outside  on the sidewalk could lead to multiple cycles of inquiry. It can inspire learning about worm anatomy, worm ecosystems, and even ethical questions on whether we should move the worm or intervene with nature. Therefore, it's essential to engage with the world outside in multimodal ways, as lived experiences play a cr ucial role in learning, sensemaking, and developing  a deeper care for the world.

Learning with care Science can “emerge within particular political, social, economic, and cultural contexts” (Philip & Azevedo, 2017, pp. 527). Thus, learning science involves engaging as authentic participants who experience disciplinary work in affective-driven ways (Jaber & Hammer, 2016; Keifert et al, 2017). This is important because it not only affirms children’s dignity (Keifert et al., 2021) but it creates space for children to react critically and respond to what they are learning—for example, feeling anxious with the  rising number of wildfires and the implications thi s has on air quality and wildlife population (Boler, 1999; Espinoza, et al., 2020). This is significant because it goes beyond viewing affect as a motivational characteristic of learning (e.g., excitement  as evidence of student engagement) to embrace the complex emotions that arise when understanding environmental systems and issues. Thus, understanding the interconnected relationship s between humans and nature involves both emotional and ethical dimensions. This is because engaging with the outdoors invites exploration of the dilemmas, frustrations, joys, and sadness that come with cari ng for nature and its conservation (Jax et al., 201 8). It also provides opportunities to recognize the reciprocity  in human-nature relationships, where humans are both caregivers and recipients of care (Mu raca, 2016). Therefore, this paper asks: How can en gagement in outdoor spaces foster affective, meaningful learning experiences that cultivate care for nature?

## Methods

The context of this study involved a mixed-age classroom of 3rd and 4th grade students (ages 8-10) at a progressive elementary school in a West Coast  city in the United States. Throughout the academic year, the students participated in a year-long gardening project where they planned, planted,  cared for, and ultimately harvested from their garden boxes. The gardening pr oject became a central focus for learning across multiple disciplines, including science, history, and writing.

The class was divided into groups, with each group overseeing a garden box. They were responsible for deciding what to plant and how to care for it. To d ocument students' experiences and reflections, the teachers provided each student with a Gardening Journal. In these journals, students sketched plans for their garden boxes, recorded observations on the weather, tracked the p rogress of plant growth, wrote poems about their ga rdening experiences, and responded to reflection prompts pr ovided by the teacher. While students largely had a utonomy over what to write or sketch in their journals, four teacher-prompted entries asked students to reflect on specific questions. One prompt was given three times through out the year, asking students to reflect on how the ir understanding of land evolved. In April 2024, a sec ond prompt encouraged students to take the perspect ive of a living thing in their garden and reflect on their experiences from that point of view (see Table 1). ## Case study

This case study followed a group of four girls—Carol, Kate, Monica, and Penny (pseudonyms)—who discovered that snails were feeding on their plants. Upset by the damage, the girls were determined to stop the s nails from eating their garden. After observing the situation for a while, they decided to use an empty glass case to carefully relocate the snails from their garden boxes. As seen in Figure 1, the four girls created a small habitat in the “snail cage” by providing water and food for the relocated snails. Figure 1 The Snail Cage The case study focused on Carol, Kate, Monica, and Penny because their experience and reaction to snails in their garden box were unique. Throughout the academic year, the four girl s debated how to deal with these “pests” as they continued to relocate snails from their garden into the snail cage. Their experiences, feelings, and dispositions about the snails changed their understanding of their relationship with land. This transformation was documented in their Gardening Journals as they responded to th eir teacher’s writing prompts. Therefore, this case  study examined Carol, Kate, Monica, and Penny’s Gardening  Journals using thematic analysis (Braun & Clark, 2 012) to identify two emerging themes around learning and  caring for nature as the girls grappled with snail s in their garden.

## Findings

Dilemma of removing living things from nature The first theme that emerged across the four Gardening Journals was the dilemma of putting snails into the cage, which significantly influenced the students' perspe ctives and experiences with gardening. This was mos t visible across the April 16th and April 17th journal entries. In the April 16th entry on “What does the word ‘land’ mean to you? What is your relationship to the land”, both C arol and Kate wrote about their complicated feeling s on how they handled the snails in their garden box. Carol wrote, “We have took some snails from our garden we keep them in a tank. We are trying to track them and see  if we can keep them away. But I think it’s right a nd wrong”. Kate also wrote, “We are taking care of snails we are trying to find what to do with them”. Unsurprisingly, when the prompt, “Write from the pe rspective of something in our environment that is not a person. What might you say?” was given to stu dents the very next day, Carol, Kate, Monica, and P enny all wrote from the perspective of a snail. Penny wrote, “Hello my name is Gerald. I live in the dirt. When it rains me and my sister come out and play.” Similarly, Kate w rote, “I like to nibble stuff from a big thing and I think it is called a garden.” However, while Penny and Kate wro te from the perspective of a snail in nature, Carol  and Monica wrote from the perspective of one of the sna ils in the snail cage. I hide..’Ugh their poking my face now;”. She even wrote, “I snuggle into the corner of my cage after eating cucumber and drinking some water. Two girls put me in here and call me Gary. They call themselves Kate and Carol.” It is important to note both the timing and stance that students took in writing these entries. In the  April 16th entries, Carol and Kate mentioned finding snails in their garden. They also noted that while they had found a solution, which was to relocate the snails into a c age, they weren’t sure if this was the right decisi on. This was captured in Carol’s entry as s he thought “it’s right and wrong.” We also saw this  tension as Kate wrote that she didn’t know what to do with the snails. The very ne xt day, as they wrote their journal entry on April 17th, the stance on their decision to relocate snails became clear—why can’t humans just let the snails be? In other words, the authors of these entries portrayed themselves as the ones responsible for bothering and hindering snails. Over the following weeks after the April 17 th journal entry, the four girls made plans to release  the snails. While they didn’t want to release them back into the garden boxes, they also didn’t want to continue keeping them in the snail cage. In May 2024, Carol, Kate, Monica, and Penny f inally decide d to release the caged snails at a different location, away from their garden boxes.

## Conclusion

Supporting young people in caring for the environment requires engaging them in outdoor spaces where they can experience the emotional and ethical dilemmas that come with the real world. In gardening, Carol, Kate, Monica, and Penny not only found joy in growing and caring for their plants, but also faced dilemmas and diffi cult decisions. It's not just about gardening; it's abou t grappling with the complex, real-world problems we encounter in the natural world. The dilemmas they encountered —removing and relo cating snails —offer critical opportunities for meaningful learning experiences. As educators, we must recognize when these challenges arise and use them as valuable teaching moments to help s tudents understand the real impact of their decisio ns on the environment. In this case study, the dilemma and ethica l decision to remove snails from nature shifted stu dents’ understanding of land toward care. Thus, it was the dilemma that arose from gard ening that became a significant and meaningful experience in understanding and inte racting with nature. By navigating these tensions, students learned how their daily choices shape the world around them. As the Learning Sciences community works to address  environmental and climate issues through education, it is essential to center affect as a key component in designing learning spaces. Affect is present in the joy of being outdoors and in our roles as caretakers of nature. It is also embedded in the dilemmas that arise when sharing the natural world with other living and non-living things. To design for more sustainable futures, we must engage with all aspects of nature —the joy of caring for our planet, a s well as the difficult decisions we face, knowing that our choices impact the ecosystem.
