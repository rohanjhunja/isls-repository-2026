# It’s Complicated: How Experts Co-constructed Knowledge in the Academic Peer Review

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Peer review, as a process of knowledge co-construction, involves reviewers and authors in critical dialogue to enhance manuscript quality. Novice reviewers learn this practice by observing experienced peers and gaining hands-on experience. This study analyzed linguistic features in expert peer reviews within an argumentative knowledge co-construction framework, highlighting distinct author and reviewer roles and participation modes. Findings revealed varied social dynamics and confidence levels across review phases, underscoring the need for further research to prepare novice reviewers across disciplines.

## Introduction

Knowledge co-construction is a collaborative process in which individuals examine and refine each other's ideas, transcending information sharing to foster deeper collective understanding and knowledge creation (Scardamalia, 2002).  The interactive nature of these exchanges is central to this process (Chapman, 2013). According to social constructivist theory, learning becomes more effect ive when learners actively exchange ideas and negot iate meanings with their peers (Scardamalia, 2002).

Peer review exemplifies knowledge co-construction, as reviewers and authors engage in critical dialogue to enhance the quality and rigor of scholarly work (Shi & Wei, 2024). However, novice reviewers often experience uncertainty and overwhelm during the peer review process (Sun et al., 2024). Novice reviewers learn peer review skills by imitating experienced reviewers and through practice (Paltridge, 2013). A team-based coaching approach can help novice reviewers learn peer review skills by sharing service and scholarship with experienced reviewers (Yarris et al., 2017). Thus, understanding how the peer review process  and knowledge co-construction happen between experts, i.e., in academic peer review, is essential for better preparing novice reviewers and further helps them engage in co-constructing discourse.

Peer review  encompasses several phases of knowledge co-construction: sharing, identifying inconsistency, negotiating meaning, testing, and ap plying new knowledge (Shi & Wei, 2024). Reviewers and authors contribute uniquely to these phases;  for instance, authors may engage more in testing and applying new knowledge, while reviewers focus on identifying inconsistenc ies (Shi & Wei, 2004). Understanding these role differences enables us to map the progression of each phase. This study aims to explore linguistic characteristi cs in expert peer review, using features pertaining to knowledge co-construction. We selected features based on frameworks in argumentative knowledge construction (Weinberger & Fischer, 2006). Several dimensions (i.e., epistemic, argumentative, social  modes of co-construction) were conceptualized using linguistic features der ived from Linguistic Inquiry and Word Count (LIWC). In addition to the three dimensions in Wein berger and Fischer's framework, we added an emotion al dimension. LIWC is a computational program that identifies patterns by counting dictionary-based word frequency and calculating the percentage of specific linguistic attributes (Tausczik & Pennebaker, 2010). Table 1 provides an overview of the dimensions and linguist ic features. With these considerations in mind, we pose two research questions for the current study: RQ1: What are the characteristics of knowledge co-construction in the peer review? RQ2: How do authors and reviewers contribute to the peer review?

## Methods

We deductively coded  32 peer-review reports from a prestigious journal  (i.e., Nature Human Behavior). Each report was manually coded at the sentence level using Gunaward ena et al.'s (1997) knowledge co-construction phases (Table 2). Three researchers  independently pilot-tested the coding framework, resolving disagreement s collectively until achieving an inter-rater reliability score above 0.8. To address  RQ1, we reported each phase's frequency and LIWC features. For RQ2, we conducted a Chi-square test to examine the behaviors of reviewers and authors and explore semantic differences between authors and reviewers in knowledge co-construction phases. Our analytical measures pertain to argumentative knowledge construction (Weinberger & Fischer, 2006), aiming to describe  the linguistic characteristics of experts ’ knowledge co-construction process. This contrasts with approaches that seek to predict successful arg umentation or model the argumentative process (Nguyen & Young, 2022).

## Results

RQ1 What are the characteristics of knowledge co-construction in the peer review? In coding 32 peer-review documents, we analyzed 7,322 data lines, consisting of 3,038 from reviewers and 4,284 from authors. Among these, there were 90 unique reviewers and 32 authors. We revisited the original data for missing values and re-coded the data  as necessary. Our analysis revealed that “Negotiating Meaning ” was the most frequently occur ring phase (2,585  instances), followed by  “ Sharing/Adding” (1,504),  “Applying New Knowledge” (1,392), “Identifying Inconsistency” (1,230), and “Testing” (571). These counts illustrated the role of each phase in the co-construction of knowledge.

The LIWC percentages for different phases of knowledge co-construction showed variations (see Figure 1). In the first phase, the LIWC percentages for social, clout, and tone were high. In the second phase, cognition scored the highest among the five phases. The third and fourth phases had similar percentages for analytic, tone, and social scores, while the fifth phase exhibited the highest clout percentage. RQ2 How do authors and reviewers contribute to the peer review? Describing homogenous features of each phase cannot fully capture the differences between authors' and reviewers’ contributions to the knowledge co-construction phases. In addition tonegotiating mea ning, the contributions of reviewers and authors varied (Figure 2). We employed the Chi-Square test to analyze the participation  of behaviors between authors and reviewers. In the “Sharing and Adding” phase, reviewers (811) and authors (693) differed significantly, χ²(1) = 121.34, p ≤.01. Inconsistency was more frequent for reviewers (1,0 82) than authors (148), χ²(1) = 1319.03, p ≤.01. Negotiation of meaning showed no significant difference (review ers: 1,059; authors: 1,526), χ²(1) = 0.40, p =.53. Testing was low for reviewers (14) but higher for authors (557), χ²(1) = 338.42, p ≤.01. Applying new knowledge was observed more for authors (1,339) than reviewers (53), χ²(1) = 1005.19, p ≤.01. Authors' and reviewers' linguistic features differed across multiple phases of knowledge co-construction (Figure 3). Reviewers’ higher analytic percentages were concentrated in the first three phases: sharing and adding, identifying inconsistency, and negotiating meaning. In contrast, the authors’ higher analytic percentages centered on the last three phases: negotiating meaning, test ing, and applying new knowledge. The authors' and reviewers' cognition percentages varied in five phases, with their percentages being higher in the second phase. In considering social modes of knowledge co-construction, the authors’ social percentages fluctuated in the first phase despite having a higher mean.  Reviewers' social percentages varied in the fifth phase.  Compared to reviewers, authors consistently had  higher clout percentages across all phases. Reviewers separate d into two tone groups, with lower and higher tone percentages in each phase, while authors maintained relatively stable higher tone percentages in each phase.

## Discussion and conclusion

The process of knowledge co-construction in peer review is multifaceted, with d istinct contributions and role expectations from authors and reviewers. Our results showed that r eviewers primarily focus ed on setting the foundation by identifying inconsistencies and engag ing in negotiation to clarify these discrepancies. In contrast, authors were more involved in responding to reviewers' questions, testing suggested changes, and demonstrating how to integrate new insights to enhance  their work. This reflects an inherent imbalance in participation (Shi & Wei, 2024). However, this imbalance does not seem to affect the co-construction of the knowledge, as these manuscripts are accepted and published. Chen et al. (2023) found that high-performance groups shared equal participation in the argumentative co-construction of knowledge; however, this is not the case in our study. Thi s divergence might be due to peer review addressing t he late phase of knowledge co-construction, i.e., aiming at examining the quality and re fining the existing knowledge product rather than brainstorming from sc ratch. A division of labor emerges and does not affect the co-construction of knowledge. High LIWC percentages of social, clout, and tone indicated the positive relationship-building dynamic in the first phase of knowledge co-construction within expert peer review. Compared to the authors’ stable tone percentages, reviewers emerged as two distinctive groups based on their tone percentages. Reviewers’ positive tone echoes affective feedback in students’ peer feedback and does not affect how the receiver responds to them (Tan & Chen, 2022; Gao et al., 2024). However, we have limited clues about what affective feedback means to experts and their knowledge co-construction process. Appraisal seems to be a norm in academic peer review, and if it does not contribute to the process, why should reviewers and authors keep doing it? LIWC results revealed different confidence levels between authors and rev iewers. Authors consistently had high percentages of clout across all five phases, indicating confidence and a ssertiveness in defending their research. Meanwhile, experienced reviewers exhibit ed relatively lower  percentages of clout in their feedback, reflecting a more tentative stance. This observatio n further develops Watts et al. (2023), who noted that novice reviewers were likelier to pose questions rather thandirectly express disagreement. Our findings suggest that even expert reviewers may avoid overt  absolute statements, potentially due to the awareness that their comments will be published along with the published manuscript.

Variations in social percentages between authors and reviewers hinted at an interesting power dynamic within the knowledge co-construction process. Authors’  social percentages peaked during the initial phase, indicating an effort to express appreciation to reviewers early on. Conversely, reviewers’ social percentages increased in the concluding phase, which may reflect  a supportive closure to their review or an attempt to reestablish an equal standing after offering critique. These patterns illustrate the complex interpersonal  dynamics that shape the collaborative yet asymmetrical nature of knowledge co-construction in peer review  (Ross & Heggestad, 2020).

Our analysis of linguistic features explores the complicated scene of knowledge co-construction between reviewers and authors in academic peer review. However, there is a long way to go to achieve the goal of preparing novice peer reviewers based on these linguistic features f indings. Based on what we have done so far, as novice reviewers, reading and trying to understand published peer review documents seems to be a more beneficial way to learn how to do peer review like an experienced scholar. We acknowledge d the limitations of this study. As the framework (Weinberger & Fischer, 2006) suggested, written linguistic documents may not represent relevant cognitive processe s well. We realized that LIWC could only capture part of the linguistic features rather than the complete context and image of the argumentation knowledge co-construction process in academic peer review. Thus, building on this study, we aim to triangulate our current exploration with more in-depth data, such as interviews and focus groups.

Our findings highlight the complexity of the peer review process and the challenges of preparing novice reviewers. With the increasing need for reviewers due to more conferenc es and journals, we advocate for further research to explore best practices in preparing novice scholars, such as graduate students and early-career scholars, for peer review, particularly in learning sciences, STEM education, and other interdisciplinary fields.
