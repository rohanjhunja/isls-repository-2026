# “CO2 Was a Wanderer”: Interdisciplinary Imaginings in a Teen Science-Based Creative Writing Workshop

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
As teens and adolescents are developing personal interests and imagining potential futures, it can be an exciting time for educators and trusted adults to make connections across domains and interests explicit. In this study, teens participated in an out-of-school science-based creative writing workshop. This work is based on the idea that STEAM (science, technology, engineering, arts, and mathematics) approaches can lead to meaningful learning, and that opportunities to create and construct are especially powerful. Findings from the analysis of written artifacts show that participants used science in their creative writing in a variety of ways, and that they imagine multiple roles for science in their current or future lives. Implications of this work suggest some ways educators might consider the relationships between identity and power, STEM/STEAM learning, and Connected Learning when designing opportunities for adolescents.

## Introduction

Teens and adolescents are at a complex stage of lif e—one where they may often be told what to do and wha t to care about while their personal interests are devel oping and transitioning from childhood toward adult hood (Crocetti, 2017; Schachter & Ventura, 2008). This m akes adolescence an important time to make connecti ons explicit between and across the multiple spheres of  their lives. Teens may worry about climate change,  be concerned about equality and inclusivity, write fan-fiction online, and consume movies, books, and online media. Educators and trusted adults can help adolescents e xplore the connections and relationships between th ese areas and to use these interests to think forward toward possible futures (Schachter & Ventura, 2008). This approach may be made harder by the prevalence of disciplinary siloes in schools. While studies have shown STEAM (science, technology, engineering,  arts, and mathematics) approaches that lead to str onger learning outcomes for more diverse groups of learners (Fletcher & Hernandez-Gantes, 2020; Hughes et al., 2022), school-based curricula tend to continue to separate individual disciplines (Termaat, 2023), rendering students less likely to see the relevance of various disciplines to their area of interest (Carrell et al., n.d.), or the role of creativity in scientific processes (Aguilera & Ortiz-Revilla, 2021). The current study sought to make connections betwee n science and creative writing explicit for a group of teens in an out-of-school writing workshop. Because creative writing i s not often considered as an art in discussions of STEAM learning, we also sough t to explore a conceptualization of STEAM that has not received as much attention in the field of learning sciences. Thus, we asked:

1. How do teens engage during a science-focused creative writing workshop?

a. What genres, themes, and topics did they explore in their creative writing? b. What science did they incorporate into their creative writing? c. To what ends was science used in their creative writing?

2. What might this mean for how teens imagine about and use science to make meaning in their own

lives?

## Background

Interests in adolescence Prior research has shown that while young children tend to be interested in STEM topics, this interest  often declines throughout primary and secondary school (S taus et al., 2020), most drastically during the tra nsition between primary and secondary school (Potvin & Hans i, 2014), indicating that teenagers and adolescents  have lower overall interest in these subjects. However, participation in out-of-school STEM activities (Staus et al., 2020), including visits to science museums (Potvin & Hansi, 2014), can i ncrease interest in STEM. Also, connecting science with issues such as climate chan ge and community change can increase youth interest  in science and their ideas about its relevance to their everyday lives (Vance-Chalcraft et al., 2024). Alternatively, adolescence is a time of life primed  for development of self-concept (Sebastian et al., 2008), exploration of creativity (Russ, 2022), and attunement of a personal sense of justice (Pandey et al., 2023). Teens today consume vast amounts of media (Twenge et al., 2019) and many also engage in creative storytelling through writing (Vetter et al., 2021) and multimedia production (Gliniecka, 2021) in their free time. Teens like to write about topics including current events, teen i ssues, and politics (Shippen et al., 2007). This su ggests that integrating science and creative writing in an out-of-school workshop may be an avenue for encouraging engagement with science in teens.

STEAM and possibilities of interdisciplinary learning STEAM is a term loaded with both controversy and po tential. The term STEAM has been taken up to varyin g degrees of fidelity and academic rigor. It has been argued that STEAM is most advantageous when the STEM or science elements and the artistic or creative elements are equally consequential and important (Mejias et al., 2020). An example of such an environment might be e-textiles, where both the textile arts and the scien ce of circuitry are equally important to the production of a functioning and appealing final product (Peppler & Wohlwend, 2018). As such, when STEAM environments are high-quality, they have potential to broaden what accept able engagement with STEM or science can look like and who can participate in it, thus inviting more and more diverse learners into STEM learning spaces (Peppler & Wohlwen d, 2018). Further, true transdisciplinary integrati on of arts and sciences in academic spaces can support le arners to leverage the epistemic practices of both,  creating more robust and lasting learning (Bevan et al., 201 9). We view the writing workshop highlighted in thi s work as a STEAM space where both engagement with scientific  ideas and the craft of creative writing were given  equal weight, aligning with a mutually instrumental balance of STEM and the arts (Mejias et al., 2020). Creative writing is underexplored in research on ST EAM education, but some prior work has demonstrated positive outcomes from making explicit connections between stories in multiple forms and science. While participants in Mawasi et al.’s 2021 study did not produce their own creative writing products, the research showed that engaging with Mary Shelley’s Frankenstein helped adolescents conceptualize and concretize mor al and ethical implications of science. In other work,  some youth engaged in personal story telling and (re)storying practices through making electronic quilt patches (Shaw et al., 2023), and others improved their understanding of computational thinking through digital making and c reative writing (Huang et al., 2024). Together, the se studies suggest that there is potential for learning at the intersection between stories and science. Our work, then, expands on this prior research by focusing on how a worksho p that provided specific instruction around both sc ience and creative writing impacted how teens use science in their writing projects. Theoretical frameworks: Constructionism and connected learning Our work builds on two complementary frameworks for  learning: constructionism and connected learning. Constructionism posits that learning happens in pow erful ways through the creation of artifacts and th e sharing of those artifacts for reflection with others (Papert, 1980). Although creative writing may not seem like a tangible product, Papert (1980) stated that even theories and abstract ideas fit in this framework as they are developed, tinkered with, and shared. In the current work, par ticipants developed multiple artifacts and different versions of those artifacts, tinkering by drafting and redrafti ng. They also received feedback from the workshop f acilitators and their peers and practiced both giving feedback and incorporating feedback into future drafts of their work. In this way, learning happed through processes of creating, sharing, and revising. This workshop also drew on ideas from connected learning, the idea that “learning is most enduring and meaningful when supported by peers and mentors, rooted in the interests and culture of the learner, and connected across settings” (Ito et al., 2020, p. 6). Participants self-selected into this workshop based on prior interest s in either writing, science, or both. Further, some par ticipants engaged in related activities before and after this workshop through connections made by adult mentors (more on this in the discussion). Connected learning seeks to help learners find opportunities for academic success, career advancement, or civic engagement through strong relationships and making connections between learning settings explicit and meaningful. This can lead to learners imagining new and diverse future pathways for themselves (Ito et al., 2020). Thus, as a constructionist and connected learning e nvironment, this workshop intended to encourage creative expression while helping teens relate scie nce to their other interests and their everyday liv es. While we did not necessarily intend for participants to memo rize science content facts, we d id seek to encourage STEM literacy through engagement with and creative use of scientific ideas (Polman et al., 2014). The workshop sought to allow space for learners to break out of the nar row definitions of both science and writing that sc hool spaces create. We share more about the specific design and implementation of the workshop in the methods section.

## Participants

This study included 11 participants. Inclusion crit eria required participants to be fluent in English and to be between 9th and 12th grade. Participants alsoneeded to have the ability to travel to the city’s Museum of Science where the workshop would take place. Existing interest in science or creative writing was encouraged and no prior experience was required. Participants were recruited from the city and suburban high schools through flyers. These flyers were hung in locations where high school stu dents might congregate, and also emailed directly to a fe w high school teachers and to organizers of teen community programs. One of the researchers had a colleague who had recently conducted a creative writing workshop for teens and that colleague sent the recruitment materials to the roster from that class. Recruitment was also conducted via the research institution’s research registry, a list of local people interested in participating in researc h. Participants completing the workshop represented ag es 14 to 18 years old and grades 9 to 12. Participants self-identified as Asian (3), Mixed (2), Black/African American (3), White (3); three participants were multilingual; six female, three male, and two gender nonconforming. In the first four sessions, each workshop introduce d participants to a specific science topic related to carbon, paired with instruction in a creative writi ng genre. These sessions encouraged participants to experiment with different forms of creative writing and to integrate scientific concepts into their writing. From Session 5 onward, the focus shifted from the i ntroduction of writing forms to the development of a single, sustained writing project. The goal was f or students to leave the workshop with a completed piece of writing that they felt proud of. In Session  5 participants learned about carbon allotropes from  chemistry researchers and worked to formulate a plan for exte nding their writing projects. They were asked to ch oose a genre to focus on and set personal goals for the sh ape and direction of their final piece. Session 6 provided dedicated time for students to work on their projec ts in a supportive environment. In addition to writ ing, participants had access to a variety of resources —books, articles, and online materials —to further explore the specific carbon-related concepts they wanted to incorporated into their projects. In Session 7 participants brought drafts of their writing to the workshop. In pairs they shared their current drafts and provided each other feedback on how they might extend and improve  their writing. In Session 8 each participants read their draft aloud to the class and the other participants provided verbal feedback—both complimentary and constructive. The aim was to encourage the participants to engage in one last ro und of revisions and edits so they could finish the  workshop with a polished piece of writing. A few weeks after  the workshop, the participants were invited back t o the museum to read their own work publicly.

## Data collection

Data were collected in the forms of semi-structured pre-and post-interviews, a demographic survey, video and field notes from the workshop, and artifacts of the participants’ work throughout the workshop. Because this paper primarily focuses on the youth-produced artifacts, we will only briefly mention the other data sources here. Pre-interviews were conducted within the first two sess ions of the workshop, and post-interviews were conducted after the conclusion of the workshop. The interviews, lasting about 10 minutes each, were mostly audio recorded, but some were also video recorded via Zoom. The pre-interviews addressed the participants’ interest in and experiences with science and creative writing, and the post interviews asked about the participants’ thoughts about the workshop, their writing pieces, and their  conceptions about science and creative writing. During each workshop, field notes were collected. Also, video was collected using two 360 cameras. At the end of each session, we took photos of the participants’ writing for later analysis. This included planning and drafts, collective writing, and final works. These writing pieces consisted of shape poems, zines and multimodal works, creative non-fiction, fiction, and poetry. A demographic survey was given  to the participants during the last day of the wor kshop, prompting the participants for their name, age, grade, school, gender identity, pronouns, racial/ethnic identity, and languages spoken.  The prompts were left open ended to allow participa nts to respond as they saw fit as this practice has been shown to better capture the complexities of identity (Harris et al., 2017).

## Data analysis

The data were analyzed using perspectives from refl ective thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2019), as  the coauthors worked collaboratively to iteratively rev iew the data as themes emerged. We started by colla ting the information from the demographic surveys as well as attendance records across all eight s essions. Then, we split the interviews evenly among three of the coauthors and separately summarized the participants’ answers  to the interview questions. The analysis and findings from  the interview data will be discussed in more detail in future work.

Subsequently, we moved to the written artifacts and first split the data among all four authors by artifact type to examine with an eye toward genre, science c ontent, literary themes, and writing techniques. Th rough the process, we reviewed the data as a group and ensured consensus before taking further steps. Although we did this for all artifacts generated during the workshop, for this article, we focus on the drafts of final projects. After the initial passes and discussions of the final projects, we split the data again so each author would focus on a different category (genre, science conte nt, literary themes, and writing techniques). This ensured that each author examined each final artifact and also p articipated in consensus-building discussions across each participant’s artifact as well as across all categories of interest. Finally, through these discussions, themes emerged about not just the use of science in writing, but w hat the uses and presence of science in the writing indicat es about how the participants imagine as the multiple roles of science in their current and future lives.  This line of inquiry was not a pre-determined plan for the analysis, but emerged as relevant as we worked to look beyond the science content to the deeper implications of the science in the teens’ writing. The emergent themes were further informed by our frameworks of constructionism and c onnected learning. Thus, we attended to how the cre ation of these  personally meaningful written artifacts allowed the  teens to tinker with new, imaginative ideas that connected back to their past and current lives and interests. The authors discussed these themes and went back to the data multiple times to refine and articulate the themes and select representative examples for presentation. Future work will follow similar processes to look across all the writing pieces developed throughout the workshop. We also note that all names presented here are pseudonyms.

## Discussion and conclusion

Our findings demonstrate that when prompted and supported to include science in their creative writing projects, teens utilize a wide range of science concepts and demonstrate complex engagement with some of the way s science plays a role in their everyday lives. The o utcomes of this work also highlight relevant implic ations for how we as researchers, educators, and designers mig ht think about the unique position and learning spa ce that adolescents occupy.

Identity and power It has been argued that youth overall are an oppressed group (DeJong & Love, 2016), because they are subject to a more powerful group (adults) constantly making choices for and about their lives and that youth often have little to no agency to impact those choices. This can lead to youth seeking to take back moments of agency, power, and time wherever possible in order to exert some contr ol over their own lives. Evidence of this is visible in many of the stories and projects that youth produced in this workshop. Several of the final pieces involved protagonists in moments of losing control, fighting to gain control, or recognizing a profound lack of control over th eir lives or the fate of the world. It is important to note agai n that these types of narratives are not necessaril y unique to this group of teens in this workshop. However, the imagi ned roles of science in these power struggles do il luminate some of the ways that science, writing, identity, a nd power come together as teens imagine both specul ative and far-fetched futures. In practice, this might suggest that educators could consider the ways adolescent students are given space to exercise some power and agency in classrooms and educational experiences. STEM learning or science literacy As noted by Polman et al. (2014) writing about scie nce can help students become “competent outsiders,” individuals who have the science literacy to find and understand scientific information and to use that information in a way relevant to their everyday life. Polman et al. (2014) argue that in addition to traditional science learning such as memorizing scientific facts and understanding the research processes used by scientists, engaged citizens alsoneed to “have means to find, evaluate, and make sense of new scientific and technical information that cannot be predicted or completely and comprehensively taug ht in high school” (p. 768). The ability to look fo r and understand the science information that is relevant to a particular situation allows individuals to “understand how new science fits within the cont ext of the fields of inquiry from which it originat es, and also contextualize that science within broader societal concerns, such as e conomics and ethics” (Polman et al., 2014, p. 768).  This workshop allowed students to decide what science content was most relevant to their creative writing projects and then to relate that science to their storylines by integrating it into their writing. A unique aspect of this work is that in contrast to  the science journalism at the core of the cited Po lman et al. (2014) work here, the teens in this workshop  did not have to use entirely accurate or practical depictions of science in their work. Since they were not tasked w ith communicating science facts to the public, they were able to make some creative choices about the role of science. While there is an argument that completely accurate use of science could still be a goal of a science-based creative writing workshop, we were interested  to see the teens deeply and authentically grappling in their writing  with the ethics of science and how science might i mpact the future of humanity. This also seemed to allow the t eens to process some of their emotional responses to science in their lives such as climate anxiety or environme ntal empathy. In practice, this might mean that eve n formal science spaces could explore the possibilities of imagined science as a tool for supporting science literacy. Connected learning As mentioned above, several of the participants lea rned about our workshop because they had participat ed in a previous writing workshop that was specifically cen tered on writing about climate and the environment.  One of the authors of the current work was able to speak w ith the facilitator of the other workshop and then reach out to the participants as a recruitment opportunity. This is an example of connections at the intersection of relationships, interests, and opportunities that can be possible i n a connected learning environment (Ito et al., 202 0). Several teens were able to begin or extend their creative w riting practices and meet other peers with similar interests. Further, a few of the participants reached out at the conclusion of the workshop to join the Museum’s Teen STEM Initiative which seeks to bring together teens with interests in science to help decide on programming and activities for the Museum and surrounding communities. This demonstrates the possibilities of a science museum as a local community hub, especially when trusted to act as opportunity brokers, and when interdisciplinarity is a value.

## Limitations and future work

We recognize that this small study is not representative but rather illustrative of possibilities at the intersection of science and creative writing. Future analyses will look more closely at the interview data and the vid eo data that were generated during the course of the workshop. Further, we plan to use techniques and processes from designbased research (e.g. DBR Collective, 2003) to iterate upon this workshop for future versions.
