# Embodied Transfer of Mathematical Ideas Through Gestural Replay

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study examines how students embodied their ideas about geometric relationships and how those ideas transfer between groups. High school students used The Hidden Village – Online, a motion capture video game that allows them to create new content through movements and perform these movements while evaluating the veracity of geometric conjectures. Each group (1) co-designed directed actions—a sequence of movements representing a body-based interpretation of the structure and transformation of mathematical objects—for other groups and (2) played the game with conjectures and directed actions created by their peers. Qualitative analyses of multiple cases demonstrated that students used gestures to develop directed actions that simulated the generalized geometric properties. When directed actions were spontaneously replayed by other groups, these gestural replays facilitated a form of distributed transfer. This study offers new insights into redesigning mathematics curricula that emphasize embodied interactions to support generalization and transfer.

## Introduction

Geometric thinking at the secondary education level requires students to reason about generalizable relationships between space and shap e (Hershkowitz, 2020). This type of reasoning is foundational to various contexts, including higher math ematics, sciences, arts, and daily activities like navigation  and design. E ngaging in geometric reasoning requires analysis of visual and  perceptual information, often using  spatiotemporal dimensions. Consequently, geometric reasoning is a grounded and embodied process. Indeed, a growing corpus of empirical research in mathematical thinking demo nstrates that students often understand mathematica l ideas through perceptual systems and actions (Abrahamson et al., 2020; Goldstone et al., 2008). However, a chasm remains between this research and instructional practice, especially in the classroom (Nathan, 2021). The present research seeks to bridge this gap.

Theories of grounded and embodied cognition  posit that cognition is based on perceptual and motoric processes (Barsalou, 2008) and that activating  these processes during memory encoding and retrieval enhances reasoning and learning. Grounded and embodied learn ing (GEL; Nathan, 2021) appl ies these accounts to educational contexts. For example, mathematical knowledge and reasoning are understood in operative language (Pier et al., 2019), spatial metaphors (Lakoff & Núñez, 2000),  and body states and actions (Alibali & Nathan, 2012; Gerofsky, 2011). These find ings suggest that geometric reasoning and proof production are embodied (Marghetis et al., 2014). Theories of GEL advocate for instructional activities that leverage students’ perceptions and actions to influence their geometric reasoning. For example, instructional activit ies that direct students to perform task-relevant actions enhance their ability to generate mathematically valid proofs (Nathan et al., 2014). Proof practices are a key component of geometric reasoning in secondary e ducation (National Council of Teachers of Mathematics, 2000). Proof practices allow students to evaluate the veracity of conjectu res and justify their conclusions. Transformational proofs are one type of proof that aligns with the disciplinary discourse of mathematicians who construct mathematical knowle dge. Harel and Sowder (2007)  operationalize transformational proofs as reasoning that meets three criteria: (1) exhibit generality, (2) use operational thought, and (3) follow a chain of logical inference. These criteria allow students to reason about mathematical i deas in ways similar to those in the discipline, encouraging mathematical intuition, insight, and wonder. In practice, students and teachers spontaneously use hand and arm gestures while teaching and learning about mathematical thinking  (Alibali & Nathan, 2012). For example, when evaluating proofs, novice stude nts invoke spontaneous verbal and gestural explanations  rather than formal, written ones  (Healy & Hoyles, 2000). Research has shown a strong correlation between the production of dynamic depictive gestures – gestures that enact motion-based transformations of mathematical objects – and the construction of mathematically valid proofs (Nathan et al., 2021; Pier et al., 2019).  One hypothesis emerging from this work is that inte rventions directing students to perform bodily movements (i.e., directed actions) derived from these gestures during proof production can enhance their ability to generate mathematically valid proofs. Indeed, recent research has found a moderating relationship between  students’ use of gestures that replay the directed actions  from earlier training and th eir production of valid proofs  on subsequent proof tasks (Walkington et al., 2022). We take this as evidence for an embodied account of transfer (Nathan & Alibali, 2021).

The embodied theory of transfer proposes that the t ransfer of knowledge is rooted in one’s ability to extend and reapply conceptually relevant actions across tasks and contexts (Nathan & Alibali, 2021). Nathan and Alibali further suggest that transfer can be indicated when directed actions from earlier training are replayed as spontaneous gestures that affect subsequent reasoning. While GEL research is expanding this notion of embodied transfer, the conditions under which embodied instructional interventions give rise to such transfer remain unclear. To date, most GEL interventions involve investigato r-generated directed action s in individual contexts (e.g., Walkington et al., 2022). It remains underexplored how students can generate their own directed actions and how these student-generated actions might influence reasoning and transfer in collaborative classrooms. This qualitative study addresses this gap by  investigating how students can create directed actions for their peers to perform as they reason about geometr ic proofs. We further investigate whether and how students replay these actions during proof production, and whether such replay supports mathematical reasoning. Our empirical findings contribute to the theory of embodied transfer of knowledge, which proposes that sensorimotor experiences, rather than verbal rules or symbolic f ormalisms, facilitate the transfer of conceptual knowledge by reenacting perception and action processes and thei r corresponding cognitive states in new contexts (N athan & Alibali, 2021).

## Theoretical framework

Theories of GEL leverage the reciprocal connection between action and cognition to support students’ conceptual learning. Evidence for GEL shows that reasoning and learning improve when lear ners spontaneously produce goal-directed gestures, are prompted to perform directed actions, or engage in simulated actions such as replaying actions from earlier trials (Nathan & Walkington, 2017; Walkington et al., 2022). Spontaneous gestures support geometric reasoning Gesture plays an important role in problem-solving (McNeil, 1992), including in mathematical reasoning (Nathan, 2024). Gestures are spontaneous arm and hand movements that often accompany speech and thought. Functionally, gestures enact mental simulations (Hostetter & Alibali, 2019) and, in mathematical contexts, candepict conceptual metaphors or enact operations on mathematical objects  (Alibali & Nathan, 2012). Gestures not only facilit ate communication, but they also augment and enhance thought (Novack & Goldin-Meadow, 2015). Some gestures support mathematical reasoning by ena cting mental simulations of mathematical transformations (Hostetter & Alibali, 2019). In doing so, these simulated actions can augment communicati on about spatiotemporal information (Beilock & Goldin-Meadow, 2010), facilitate encoding (So et al., 2014), and support the generalization of cognitive processes  (Novack et al., 2014). Dynamic depictive gestures  are one specific type of simulated actions that enact spatial-temporal transformations of mathematical entities (Garcia & Infante, 2012). For example, a dynamic depictive ge sture might illustrate the invariance of a triangle ’s interior angles under dilation by re-scaling (transforming) its size. The production of dynamic depictive gestures has been shown to predict students’ production of generalized mathema tical proofs (e.g., Nathan et al., 2021; Pier et al., 2019). Therefore, one goal for GEL research is to e xplore ways to facilitate students’ task-relevant gesture production during encoding and subsequent reasoning (Walkington et al., 2022). Engaging students to collaborate may enhance gestur e production, as gesture rates tend to increase in a social context (Alibali & Nathan, 2012). Gestures c an serve as a means to represent spatiotemporal inf ormation while students communicate and build a shared understanding of the problem space (Roschelle & Teasley, 1995). By fostering shared common ground, collaborative co ntexts create opportunities for meaningful gesture use (Nathan & Alibali, 2011). Thus, there is value in d eveloping collaborative embodied interventions that  promote gestures and other bodily movements for supporting reasoning and learning. Directed actions promote gesture production and geometric reasoning While gestures are often spontaneous, directed action s—physical movements that learners are instructed to perform—can also influence cognition and gesture production (Donovan et al., 2014; Goldin-Meadow, 2011). In this study, we use the term directed actions, which aligns with what some studies refer to as directed or instructed movements (e.g., Zhang et al., 2021). Prior research found that performing directed actions during a training phase increases gesture production (Donovan et al., 2014). For example, Cook & Goldin-Meadow (2006) demonstrated that children were more likely to produce gestures when given instructions that included directed actions related to a solution strategy. Producing gestures, in turn, enhanced their problem-solving performance on a post-test compared to children who conveyed the solution strategy using speech alone. When directed actions are designed to be conceptually relevant, they can enhance reasoning and learning. For example, Nathan and Walkington (2017) demonstra ted that performing investigator-generated, task-relevant directed actions  led to significantly more correct mathematical intu itions (snap judgments) and key insights compared to matched actions that were conceptually irrelevant to the tasks. Notably, this effect occur red even when participants were unaware of the task-relevance of the directed actions. Gestural replays facilitate embodied transfer Recent findings, however, suggest that directed action s alone are insufficient for fostering key mathemat ical insights and valid proofs (Walkington et al., 2022). Instead, the effectiveness of directed actions for supporting geometric proof production relies on their reactivation during students’ explanations as they justify their reasoning.

The study demonstrated that students’ reasoning improved significantly when their explanations included gestures that replayed task-relevant directed actions performed during training. Qualitative analyses showed how gestural replays of earlier directed action s directly contributed to students’ abilities to transfer information from training to later formulation of valid proofs that were general, operational, and logically sound. Gestural replays are explanatory gestures  that match the directed actions previously given to students. Gestural replays  can be either exact replays—the same actions performed with the same body parts —or corresponding replays, in which the same actions are performed using different body parts (Xia et al., 2025). Prior work has shown that gestural replays moderate the effect of directed act ions on geometric proof performance. Specifically, they predict better mathe matical insight and proof performance, especially when the  replays are dynamic depictive gestures (Walkington et al., 2022; Xia et al., 2025). Gestural replay is consistent with an embodied theo ry of transfer (Alibali & Nathan, 2021). From an embodied perspective, transfer occurs “when learner s and teachers establish cohesion of their experien ces by mapping modes of perceiving and acting that they successfully used in one context to a new context.” (Alibali & Nathan, 2021, p. 34). This cohesion is often expressed through speech and gestures. In mathematics, transfer takes place when the ways of perceiving and acting on a m athematical relation in one context can be extended and reapplied in another. Gestural replays are theorized to facilitate this transfer process by highlighting the invariant properties of mathematical objects and relations that are embodied in the directed actions. As a form of embodied “revoicing,” gestural replays help learners bridge between concrete actions and conceptual abstraction s (Novack et al., 2015), enabling them to generalize insights across tasks and contexts. Despite these theoretical insights, few empirical studies have explored whether and how gestural replays facilitate the transfer of knowledge in abstract do mains such as geometry proof. Specifically, little attention has been given to whether student-generated directed actions can influence other students’ mathematical reason ing or whether gestural replays of student-generated directed actions can support a form of distributed transfer from one group of students to another group. Notably, student-generated movements may align more closely with students’ emerging understanding of geometric proof s compared to movements selected by experts (e.g., investigators). Inviting students to generate these actions could alleviate time constraints on instructors and make scaling up these methods more efficient and more effective.

## Research questions

This paper documents interactions from a collaborative classroom where h igh school students communicated geometric ideas and collaboratively designed directed action s for their peers. We investigated two research questions: (RQ1) How do student groups collaboratively co-construct directed actions intended to foster geometric reasoning for other  student groups? (RQ2): How do student-generated directed action s impact other groups’ mathematical reasoning and proof production ? We hypothesize that (H1) when student groups encode their mathematical ideas into directed actions, this process will lead to deeper mathematical insights for the designers, and (H2) when other groups spontaneously replay these directed actions through gestures, the gestural replays will support the transfer of knowledge and enhance mathematical reasoning.

## Participants

Fifteen high school students enrolled in a “Psychology of Learning and Motivation” course within an acceleratedlearning summer program at a large Midwestern U.S. university participated in this study. This in situ study was embedded into the curriculum of a lesson about embodied cognition. Participants were divided into six dyads and one triad to design new content and play an embodied video game. Materials The Hidden Village – Online (THV-O) is a web-based motion-capture video game designed to foster learning of transformational proofs for high school geometry th rough embodied interactions  (Fogel et al., 2022). We used THV-O to create an embodied geometry curriculum. Players were directed to emulate the movements of a n ingame avatar (i.e., directed action s) to advance by successfully mirroring these actions. Afterward, students were presented with a geometry conjecture (a statement t hat is either always true or false) and asked to assess its veracity, providing a verbal explanation to justify their assessment. A key component of THV-O is the Pose Editor, which allows students to crea te custom, action-based game content. Using the Pose Editor, students can collaboratively create specific directed actions using their hands, arms, or upper body, for each geometry conjecture. These movements were captured by THV-O to generate three poses (i.e., start, intermediate, and end; see Figure 1). Pose sequences are animated for preview, saved, and stored in an online database for use as directed actions in future gameplay. Figure 1 Creating a Directed Action (Left: THV-O Pose Editor. ## Procedure

The embodied curriculum was integrated into the embodied cognition class that began with a brief lecture on the role of embodiment in mathematics education. Next, student groups used the Pose Editor, guided by work sheets with prompts, to create directed action s for a provided conjecture. Specifically, each gro up was assigned a conjecture, either true or false, and asked to collaborativel y justify it. At the same time, they were encouraged to explore the possible movements and select three specific poses (i.e., start, intermediate, and end) to illustrate the conjecture (see Figure 1). These poses were saved in THV-O. Groups then played THV-O by performing directed actions created by other groups  (see Figure 2) and assessing the veracity of each c onjecture with a verbal explanation. Each group completed three conjectures. The session concluded with a group discussion, wher e students reflected on their design process and gameplay. Students’ speech and bodily actions were video recorded, transcribed, and coded throughout the co-design, gameplay, and discussion sessions. This pap er focuses on analyzing data from students’ co-design and gameplay sessions.

## Discussion

Embodied interactions offer promising pathways to reimagine mathematics curricula through bodily movements. Prior research has shown that directing students to  perform task-relevant, investigator-generated directed actions can enhance mathematical reasoning and learning (e.g., Abrahamson & Trninic, 2015; Nathan et al., 2014; Zhang et al., 2021). The present study expands this line of research by exploring whether and how student-generated directed actions influence peers’ geometric reasoning in a collaborative setting. To ground new ideas, we engaged students in sensorimotor processes using THV-O, which enabled them to create and perform movemen ts via ingame directed actions embodying geometric shapes and relationships. Our  findings demonstrate how a student group used dynamic depictive gestures as directed actions to nonverbally communicate mathema tical ideas and relationships valuable for assessing the veracity o f geometric conjectures (RQ1). When other student g roups played THV-O performing these directed actions, their subsequent explanations spontaneously included exact and corresponding gestural replays of earlier actions that appeared to facilitate the transfer of those mathematical ideas and benefit their geometric reasoning (RQ2).

In Group 1, students produced co-speech gestures to communicate their ideas about the Opposite Angles conjecture during their discussion and design of directed actions. These gestures served two primary functions in their communication. First, gestures were used to establish and maintain common ground among speakers (Nathan & Alibali, 2011). Students employed gestures to gui de their peers’ attention and delineate share d referents, supporting effective communication and collaborativ e reasoning. Second, students used dynamic depictiv e gestures to represent and simulate the movement of two lines, demonstrating—through body-based simulations— the generalized relationship between “any” intersec ting lines and the angles they form. Notably, their  verbal explanations did not reflect this generalized reaso ning, indicating that gesture can convey complement ary and unique information that reveals students’ thought processe s (Church & Goldin-Meadow, 1986; Goldin-Meadow et al., 1993). Furthermore, students subsequently refined these gestures into a sequence of directed actions, starting with two forearms forming intersecting lines and sc aling to full-arm movements upward and downward to represent the lines and angles. Like the dynamic de pictive gestures, this sequence of directed action s also highlights the invariant congruence of opposite angles even as they varied the angles, embodying the generalizable geometric relationships they were meant to convey.

Students in Groups 2 and 3 performed the in-game directed action s designed by Group 1 and subsequently justified the Opposite Angles  conjecture. Their justifications included gestural replays of these directed actions, either as exact replicas, or corresponding repla ys using different body parts (crossed fingers) to form the same shapes. These gestural replays of stu dent-generated directed actions supported student groups in generating correct mathematical intuitions and insights, consistent with and extending prior studies on the effects of investigator-generated directed actions on reasoning and learning (Walkington et al., 2022). We hypothesize that these gestural replays demonstr ate the transfer of knowledge between groups. An embodied account of transfer suggests that sensorim otor experiences, rather than verbal rules or symbo lic formalisms, can facilitate transfer of conceptual knowledge by reenacting modes of perception and act ion and their corresponding cognitive states in new contexts (Nathan & Alibali, 2021). In our study, transfer among groups occurred as gestural replays enacted key invariant relations of the Opposite Angles conjecture during explanations (e.g., Figures 5a and 5b). These gestures, as simul ated actions, schematized the idealized mathematical relations important for reasoning about the Opposite Angles  conjecture in embodied ways (Kita et al., 2017). Th is schematizing function of gestural replays may help students form embodied abstractions of key invariant relations, thus facilitating transfer across contexts.

This analysis is limited to the behavior of three g roups of high schoolers who are reasoning about one conjecture. Still, this study offers two insights. First, it highlights the value of eliciting student s’ body-based resources to support successful mathematical reasoning and proof. Prompting students to design directed actions can give rise to gestures that simulate geometric t ransformations, helping to scaffold their reasoning. Second, it reveals that the embodied transfer of knowledge can  occur as students engage in simulated actions, suc h as producing gestural replays of directed actions from earlier training. This study demonstrates some of the promise of conceptual learning using embodied curricula that emphasize students’ cognitively relevant movements as a complement to logic-based, written forms that dominate traditional math ematics classes. One area of future research will explore how to support teachers in guiding their students to co-design embodied actions, helping to further bridge the gap between research and classroom practice.
