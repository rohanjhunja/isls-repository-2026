# Facilitating High School Students’ Socially Shared Regulation of Learning Through STEM Inquiry into Local Air Quality and Asthma

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This exploratory study examines how collaborative learning environments mediated by mobile technology facilitate socially shared regulation of learning (SSRL) in high school STEM education. Ten high school students in three small groups investigated local air quality and asthma using handheld sensors to collect indoor and outdoor data. We analyzed 3.5 hours of video data using interaction and thematic analysis guided by the cognitive dimensions of SSRL. Findings show that SSRL emerged most prominently in moments of uncertainty which catalyzed collective monitoring, hypothesis generation, and goal-directed planning. Three design features supported these processes: (1) structured autonomy that shifted troubleshooting from external support to student-led, (2) authentic inquiry that turned technological breakdowns into regulatory opportunities, and (3) real-world constraints that extended collaboration. The study contributes to understanding SSRL as a naturally occurring phenomenon in authentic high school STEM contexts and offers design principles for leveraging uncertainty for facilitating SSRL.

K-12 STEM education increasingly calls for collaborative, interdisciplinary inquiry that connects school science to real community problems. Bioinformatics, an emerging STEM field, offers such a context by linking genetics, environment, and health in ways that position biology as a living, socially relevant discipline (Yoon et al., 2025). In many urbandistricts, asthma and poor air quality disproportionately affect students and families and making local air quality investigation a compelling and personally meaningful topic for STEM learning. To capitalize on this kind of socio-scientific issue, students must not only engage with complex real-world content but also to regulate their own learning individually and collectively (Michalsky & Cohen, 2021). Research on socially shared regulation of learning (SSRL) demonstrates that groups can regulate behavior, cognition, and affect at the collective level  (Hadwin et al., 2018). However, much of this work has examined SSRL under conditions of external support or interventions such as structured classroom/online learning environments, digital tools  including AI, or direct teacher facilitation (Järvenoja et al., 2020; Kim et al., 2025; Michalsky & Cohen, 2021; Panadero & Järvelä, 2015;  Sharma et al., 2024; Sulla et al., 2023). Less is known about how SSRL emerges organically in student-led, real-world inquiry, where newness, uncertainty, and collaboration are inherent.

This study addresses that gap by examining how SSRL emerged naturally during place-based fieldwork and identifying the design features that elicited it without over-structuring collaboration. In this project, students in small groups used mobile sensors to engage in first-hand empirical data collection and scientific reasoning about socioscientific issues directly relevant to their lives. Our contribution is twofold: theoretically, we elaborate SSRL as a naturally occurring phenomenon most visible in moments of uncertainty; practically, we offer design principles for creating collaborative STEM environments that use uncertainty as a pedagogical resource. To explore these dynamics, we ask, How did the STEM learning environment support students’ SSRL? SSRL and uncertainty Meaningful collaborative learning requires students to strategically regulate their collective behavior, cognition, emotion, and motivation to meet shared goals. SSRL is a qualitatively different, group-level phenomenon that extends regulation from the "I"  to the "we" level emerging through transactive exchanges where members negotiate shared goals, monitor collective progress, and co-construct understanding (Hadwin et al., 2018; Isohätälä et al., 2017; Järvenoja et al., 2020). Increased participation, well-rounded contribution patterns, and transactive talk are key empirical markers of its emergence (Isohätälä et al., 2017; Törmänen et al., 2022). 519 A growing body of literature positions uncertainty as central to this collaborative regulatory process. Chen et al. (2024) distinguish between two forms of scientific uncertainty: conceptual uncertainty, related to gaps in content knowledge, and epistemic uncertainty, defined as the subjective experience of being unsure about how knowledge is generated. Epistemic uncertainty, in particular, drives students to generate tentative hypotheses, claims, and evidence as they work to deepen understanding. In collaborative group settings, learners’ success at social regulation is shaped by how well they collectively manage such uncertainty (Dragnić-Cindrić et al., 2020). Schnaubert (2023) extends this by theorizing uncertainty as a metacognitive experience that links  cognitive and emotional regulation: when learners make their uncertainty legible to peers, it prompts others to offer support, realign strategies, or deepen engagement. Manz et al. (2025) further argue that when students engage with and seek to resolve un certainty, they develop deeper conceptual understanding and take greater ownership of their scientific thinking and decision-making. While these studies establish the relationship between uncertainty and regulation in online and classroom-based settings, less is known about how these dynamics unfold in field-based inquiry where uncertainty arises from real-world constraints such as sensor malfunctions, missing data, and unpredictable environments.

## High school STEM inquiry and CSCL as a design context

This study situates SSRL within an interdisciplinary, socio-scientific curriculum focused on asthma and air quality. The curriculum integrated genetics, environmental science, and biology across a 20-lesson unit in which students used mobile sensors to collect, analyze, and compare, air quality data across indoor, outdoor, and national contexts (Yoon et al., 2022). This problem-based design engaged students in authentic scientific practices while connecting inquiry to real civic and health concerns in their communities.

The study draws on Computer-Supported Collaborative Learning (CSCL) to examine how technologymediated environments foster social regulation and meaning-making. Mobile air-quality sensors and data visualization tools functioned not simply as instruments for measurement but as mediating artifacts that encouraged collaboration and supported students in externalizing reasoning, coordinating actions, and constructing shared understandings of real-world phenomena (Jeong et al., 2019). Episodes of technological breakdown and unexpected data created moments of uncertainty that invited groups tonegotiate strategies, share responsibility, and sustain engagement. This study contributes to CSCL by demonstrating how tool-mediated, place-based inquiry can be designed to transform uncertainty into a catalyst for SSRL, and how technology and task structure can be orchestrated to cultivate collaboration in high school STEM contexts.

## Context, participants, and data sources

The study involved ten high school students, organized into three small groups, participating in a bioinformatics project designed to investigate the high rates of asthma in urban areas caused by poor air quality. Groups were formed by gender and grade level: one group of four 9th grade females, one group of three 11th grade females, and one group of three 12th grade males. The students were enrolled in three different public high schools in the large metropolitan area in the Northeast, and each group was taught by a different teacher. The teachers of the participants were recruited as a small, highly motivated sample with strong interest in the project which aligns with recommendations for early-stage exploratory design work (IES & NSF, 2013). The analysis drew from Lesson 11: Data Collection Around the School which introduced students to the practice of conducting field-based investigations with mobile air quality sensors. The lesson was designed to help students recognize that scientific investigation requires not only disciplinary knowledge but also the development of practical skills and collaboration. The intended learning outcome was to learn how to plan and carry out investigations as a group while using technology to collect physical data outside of the classroom. These activities positioned students to participate in authentic scientific practices including generating, analyzing, and interpreting data while working collaboratively to address a real-world problem. The data consisted of 3.5 hours of video recordings capturing students’ use of mobile air quality sensors to measure carbon monoxide and fine particulate matter at both indoor and outdoor sites around their school buildings. Student groups began by planning and coordinating routes for data collection, then proceeded to gather and archive sensor readings in real time. Along the way, they troubleshot device malfunctions and negotiated procedural decisions, such as whether to substitute or revisit locations when data were missing. The classroom phase captured students as they organized, compared, and interpreted their findings by situating their local data within broader environmental concerns. Throughout these activities, students expressed and regulated a range of emotions, from excitement to curiosity to frustration which influenced how they sustained momentum and 520 supported one another. By planning, measuring, troubleshooting, and hypothesizing together, students engaged in authentic scientific practices.

Thematic analysis of students’ SSRL observed in video data The first and second authors used an interaction analysis (Jordan & Henderson, 1995) to examine both theoretically grounded and contextually emergent aspects of SSRL in the video data. The interpretations were anchored in what was directly observable in the video record (e.g., talk, gestures, use of tools) and had room for unexpected patterns of interaction that emerged beyond the initial analytic frame. We connected theoretical constructs of SSRL with the situated practices visible on video. Our initial an alytic frame drew on established SSRL literature, and our focus was structured around cognitive and metacognitive regulation (Hadwin et al., 2018; Isohätälä et al., 2017; Panadero & Järvelä, 2015). Building on this foundation, we adapted an exploratory sta nce to refine and expand categories in response to recurring interactional features observed in the data. For eight hours of interactional analysis sessions, we watched the videos together, paused recordings at key moments, and took individual memos. Pausing and replaying the recordings allowed us to observe verbal and nonverbal behaviors carefully, trace actions in sequence, and challenge assumptions through repeated review. At the conclusion of each hour-long session, the first author synthesized both sets of memos into an integrated record to identify recurring practices observed in the clips. The synthesized memos structured the findings into three parts: (1) key instances categorized into the cognitive and metacognitive dimensions of SSRL; (2) key practices with direct quotes and interpretations; (3) emergent overarching themes from researcher discussion and memo comparison. These synthesized memos served as a living document to iteratively test, refine, and revise category definitions across sessions. Joint viewing and memo exchange helped us surface and question different interpretations, counteract confir mation bias and reveal unnoticed aspects of interaction. By documenting individual perspectives, comparing observations, and negotiating interpretations, we worked to surface assumptions and strengthen the trustworthiness of our claims. The analysis across three groups revealed that SSRL emerged most prominently during moments of uncertainty, instances where students were unsure how to generate, interpret, or verify knowledge in the midst of fieldwork. These moments, triggered by unclear task goals, unexpected data patterns, or technological malfunctions prompted groups to engage in reciprocal cognitive regulation to sustain inquiry. Key indicators included questioning, help-seeking, reframing of information, and negotiated explanations wi th monitoring distributed across members. For instance, three 11th-grade female students collecting indoor air-quality data across four school sites encountered a sensor that failed to update its location, creating uncertainty about whether their measurements were registering correctly: Student 1: “Wait, refresh data.” Student 2: “Doesn't do anything.” Student 1: “I mean, our location definitely did change, but please, no, it did not [on the sensor.] I mean, are we doing something wrong?

This shows cognitive SSRL as a cycle of identifying a problem, generating a hypothesis, and aligning explanations. The mobile sensor was the source of uncertainty that catalyzed the group’s joint reasoning.

Across both episodes, uncertainty was converted into productive joint reasoning. Students distributed monitoring, externalized doubts, generated and tested hypotheses, and aligned on next steps. Their practices were core features of cognitive SSRL that kep t inquiry coherent despite real-world constraints and positioned uncertainty as a resource for collaborative scientific reasoning. The findings suggest that SSRL in the authentic STEM learning  environment was driven by transforming uncertainty into collective cognitive problems. The most generative SSRL episodes arose during breakdowns including device failures, inconsistent data, blocked sites. These tensions triggered cycles of monitoring, troubleshooting, and sense-making. As a constitutive feature of scientific practice and a pedagogical resource for fostering student engagement, uncertainty facilitated student inquiry (Chen et al., 2024; Manz, 2018). These findings extend scholarship on SSRL and productive uncertainty. Chen et al. (2024) argue that epistemic uncertainty drives students toward hypothesis generation and evidence construction. The current study shows this process operating at the group level with monitoring distributed and explanations negotiated collectively through transactive exchanges (Hadwin et al., 2011; Isohätälä et al., 2017; Järvenoja et al., 2020) and reciprocal participation and contribution (Isohätälä et al., 2017; Törmänen et al., 2022). Similarly, Manz et al.

(2025) argue that students who engage productively with uncertainty develop more agency in scientific practice.

For CSCL, this study offers design principles for place-based, mobile-technology-mediated STEM learning environments as sites for authentic scientific inquiry. We show how cognitive moves evolve within short episodes, extending SSRL beyond classroom talk to the engagement with environments and tools in collaborative groups (Jeong et al., 2019). First, structured autonomy supports a shift from teacher-reliant troubleshooting to peer-led regulation. Second, authentic scientific inquiry turns technology into both mediator and catalyst where breakdowns become opportunities  for learning. Lastly, place-based resources broaden CSCL’s focus from technology use to spatial orchestration. The findings yield actionable principles including pairing minimal roles with real choice over sites and procedures, making uncertainty legible and consequential, and cultivating agentive norms that celebrate micro-wins, clarifying how to turn uncertainty into a resource for collaborative learning.

The study has limitations that future work should address. The sample is small and drawn from a specific local urban context, limiting generalizability. The analysis focuses on cognitive SSRL; the behavioral and socioemotional dimensions co-present in these episodes warrant dedicated investigation.
