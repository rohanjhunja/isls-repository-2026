# Anchoring Code in Phenomena: Approaches to Integrating Domain-General Agent-based Modeling for Scientific Sensemaking into High School Classrooms

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Drawing on co-design work with teachers, this poster presents four approaches to developing units that bring domain-general agent-based modeling into high school science classrooms. In each unit, scientific phenomena contextualize and support sense-making of code while students engage with code to make sense of phenomena. Across the approaches, students use domain-general modeling to attend to structural and conceptual similarities and differences across phenomena and models, and extend their model/s to explore key scientific ideas.

## Introduction

Computational modeling has transformed professional scientific practice (Chandrasekharan & Nersessian, 2015). Yet, many high school students graduate without any formal engagement in computational thinking (CT) practices (Proctor et al., 2019; Wilson et al., 2010). This paper draws from a Research-Practice Partnership (RPP) (Coburn et al., 2013) with a large urbandistrict in the Mi d-Atlantic region of the United States. The district’ s vision was to integrate computing into mandated science course s as a way to provide all its students with acces s to CT practices. Drawing on studies that have shown that computational modeling, particularly through agent-based modeling (ABM), can foster science learning while e ngaging in CT practices (e.g., Dabholkar et al., 20 20; Hutchins et al., 2020; Wagh et al., 2017), we use ABM as a central CT practice in this work. Elsewhere, we report on how we leverage ABM to engage students in key modeling and CT practices (Wagh et al., 2024). Research on ABM has used both domain-general and domain-specific modeling paradigms for science learning. While domain-specific modeling can lower the barriers to entry to programming models (e.g., Hutchins et al., 2020; Wilkerson et al., 2015), domain-specific environments typically contain a small library of high-level primitives (e.g., Gou et al., 2016), thereby limiti ng expressivity for students. On the other hand, do main-general environments include a wider range of lower-level primitives that can enab le greater expressiveness but need to come with greater support for students, particularly those who are new to programming. In our work, we “anchor code in phenomena” as a way  to bring students into domain-general modeling for scientific sensemaking. Drawing on a multi-year co-design experience with high school science teachers from the district, we report on four approaches we have identified to integrate domain-general modeling into the design of 6 units across 3 science subjects. Our aim in th is design work has been to align the units with the  Next Generation Science Standards (NGSS Lead States, 201 3), providing students with  opportunities to engage in computational thinking (CT) practices in ways that support sensemaking of anchoring phenomena. Modeling with domain-general versus domain-specific code Computational modeling in the agent-based paradigm includes environments that use domai n-general modeling languages (DGMLs) as well as domain-specific modeling languages (DSMLs). DGMLs, such as NetLogo (Wilensky, 1999), include a larger number of primitives not associate d with any single subject domain. On the other hand, domain-specific modeling platforms such as NetTango and Mo Da allow curriculum designers to create small DSMLs aligned with—and limited to—a specific domain or conceptual area (e.g. Horn et  al., 2014; Wagh et al., 2022). Our RPP’s vision is that repeat edly engaging in computational modeling across mult iple science subjects will contribute to cumulative grow th over four years of high school. For this reason, we have chosen to use StarLogo Nova (Wendel, 2013), a blocks-based, domain-general environment, to enable students to model the variety of scientific domains encountered throughout high school and in our units. There have been several approaches to integrating domain-general environments into science classrooms such as the use, modify, create  framework (Lee et al, 2011); integrating domain-specific custom blocks  with domain-general primitives (Anderson & Wendel, 2020); focus ing on decoding to support connections between scientific concepts and coded abstractions (Meyer et al., 2025), and others. This paper builds on this current body of work to focus on approaches for integrating domain-general modeling into high school science curricula. Four domain-general design approaches for ABM in science classrooms Our poster reports from co-design work (Rochelle et al., 2006) done with eight high school science teachers. Over the last 2.5 years, we designed six ABM units, two each in biology, chemistry, and physics, that use S tarLogo Nova, a domain-general modeling environment. While StarLogo Nova s upports creating domain-specific primitives in the form of “custom blocks”, we chose  to have students primarily work with domain-general code to facilitate coherent experiences across science s ubjects. To support the varia tion in students' backgrounds and progressions through science, it was important to d esign experiences that would be accessible regardless of prior experience. Through our iterative design work, we i dentified four novice-friendly approaches to supporting sensemaking with target anchoring phenomena using domain-general code:

• Leveraging mechanistic similarities and highlighting differences involves introducing students to similar

mechanisms across different contexts to attend to both similarities and differences in them.  For example, in the Fast Carbon Cycle unit, students explore how carbon cycles through a marine ecosystem to answer the driving question “Do Whales Remove Carbon from the Atmosphere?” Students explore a model with only a producer (phytoplankton), and extend the model to include the first level consumer (zooplankton) by modifying code from the producer to reflect the behavior of the consumer. To do this, students must recognize differences in the ways that producers and consumers obtain carbon.

• Using a generalizable construct to show multiple ideas involves introducing students to a computational

construct that they can repeatedly use to represent a range of ideas to explain a specific phenomenon. For instance, in the Evolution unit, students use conditionals to program and test multiple possible processes that could contribute to microevolutionary outcomes. Specifically, students use conditionals to represent selection pressures due to guppies’ coloration, via sexual selection or differences in predation.

• Leveraging structural similarities between processe s involves drawing students’ attention to the

structural similarities between a familiar process and a novel one as they program and extend a model. For example, in the Reaction Rates unit, students build a model of a familiar mechanism: frogs catching flies. They are then invited to extend the model to attend to and reason about how this familiar mechanism relates to three factors related to reaction rates:  temperature, concentration, and surface area (Wendel et al., 2025). For instance, if one thinks of the frog s and flies as molecules moving about randomly in a given space, the speed a frog moves is analogous to  the temperature of a substance; just as faster fro gs catch flies more quickly, hotter reactants collide more often, increasing the reaction rate.

• Extending a given model to represent a key mechanis m involves students modifying model code to

represent and test new ideas. In particular, studen ts might explore a model designed by our team, and then focus their attention on modeling a specific m echanism that is central to sensemaking of the phenomenon. For example, in a physics unit called Can We Stop Global Warming?, students first manipulate a model to examine how the balance betwe en incoming sunlight and outgoing infrared radiation impacts the Earth’s equilibrium temperature. Students are then invited to add and modify code for sunlight reflecting off of ice cover (e.g. at the poles), and explore how this impacts the balance.

## Conclusion

In this poster, we present four approaches to enabl ing students, including those who are new to progra mming, to engage in domain-general modeling for sensemaking of scientific phen omena. Albeit different in form, collectively, these approaches are intended  to provide coherent experiences for students both w ithin a single subject and across three subjects. Across the four approaches, students are invited to use this domain-general environment to attend to structural and conceptual similarities and differences between phenomena and models, and extend their model/s to represent and explore key scientific ideas. Indeed, the RPP’s vision is that repeatedly encountering computational modeling across multiple science subjects will contribute to cumulative growth over four years of high school.  Going forward, we aim to study this cumulative grow th to advance the field’s understanding of how to support longitudinal development of integrated CT and scientific sensemaking.
