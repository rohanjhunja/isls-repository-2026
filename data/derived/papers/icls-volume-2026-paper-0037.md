# “I Know It Because I've Seen It”: Epistemic Agency and Tool Selection in Student-LLM Mathematical Problem Solving

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Though students are using large language models (LLMs), we do not yet know how students interact with LLMs as they solve problems or how they evaluate whether or not to use a LLM compared to other technological resources. This study addresses both of these gaps. We recruited ten undergraduates to participate in think aloud, mathematics task-based interviews. We analyzed students’ selections of technologies and the extent to which they built and verified their solutions through progressive inquiry. We found that students select LLMs more frequently than internet-based resources, and that progressive inquiry was related to students’ instrumental genesis (e.g., knowledge of tools and their affordances). We discuss implications for knowledge building with LLMs as they relate to human epistemic agency.

This study addresses both of these gaps. We recruited ten undergraduates to participate in think aloud, mathematics task-based interviews. We analyzed students’ selections of technologies and the extent to which they built and verified their solutions through progressive inquiry. We found that students select LLMs more frequently than internet-based resources, and that progressive inquiry was related to students’ instrumental genesis (e.g., knowledge of tools and their affordances). We discuss implications for knowledge building with LLMs as they relate to human epistemic agency. As LLMs become normalized as tools that impact learning, educators must investigate how LLMs influence the ways that students approach problem solving and, more broadly, how they contribute to cultures of collective knowledge building (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2006). Recently, Wu and colleagues (2025) raised concerns that increasing LLM use without interrogating how students use knowledge produced during human-LLM interactions to contribute to collective community knowledge could discourage human epistemic a gency. Epistemic agency refers to individuals’ or groups’ collective responsibility to shape knowledge and practices in communities (Scardamalia, 2002). Human epistemic agency  connotes that when working with intelligent agents, humans still hold collective responsibility to validate information using prior knowledge and resources that validate knowledge (Wu et al., 2025). Downplaying human epistemic agency as LLMs take on more centralized functions in schooling could have negative consequences. If, for instance, students learn to rely on LLMs to personalize learning without gaining practices to confirm and publicly communicate their knowledge, they may subconsciously remove humanistic aspects of learning that are necessary to construct new knowledge. Foregrounding human epistemic agency in an era of increased intellectual automation should be a top priority for educators, researchers, and policymakers. By focusing on what human learners know, how they know it, and how they communicate it, we aim to be  part of an effort to preserve knowledge building as a cornerstone of communal knowledge advancement. To that end, we devised a study about how often and why students choose to use LLMs to solve problems. We asked undergraduates to solve open-ended mathematics tasks and gave them a variety of technological resources, including ChatGPT. We were curious to explore when and how often students used ChatGPT, particularly when given the choice to select non-stochastic technologies. We answer: (1) What tools, including LLMs, do students select to solve mathematical problems? And (2) How (if at all) do students use tools, particularly LLMs, to reason mathematically and construct knowledge? We explore how students use and combine technologies to solve mathematics problems when they are uncertain how to proceed, and attend to how they interact with ChatGPT. Building from Wu and colleagues (2025), we conjecture that students’ epistemic agency depends in part on the intellectual authority they confer to ChatGPT, particularly when reasoning about unfamiliar mathematics. We further hypothesize that students who select ChatGPT as their singular technology for knowledge construction may exhibit human epistemic agency by using one set of agentic moves – progressive inquiry (Muukkonen et al., 1999) – to triangulate LLM output with their prior knowledge. We question whether progressive inquiry can serve as a process for two-way knowledge building between humans and LLMs without the machine shouldering the cognitive burden.

## Theoretical perspectives: Mathematical knowledge building with LLMs

Knowledge building is generally regarded as a collective and progressive social endeavor (Bereiter & Scardamalia, 2014). In this framing, students are active contributors of knowledge within a community, creating new knowledge for the purpose of benefitting the community, rather than consumers or recipients of information. To do this kind of work, students must make their thinking visible to collaborate and build upon it (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2006). Although knowledge building was not originally conceive d with mathematics education in mind–since math has historically relied on adherence to procedural precision –good mathematics teaching today 282 promotes communal knowledge building practices to support collective engagement with real-world problems. For instance, mathematics teachers create opportunities to make collective student thinking visible through externalizable representations such as sym bolic notation, spoken explanations, and multiple solution strategies (Stein & Lane, 1998). These opportunities can help students move toward using mathematics concepts in nonroutine ways to create new knowledge with peers (Ball & Bass, 2003). Learning how to think and reason mathematically inherently involves grappling with uncertainty to create conceptual reorganizations in learners’ schemas that are necessary for building knowledge (Confrey, 1990; Warshauer, 2015). When LLMs become integrated in problem solving as mathematical collaborators, it is unclear if students will achieve the same type of conceptual restructuring that shows evidence of learning. After all, today’s LLMs can solve some types of mathematics problems independently (Pardos & Bhandavi, 2024). If a student uses an LLM to solve a problem independently, without contributing humanistic aspects to problem solving (e.g., situatedness, real-world evaluation), then there is noneed for human-LLM collaboration (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2020). Instead, the interaction becomes one of transactional information consumption rather than active knowledge construction, leaving students without opportunities to develop conceptual reorganization schemata that emerge from thinking deeply through mathematical complexities. Student-tool selection during mathematical problem solving Students who select tools for problem solving (rather than being told what tool to use) must maintain knowledge of tools and their epistemic affordances. One conception of students’ tool selections can be described by instrumental genesis (Vérillon & Rabardel, 1995), whereby learners develop knowledge of tools and ascribe meaning to their problem solving affordances (Drijvers et al., 2010). Ascribing mathematical meaning to tools involves two interrelated dimensions: discovering and navigating tools, including their constraints, and reciprocally using tools to deepen their mathematical problem solving abilities (Ruiz -López, 2018). Instrumental genesis centers students' evolving conceptions of the relationship between mathematical tasks, technological resources, and their own cognitive capabilities. Research demonstrates that students who show problem solving expertise link tools’ affordances with the problem’s demands and their own knowledge of how to use the tool toward problem solving (Shvarts et al., 2021). Historically, instrumental genesis develops through teacher guidance (Clark-Wilson et al., 2020). As many teachers are still grappling with how and what to teach about LLMs, students who have used LLMs with school-based tasks by their own means may have developed their own evaluation criteria about LLMs’ (mathematical) affordances. Progressive inquiry as one possible instantiation of human epistemic agency We wish to support students’ instrumental genesis of LLMs so that they may use them to promote human-driven knowledge building. We hypothesize that one way to do this is through progressive inquiry  (Muukkonen et al., 1999), which proposes knowledge construction with technology via theorizing, evaluating, searching, questioning, and re-theorizing (Figure 1). Students develop new knowledge by repeatedly engaging in cycles of progressive inquiry until they determine that they have reached a satisfactory new working theory. We regard this process as an instantiation of human epistemic agency when learners intentionally evaluate, accept or reject, and build on generated outputs to transform their own knowledge. Figure 1 Progressive Inquiry During Mathematics Problem Solving (adapted from Muukkonen et al., 1999) While progressive inquiry was originally conceived as a framework to examine collaborative scientific inquiry between students and technology, we repurpose it for mathematics because the cyclical progression of knowledge building may mirror how students collaborate with LLMs to solve problems. Since students form representations based on their prior knowledge and conceptual organization (Chi & Glaser, 1985; Confrey, 1990), their representations will form the basis of their initial working theories, critical evaluations, and inform what they 283 ultimately ask a LLM. If students have incomplete representations, then they may search and deepen their knowledge with a LLM in ways that do not make progress. After all, disembodied LLMs cannot “see” the problem unless a student enters it directly. Therefore, LLMs can collaborate only to the extent that the learner can envision what they need to know and ask a query that results in the LLM returning information about what they need to know. Learners who cannot bridge what they know, need to know, and need to query in order to know may succumb to a gulf of envisioning (Subramonyam et al., 2024). We hypothesize that to build knowledge with LLMs, students must maintain LLM envisioning skills to accurately self-monitor their progress, or use non-LLM tools to search and evaluate information from non-stochastic sources. Our goal was to explore the nature of students’ mathematical problem solving with LLMs. We recruited participants by posting flyers in publicly accessible locations on campus. Undergraduates from all majors could participate as long as they had taken at least one mathematics course in the past year to increase the likelihood that they were familiar with the math concepts in our tasks. Three 20-minute tasks were given, although we analyzed only the first two because they required similar levels of prior math knowledge (Figure 2). The tasks were selected because (1) they contained introductory math topics that a wide variety of majors would likely be familiar with, and because (2) some features of the tasks (an image, an elongated data table) caused ChatGPT-4o, the educational chatbot subscription purchased by the university, to occasionally hallucinate. For this paper, we analyze a subset of 10 participants (a mixture of STEM and non-STEM majors) that were given ChatGPT-4o, a calculator, and the internet as tools for problem solving. A lead interviewer facilitated the sessions, which lasted approximately 90 minutes each. First, participants were asked to describe their LLM use for mathematics, school, and daily life, to gain a sense of how and how much they used chatbots (Delaney et al., 2025). Then the interviewer encouraged participants to “think aloud” while completing each task for up to 20 minutes on iPads, including describing their rationales for selecting tools when applicable (Goldin, 2000). The interviewer clarified explicitly that we did not place value judgments on tool use; rather, we wished to encourage students to solve problems however they liked. Occasionally, the interviewer asked participants to elaborate on their technology choices (e.g., “Please explain your decision to use ChatGPT?”). A second researcher managed the video, audio, and sc reen recording (iPad) devices and took descriptive field notes. After all three tasks were complete, the interviewer debriefed the session in a semi-structured manner by asking the participant to reflect on their experience. ## Data analysis

To answer our first research question, two researchers analyzed video recordings of each problem solving episode for participants’ tool uses (Derry et al., 2010). Each instance of technology use was visible from the video recording, although we triangulated video recordings where students used ChatGPT with a written chat log that contained their exact queries and ChatGPT’s responses. One researcher created a detailed accounting of where in each task technologies were used, which technologies were used, and students’ rationales for selecting each tool, using an inductive thematic approach (Braun & Clarke, 2021). We counted unique types of technologies: 284 ChatGPT, other LLM, non-AI internet resources, and calculators, at the level of subtask (e.g., “ATM Part 1”). We only counted whether or not the technology was used; for instance, if students used ChatGPT twice during a task subcomponent, we counted that once. Students’ rationales that emerged about technology use yielded themes that pointed to their instrumental genesis. Inductive methods were appropriate as we had no a priori conception about what students would do during the tasks with the technologies available.

To address our second research question, two researchers analyzed each problem solving episode using a deductive coding approach guided by progressive inquiry coding, again at the level of subtask (Creswell, 2022). We first examined all 79 episodes to determine which episodes contained evidence of progressive inquiry and followed the phases outlined in Figure 1. To count as a progressive inquiry instance, students needed to engage in all five phases at least once during the episode: state an initial working  theory, critically evaluate that theory, search for new information with technology, generate subordinate questions, and construct a new theory. Each researcher coded half of the dataset and met on four occasions to discuss coding and reach intersubjectiv e agreement (Krippendorff, 2018). For instance, we iteratively discussed the critical evaluation phase in the context of mathematics, and eventually agreed that the student needed to express doubt, or a tension, in their initial theory before searching for  new information. Otherwise, there was no basis to motivate a reconfiguring of their initial theory with new information. We resolved coding disagreements through discussions until consensus was reached (Cornish et al., 2014). During our discussions, we observed that students’ progressive inquiry cycles (1) varied in their degree of effectiveness toward knowledge building, and (2) were highly dependent on the tools they selected. We constructed analytic cases of four students whose progressive inquiry engagements illustrated these phenomena (Yin, 2009), which form the basis of the findings. ## An Overview of Participant Tool Use During Problem Solving Episodes

Across the two tasks, the most common reasons participants reported for prompting ChatGPT were to recall information (11) and to gather information about unfamiliar topics (6). Other reasons included verification of information/ideas (2), help getting started (2), feature specification (1), and idea generating (1). Students who used ChatGPT for help recalling information often expressed that they had seen the mathematical objects before but could not remember specific details. For example, “I haven’t used the parabola equation in a while”  (Roller Coaster, subtask 4) or, “from memory, I know that ‘given’ means under a condition,” (ATM subtask 4). Students who expressed unfamiliarity either did so explicitly, “this is an unfamiliar question”  (ATM subtask 4), or wondered about relevant mathematical concepts, “there might be an equation for that, I just don’t know”  (ATM subtask 2). We coded prompts as “verification of information or ideas” when students explained prompting to validate their own knowledge: “I know where it’s decreasing and increasing but honestly I would probably just check to make sure.” We coded prompts as “help getting started” including those written as a result of uncertainty 285 about a problem statement, “I was thinking they were asking about the probability of x = 3 but ‘given that at least 1 ATM is working.’ I don’t know what is ‘given that at least 1 ATM is working.’” We coded “feature specification” to a prompt written by a student who had already graphed a parabola but was unsure about what feature of the equation to modify in order to “widen the x range.”  Lastly, the “idea generating” code resulted from one participant’s knowledge of parabolic graphs which resulted in speculation about whether a different function would better fit the shape of the roller coaster, “I’m going to describe a function that looks like a sine wave except the amplitude gets smaller as the x increases.”  This suggests that the student used ChatGPT to extend their knowledge by allowing them to explore new mathematical objects. In summary, students’ ChatGPT uses and prompts were heavily mediated by their representation of the task (Chi & Glaser, 1985), how well they understood what was being asked of them, and what they expected to learn from ChatGPT’s output. However, a majority (43 out of 79 students) did not use technology at all during particular task subcomponents, suggesting that their instrumental genesis was developed enough to know when they needed to use a tool and/or whether or not the tools available to them would help them make progress. RQ2: How did students use LLMs to build knowledge via progressive inquiry? We present four cases of students’ mathematical problem solving that illustrate, to various degrees, how students used tools (including ChatGPT) and engaged in progressive inquiry to build knowledge. We begin with Mari’s case as it presents a pattern of mathematical reasoning and LLM use that was common among students. We then present three other cases to showcase the diversity of our results. Mari: “I know it because I’ve seen it before.” Mari, a senior-year mechanical engineering major, exemplified a common pattern among participants: trust in ChatGPT’s immediate responses to a prompt because they recognized the mathematical topics in its output. In cases like Mari’s, inquiries were not pr ogressive because students did not generate subordinate questions from their ChatGPT-based searches. They instead agreed and took up its output, then moved on. We illustrate this phenomenon during Mari’s ATM task, subtask B, finding the average number of ATMs working on a given day. Mari reacted aloud: “ That’s confusing to me, because I don’t know what average they want.” Her initial theory  was that she needed to calculate a particular type of average. When pressed by the interviewer to say more, Mari elaborated, “Because it gives me the probability…so I don’t know what the basis is for like an average on that.” Mari is referring to each number of ATMs corresponding to a probability of working or not on a given day. She later explained that her conception of average is “thinking about 50%” but was unsure about how to incorporate probabilities and object counts together (critical evaluation). After about two minutes, she prompted (searched) ChatGPT: “I have a probability table that includes the probability that an ATM will be working when a mall opens. I am being asked to find how many ATMS will be working on a given day.” ChatGPT returned information about discrete  probability distributions and a description about “expected value, a kind of weighted average (sum of all x times p(x)).” Rather than generating subordinate questions, Mari responded, “Oh, that makes sense” to ChatGPT’s weighted average description. She elaborated that she had, “seen this before in a class…not that I necessarily understand it, but I’ve seen this topic, and that’s how we solved for average.”  The interviewer generated a subordinate question in response: “What about this math…looks trustworthy?…How do you know that AI is not making this up?” Mari responded: Because I’ve seen it before. And it makes sense …if I think about the context of the problem. Because there’s a 28% chance that one ATM is working. So then…I don’t know stats very well. If this is the chance that all the ATMs are working…then the average would be that multiplied. I don’t think it makes sense to me in a way that I can explain it, more than it makes sense in a way that I’ve done this type of problem before…like, I have learned this. Mari’s interaction highlights challenges of knowledge building with LLMs that many students in our study faced. First, it is not apparent that Mari learned from the interaction with ChatGPT, nor that Mari and ChatGPT collectively built knowledge. ChatGPT aided her to recall learning about a weighted average, but even after reading its explanation, she was unable to explain its meaning or justify why it applied to the task. Second, Mari did not engage in any progressive inquiring with ChatGPT that would suggest they were co-constructing meaning. She could have, for instance, asked it to explain why multiplying x (counts of ATMs working) with p(x) (probability of each number of ATMs working) gave the weighted average. Further, she did not verify ChatGPT’s output, relying on her recollection of weighted average to trust its answer. Wu et al. (2025) call this least-agentic stance absolutist, suggesting that students rely only on LLMs for verification. Louise: “No, the range is not-10.” 286 Louise, a liberal studies major with a focus in elementary math teaching, was one of the few instances where a student interacted with ChatGPT without conferring intellectual authority. This case illustrates her encounter with ChatGPT during the Roller Coaster task, where she prompted ChatGPT eight times. Louise first took a screenshot of the task and entered it into ChatGPT. It provided a step-by-step solution, but she ignored it and instead calculated distances (with a calculator) between roller coaster peaks to make sense of its dimensions. Louise recalled that she had given ChatGPT the task and asked, “What does the domain mean?” ChatGPT gave a contextual definition (“The domain means all the possible input values for a function — in this case, it’s the horizontal distance along the roller coaster track.”) and returned the interval 0 ≤ x ≤ 60. Louise inspected the graph then responded, “Wrong, it’s 55.” Then she prompted, “so it started at 5 and ended at 55 it looks like?” Although Louise had actually mistaken the range value 5 in the first point (0, 5) as the beginning of the domain, ChatGPT responded, “Ah! Thanks for clarifying — you're looking more closely at the graph — and you're right to do that!...So the corrected domain would be 5 ≤ x ≤ 55, good eye! ” When pressed to share more by the interviewer, Louise explained that ChatGPT can “be tricky” and moved to inspect the range. Figure 4 Louise’s Progressive Inquiries with ChatGPT during the Roller Coaster Task: Finding the Range Louise’s investigation of the range involved three progressive inquiry rounds (Figure 4). She began by inspecting ChatGPT’s initial range [-5, 35] and realized it was incorrect, because the track did not reach-5. However Louise was uncertain about her own conception of range, and asked ChatGPT to “explain range again, as if I were new to this topic.” When it returned the same answer, Louise wondered if it was programmed to follow a particular numbering schema, or if its conception of the points plotted on the task were inaccurate. Still dissatisfied with ChatGPT’s response, her next prompt embodied her belief that giving it specific numbers will help it clarify its answer. She said aloud that ChatGPT needs direction, “ Otherwise it’ll just keep going forever and ever.” ChatGPT then returned a new range of [-10, 30]. Louise exclaimed, “ Where the heck did-10 come from? That’s even farther down!” suggesting that ChatGPT’s new answer was less knowledgeable. In her final prompt, she directly countered ChatGPT, prompting, “I don’t see anything about-10 on the graph.” When pressed by the interviewer, Louise gave her own evaluation that the range should begin at “ Four, where the dots [on the track] are.” Louise evaluated ChatGPT’s final new range, [0, 30], as incorrect, but then realized she had not begun the other task sub-questions yet, and moved on without a new theory. Louise’s interactions with ChatGPT show what Wu and colleagues (2025) coined as an evaluative stance with high human epistemic agency. Evaluative  here means that Louise used both her prior knowledge and resource-based evidence (in this case, the roller coaster graphic) to raise questions about the validity of ChatGPT’s responses. Because she inquired in progressive rounds and evaluated ChatGPT’s re sponses multiple times, she realized that ChatGPT did not make a simple mistake, but rather was attending to fe atures of the graphic that did not represent the range (we hypothesize it was attending to the bottom of the coordinate grid). Furthermore, ChatGPT did not reciprocally serve as a collaborative knowledge building partner as Scardamalia and Bereiter envisioned in rich learning environments (2006). Rather, it operated as the knowledge-holder, with the student as a receptacle of its knowledge, and it revised its answer without asking for Louise’s input, even when she raised a valid critique based on her skills and what she observed from her stance in the situated world. Anish: “It’s on the tip of my brain.” Anish, a sophomore computer science major, was the only participant to use more than two tools during problem solving. While working on roller coaster subtask 4 (equation of the first scream), Anish alternated among Google Gemini’s AI overview, Google image search, and YouTube tutorial videos. He began by circling point B on the graph and verbalized, “the first scream would be here and it looks like a parabola.” Thus, his initial theory  was that he needed to construct an equation of a parabola. He then expr essed doubt (critical evaluation): “it’s been a while since I’ve done this…for the equation would it be a parabola?”  In response to this tension, he began a Google search for “equation for curve with max and mins.”  He explained that he preferred to “look into Google 287 first,” before using ChatGPT because “ it’s a more vague question. ” Ironically, he then attended to Google Gemini’s AI overview above the search results. He explained, “I'll read the AI overview because it'll give me contextual information. And…it’s also the first thing you see.” Continuing his search, he found an image that reminded him of function derivatives and critical points. When asked what he was attending to, Anish generated a subordinate question about whether he could use a derivative with the task: I was thinking of the roots. But it doesn’t look like there’s any roots in this problem? Because you can formulate the equation using the roots…it’s on the tip of my brain but, it’s like you can use whether it’s the maximum or minimum at that root to help you find things. After reviewing the images screen, search results, and Gemini AI overview again, Anish saw an equation that triangulated his two theories. He summarized in a new working theory, “Finding critical points, setting to zero, yes, that’s what I thought, a(x-h) 2…I’m thinking it would be a quadratic,” (Figure 5). As Anish realized he still needed more information to implement this idea, he clicked on a link to a YouTube video titled, “How to find max and min turning points & the second derivative. ” Anish recognized the procedure in the video, remarking, “that makes sense, I remember all this stuff.”  Although Anish ran out of time before he could pursue this solution strategy, he ended the task with a more mathematically precise idea of how to solve it. Each tool that he chose served a different purpose. Marcel: “I don’t understand it enough to ask a structured question.” Marcel, a sophomore electrical engineering major, demonstrated how unfamiliarity with mathematical subject-matter may lead to progressive inquiries that do not advance the learner toward the task goals. He began ATM subtask 3 (inspecting the data table) by reading the problem multiple times. He realized that he did not have enough prior knowledge to generate ideas for a solution strategy (“I haven’t done stats since elementary school.”). He then prompted ChatGPT, “ how does probability work,” which resulted in 24-lines of output, including a portion Marcel read out loud: “number of favorable outcomes and total number of outcomes.” After reading this output, Marcel realized that he needed to be “more specific toward what I’m looking at [to what he needs to learn].” Marcel’s first initial theory was that asking ChatGPT about probability would yield information that was understandable. However, he critically evaluated  his theory, realizing that he did not know what “number of favorable outcomes and total number of outcomes” (ChatGPT’s first response) meant in this context, since probability was presented as decimals rather than as two s ets of outcomes. He then initiated a more specific search  and attempted to bridge probability as it was represented in the task: “ if we have multiple cars and we want some of them to be running, what is the probability that 0 are running? How would you decide that probability?” This prompt resulted in output detailing an example of 5 cars each with a 0.8 probability of running. Although Marcel expressed, “ that kind of makes sense, ” he was unable to connect the example to a process that would enable him to find a solution strategy for the task. He reflected, and eventually generated a subordinate question, stating, “I don’t know how to find that here…they didn’t show how they would find [the probability].” He continued his search, asking, “how did you find the 0.8 probability that a car runs?”  ChatGPT responded with hypothetical sources including: “Example: Out of 100 times you tried starting a particular car, it ran 80 times,” as well as suggesting manufacturer reports, and educated estimates as sources of data. Again unable to connect the output to the subtask, Marcel thought, "you can’t be like ‘I tried starting the ATM’...it’s either working or it’s not.” After rereading the problem, he correctly reasoned, “I feel like they would definitely use the 288 number of working ATMs in the beginning of the day, ” but remained uncertain. He concluded that ChatGPT was not giving him information that helped him build a conception of probability as it was represented in the task. He constructed a new working theory: that his stalled progress was related to his probability subject-matter knowledge. He reflected, “I feel like I’m not asking the correct question because I feel like I don’t understand it enough to be able to ask a good, structured question.” Afterward, Marcel shared that he struggled to communicate with ChatGPT in ways that enabled progress. The interviewer asked why he chose to continue working with ChatGPT over other web-based tools. He responded: “I got tunnel vision…to use AI instead of going outside of AI.” He expressed that in his daily life he has “gotten so used to how fast AI is” that he finds it undesirable to conduct an internet search which “would take 20 times longer.” Although Marcel exhibited an evaluative stance  relative to ChatGPT–he knew that ChatGPT’s responses would not lead him to legitimate solutions, nor were they precisely conceptually related to what he needed to learn –he did not navigate toward different technologies that may have enabled him to make more progress. This interaction suggests that Marcel had not yet developed the instrumental genesis necessary to use ChatGPT for mathematics problem solving; either because he was not aware of how the tool could support him, or because he was not familiar enough with the concepts needed to solve the task.

Our findings illustrate students’ instrumental genesis and reasoning processes when they solved mathematics problems with technology, particularly LLMs. We were struck by how many episodes contained students who exclusively used ChatGPT (17 instances) over  the internet (1 instance) and combinations of resources (7 instances). However, since many students appeared toneed help interpreting task questions, generating ideas, or executing a solution strategy, it is logical that they would use ChatGPT as its output can adjust to specifications of those queries instantaneously and more fluidly than internet search. Furthermore, most of the 17 instances of ChatGPT-only use resembled episodes like Mari (“I know it because I’ve seen it ”) rather than Anish, who used multiple resources to build new theories, or Louise, who used her prior knowledge to contest ChatGPT. Marcel did not appear to learn with ChatGPT but at least was aware that his prompts did not close the gap between what he knew and what he needed to know (Subramonyam et al., 2024). These findings provide evidence that students’ instrumental genesis processes that they use to learn individually or collectively build knowledge can be complex and task-dependent, as some students used tools’ affordances to engage in productive progressive inquiries while others remained stuck because their tool use did not advance them toward the task goal. We can learn a great deal about whether human-LLM knowledge building is a plausible construct from these episodes. Louise’s case is of interest because she interacted with ChatGPT in ways that most closely resembled pro-social knowledge building (Scardama lia & Bereiter, 2006). She made her mathematical thinking apparent, gave ChatGPT feedback on its incorrect representational error, and monitored its ideas to attempt to understand them (Goos et al., 2002). We argue that these actions afforded her epistemic agency, because both her prior knowledge of mathematics and her knowledge of how ChatGPT works informed how and what she prompted (Wu et al., 2025). However, we observed that ChatGPT was not skilled at reciprocating Louise’s collaborative knowledge building efforts. After it praised her noticing skills, it generated new estimates for the roller coaster’s range without utilizing any pro-collaborative moves (e.g., asking for feedback). From this, we question if it is possible for ChatGPT-4o to be a good member of a knowledge building community. If so, we raise questions about whose responsibility it is to engage intelligent agents in knowledge building (the learner, or the agent’s designers). If not, we echo Fan et al.’s (2024) cautions about using intelligent agents in educational settings where the goal is for students to develop awareness about building knowledge. Given that ChatGPT is likely the tip of the LLM-enhanced learning iceberg, it is important to continually study what technologies students select for their learning, why they select them, how the student-tool-task relationship evolves as automation improves, and ultimately the degree to which students view themselves as agentic knowledge builders. We point to Mari as a case of non-agentic human-LLM collaboration because she took an absolutist stance (Wu et al., 2025), did not integrate her prior knowledge, and did not progressively inquire to attempt to build knowledge. However, our study is limited in that we did not ask students to self-evaluate their agency after problem solving. It is possible that some students may consider prompting ChatGPT for mathematics task answers to be an agentic form of learning. Moreover, students may not consider human epistemic agency to matter to their own or others’ education, even if learning scientists do. Open questions remain about what knowledge building demands will be expected from human learners in the era of LLMs (Scardamalia & Bereiter,

2020) and whether or not they believe knowledge building is worth pursuing in the first place. We leave such

questions for future ICLS convenings.
