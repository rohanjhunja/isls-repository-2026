# Unveiling the Role of Learning Design on Feedback in MOOCs

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Massive Open Online Courses (MOOCs) frequently overlook learning design aspects and pedagogical theories in feedback provision. The current paper presents an exploratory study aiming to understand how different learning design elements (e.g., course difficulty and resource types) influence instructor-led feedback in MOOCs. We conducted six design sessions with MOOC instructors who used a feedback design tool, e-FeeD4Mi, to outline their course components, anticipate potential student problems and create feedback interventions with different timing and focus. The results showed the type of problem, the task difficulty and the instructor’s educational philosophy influence the timing of feedback.

## Introduction

Instructors acknowledge feedback among their main c hallenges in Massive Open Online Courses (MOOCs) du e to the difficulty of monitoring the learners and pr oviding tailored interventions (Sari et al., 2020).  Learning Analytics (LA) has been used for scaling  up learners’ monitoring and feedback provision (Par do et al., 2017). Nevertheless, usually such LA-based feedback approaches lack contextual relevance  and theoretical grounding (vande Oudeweetering, Knox & Decuypere, 2023). Con cerning feedback, the conte xtual relevance can regard the course components (e.g., type of tasks), the co urse structure or other aspects that affect the lea rning process. Likewise, the theoretical underpinning can regard the consideration of different feedback purposes (e.g., feedback related to one's cognitive state or regulation) (Ha ttie & Timperley, 2007) and of timing to deliver in terventions (i.e., immediate or on purpose-delayed feedback) (Mason & Bruning, 2001). Mangaroska & Giannakos (2019) indicated that the synergy between LA and the field of Learning Design could provide stronger theoretical and pedagogical grounding when informing feedback interventions. Having the course components explicitly described in a learnin g design (LD) makes it possible to take them into a ccount when designing feedback. While several LD tools aim  to align course elements with learning outcomes, s uch as PeerLAND (Papanikolaou et al. 2022) or EdVee (Trowsdale & McKay, 2023), none of them were developed for MOOCs and for the design of LA-based feedback grounded on pedagogical intentions. This paper reports a study on how different learning design components influence LA-based feedback in MOOCs, through the following research question (RQ): “ How does instructor-led feedback relate to different learning designs in MOOCs?”. To this end, 6 MOOC instructors employed e-FeeD4Mi, a feedback design tool to create interventions based on their own course LD. The tool aims to support instructors along the proc ess of designing personalized LA-based feedback considering the different LD components. e-FeeD4Mi is grounded on feedback theories related to feedback focus (Hattie & T imperley, 2007) and timing (Mason & Bruning, 2001) in relation to learners’ trace data. Understanding how these components of the LD may affect the feedback decisions might improve the support provided by feedback design tools such as e-FeeD4Mi.

## Methodology

We conducted a single instrumental qualitative case  study (Creswell, 2006) to explore: (a) how the dif ferent LD elements designed by the instructors, such as the r esource type, can be linked to problems that learne rs can face during the course enactment [RQ.a] and (b) how the theory-informed feedback strategies employed can be linked to the problems that learners face during the course enactment [RQ.b]. We conducted six design sessions in which six different MOOC instructors were involved using their own course designs. Each session lasted approximately 1:30h. During the sessions the participants were asked to use e-FeeD4Mi, outline their course structure, and design feedback interventions in form of if-then rules by (a) anticipating potential learner problems, (b) associating such problems with LA-based indicators, and then (c) selecting feedback interventions from a pre-defined catalog. The if-then rules are supposed to detect concrete cohorts of learners potentially experiencing an anticipated  problem and deliver interventions of different purpose and timi ng. The e-FeeD4Mi interventions are grouped under the taxonomy of Hattie & Timperley (2007): [TR] task-related (e.g., correct/wrong answers); [PR] progres s-related (e.g., provision of additional material); and [R] regulation related feedback (e.g., motivational emails). The employed data sources were the instructors’ artifacts [Art] (i.e., the LD outline, the identification of potential students’ problems and the designed feedb ack interventions), think-aloud comments [Thin]; interviews [Int] about instructors’ current fee dback practices and, researcher observations [Obs].  The qualitative data were coded using deductive and inductive coding. The participants (N = 4 male, N = 2 female) were from three different nationalities (i.e., Greece, Spain, and Germany) with different previous experience (4 participants have designed more than 5 MOOCs, and 2 participants 1-2 MOOCs). Finally, three courses were on Computer S cience topics and the remaining 3 were about Statistics, Scientific Writing and Well-Being.

## Results

Figure 1 provides an overview of the six MOOCs reso urces. The examined MOOCs had similarities in terms  of structure and resources [Art]. Concretely, the cour ses were organized in 5 to 10 weekly modules containing content material in form of video lectures and docu ments to read, and discussion/group forums. Yet, th e courses varied in the types of assessment tasks. That is, all MOOCs included quizzes, but onl y 5 MOOCs had weekly exams, 4 had open-ended assignments, and 3 had peer-assignments and final projects. Regarding [RQ.a], instructors anticipated various learner problems that might happen during the enactment. There were cases where instructors consi dered that the same problem may occur many times, i n different course moments. Table 1 shows the relatio nship between the identified problems and the MOOC resources. The most common learner problems pointed out were: self-regulation issues (N=10), lack of useful and timely feedback (N=5), collaboration-related problems (N=5); technical difficulties (N=5), content-related concerns (N=4), problems due to the diversity in prior knowledge (N=4), and community building (N=2). Most anticipated learners' problems were associated  with various learning and assessment activities (Table 1). For instance, ‘lack of self-regulation’ was associated with six different cours e elements, especially, with content and assessment material (quiz, weekly exams) -because learners might skip critical components (reported by 4 instructors)- and with the time spent at the course. That is, “ Students have problems because they do not plan their course dedication and by entering  once per week, they omit critical content material ” [Int-MOOC#2]. Other problems regard ‘feedback issues’ in terms of lack of instant feedback (N=3), in weekly exams or assignments, e.g.,  “ Learners expect quick feedback, especially in cases  of weekly exams, but we do not have the possibility to react directly” [Int-MOOC#2], and lack of useful feedback (N=2) in peer assignments e.g., “learners received poor reviews in terms of content and feedback by their peers, so they reported the i nput was not useful to them ” [Int-MOOC#5]. Additionally, problems related to ‘content understandin g’ were only related to content material, even though problems with cont ent understanding could be reflected through low sc ores in assignments. The least reported problem was ‘community building’. Additionally, we initially thought that MOOC ‘densi ty’ -in terms of number of resources and activitiescould affect the number of learners’ problems (MOOC s #1, #3 & #5 were ‘denser’). Yet, in MOOC#6 the instructor expected 5 problems to occur, while the course density was rather low and the foreseen stud y time per week was less than 5h (see Figure 1). Likewise, and despite its density, the instructor of MOOC#5 considered that the course was rather straightforward and only 3 problems would challenge the learners. That is, “I cannot think of more than 3 problems that might emerge. I deem that the course will be easy, probably too easy, that is an issue I am worried ” [Int-MOOC#5]. In general, concerning the rest of MOOCs, we found that, for MOOC#1 the instructor anticipated 4 learner problems, 7 proble ms for MOOC#2, 4 learner problems for MOOC#3, and 3 learner problems for MOOC#4. Thus, instead of the c ourse density, this evidence suggests that the inst ructors’ self-perception about the course difficulty influenced the number of learner problems. Concerning [RQ.b], participants prioritized two or three anticipated  problems to design feedback. The selected problems regarded either the most frequent problems or the most easil y treatable. Participants selected mainly problems connected with easily monitored resources  (e.g., content or assessment material) avoiding the resources related to learners’ interaction (Figure 2). Most feedback interventions concerned process feedback [PR] (N=8) and regulation feedback [R] (N=5). In most cases (N=11), participants selected the delivery of predefined adaptable messages such as motivational or informative messages (N=8), or platform notifications (N=3), e.g., “The instructor of MOOC#4 selected the personalized messages to treat 3 different problems. While he wa nts to target concrete learner cohorts, he also seems more comfortable with this option than with the alternatives” [ObsMOOC#4]. Additionally, three instructors with high experience in delivering MOOCs found unexpected feedback ideas as useful for their cour ses, e.g., “I would have never considered gamification as a mea ns of feedback, but indeed it can boost learners’ engagement” [Thin-MOOC#1]. Figure 2 also provides information about the interv ention timing. Delivering instant feedback  was the main preference in most cases (N=12). Two participants were positively surprised by the possibility of providing feedback with different timing, and reflected how t iming relates to the learners’ problems and course contents, e.g., “I had not thought of automated feedback of differen t timing, but yes there are problems that I like to  treat immediately, so I have time to attend personally more challenging problems” [Thin-MOOC#5]. Problems related to self-regulation, content understanding, collaboration and lack of prior background were considered as less pressing problems. Six feedback reactions were configured to react b elatedly, either after a predefined period (N=2) or after instructors’ confirmation (N=4). Additionally, t he instructors’ educational philosophy, e.g., embracing problem-solving approaches, also influenced the timing of f eedback, e.g., “ I want to check the learners’ effort by video re-viewings, before I intervene because a difficult vi deo might require more reviews ” [Thin-MOOC#3], “In  content-related problems, I want to give delayed feedback because the learners are forced to attempt more by reading further. I think then the learning gain is bigger” [Thin-MOOC#5].

## Discussion and conclusions

This study helped us uncover some relations between  learning design elements and feedback decisions. F irst, specific learner problems seem to be more repeated and frequently linked to the same course resources. Concretely, self-regulation was the most freq uently mentioned problem, while community building was considered only by two out of six instructors. Self-regulation problems were linked mostly with content  and assessment resources, while other problems such as collaboration issues were only related with group assignments and group forums. In the future, it is relevant to study if these findings apply to other courses. The n, we could provide recommendations to MOOC instructors about p otential learner problems and their connection with  the course resources to help them anticipate and design effective feedback interventions. Furthermore, the study showed that instructors prio ritized the learner problems that either occur more frequently or that are easy to monitor. Instructors  focused on LA-based indicators related to views of content material, quiz scores, assignment submissions/attempts, etc., rather than on indicators related to posts and learners’ activities in groups and discussion forums. Indeed,  in our study, group and general discussion forums were the least considered resources to spot learner problems. Likewise, de Lima et al. (2019) reported MOOC instructors’ resistance to monitoring discussion forums, because they can become overwhel ming. Thus, on the one hand, we deem relevant the creation of prefixed if-then rules which can be fine-tuned by the instructions given their course objectives. On the other hand, since instructors will not design feedback for resources that they cannot manage, it could be relevant to inform instructors about their automatic feedback possibilities. Considering the timing of feedback, instructors mainly preferred instant feedback, not aiming to provide direct answers but to support the learners’ cogniti ve progress. We found that the type of problem, the  task difficulty and the instructor’s educational  philosophy influence the timing of feedback, as Mas on & Bruning

(2001) also noted. Building on the benefits of purposely belated feedback (K apur, 2008), the study showed the

relevance of being alerted on students’ progress in difficult tasks for some instructors. Additionally, learners could indicate those activities where they want to receiv e instant feedback (such as in quizzes or assignmen ts), so that instructors could prioritize such activities and plan accordingly their feedback strategies. This work has limitations that can guide future research. The paper reports an exploratory study, and the data have a preliminary character. Further studies are needed to establish solid connections between t he various LD elements and the effective feedback strategies. Additionally, we deem it necessary to explore the r ole that Generative AI might play in the automatic provision of personalized feedback. As future work, we aim to train eFeeD4Mi with data from different LDs to automate th e connections between course resources, learner pro blems and feedback interventions to better support instructors in the feedback design.
