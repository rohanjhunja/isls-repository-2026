# Learning on the Move: Ljavek’s Indigenous Mobilities and Relationalities in Taiwan’s Urban Spaces

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This project centers the intergenerational teaching and learning practices necessary within Indigenous sovereignties during forced and coerced relocation and displacement, collaborating with Ljavek, a Paiwan Indigenous community in Taiwan. Globally, Indigenous mobilities are shaped by ongoing settler colonialism that perpetuates Indigenous erasure and elimination, but Indigenous mobilities are also informed by Indigenous agency, self-determination, and survivance that re-makes relational structures to ensure the flourishing of communities, lands, and waters. Displaced by urban expansion, Ljavek resisted relocation through legal trials, built a Paiwan community on lands and waters that they were not familiar with, and created kinships that subverted capitalistic pressures and perpetuated Paiwan languages and cultural practices. By working alongside Ljavek and informed by Indigenous Research Methodologies and Participatory Action Research, this qualitative research project expands definitions of where learning takes place and what constitutes learning by focusing on learning in resistance, community building, partnerships, and placemaking.

## Introduction

As human mobilities become more entangled with hist ories of racism, colonialism, and territorial dispo ssession (Coulthard, 2014; Simpson, 2017), envisioning a wor ld without Indigenous wisdom at the center risks bu ilding worlds that continue and exacerbate the breakdown of our ecological systems (Kimmerer, 2002). Thus, this project seeks to work alongside Indigenous communities to u nderstand our axiological and affective commitments  in mobilities and the rescalings of landscapes (Bang, 2020) and ask what we can collectively do differently to move towards decolonizing futurities.

This project centers the stories of Ljavek, a Paiwa n Indigenous community in Kaohsiung City, Taiwan who moved to Kaohsiung in the 1950s in search of economic opportunities, and in the process, built community, perpetuated Paiwan language and cultural prac tices, organized and resisted capitalistic pressure s from the city government to relocate. Ljavek’s stories are important not only to assert Indigenous presence in urban spaces and resist settler colonial time frames that work to el iminate all traces of In digeneity (Bang et al., 2014; Coulthard, 2014), but also because they unveil the processes of racialization, racism, and settler colonialism in a non-Western country. Settler colonialism in Taiwan has often been depicted as mimicry of European colonialism (Ching, 2012), obscuring the real effects of colonial logics in Taiwan throughout a succession of European, Japanese, and Chinese colonial states (Hirano et al., 2018; Mona, 2019). This project aims to center Ljavek’s own descriptio ns of themselves, their ontologies, axiologies, and epistemologies while le arning alongside them about building kinships while moving across different locations. Guiding questions include:

1) What are the ontological perspectives of being Ljav ek? How do Ljavek community members describe

themselves and what it means to be from Ljavek? What values does Ljavek hold?

2) What axiological and affective commitments undergir d Ljavek’s relationalities? What relational

practices are learned and taught across Ljavek’s generations and in Han settler-dominant urban spaces?

## Conceptual Framework: Indigenous mobilities and community-based learning

Leanne Betasamosake Simpson (2017) writes Indigenous mobilities can include, mobility within grounded normativity as an embedded  Indigenous practice, mobility as a response to colonialism as resistance, mobility as a deliberate and strategic resurgence, and mobility as direct or indirect forced expulsion, relocation, and displacement and the creation of Indigenous diaspora. Indigenous movement can be any  one of those things or a fluid combination of any or all of those elements. (p.196-197) Simpson (2017) writes of a more complex perspective  on Indigenous mobilities, in which settler colonia lism, Indigenous grounded normativity, agency, self-determination, and resistance all play a role. This  perspective counters deficit and damage-centered narratives about Indigenous Peoples, a harmful pattern found in educational research that reifies and reinforces material diffe rences (Patel, 2015; Tuck, 2009), and instead invit es the simultaneous recognition of colonial destruction and Indigenous wisdom, presence, strength, and hope. This project thus seeks to “research back” (Tuck & Yang, 2014) against victimizing portrayals of Indigenous mobilities by documenting Indigenous agency within settler colonial realities. Indigenous mobilities necessitate learning and buil ding new relationships across places, cultures, and systems of power (Bang, 2020). These kinds of learn ing are often overlooked when we emphasize “formal” learning over “informal” learning, a dichotomization grounded in colonial and racist distinctions between school and community learning that has historically made violent definitions of who is “civilized” and who is not (Patel, 2020; San Pedro, 2021). This limits knowledge produ ction to modern, Western educational spaces, disregarding how Indigenous communities have been teaching and l earning intergenerationally since time immemorial (Sabzalian, 2019). Thus, this project seeks to put theorizations of Indigenous mobilities (Simpson, 20 17) in conversation with the learning sciences, such as literature on learning in social movements (Curnow et  al., 2019; Erickson, 2021; Jurow et al, 2016) and learning in cultural ecologies (Bang et al., 2016).

## Methodology

This is a qualitative research project and the meth odologies that I have chosen aim to center ethical research relationalities in educational research, specifical ly, the methodologies are Participatory Action Rese arch/PAR (Lenette, 2022), Indigenous Research Methodologies/ IRMs (Kovach, 2021; Tuck & McKenzie, 2014), and ethnographic methods (Bhattacharya, 2017). I identify as a Hokkien Han person with settler and colonized roots, who was born and raised in Kaohsiung City. These pr ivileges continue to shape the ways that I interact with my environments and vice versa, and limit how I unders tand Paiwan ways of knowing and being, while my accountabilities continue to drive what I do in this research project and my personal life, particularly in the context of Kaohsiung City.

Data collection mainly consists of holding research  conversations, which invite relational care and humility into the data collection process, providin g space for Indigenous ways of knowing and being th at are otherwise dismissed and silenced by Western academic practices and norms (Kovach, 2021). Data gathered from this project, including the conversation transcript s, field notes, and research memos, will be analyze d with qualitative open coding (Bhattacharya, 2017). The g oal of this project is not to presc ribe an “authentic” Ljavek identity by identifying themes, but rather to present heterogeneous stories and densities that are all a part of Ljavek. To do so, I approach qualitative coding analysis through Kovach’s Indigenous Methodologies (2021), which calls for creating story summaries of each individual or community, instead of risking the decontextualization of their stories by prioritizing themes over the nuances of each person.

## Significance and analytic offerings

In global discussions of immigration and forced dis placement, Ljavek’s stories resonate with settler-colonial realities elsewhere, suggesting that Indigenous mobilities, community building, and resistance are critical sites of learning that teach us all about decolonization and rekindling ethical relationships with humans, places, and morethan-humans.
