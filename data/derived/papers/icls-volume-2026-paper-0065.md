# Students’ Mental Models of AI Chatbots in Inquiry Learning: a Friend, an Instructor, a Browser, and a Partner

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study investigates how high school students perceive and interact with an AI chatbot during a science lab experiment. Analysis of chatbot transcripts revealed four enacted roles in student-AI interaction (Instructor, Browser, Human, and Reflective Partner) that reflect students’ underlying mental models and how they conceptualize the chatbot’s capabilities and relationship. Students’ conversations shifted across these roles, with Browser and Instructor predominating. In contrast, post-activity surveys offered retrospective perspectives that largely framed the chatbot as an Instructor and, less frequently, as a Browser, overlooking the reflective and human-like engagements evident in transcripts. By confronting students’ talk about AI with their live interactions during inquiry, this study highlights mental models as dynamic, enacted in practice, and locally and culturally constructed in conversation, suggesting directions for designing educational AI systems that can accommodate dynamic roles.

Artificial intelligence (AI) chatbots are increasingly integrated into educational contexts, where they have been positioned as tutors, peers, or learning companions due to their potential to provide support, answer questions, and foster engagement (Labadze et al., 2023). Learners’ role attributions to AI can reveal their underlying mental models AI chatbots, which shape how they interpret their responses, evaluate reliability, and engage in inquiry by enabling them to flexibly move between retrieving, interpreting, and reflecting on information, rather than relying on one-way exchanges (Cho & Cho, 2024; Duncan, 2025).

Although mental models play a central role in supporting inquiry (Greca & Moreira, 2000), little is known about how learners’ models of AI shape their engagement during hands-on science activities and how these models are negotiated over time. This gap is particularly significant in science education, where inquiry involves iterative movements between retrieving information, following procedures, reflecting on results, and making sense of unexpected outcomes (National Research Council, 2012). AI technologies add a new dimension to this conversation: unlike traditional search engines, chatbots afford multi-turn dialogue, customized feedback, speculation, and tone mirroring, enabling students to engage in co-constructed reasoning rather than me re information seeking (Luckin & Holmes, 2016). Building on these affordances, our study investigates how students’ mental models are enacted and negotiated in practice, confronting how students talk about AI with how they actually interact with it in inquiry contexts.

This study examines how high school students interacted with a science-focused generative AI chatbot during a cyanotype experiment, a chemistry-based activity embedded in our Lab-on-a-Book (Sotério & Blikstein, 2025), and connects to our broader research on STEM-Messaging System (SMS), a low-bandwidth approach for inquiry-driven learning through mobile messaging (Furmann et al., 2024). Drawing on both chatbot transcripts and survey responses, we identified four enacted roles (Instructor, Browser, Reflective  Partner, and Human) that map onto distinct ways of conceptualizing AI: as an authoritative teacher, as a search tool, as a peer-like collaborator, and as a social actor. We argue that these role enactments can provide empirical traces of students’ mental models of AI, capturing how these models are enacted, revised, and hybridized in real time. By analyzing these interaction patterns, we investigate how learners negotiate the role of AI in science learning. This leads us to two guiding research questions: RQ1. In what ways do students interact with an AI chatbot during a hands-on science activity, and how do they retrospectively describe those interactions? RQ2. What do students’ interactions and reflections reveal about their mental models of AI? Comprehending learners’ mental models – their internal representations of how a system works and what it cando – is central to explaining how they interpret, predict, and respond to AI tools. In cognitive science, mental models are constructed, revisable,  and often incomplete representations that people use for reasoning and prediction (Johnson-Laird, 1983). In science education and constructivist traditions, knowledge is seen as fragmented and context-dependent, which DiSessa (1993) described as “knowledge in pieces.” Learners 546 reassemble these pieces through interaction rather than applying fixed conceptions. Conceptual change theories further emphasize that learners assimilate new systems into familiar schemas while also accommodating novel aspects that challenge their expectat ions (Piaget, 1970; Vosniadou, 1994; Greca & Moreira, 2000). Recent research reinforces this view of mental models as dynamic and evolving. Svoboda (2023), for example, frames misconceptions as emergent patterns in continuous cognitive processes. Pedrera et al. (2025) show how students’ conceptualizations shift over time during scientific activities, while Pacaci et al. (2023) demonstrate that conceptual change strategies systematically restructure learners’ conceptions by engaging and reorganizing their existing knowledge through cognitive conflict, bridging, or ontological shifts. Applying this dynamic view to AI use allows us to examine not just what students “think” the chatbot is, but how their understandings evolve through interaction, shaping the roles, questions, and epistemic moves they bring into the exchange. In human– computer interaction, mental models emerge from both personal experience and formal training, guiding how people perceive the capabilities, limitations, and purpose of a system (Cho & Cho, 2024). In educational contexts, mental models influence how learners interpret AI responses, evaluate their reliability, and decide when to trust or question them (Solyst et al., 2024). Pre-existing conceptions, often anthropomorphic and shaped by everyday encounters with human-like AI, can lead to overtrust or misconceptions that persist unless explicitly addressed (Mertala et al., 2022). Studies examining students’ sustained engagement and sense-making of AI show that such experiences can foster more integrated and critical understandings of both technical and socio-political dimensions (Irgens et al., 2022; Solyst et al., 2024). This underscores the value of learning environments that frame AI as a material that can be shaped and reworked, where its form is influenced by humandecisions about data, algorithms, and ethics, enabling students to actively build and refine these systems (MoralesNavarro et al., 2025). Critical Machine Learning (ML) extends this agency by prompting students to examine who designs AI, for whom, and to what ends, and to propose redesigns that address inequities and promote social good (Irgens et al., 2022). This perspective aligns with critical ML approaches that foreground learners’ agency in shaping AI systems.

Building on this framing, we approach students’ interactions with an AI chatbot not as fixed indicators of pre-existing conceptions but as enacted roles that surface in context, offering methodological windows into the construction of mental models. This perspective extends prior work on mental models as fragmented, revisable, and culturally situated by showing how they are negotiated in moment-to-moment interaction. It also allows us to connect cultural schemas such as “teacher” or “Google” with the dynamic roles that emerge in practice, thereby offering a more situated account of how learners conceptualize AI in science learning. Design and settings This intervention took place at an independent school in Hong Kong, recognized for its strong emphasis on inquiry-based and project-centered science learning. The study used a design-based research approach to investigate how students engaged with a hybrid learning system that integrated a physical science experiment book, Lab-on-a-Book (Sotério & Blikstein, 2025), and an AI-powered chatbot, Lab-on-a-Bot. Lab-on-a-Book is a 40-page printed activity book enabling at-home experimentation through embedded cyanotype paper, scaffolded inquiry prompts, and thematic sections (Explore Your Space, Develop and Build, Understand Your Results, What If, Design Your Next Adventure, Share Your Experience) aligned with the National Research Council (2012) scientific practices.

Lab-on-a-Bot, a chatbot built on ChatGPT-4 and deployed via Poe, was preloaded with the book content to extend this approach. It was customized through a structured system prompt embedding activity stages, response length limits, and interaction guardrails that encouraged questions rather thandirect solutions. Grounded in constructionist principles, the bot used concise curiosity-driven prompts and playful language to support student agency. These constraints standardized interaction style without prescribing reasoning or decisions. Conversation logs were anonymized and collected through school accounts following institutional consent procedures, positioning the chatbot as a partner in inquiry rather than an authoritative source. Implementation unfolded in four phases: (1) Design & Fabrication – creating the Lab-on-a Book and Lab-on-a-Bot with tailored content for high school students; (2) Orientation – introducing the activity in class and ensuring chatbot access; (3) At-Home Experimentation – students conducted cyanotype experiments, optionally using the chatbot for support; and (4) Reflection & Redesign – sharing results, discussing experiences, and suggesting improvements to what ends, and to propose redesigns that address inequities and promote social good (Irgens et al., 2022). ## Participants

Fourteen students (seven in 9th grade and seven in 10th grade) participated in the study. All students were enrolled in a special extracurricular science course designed to support extended inquiry beyond the core curriculum, and were proficient in English. The group was academically high-performing and had prior exposure to AI technologies and hands-on experimentation. One experienced science teacher supported the activity by facilitating classroom sessions and coordinating logistics. The authors of this study led the design, development, facilitation, and data collection and analysis stages. All student names appearing in examples are pseudonyms to protect participants’ privacy.

The central data sources for this study were the full transcripts of students’ chatbot conversations (278 messages exported via Poe’s sharing feature) and post-activity survey responses. The transcripts captured real-time interaction patterns, student-generated questions, and reflections during at-home experimentation, while the surveys provided structured feedback on usability, perceived helpfulness, redesign suggestions, and students’ perceptions of the chatbot’s role. Supplementary data included classroom discussions, student-generated artifacts (cyanotype prints and annotated notebooks), and video recordings of class sessions documenting peer exchanges, tool reflections, and presentations.

## Data analysis

This paper analyzes data from both chatbot conversations and post-activity surveys. Both data sources were coded using the same four-role interaction scheme, as shown in Table 1. The four-role coding scheme emerged through an iterative, theory-informed process. We first drew on existing literature on learner– AI interaction to develop preliminary categories (Cho & Cho, 2024; Mertala et al., 2022; Irgens et al., 2022; Solyst et al., 2024), then refined them through repeated coding of transcripts, analytic discussions, and data revisitation. The final roles (Instructor, Browser, Human, Reflective Partner) reflect both established frameworks and patterns that consistently surfaced in students’ conversations. For the transcripts, two researchers jointly coded five samples to refine the scheme and then independently coded the remainder, resolving discrepancies through discussion using a resolution tree. Survey responses were analyzed thematically, with answers categorized according to the same role framework to enable direct comparison between enacted and self-reported roles. We used purposive sampling (Patton, 1990) to select four focal students (one per interaction type) whose engagement was representative of the broader class.

Using Python, we normalized role labels, mapped them to the four categories, and computed segment start-end percentages for visualization.

Human type interaction Vanessa (student #1) opened her session with the classic “Why did the chicken cross the road?” joke rather than a content-related question. The bot responded in kind, linking the joke to the experiment: “To test if its shadow could be trapped on the other side! Shadows and light are key to cyanotypes.” When Vanessa corrected it with the classic punchline, the bot acknowledged her answer but again gently returned to the experiment by saying, “If the chicken left a shadow behind, we could trap it using cyanotype paper and sunlight! Want to try that out?” Vanessa resisted, replying “nah, not really,” and only then shifted to a scientific question about using leaves, to which the bot responded with an explanation of how opacity and texture affect cyanotype patte rns. Later in the session, Vanessa deliberately repeated the same chicken joke to see whether the bot would remember. The bot produced a new, context-specific answer that tied the joke to overexposure issues, and Vanessa replied, “Bot, we’ve gone through this before,” playfully testing its memory and conversational continuity. At another point, she explicitly tested the bot’s joke-generating abilities by prompting, “Can you tell me a joke?” to which the bot responded with a cyanotype-themed pun: “Why did the UV light get promoted? Because it always made a brilliant impression!” Her conversation alternated between off-topic jokes and experiment-focused exchanges, with the bot consistently redirecting humor back to cyanotype concepts (for example, suggesting that Santa’s sleigh would need multiple sheets of paper to fit his print). Human-like interactions were her predominant interaction style, accounting for 75% of her conversation. Across the class, 50% of students used Human interactions at least once, but only 14% relied on them more than on any other role, as Vanessa did. Instructor-type interaction Henry (student #8) primarily interacted with the bot as if it were an instructor. He asked practical, step-by-step questions to guide his setup, beginning with “Can I use lights in rooms for UV light?” The bot responded with clear procedural guidance, explaining that “you can use UV lamps or blacklights as your UV light source indoors, but handle UV light safely and avoid looking directly at it.” Henry then followed up by asking whether other materials could be used as a light source: “What about a chandelier?” The bot promptly explained that a chandelier “likely won’t work unless it has UV bulbs” and suggested alternatives such as UV lamps or sunlight. After that, Henry sought further clarification on how to proceed with the materials he had on hand. He asked, “What should I do if it’s at night and I don’t have UV light?” The bot replied with a sequenced set of options, advising him to “wait for daylight, use a UV lamp, or spend the time planning your cyanotype design.” Across these exchanges, Henry positioned the bot as a procedural guide, asking it to solve immediate problems or clarify basic concepts rather than exploring or joking with it. This procedural orientation dominated his interaction, accounting for 60% of his conversation. Across the class, 36% of students used the Instructor role at least once, but only Henry relied on it more than on any other role.

Reflective-partner type interaction Sarah (student #6) began by asking about the envelopes embedded in Lab-on-a-Book, to which the bot explained their purpose and encouraged her to explore the materials inside. After conducting the experiment, she returned with a troubleshooting question: “Why is it that when I put the paper with the samples on top and then rinsed it, it came up blank? (it was under a really strong and bright sun).” The bot responded with a diagnostic sequence, prompting her to revisit her steps: “If you used the wrong side of the paper, no reaction would occur. Even in bright sun, UV can be blocked, and short exposure or poor rinsing can lead to blank results. Do you want to double-check your steps?” When Sarah clarified that she had followed the procedure correctly, the bot refined its response, suggesting issues like expired paper, blocked UV, or humidity, and inviting her to “test again with a fresh piece of paper.” This back-and-forth focused on her prior actions and results, illustrating how reflectivepartner interactions unfolded through iterative troubleshooting with the bot. Across the class, 43% of students 550 used Reflective Partner interactions at least once, but only Sarah relied on this role more than on any other (accounting for 50% of her conversation).

Part 2: Self-reported roles from surveys While the transcript data revealed the roles students enacted during their conversations with the AI-chatbot, the post-activity self-reported surveys provide insight into how students retrospectively described the bot’s role in their experiments. To align these perspectives, we compared the roles enacted in transcripts with those self-reported in surveys (see Figure 3).

The comparison between students’ self-reports and their actual interactions with Lab-on-a-Bot highlights a notable discrepancy. As shown in the transcript analysis (Figure 3a), students engaged with Lab-on-a-Bot primarily in a browser-like manner (93%), followed by human-like exchanges (50%), reflective prompts (43%), and instructor-like interactions (36%). However, when asked to self-report on the role Lab-on-a-Bot played (Figure 3b), the majority of students (64%) described it as an instructor, while only 14% characterized it as a search engine. Another 14% reported not using ChatGPT at all, and 7% (one student) did not respond. This divergence suggests that while students often interacted with ChatGPT as if it were a search engine, they were more likely to retrospectively frame its role as a pedagogical support. Such differences between observed use and perceived role raise important questions about how students conceptualize AI tools in learning contexts and the extent to which their perceptions align with actual interaction patterns. Our analysis found that in a hands-on science activity on cyanotypes, students engaged with the AI chatbot through four roles: Instructor, Browser, Human, and Reflective Partner. Most displayed a dominant role throughout their conversations but rarely relied on a single role exclusively; instead, they shifted fluidly depending on their goals, the task, and the chatbot’s responses.

Regarding RQ1, on how students interacted with the AI chatbot during the science activity, we observed from conversation transcripts that, despite the chatbot’s dialogic potential, many students relied primarily on browser-like behaviors, using it as a quick information source rather than a conversational partner (Solyst et al., 2024; Vanderlyn et al., 2025). Several factors may contribute to this tendency: students’ familiarity with Googlestyle search schemas, the time constraints of classroom settings, a nd the chatbot’s design, which can easily accommodate short Q&A interactions. This mirrors prior findings that learners often adopt transactional Q&A habits with AI, limiting opportunities for reasoning or joint problem-solving (Morales-Navarro et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2025).

Instructor-like interactions were largely procedural, with students using the chatbot as a step-by-step guide for instructions, timing, or permissions – akin to a “pedagogical agent” (Chiu et al., 2023; Luckin & Holmes, 2016). While this role supports efficiency and reduces cognitive load (Knoth et al., 2024; Labadze et al., 2023), it can limit exploration when AI remains directive. Balancing guidance with exploratory prompts could shift students from “what to do” toward “why and how” questions (Druga et al., 2018; Morales-Navarro et al., 2024). In reflective-partner interactions, though less frequent, students would look back at their experimental 551 the bot (Irgens et al., 2022; Luckin & Holmes, 2016). These interactions created conversational spaces for students to articulate their observations and refine their interpretations with the bot’s diagnostic prompts. Human-like interactions, where students engaged socially with the chatbot through jokes, role-play, or meta-prompts, highlighted how anthropomorphic mental models (Cho & Cho, 2024; Mertala et al., 2022) influence engagement in science learning. These exchanges, often initiated by students, coexisted with shifts to Browser or Instructor roles, reflecting fluid and context-dependent role negotiation (Cho & Cho, 2024). Rather than serving as distractions, such interactions can be leveraged as entry points for inquiry by linking humor or social cues to scientific content, embedding adaptive prompts to encourage role shifts, and clarifying AI capabilities, thereby harnessing their motivational potential for deeper reasoning (Huang et al., 2025). Overall, RQ1 shows that students enacted fluid roles, oscillating between transactional, procedural, reflective, and social forms of engagement. This confirms prior findings that chatbot use is rarely linear (MoralesNavarro et al., 2024) and underscores the design challenge of scaffolding students to move beyond browser-like habits toward deeper collaborative reasoning. However, it is essential to note that information retrieval is not inherently passive; in principle, it can form part of a broader inquiry process. What distinguishes AI from search engines is its capacity for conversational follow-up, scaffolding, and adaptation, which, when deliberately designed and enacted, can support sensemaking (Yang et al., 2020). Future work should examine how retrieval and inquiry can work in tandem rather than in opposition. Regarding RQ2, which examined what students’ interactions reveal about their mental models of AI, the contrast between surveys and transcripts provides key insights. In the surveys, most students retrospectively described the chatbot as a pedagogical-instructional actor, often referring to it as a teacher or helper. This may partly reflect the cultural framing of the school context: students are used to associating learning tools with authoritative figures, especially in structured, high-achieving educational settings (Cho & Cho, 2024; Mertala et al., 2022). In practice, however, transcripts showed that students often treated the chatbot as a search engine, a playful partner, or a reflective collaborator. Self-reports assimilated the chatbot into conventional categories of school learning, whereas transcripts captured accommodation and hybridization, in which  students revised their models in use (Johnson-Laird, 1983; diSessa, 1993; Pedrera et al., 2025). This divergence suggests that students’ mental models o f AI chatbots are dynamic rather than static. One possible explanation is that students are combining and equilibrating, on the fly, their previous (and limited) knowledge about AI, cultural expectations of what it should do and be, and their actual situated interactions. This dissonance was one of the most surprising While our analysis emphasizes students’ agency in enacting and revising roles through interaction, part of the explanation for the divergence could be that generative AI models do not enter learning environments as neutral tools. As Rama and Airoldi (2025) argue, chatbots embody a “machine habitus”: patterns of taste, stance, and authority encoded through their training data and reinforced by design choices. In high-achieving school contexts, this habitus often resonates with institutional expectations of expertise, which may partially explain why students retrospectively labeled the chatbot as a teacher even when their enacted interactions were exploratory or playful. Rather than a contradiction, this tension reflects a negotiation between culturally availa ble categories (teacher, helper) and situated, emergent roles enacted in the course of inquiry. Our findings suggest that educational AI design should support learners in shifting between retrieval, exploration, and reflection, enabling AI to function not only as a source of answers but as a partner in sensemaking, hypothesis testing, and iterative inquiry (Luckin & Holmes, 2016; Irgens et al., 2022; Huang et al., 2025). Our paper contributes to extending AI in education research by reconceptualizing mental models of AI as enacted and dynamic. Rather than treating them as static, our study shows how students’ mental models surfaced, shifted, and hybridized through moment-to-moment engagement with an AI chatbot. By triangulating transcripts with post-activity surveys, we found that self-reports often flattened the chatbot into familiar cultural schemas, most prominently as a teacher or helper, or as a Google-like tool. In co ntrast, transcript analysis revealed dynamic, context-dependent enactments, which we categorized into four roles: Instructor, Browser, Human, and Reflective Partner. These enactments point to distinct mental models: AI as an authoritative instructor, a search tool, a social actor, and a peer-like collaborator. Students moved fluidly among these models, revising their expectations of the chatbot throughout the interaction. This finding highlights the importance of considering these mental models in AIEd design, as they may drive students’ interaction in decisive ways even if they are not fully aware of it. While AI has the potential to support exploratory and collaborative learning, most students still rely on it primarily as a browser. This pattern reflects broader tensions in AI and educational technology between “answer engines” and tools for inquiry. Current public AI interfaces are optimized for rapid factual retrieval, reinforcing existing search habits rather than encouraging dialogic exploration. This underscores the importance of both 552 design and pedagogy in shifting interactions from transactional Q&A toward more reflective, social, and collaborative engagements.

This study has some limitations. It draws on a relatively small sample of students in a private school setting, limiting the generalizability of the observed interaction patterns. The roles identified here may manifest differently in other educational settings and among more diverse student populations. Future work should involve larger and more varied samples to examine how different contexts shape role enactments and mental models of AI. Further studies may also investigate how instructional design, interface features, and classroom practices can foster role fluidity and support students in transitioning from answer-seeking to sustained inquiry with AI.
