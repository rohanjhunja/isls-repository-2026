# Two Spaces, One Goal: Examining a Dyad’s Scientific Inquiry in Game-Based Learning and Beyond

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Engaging students in scientific inquiry across digital and physical spaces presents both opportunities and challenges in K-12 STEM education. This study explores how a pair of learners collaboratively engage in the inquiry process through a single-player educational game. Using interaction analysis, we examine how the learners construct, refine, and revisit their hypotheses, leveraging both digital and physical tools designed to support inquiry learning. Our findings emphasize how these hybrid scaffolds facilitate collaborative discourse and hypothesis generation, while also identifying gaps in students' evidence evaluation and justification processes. This study provides empirical evidence on the potential of hybrid scaffolds to support collaborative inquiry and highlights the necessity of instructional strategies to help students develop robust criteria for evidence-based reasoning and hypothesis reflection.

## Introduction

Scientific inquiry is a systematic approach to expl oring the natural world and proposing explanations grounded in empirical evidence (Pedaste et al., 2015; Scalise & Clarke-Midura, 2018). When scientific inquiry becomes collaborative, students engage in robust dialogues, build upon each other's ideas, and jointly navigate through the inquiry process from posing questions to generating  solutions (Hmelo-Silver et al., 2007; Saleh et al., 2021). Game-based learning environments can support scientific inquiry by providing structured spaces for experimentation and evidence gathering, enabling studen ts to iteratively generate, test, and refine hypoth eses through self-directed exploration (Lester et al., 2014; Clark et al., 2016). While most educational games are designed for individual use, recent studies have be gun to explore how pairs of students coordinate the ir inquiry activities when sharing these environments. Pair discourse allows students to dynamically shift roles and distribute cognitive responsibilities while actively engaging with science concepts  and practices  (Chi & Wylie, 2014; Roschelle, 1992). Students' engagement with educati onal games requires them to coordinate their invest igation across digital game environments and physical interactions. This complexity intensifies when two students share a game designed for individual play. To support stu dents navigating these spaces, scaffolds embedded a cross digital and physical tools can guide collaborative inquiry and discourse (Reiser & Tabak, 2014; vandi jk & Lazonder, 2016). However, little is known about how pairs of learners coordinate their investigation when sharing tools designed for individual use, particularly how they utilize mediating tools to support their collaborative work. This study examines how pairs engage in inquiry while sharing a single-player educational game, addressing two research questions: RQ1: How does a pair navigate between digital and physical spaces during scientific inquiry? RQ2: How does the pair utilize designed tools to support their collaborative inquiry process?

## Research design

The game used in this study is a narrative-based educational game where students act as scient ific investigators solving a medical mystery at a research camp. Stude nts explore multiple interactive spaces to gather e vidence, conduct experiments, and interact with characters w hile investigating bacterial or viral diseases. The  game provides scaffolds such  as reading materials and object scanners that suppo rt students in learning content knowledge, testing objects, and recording results (Rowe et al., 2011). Complementing these in-game tools, a structured workbook was designed to support pairs' collaborative investigation in the physical space. The workbook provided spaces for documenting evidence, compar ing potential diseases, and making action plans through two main sections: shared evidence worksheets and investigation diaries. Both in-game and physical tools aimed to guide pairs through their inquiry process and facilitate collaborative discussion.

## Data collection and analysis

This study was conducted at a public charter school in the Midwestern United States, where eleven pairs of middle school students participated across three learning sessions. Data included video recordings, game log files, completed workbooks, and pre-posttests. The analysis proceeded in two phases. In the initial phase, we created content logs for all eleven pairs using video data and game logs. From these, we selected one pair for  detailed case analysis based on their rich collaborative dia logue, systematic note-taking practices, and self-directed investigation with minimal teacher support. This pa ir, consisting of two 8th-grade students, demonstrated significant learning gains (Henry: pre=2, post=10; Erin: pre=3,  post=7) compared to the class average improvement from pre-test (M=3.53, SD=1.37) to post-test (M=6.23, SD=1.9). In the second phase, we used interaction analysis (Jordan & Henderson, 1995) and   identified representative episodes showing distinctive patterns of physical workspace use and collaborativ e discussions, which were triangulated with two other researchers.

Systematic tool use for scientific investigation (RQ1) The pair engaged in systematic scientific investiga tion across learning sessions totaling 80 minutes. Throughout their investigation, they thoroughly utilized digit al resources, accessing 14 out of 17 available resources before conducting diagnostic tests in their final session. By the end of data collection, they had nearly solved the mystery, having eliminated two of three potential contaminants shared by all patients through testing. Without explicit discussion, the pair naturally adopted complementar y roles that supported their investigation: Henry m anaged game navigation and read materials aloud while Erin  maintained detailed documentation in the workbook. ## Discussion and conclusion

This case study of a student pair navigating a sing le-player educational game  as a dyad  provides insights for designing collaborative learning environments. Thro ugh analyzing interactions, we observed how the pair used available resources and systematically documented t heir investigation, narrowing down their hypothesis  through both digital in-game interactions and detailed physical documentation. Yet, their reliance on partial evidence and implicit reasoning points to gaps in fully expressi ng scientific reasoning and reflection. The findings reveal that while these tools enable inquiry progress and hypothesis refinement, they also const rain the depth of scientific reasoning, primarily focusing students on concise s tatements of claims and evidence. This simplificati on limits the iterative, reflective nature that is essential for developing authentic scientific reasoning practices (Manz, 2015).

To address these issues, we propose two key recomme ndations. First, the design of these tools should facilitate the dyad learning process and actively support students’ epistemic development  (Duncan et al., 2018). Tools could incorporate features that allow student s to track and review the evolution of their claims  and their shifting evidence selection over time. Such features would help students engage in a more reflective and iterative inquiry process, promoting  deeper understanding and insight into how their rea soning develops. Additionally, reflection prompts that encourage students to artic ulate the rationale behind changes in their hypothe ses could foster the establishment of criteria for valid reasoning and epistemic values in a domain-specific subject. Second, the role of teachers is essential in bridging the gaps where tools fall short. Teachers can provide adaptive support that encourages students to questi on and critique their hypotheses beyond surface-level conclusions but to understand the reasoning behind scientific inquiry practices (Lazonder & Harmsen, 2016). For example, teachers can facilitate discursive practices in physical spaces, where groups can articulate and elaborate on their ideas, transitioning from pair or small group discussions to larger community exchanges. These practices mirror the collaborative nature of scientific disco very and align more closely with how scientists sha re, critique, and refine ideas in professional se ttings. To conclude, enhancing designed scaffolds and integrating teacher support can better enable students to engage in ite rative, evidence-based reasoning, aligning their learning experiences more closely with authentic scientific practices. We hope these insights can contribute to ongoing efforts to develop and advance  inquiry-based learning environments in K-12 STEM education, particularly in supporting collaborative scientific discursive practices.
