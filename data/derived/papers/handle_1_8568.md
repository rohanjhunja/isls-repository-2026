# Design-Based Research to Improve the Lesson Study Project for Co-Evolution of Research, Development and Practices

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In order to demonstrate that applying design-based research (DBR) methodology to improve a lesson study project provides an arena for co-evolution of implementation research, system development and everyday practices, this paper analyzed a 12-year learning sciences project in Japan. The project consisted of DBR on three levels: 1) a continuous cycle of DBR on each of the practices in the lesson study cycle, 2) DBR on the development of technologies to support the lesson studies and 3) long-term implementation research on the whole sociotechnical system to bridge theory and practice. The analysis revealed that the scheme of the project shifted to support fast but constructive lesson study for practitioners to create design principles based on an understanding of how students learn, requiring the co-evolution above.

## Introduction

The gap between theory and practice in education re mains a central issue in the learning sciences. Thi s paper demonstrates that the issue is best handled by co-evolution of implementation research, system development and everyday practices through our design-based research (DBR) of the lesson study approach (Lewis et al., 2006). The shift from “theory into practice” to “theory th rough practice” approach (Schon, 1983) requires researchers to support teachers to create their own theory or principled practical knowledge (PPK: Bereiter, 2014). To increase the practicality of PPK, Janssen et al. (2015) proposed fast and frugal heuristics  (cost-effective procedures for design and enactment of educational innovations), which indicates the importance of continuous cycle of creating knowledge from lesson to lesson. Lesson study is one of promising candidates of the theorythrough-practice approaches, but has swung back and forth between two poles: “teacher-centric” pole (how and what teachers  should teach) and “learner-centric” pole (how each student learned). There is room for improvement in the approaches of lesson study towar ds integrating the strengths of the both, for examp le, combining subject matter knowledge with insights into student learning. In addition to the issue of this how-to-do lesson study, a who-is-to-be-involved issue remains. The aforementioned integration requires contributions from the boards of education (BOE) to create a partnership among teachers and researchers in order to afford teachers with opportunities of learning subject matters across schools and reflecting on their own students within schools. Lesson study also develops with the help of technology. For the how-to-do issue, technology supports a lesson study cycle of plan-do-reflect in a timely manner for teachers; for the who-is-to-be-involved issue, it helps teachers turn the cycle collaboratively by allowing  them to keep a record of lesson study beyond space-time constraints. Vrikki et al. (2019) developed a syste m for lesson study which made time-consuming dialog ue analysis feasible. More importantly, their work cam e from the accumulated research where they found an integration of various elements essential: support systems, models of lesson study, a teachers’ learning community and teacher-researcher partnerships. Their trajectory of research was an evolutionary one in the face of a scalingup pressure, through shifting from working under favorable conditions to less favorable ones. These findings imply that we should consider long-t erm implementation research on the whole sociotechnical system to bridge theory and practice. For the approaches of lesson study and its supporting system, we need to design, test and improve them in order for teachers to be able to learn the sciences of how students learn. In order to clarify how such DBR of lesson study takes place and what is needed for that especially in the face of the upscaling pressure, we conducted a collaborativ e lesson study project utilizing an instructional f ramework, the “Knowledge Constructive Jigsaw” method (hereafter KCJ; Miyake, 2013), which was undertaken over a 12year period by the Consortium for Renovating Educat ion of the Future (hereafter CoREF) of the Universi ty of Tokyo and its regional board of education partners from the year 2010. The project builds on the theor y of constructive interaction (Miyake, 1986) to design 1) a continuous cycle of DBR on each of the practices in the lesson study cycle, 2) DBR on the development of to ols and technologies to support the lesson studies,  and 3) long-term implementation research on the whole socio-technical system of the lesson study project. Hereafter, we explain the initial research scheme of the project from the three aspects: research, practice and technology in the Method, and analyze changes in scale, practical and technological aspects through CoREF’s eleven annual reports (CoREF, 2011-2021), in which we define the appearance of new ways, formats and digital tools for lesson study as innovations in the Results, followed by the analysis of research change in the Discussion. ## The Initial Research Scheme as the Method

Research aspect: Theory of constructive interaction The project builds on the theory of constructive in teraction which came from detailed dialogue analyse s of collaborative problem solving in cognitive science (Miyake, 1986). A series of research demonstrates t hat two persons, when engaged in solving a shared problem, exchange the roles of a task-doer who proposes solu tions and a monitor who reflects upon such proposals. Suc h role exchange potentially deepens each participat ing individual’s understanding of the problem, and eventually leads her/him to arrive at their own solutio n. Miyake and her colleagues brought such a mechanism into the classroom, finding that students were able to modify their experience-based, folk concepts towards more scientific ones through dialogues (Miyake, 2013). The project also uses that theory as the implementation theory, that  is, the core of educational reforms at every level  from the classroom level to the policymaking level, in order  to “craft coherence” of learner-centered, collabor ative knowledge construction among all members including the students and teachers. Practical aspect: Knowledge Constructive Jigsaw (KCJ) and lesson study To bring constructive interaction into school learning, the project developed an instructional framework, the KCJ (CoREF, 2011), which consists of five learning acti vities for students: 1) pre-writing (individual), 2) expert activity (group), 3) jigsaw activity (group), 4) cross-talk activity (class), and 5) post-writing (individual). The KCJ motivates teachers to engage in lesson study, as they are required to design the “problem (jigsaw task)” and “learning materials (for the expert activity)” by themselves and to be proactive in discussing the content prior to the lesson. It also gives teachers a tool for reflection on learning. Since it lets students write down answers to the same question twice, the improvement between the two answers gives hints to infer what each ind ividual learns. Students’ dialogues during the expert, jigs aw and cross talk activities inform teachers what k ind of interaction deepens understanding of each. Such a shared framework makes planning, observation and reflection easier before, during and after the lesson, allowing the teachers to focus on the content and learning processes. Technological aspects The project uses a mailing list for online discussion on the planning and reporting of KCJ lessons in each subject group across the schools and the BOEs. Recording of  class and students’ dialogues was recommended, but  the project partners were given discretion as to what device to use for the recording (video, IC-recorder, notes etc.).

## Context of the study

CoREF started a “new learning project” from fiscal year 2010 with nine municipal BOEs from six prefect ures (see Table 1). Each BOE registered one or more core teachers who volunteered to propel the research. The teachers also enrolled themselves in one of the subject discussion groups on the mailing list chosen from Japanese language, social science, mathematics, science, and English. Thirteen core teachers from elementary and middle s chools participated in the first on-the-job training workshop run by CoREF to experience the KCJ lesson as students and understand the background theory. The teachers designed lesson plans in the subject discussion groups via faceto-face workshops and online means. They tested the lessons in their schools and opened select practices for the purpose of lesson study. Yet, each BOE was given di scretion as to how they conducted the lesson study,  which was often done in a teacher-centric way. At the end of the fiscal year, the teachers shared their experiences in an open research meeting to extract generalizable know ledge about student learning and lesson designs. Co REF summarized the entire activities in its annual report, including a DVD of the teaching materials of the core teachers complete with teaching plans, learning materials such as worksheets, and reflection sheets to review the lessons. The research team started with three university faculty members at its core, and has still not increased.

Practical aspects “Key events” in Table 1, with the years of their first appearance in parentheses, were grouped into four periods in order to find commonalities among the innovations. The key events (practice) indicate that CoREF’s lesson study shifted first to putting more weight on evidence of  actual student learning (revised reflection sheet,  analysis of pre-and post-lesson comprehension), followed by an alyses of the learning processes including dialogue s (multilateral dialogue analysis), and finally, the pre-lesson study to turn the cycle of lesson study as the hypothesisformation and testing cycle (simulation sheet, hypothesis-testing lesson study). CoREF revised the reflection sheet in 2012 to explicitly ask the teacher to pick any three students, copy their pre-and post-lesson answers, compare them with the learning outcomes he or she  had expected, and find points of improvement in the lesson. This additiona l format was necessary to take advantage of the ass essment functions built in the KCJ procedure, as CoREF (2020) reported that over 60% of the materials of 2010 and 2011 omitted pre-and post-lesson questions in the lesso n, but less than 10% of them did so from 2012 onwar ds. In addition to the “assessment of pre-and post-lesson  comprehension” using such data, CoREF introduced t he “multilateral dialogue analysis” in 2016, by which the teachers analyzed students’ dialogues collaboratively from various viewpoints such as cognitive, social, motivational and instructional. Faced with a scaling-up issue, CoREF devised the “s imulation sheet”, by which the teachers simulate how students learn and make their own hypotheses about the students’ learning processes, and a “hypothesistesting lesson study (HTLS)” that combines hypothesis formation (how students will learn) and testing (how they have learned). Specifically, HTLS lets teachers 1) engage in the simulation activity before the lesson, 2) continue to observe the same student or group together with multiple teachers during the lesson, 3) share and d iscuss observations after the lesson on the learning processes of target students compared with the hypothesis of the main teacher who conducted the lesson, 4) make recommendations on the lesson design, 5) listen to the self-reflection of the main teacher and 6) write down their own findings from the lesson study. A typical lesson study often skips steps 1), 3) and 6), lets participants observe free ly in step 2), and places step 5) before 3), result ing in negative comments from veteran teachers to the main, often young teacher and aggressive exchanges of lesson techniques and knowledge of the subject matter, lacking any evidence of student learning. LV provides the function of auto-transcribing the students’ dialogues in all of the groups during the class, and CA does that of providing transcripts, which were electronically searchable using keywords. Two systems were integrated so that  the teachers analyzed the transcribed dialogues multilaterally while listening to the actual studen t voices. About half an hour after the lesson, the teachers were able to use the transcription of the dialogue of at least one jigsaw group synchronized with video data. The latter shift is well represented by the develop ment of a “Learning Note (LN)” system, a website specifically for the project members, which collect s and categorizes the emails posted on the discussi on list by lesson topic, displays posts with attached files in  chronological order, enables searches by keywords and recommends related topics. ## Discussion

The shifts in the practical and technological aspec ts coincided with the upscaling and involvement of diverse teachers around 2016 and 2017, implying that, in the face of challenges, CoREF had prepared for a more teachercentered, self-propelled form of lesson study decentering the role of the researchers. Looking back at the inception phase from 2010 to 2011 and the transition phase from 2012 to 2016, CoREF worked closely with select teachers as if handing over our version of learning sciences along with the dialogue analysis skills. Yet, a full analysis of the whole class takes time, finding no place in a r egular cycle of lesson study, and analysis without hypothesis tends to end in vain. Then, we supported the lesson design step, especially on how each student will learn, which leads to deeper understanding of actual processes, or how each student really learned. These changes imply that how CoREF utilized the theory of constructive interaction changed: we first presented it through student learning processes which were taking place in the KCJ lesson s, then let teachers analyze them, and finally util ized the theory to set up the learning environment for teachers by reconstructing the activities of lesson study, where the teachers solved the shared problem of designing bet ter lessons. The researchers stayed behind the scen es, expecting the fast but constructive interaction to take place among the teachers. Such DBR of lesson study project accompanied co-evolution of research, development a nd practices, in the sense that practically, the to ols and activities expanded to support a handier and quicker lesson study at the sacrifice of full-fledged and neat works of analysis, yet securing opportunities to create h ypotheses prior to the lesson and to test them with  observed processes of student learning; technologically, the  accompanying systems came to support such a handier and quicker lesson study, yet taking greater advantage of collaboration among teachers and accumulation of data.
