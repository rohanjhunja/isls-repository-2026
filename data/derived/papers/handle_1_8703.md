## Introduction

This study focuses on innovative pedagogical practice informed by theories of consequential learning and rightful presence in STEM and enacted by one middle school teacher in an evolving classroom context across three project cycles. Specifically, we explore how the teacher sought to support students’ agentive and productive participation in community-based engineering activity as Covid responses evolved in her district. The research question guiding our analysis is, what can we learn about how to support real work with real consequences in classroom settings by examining one teacher’s instructional practices as she aimed at engaging her students in real, consequential energy engineering across virtual, hybrid, and in-person STEAM classes? Drawing on Jordan et al. (2021), we believe that su pporting real work with real consequences means designing for PDE with consequential and connected learning. Productive disciplinary engagement (PDE) (Engle & Conant, 2002) involves students in making intellectual progress on the real problems of a given discipline. To foster PDE, teachers should give students opportuni ties for problematizing disciplinary concepts and a uthoring solutions to problems they or others raise, hold students accountable to each other and disciplinary norms while providing resources and scaffolding. While PDE is a necessary component of real work with real consequences, it is not sufficient. Jordan et al. (2021) argued that the concept of PDE alone lacks the elements of consequentiality and connectedness needed in “risky design work” (p. 251). Consequentiality means that students are not invited guests into someone else’s world, but are rather rightful participants and problem solvers in a shared world of real problems that they, the students, can have a hand i n shaping and solving (Calabrese Barton & Tan, 2020). Connected learning means  that learning and work is connected with community  beneficiaries, community purposes and a “social infrastructure of care” (Greenberg et al., 2020).

## Method

The context for this design-based research was a 7t h grade STEAM class in a public Title I school loca ted in a metropolitan area of the southwestern U.S. in a Latin@-dominant community. In the summer prior, the teacher, Ms. Catrell, participated in a research experience program focused on agrivoltaics (coupling solar pow er with food crops) and creating equity-oriented curricula that positioned youth with agency in community-base d engineering projects. School closures left Ms. Catr ell with both the disappointment of frustrated plan s and the opportunity to plan responsively with the evolving contingencies of pandemic schooling. In fall 2020, the class met virtually. In spring 2021, learning was in a hybrid setting; the teacher met FTF students in the classroom and used Webex to bring virtual students in simultaneou sly. In fall 2021, a new cohort returned to fully i n-person instruction. Data interpretation relied on analysis  of recorded classroom interaction focused on the t eachers’ instructional practices within and across the virtu al, hybrid, and FTF instruction (~19 hours), as wel l as interpretive analysis of artifacts. We first classified learning activities that met our criteria for “real work”, those that coupled PDE with opportunities to plan and ena ct disciplinary projects that benefit the community  beyond the classroom. We then analyzed the teacher’s pedagogical actions, coming to see her expansive framing of the activities as central to her support of real work across the 3 settings (Engle et al., 2012).

Ms. Catrell supported students’ PDE through expansively framing activities as significant, not as discrete moments of learning but over the long term, in a variety of places, for a variety of people. Can you imagine leaving that legacy?!” This move to help students envision a bright future they helped create seemed especially appropriate to faci ng the challenges of online learning, but could als o be a strategic practice in any setting, strengthening the connections between present and future.

## Discussion

Ms. Catrell’s instructional moves suggest that expansively framing across time, places, and people supports real work with real consequences. However, studies of ex pansive framing to date have largely been concerned  with creating encompassing contexts for transfer of intellectual knowledge and skills. Ms. Catrell’s expansive framing went further. Through moves appropriate to each of the three settings, Ms. Catrell framed place not on ly as locations in which students would apply knowledge but also as contexts that could be transformed by knowledge. She framed people not only as audiences for student  learning but also as partners and beneficiaries of  student action. If we want teachers to cultivate rightful presence and consequential learning in their classes through real work, we should consider how we can support them in framing students as changemakers purposefully acting to benefit the socio-cultural and geographic communities where they live.
