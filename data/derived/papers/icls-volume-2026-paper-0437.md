# Small Scientists, Big Insights: What First Graders Teach Us About Science Assessment Design

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study applies Evidence-Centered Design (ECD) to develop first-grade science assessments aligned with NGSS and early learners’ needs. Observations of five teachers and student responses revealed key design considerations: scaffolds for interpreting graphics, multimodal response options to account for students’ language and literacy variability, and adjustments accounting for development of fine motor skills. Classroom management also shaped task administration. Findings show ECD supports practical, developmentally appropriate assessments that reduce barriers, encourage peer discourse, and authentically capture young learners’ scientific understanding.

The Framework for K–12 Science Education (NRC, 2012) and the Next Generation Science Standards  (NGSS; NGSS Lead States, 2013) call for all students -- including those in the earliest grades --  to have meaningful opportunities to engage with science. Achieving this vision, however, requires more than strong instruction; it demands thoughtful, high-q uality assessments that are responsive to the needs of both students and teachers (Nobes et al., 2023). As Billman et al. (2021) explain, it requires attention to two critical aspects: the three-dimensional nature of NGSS performance expectations (PEs) and the developmental readiness of early learners. Young children are not only beginning to understand scientific ideas, but they are also still learning to use both general and scientific languages to express their thinking (Hobbs et al., 2012). In designing our formative science assessment tasks for early learners, we used the Evidence-Centered Design (ECD; Mislevy & Riconscente, 2011) framework to guide our process. This ensured that we adhered to the 3-dimensionality of NGSS while accounting for the developmental needs of early learners in science. We began by developing the domain model, identifying the specific science concepts and practices we wanted to assess, along with developmental considerations relevant to first graders. From there, we built out the student model to clarify what we hoped to learn about students’ understanding, and the task model to design activities that could elicit that evidence in ways that were age-appropriate, accessible, and engaging. We also used the evidence model to define what kinds of student responses would demonstrate understanding, and the presentation model to ensure tasks were delivered in a way that supported both comprehension and classroom implementation (Mislevy et al., 2003).

With these principles in mind, we used two formats (Llosa et al., 2021): (1)  small-group checks, where teachers interact with groups of two to five students, and (2) individual accountability tasks, where students demonstrate their individual science proficiency after having opportunities to practice with their peers in small groups. In our analysis,  we uncovered nuances pertaining to the different ECD models of the design process-especially where features like graphical representation, expression through language, fine motor skills, and classroom management play a role in productive task administration and student engagement with the task. In this paper, we explore those findings to highlight and inform design implications for early elementary science assessments.

We observed five experienced elementary teachers (11 –30 years) across first-grade and specialist (science/STEAM) settings, including classrooms with language-diverse and developing learners as well as varied instructional schedules. Teachers used a range of formative assessment practices, from observation and discussion to journals and engineering tasks.  We analyzed the observation data by having two coders meet and develop a coding scheme for the qualitative analyses. The two coders independently coded all the classroom observation data and came up with the emerging themes or noticing. Similarly, for student response data, two coders independently coded the data using the pre-developed task rubrics that teachers used to evaluate their students’ responses. The coders then met to discuss and resolve any discrepancies. We then triangulated the themes from two data sources by focusing on design considerations for the assessment tasks. 2740 Supporting the Integration of Graphics. Our assessment tasks include different ways to represent science phenomena, including graphical representations. However, tasks that involved graph interpretation, measurement, or symbolic representation at times exceeded students’ developmental readiness and the teacher had to intervene. For example, we administered a task called Sun Moon Power. This task required students to determine the amount of daylight at different times of the year using a shaded circular timeline, similar to a clock. To support students’ ability to access the task construct, the teacher had to familiarize the students to the task’s graphical representation of time (Figure 1), for instance by orienting them to the time on the outside or pointing out what black and white portions of the graph represent. These teacher adaptations point to the importance of embedding scaffolds into the task model to support representational fluency and conceptual clarity. Some of our assessment tasks include writing, drawing, and verbal explanations to offer multimodal response options and address diverse literacy and language needs of students (Billman et al., 2021, Pearson et al., 2010; Snow, 2008). Although the tasks provided different support, like sentence frames, cloze sentences, and partially filled-in images, teachers made adaptations to how students could respond to the task items. For example, a student struggled to finish the sentence in writing (“The difference between daytime and nighttime is that the Sun…”) and the teacher told him to speak his response while she wrote it down for him. These kinds of adaptations suggest that the student model in ECD should account for developmental variability in literacy and ensure tasks allow students to demonstrate understanding through a range of modalities. Additionally, although several tasks were designed to encourage peer-to-peer discourse, most observed interactions were teacher-directed; students mainly interacted with the teacher, rather than each other. These findings point to the need for more explicit scaffolding to promote productive student-to-student dialogue. Addressing the Development of Fine Motor Skills. The task design elements aligned to the domain model that adhere to focal knowledge, skills, and abilities (FKSAs) must account for the developmental progression of fine motor skills in first-grade students (Bukva & Memisevic, 2024), as these capabilities influence how learners interact with assessment task s. For example, some students had difficulty drawing arrows, matching items, or making distinct circles and squares. These challenges also suggest that task design must be informed by FKSAs across disciplines and that formative science assessments should integrate foundational skill support or simplify symbolic demands to avoid construct-irrelevant barriers. Classroom Management. We found that many of the teachers we observed revised the administration of the small-group tasks, which were designed  to be administered to groups of 3 to 5 students at a time,  to be less autonomous. Instead, teachers conducted synchronous small group tasks, where all of the groups did the same things at the same time to monitor student progress. These observations highlight the need for ECD’s presentation model to consider not just the cognitive load of a task, but the logistical and instructional support necessary for effective administration.

## Implications for assessment design

This study highlights the need for first-grade science tasks that are developmentally appropriate, instructionally feasible, and informative for teaching and learning. Using an ECD framework supports systemic, iterative refinement by identifying breakdowns  and guiding design. Future efforts will include teacher support (task embedded and professional learning), multimodal strategies, and alignment with math and literacy standards. To ensure more accessible assessment tasks, we plan to reduce construct-irrelevant barriers, such as fine motor demands or unfamiliar symbolic representations, through alternative response formats. Additionally, explicit scaffolds to promote peer discourse and familiar contexts ma y enhance student engagement and more authentically capture understanding.
