## Introduction

As an alternative to Cartesiandualism, embodied le arning is based on the belief that the mind is not the only source of knowledge, but rather that learning is taking place within an integrated form of mind and body through sensorimotor activity (Abrahamson & Bakker, 2016). Based on this perspective, embodied learning has be en considered an alternative way of teaching and learning in education, garnering the attention of learning scientists and educational researchers working in STEM education (Lindgren et al., 2016; Núñez et al., 1999). With the development of motion-tracking devices, s tudies in STEM education have been conducted incorporating embodied learning with digital technologies in various contexts. Lindgren et al. (2016) used a laserbased motion tracking system in a mixed-reality environment to prompt understanding of physics concepts, and Fischer et al. (2016) used digital dance mats arran ged in a 3x3 layout to promote mental number-line representation. Focused on learning geometry, Smith  et al. (2014) used Kinect for body-based angle tas ks to enhance developing ideas about angles. With  motion-tracking devices, these research projects have pro vided significant implications for technology-enhanced embodied interventions; however, it was difficult to bring these technologies into classroom settings.

To bring embodied interventions into K-12 classroo m settings, this research used machine-learningenhanced computer vision techniques in a geometry m ath game. With the help of machine learning techniq ues, body movements of students can be precisely tracked  by webcam of a laptop or mobile device, without an y external devices. Considering an important premise that mathematical understanding should be construct ed by connections between mathematical concepts (Hiebert & Carpenter, 1992), I chose geometric concepts to b e learned together, based on the mathematics standard s in Common Core State Standards. To connect geomet ric concepts and embodied learning experiences, three hand gestures were designed and implemented in the geometry math game. This poster will lead to productive discussions regarding embodied interventions in STEM education and will provide meaningful ideas on how technologies could impact the way of teaching and learning. A machine-learning-enhanced computer vision geometry math game This research used machine-learning-enhanced comput er vision techniques with a webcam, instead of usin g external devices. To detect body movements and hand gestures in real-time, MediaPipe was used. A game engine, Unity3D, was used to display webcam video, game con tents, and detected body segments on the device scr een. Game contents were gamified with giving rewards systems to increase students’ motivation. A math game focused on learning geometric concepts on two-dimensional shapes. With six twodimensional shapes ranging from triangles to octago ns, students are requested to identify vertices, si des, and angles on the two-dimensional shapes using hand ges tures. Various game tasks guide students to embodie d learning experiences, and students are requested to engage in certain types of body movements to achieve the goal of the game task. This game design was based on an assumption that students can enhance their understa nding when they are engaged in mathematically relevant bo dy movements. Three different hand gestures involve outlining, clapping, and making angles. These hand gestures were implemented in a game us ing the machine learning enhanced computer vision technique (Table 1).

Outlining is a hand gesture students need to do when they learn about sides of two-dimensional shapes. It refers to moving an index finger from the start to the end of a side on shapes. This hand gesture requires students to infer what sides look like and how many sides are on the shapes. For instance, in a game task for learning about triangles, students need to identify what sides are  and move their index finger along the sides of a triangle. Clapping is a hand gesture used when students learn about v ertices on two-dimensional shapes. As its name represents, clapping refers to two hands clapping. This gesture was implemented to provide experiences of identifying the location of vertices and counting their number. Making angles is two hand gestures that students ICLS2022 Proceedings 1926 © ISLS need to use for game tasks about angles on two-dimensional shapes. It refers to making a certain angle with two hands, holding the wrists or middle fingers connected, to make an A or V-like shape. These hand gestures request students to think about the concept of angles and can promote the mathematical meaning-making regarding angles. For instance, in a game task for learning angles on a triangle, students need to identify the center of angle, a vertex, and then represent the size of an angle using two hands. ## Discussion and future work

With the continuous improvement of machine learning techniques and their pedagogical potentials, educational researchers now have more opportunities to facilitate embodied learning in classrooms. In this poster, a machinelearning-enhanced computer vision game was introduced as an effective way of enhancing embodied learning for geometry in classrooms. For future work, this project will investigate the impact of embodied learning on students’ geometry understanding, self-efficacy, and engagement, in classroom settings.
