# Co-Regulation and Multimodal Synchrony in Pair Programming

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Gaze, emotional, and physiological synchronies are predictive of collaborative performance and quality. However, these synchronies are studied mostly in unimodal settings. We present a pair programming study to understand the relationship between multimodal synchrony and co-regulation processes. We collected eye-tracking, heart rate variability, electrodermal activity, facial expressions, dialogues from 40 pairs while they were collaboratively debugging a given program. We then coded their dialogues to examine co-regulation-based differences between the high and low performing pairs. Next, we computed gaze, facial expressions, stress and engagement based synchronies. We then examined the relationships between different synchronies and co-regulation dialogues. Our results show that argumentation and negotiation are negatively related to performance while cooperation, monitoring, goal-setting and co-regulation have a positive relationship with performance. We also find that argumentation and negotiation have lower joint attention and lower joint mental effort with high average mental effort and lower joint stress with high average stress.

## Introduction and background

With the rapid advancement and widespread adoption of generative artificial intelligence, the importan ce of self-regulated learning (SRL) has become more pronounced  (Järvelä et al., 2023; Molenaar, 2022). Learners a re now required to navigate complex and dynamic inform ation landscapes while effectively utilizing AI too ls to support their learning processes. SRL is widely rec ognized as a vital approach for fostering autonomy, enhancing metacognitive abilities, and strengthenin g intrinsic motivation, making it indispensable in this evolving context (Hadwin et al., 2018). Through SRL, learners actively plan, monitor, and evaluate the ir own learning processes, enabling them to achieve person al goals and adapt to diverse challenges in learnin g (Winne & Hadwin, 1998). However, in collaborative learning  contexts that involve students working in pairs or  small groups, individual self-regulation alone may not suffice to address the com plex demands of joint tasks. To this end, the concept of co-regulation of learning (CoRL) has gained prominence. CoRL refers to the dynamic, reciprocal interactions in which individuals regula te their learning collaboratively, often supported by peers, instructors, or external tools (Hadwin et al., 2018). It integrates shared goal-setting, mutual scaffolding, and adaptive feedback mechanisms, creating a socially mediated framework for learning. Despite its significance, understanding and support ing SRL and CoRL have been challenging due to the inherently non-observable nature of the emotional and cognitive pr ocesses involved (Järvelä et al., 2019). Supporting CoRL is particularly complex, as it requ ires addressing the interplay between individual SR L processes and the evolving dynamics of interaction among learners throughout a learning session. While traditional methods offer valuable insights into learners' initial and final states, as well as their perceptions of the learning experience for informing CoRL, these approaches fall short in capturing the dynamic, moment-tomoment processes that characterize CoRL (Järvelä et  al., 2023). Fortunately, advancements in multimoda l learning analytics (MMLA) have introduced innovativ e data sources and methodologies, offering promisin g solutions to address the challenges of understanding and supporting the dynamic processes of CoRL. MMLA has been used within the collaborative setting s for the past few years to understand and predict collaborative success (Spikol et al, 2017a, Olsen e t al, 2020), regulation (Noorozi,et al, 2019) and r eflection (Yan et al, 2024a,b), collaborative processes (Spik ol et al, 2017b; Xu et al, 2023), discussion during collaboration (Riquelme et al, 2019; Spikol et al, 2016; Ezen-Can 2015), assessment (Moon et al, 2024) and classroom orchestration (Prieto et al, 2016; Rodriguez-Triana et al, 2018). Spikol et al (2017a) used audio, video and user generated data to predict success in colla borative physical computing. Similarly, Olsen et al  (2020) used gaze, audio and dialogues to predict success i n collaborative tutoring systems. Spikol et al (201 7b) used facial and gestural data to explain the learning pr ocesses during collaborative problem solving relate d to physical computing.  Yan et al (2024a) used positio n and audio data to explain how healthcare students  reflect on their understanding of teachers’ feedback. Yan e t al (2024b) operationalized the linguistic, contex tual, and bodily elements of each theoretical perspective, fo cusing on analyzing the verbal communication, spati al behavior, and physiological responses. Xu et al (20 23) used pair programming as an example setting and explained the collaborative patterns based on the p airs’ discourse, behaviours and emotions. Riquelme et al (2019), used speech writing, gestures and sight dat a to analyze and visualize student discussion group s working in a collaborative way to accomplish a task.

Using the recent work within collaborative learning  and MMLA, we can observe three different types of synchrony. The first is the physiological synchr ony, characterized by the interdependent physiologi cal processes of multiple individuals, has been linked to students’ learning success and social regulation  (Haataja et al., 2018; Liu et al., 2021). The second is the emo tional synchrony, that has been shown to predict bo th subjective and objective performances within collaborative settings. Finally the gaze synchrony, that has been studied in the CSCL settings using dual eye-tracking almost for the past two decades (starting from Richardson et al. 2005; Sharma et al, 2013; Jermann & Nuessli, 2012), in various collaborative learning (Sharma et al,

2021) and problem solving tasks (Nuessli et al., 2009). The recent MMLA focus on understanding the

synchrony of physiological, emotional and gaze synchrony motivat ed this work to combine gaze and physiological synchrony in one context, that, is pa ir programming. Our main aim is to understand the i nterplay of discourse with the two other types, that is, physiological and gaze synchrony. Pair programming is a complex phenomenon that invol ves multimodal interaction and coordination between individual student, student group, learning  environment, and the knowledge artefact (Stahl & Hakkarainen, 2021). Specifically, the multimodality  can be reflected through student pair’s communicat ion (Barron, 2000; Ouyang & Xu, 2022), behavior (Stahl,  2017), emotion (Kwon et al., 2014), interaction (Z emel & Koschmann, 2013), etc. Furthermore, the multimodali ty emerges during the collaboration with complex, multilevel, multilayered characteristics, which may  influence the quality and success of collaborative  learning (Byrne & Callaghan, 2014; Hilpert & Marchand, 2018). One of the key aspects of pair programming is debugging. In the past it has been shown that pair debugging increases the debugging performance as co mpared to solo debugging (Dawande et al, 2008). In CSCL, pair debugging also brings i nteresting aspects of collaboration such as argumentation, explanation, n egotiations and co-regulation. In this study, we attempt at understanding how the co-regulation in collaborative debugging can explain t he performance and how multimodal synchrony can explain the co-regulatory patterns. Specifically, we address the f ollowing research questions (RQs): RQ1: what are the co-regulation based differences between the high and the low performing dyads? RQ2: How is the coregulation explained using multimodal data and multimodal synchrony? Pair programming, where learners often engage in co llaborative problem-solving and iterative development processes, represents an ideal context for exploring co-regulation. These tasks frequently require students to share their strategies, debug collabora tively, and adapt to evolving project requirements.  This collaborative approach inherently requires paired l earners to regulate their collective efforts, ensur ing alignment in their understanding, problem-solving strategies, and task goals. CoRL supports t his process by facilitating open communication, mutual scaffolding, and the man agement of cognitive and emotional challenges that may arise during programming. For example, learners can  co-regulate their workflow by negotiating coding decisions, providing each other with constructive f eedback, and adapting their approaches based on tas k progress or challenges encountered. By promoting sh ared monitoring and adjustment of learning processe s, CoRL competence in pair programming not only enhanc es coding efficiency but also develops critical teamwork and communication skills, which are essent ial for success in both educational and professiona l programming contexts.

## Methodology

This study was carried out at an European Universit y in the spring of 2023. The study consists of debugg ing as the main task, continuous monitoring of students' g aze, physiological and video data, and assessing pe rformance as captured by students' progress with the task.

## Participants and procedure

There were 80 university students who participated in the debugging study (32 females). The average ag e was 23.6 years (SD = 2.4 years), and all participants h ad at least two years of Python experience. Upon th eir arrival in the laboratory, the participants signed a consen t form, and the eye-tracker was calibrated for each individual participant using a 9-point calibration. Then the participants were expla ined their task. The simple instruction to the participants was to find and fix six bugs in th e given Python code (\ref{fig:AOIs}, right). The Python code was developed using pygame library to create Tetris. The participants were shown how the clean (withou t bugs) version of the program was supposed to work. They w ere also told that there were no syntactical errors  in the experimental code (with bugs) and the bugs were rel ated to the physics of the game (e.g., the collisio ns were not working properly, the arrow keys would not do their  intended tasks, the score was not incrementing pro perly and at the correct event). The total duration of th e session was 45 minutes (30 minutes for debugging and 15 minutes for signing, calibration, and explanations), after which the participants were told to stop de bugging (to remove the time-on-task effect) and were given a gift card equivalent to USD 35. We saved all the programs once the sessions were over and counted how many bu gs were resolved. We then applied a median split on  the number of bugs resolved. This was our dependent variable, i.e., debugging performance.

## Data collection

For the present study, we developed a code editor t hat supports concurrent collaboration, integrated w ith extensive logging, gathers data from multiple data streams (i.e., eye tracking, webcam, audio and wristband) and synchronized all gathered data from editor and user  sensors. For the collaborative code editor (Figure  1, left panel), we extended the firepad code editor to enab le the logging of the user events. After developing  a functioning collaborative editor, the last step was  to find a way for users to compile and run their c ode. Initially, this was done using the library Pyodide (The Pyodid e development team, 2021) which is a Python distrib ution for the browser. In this experiment, we collected the following data from the pair programmers. Eye-tracking: To record participants’  gaze we used two Tobii X3-120 eye-tracking device at a 120 Hz sampling rate and using a 5-point calibration. The device is non-invasive and mounted at the bottom of two computer screens. Both screen resolutions were 1920x1080 and the participants were 50-70 cm away from their respective screens.

Heart rate Variability (HRV) and Electrodermal Acti vity (EDA): To record HRV and EDA data we use the Empatica E4 wristband. Participants wore the wristb and on their  non-dominant/non-playing hand. We recorded: HRV at 1 Hz and electrodermal activity (EDA) at 64 Hz. Facial Videos: To capture facial expressions from the participants, we used two LogiTech web cameras, pointed straight at the participants from their respective screens, capturing video at 30 frames-per-second (FPS). The web camera focus zoomed at 150% onto the faces of t he participants. During the tasks, the participants exhibited minimal body and gesture interaction; hen ce, the video recordings hold high-quality data from students' facial expressions. The video resolution from both cameras was 1920x1080. Figure 1 The Collaborative Code Editor and the Pair Programming Setup Measurements Joint Visual Attention (JVA):  is the amount of time the peers spend looking at the same set of objects in a given time window. Using each token in the program, we fi rst created a proportionality vector. This vector c ontains the total time spent on each token in a given time window of 10 seconds (Sharma et al, 2021). Once we have this proportionality vector for both the participan ts in a dyad, we simply compute the cosine similarity of the two vector. A value of 1 would indicate perfect synchrony, and a value of 0 will indicate no synchrony at all. Average Mental Effort (AME) and Joint Mental Effort  (JME): is the amount of time the peers spent in exerting the similar amount of cognitive load on the task.  To compute JME, we first calculate the cognitive lo ad as the Index of Pupilary activity (Duchowski et al, 2020).  Then we compute the percentiles of the cognitive l oad (this is simply a minmax normalization with a multiplicat ion with 100). We compute individual cognitive loads for a period of 10 seconds. Then we compute the cross rec urrence of the two cognitive load time series. For marking the similar cognitive load on the cross-recurrence matrix, we use a value of +/ - 5 percentiles. That is, if Alice’s cognitive load is within the 5-percentile range of Bob’s cognitive load, we consider them similar. Apart from the JME, we also compute the average mental effort for the dyad. The main idea is that JME will tell us ho w similar the cognitive load of the dyad and AME is will tell us what the level of cognitive load is. Average Stress (AStr) and Joint Stress (JStr):  is the amount of time peers spent being in similar physiological stress. Once, we calculate stress from heart rate variability (Lim et al, 2018)  for individual peers. We use the exact cross recurrence method to compute the JStr a s in JME. Apart from the JStr, we also compute the average stress (AStr) for the dyad.

Average Engagement (AEng) and Joint Engagement (JEn g): is the amount of time peers spent being at a similar engaged level. Once, we calculate the engagement fr om electrodermal activity (Hernandez et al, 2014)  for individual peers. We use the exact cross recurrence  method to compute the JEng as in JME. ## Discussion and conclusions

From the results, we can observe clear differences between the high and low performing pairs consideri ng the dialogue codes  (see figure2). One of the counter-intuitive findings is that argumentation and negoti ation are counter-productive for the dyads. There is a considerable e vidence from discourse analysis in collaborative learning research that both argumentation and negot iation are beneficial for collaborative outcomes (Sampson & Clark, 2011; Jeong 2006; Crook 2022; Shin et al, 20 20). However, most of the studies looking at the argumentation and negotiation had either argumentation prompts or there was an collaborative script sc affolding the peers’ discussion. In this contribution, we hav e neither of the two factors included. Moreover, we  argue that over-argumentation and negotiation might be detrimental for the collaborative performance in pair programming. Pair programming relies on maintaining  a productive flow between the two programmers (Williams & Upchurch, 2001). If the pair spends too  much time arguing or negotiating over decisions, i t interrupts this flow, reducing productivity and the  effectiveness of the collaboration. This is also e vident by lower JVA, lower joint and average mental effort, l ower joint engagement, lower joint stress and highe r average stress. Similarly, negotiation about irrelevant or less critical aspects of the task candistract from  the primary goals of pair programming —problem-solving and code quality. In pair programming, quic k decision-making is essential to maintain momentum (Williams & Kessler. 2003). Lengthy negotiations can slow the pair’s pr ogress. During negotiations we also observe, lower JVA, lower joint and average mental effort, lower joint engagement, lower joint stress and higher average stress. These  joint multimodal behavioural patterns are indicati ve of low synchrony among the participants which can be detri mental for the collaborative performance (Haataja e t al., 2018; Liu et al., 2021; Richardson et al. 2005; Sha rma et al, 2013; Jermann & Nuessli, 2012; Sharma et  al, 2021 & 2022).

On the other hand, we observe that the high perform ing pairs have higher proportion of goal-setting, co-regulation, cooperation and metacognitive dialogues. These diff erences are also highlighted in the relevant literature related to collaborative learning (Richardson & Dale, 2005; Jermann & Nuessli, 2012), Socially shared regulation (Jarvela et al, 2019)  and co-regulation (Jarvela et al, 2021). For example, goal-setting ensures both programmers have a high level of mutual understanding, reducing  misunderstandings  (Jarvela et al, 2004) because clear goals minimize conflicts over priorities or me thods, as both programmers understand what they are working toward. Moreoer, co-regulation and metacognitive reflectionalso have a positive impact on collaborative performance (Jarvela et al, 2016; Haataja et al, 2022). These four dialogue categories (i.e.,  goalsetting, co-regulation, cooperation, and metacognition) are mos tly accompanied by high levels of synchrony (e.g., high joint visual attention, high joint mental ef fort, high joint stress and engagement). Moreover, the high joint mental effort is with high average mental eff ort and high joint stress is with low average stres s. These patterns also show that the average levels of menta l effort and stress are in line with high performan ce as shown by related research (Sharma et al, 2021; Lim et al, 2018). We also observe a clear difference between the mult imodal (MMD) joint profiles for all the dialogue codes (except Explanation). With these joint MMD profiles, we can clearly distinguish the dialogue codes (see figure 3). For example, goal-setting is characterized by high joint mental effor t, high joint stress with low average stress and high joint engagement; regulatio n is characterized by high JVA, high joint mental e ffort with high average mental effort, high joint stress and h igh joint engagement; cooperation has MMD profile o f high JVA, high joint mental effort with high average mental effort  and low joint stress; and metacognitive dialogues have the joint profile of high joint mental effort with high average mental effort and high joint enga gement. There are two exceptions, first, we could not find a pattern for explanation; and second, we could not distinguish between argumentation and negotiation. Both argumen tation and negotiation are accompanied by low JVA, low joint mental effort with low average mental effort,  low joint stress with high average stress, and low  joint engagement. One plausible explanation can be that a rgumentation and negotiation most often occur together and therefore the joint MMD profiles  are similar for the two dialogue codes. However, mo re analysis and experimentation can explain the difference between argumentation and negotiation in collaborative learning settings.

This study employed MMLA using data from eye-tracking, dialogues, and physiological signals to investigate CoRL in pair programming contexts. Spec ifically, we aimed to understand how CoRL during collaborative debugging relates to performance outc omes and how multimodal synchrony can reveal patter ns of CoRL. By addressing these objectives, the study con tributes to both CoRL research and the evolving fie ld of MMLA.While prior research has demonstrated the pote ntial of MMD to provide insights into regulatory processes (Azevedo & Gašević, 2019), it also highlights that not all data are equally informative (Järvelä et al., 2021). It has been suggested that different data mo dalities may better capture specific aspects of CoR L. Although opinion-based literature has acknowledged that varying moda lities may reflect different dimensions of CoRL (e.g., Järvelä et al., 2021), there remains a lack of empirical evidence systematically linking M MD to specific SRL constructs and processes. Our study ad dresses this gap in the literature through insights into the relevance of different multimodal synchronies for c apturing different aspects of CoRL in pair programm ing contexts.Previous research has highlighted the role of physi ological synchronies as indicators of regulatory processes (Dindar et al., 2022) and identified join t attention (Whitehead et al., 2024) as a crucial f actor in facilitating CoRL. Building on this, our study prov ides insights into relationship between CoRL activi ties and constructs such as physiological synchronies and jo int gaze attention. Specifically, our findings reve al that negotiation activities exhibit a significant negati ve correlation with Joint Visual Attention (JVA), J oint Mental Effort (JME), and Average Mental Effort (AME). In c ontrast, cooperation dialogues demonstrate a signif icant positive correlation with these constructs, suggest ing that the nature of interaction-whether collaborative or contentious-candistinctly influence shared cognitive and emotional states setting stages for CoRL.

Our findings inform the design of ACLS (adaptive co llaborative learning support). These systems can support s hared regulation of learning to improve  the co-regulation of learning behaviour. For example, we simply mirror the current joint MMD profiles to the  peers and this can create a sense of awareness amo ng them. Second, we can use the MMD profiles to infer the di alogue code to scaffold the discourse and provide p roactive and/or reactive feedback to the programmers about t heir current processes. Finally, we can also foreca st the MMD profiles (Sharma et al, 2022) and provide preventive support by alerting the peers about their forthcoming problems. These systems can improve the quality and outcome of collaborative learning by e nsuring that the cognitive workload is evenly distributed between th e driver (writing the code) and the navigator (revi ewing and strategizing). Moreover, w ith both programmers getting support for regulating the ir thoughts and problem solving process, they can combine perspectives to solve com plex problems more effectively.  However, the evaluation of such systems remains a future work for us.
