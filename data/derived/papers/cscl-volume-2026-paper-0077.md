# Epistemic Authority and Interface: A Typological Framework of AI’s Roles in Knowledge Building

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Knowledge Building distinguishes itself from other educational approaches by engaging students directly in the means by which knowledge advances in the world. As artificial intelligence (AI) advances, fundamental questions arise about its implications for KB. This study extended a workshop at the 2024 International Society of the Learning Sciences (ISLS) annual meeting into a four-month dialogue on Knowledge Forum. Using a dialogical knowledge-making framework, 23 contributors from diverse disciplinary backgrounds and career stages collectively examined theoretical, pedagogical, design, ethical implications at the intersection of AI and KB. Diverse AI roles were discussed and organized into a typological framework along interface type and epistemic authority levels, producing six configurations: directive interaction, collaborative interaction, exploratory interaction, diagnostic environment, reflective environment, and experimental environment. Given the configurations of AI, we propose a design principle of making AI's epistemic position visible and enabling learner-driven navigation across AI roles.

The recent advances of generative artificial intelligence (genAI) have renewed important epistemological questions around knowledge building for societies: What is knowledge and how is knowledge constructed, by individuals and collectively, in the context of AI models capable of human-like linguistic productions (Jones & Bergen, 2024). Despite a growing body of research examining AI’s role in education and research (Celik et al., 2022; Zheng et al., 2024), significant gaps remain in our understanding of how it might reshape learning, education, and Knowledge Building/Knowledge Creation.

Knowledge Building (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2006) offers a particularly valuable lens for the discussion of AI and education, as it has historically championed student epistemic agency that augment rather thandiminish human intellectual capacity and agency. Knowledge Building (KB) positions students as active epistemic agents who search, review, critique, and build on authoritative knowledge sources to advance the state of their community 484 knowledge rather than simply consume and regurgitate for standardized testing. However, our understanding of how AI functions within KB contexts remains nascent and evolving. While AI technologies advance rapidly, frameworks for conceptualizing AI's role in KB are still emerging. Given the complexity and rapid evolution of this landscape, there is a pressing need to bring together a plurality of perspectives and experiences to collectively examine how AI intersects with KB to build frameworks that capture and meaningfully include AI in KB. Using a dialogical knowledge-making framework inspired by postdigital dialogue methodology (Jandrić et al., 2019; Markauskaite et al., 2022), the present study brought 23 scholars together  across disciplines who share common interests in KB. The goal of this endeavor is to initiate a process of identifying theoretical, pedagogical, design, and ethical implications of AI in KB communities and to start mapping out the evolving relationship between AI and KB. This short paper reports on one research question that was explored during the collaborative dialogue: What roles can AI play for Knowledge Building?

## Method

The study’s design is informed by and built upon Markauskaite et al.’s (2022) cyclical reflection approach with post digital dialogue and experimental multi-authored methodology. Given the novelty of technology and a lack of studies addressing the theory o f KB and AI, we chose a critical communicative methodology (Gomez et al., 2018), to include stakeholders and actors from different backgrounds in a continuous, dialogic, constructive approach, allowing for nuanced, co-constructed perspectives that traditional methods (such as surveys and interviews) cannot provide.

Contributors This collaborative dialogue involved 23 contributors from 13 distinct disciplines, with many identifying across multiple fields (n>23 as participants could select multiple disciplines). The most represented discipline was Learning Sciences (n=15), followed by Computer-Supported Collaborative Learning (n=7), and Educational Technology (n=4). Other disciplines included Computer Science (n=2), and single representations in Psychology, Computational Biology, Language Education, Human-Computer Interaction, Learning Analytics, Developmental and Educational Psychology, and Vocational and Health Sciences Education.

## Research context and procedure

The dialogic space for participants in this study emerged during a workshop titled “ Knowledge building and generative AI to advance community knowledge”, at the International Society of the Learning Sciences (ISLS) in Buffalo, US, June 2024 (Cao et al., 2024). This study reports on the dialogue continued from the workshop, a process that lasted from July to October 2024 and culminated in this collective paper.  The collective dialogue took place through written exchanges in Knowledge Forum (KF), which allows participants to create notes on an infinite canvas for their ideas, on which other participants can then build on. The collaborative dialogue was organized into three phases. Phase 1 Open Discussion:  Participants were invited to contribute to a shared space on Knowledge Forum in response to prompting questions after the workshop. After posting initial responses, participants engaged with at least three other contributors’ KF notes. Phase 2 Clustering of Notes: In Knowledge Forum, the first and second author manually organized contributors’ notes into clusters based on the convergence and divergence of ideas in ways to maximize the potential for further idea improvement and rise-above (Scardamalia, 2002). Participants were then encouraged to review and build on their “near-neighbors” and “farneighbors” notes from different clusters. Phase 3 Report Draft: The first and second author synthesized the contributions from the two phases and the workshop to an initial report. The draft was shared with all the contributors for both member checking and refinement of ideas. Data sources and analysis Data of this study comes from the Knowledge Forum’s notes (n=91) contributed by the 23 authors. The first and second authors engaged in Braun and Clarke’s (2019)  reflexive thematic coding in ATLAS.ti, a qualitative research method for identifying, analyzing, and reporting patterns within data after the three phases of collective dialogue. Using a reflexive inductive thematic analysis approach, researchers are positioned as an instrument central to generating rich, situated insights. Rather than treating themes and insights as pre-existing truths to be “discovered,” they were iteratively constructed and refined through cycles of coding and dialogue with the data. This analytical approach is in alignment with the dialogic nature of knowledge making and the theoretical foundation of postdigital dialogue (Jandrić et al., 2019). 485 In this paper, voices from the collaborative KF dialogue are attributed by contributor surname followed by “KF” (e.g., Metni, KF), while external scholarly sources follow standard APA citation format. Through the collaborative dialogue, a variety of AI roles emerged, which we organized into six configurations along two dimensions: 1) the interface between humans and AI (either conversational or embedded), and 2) the epistemic authority assumed by or attributed to AI. In conversational interfaces, humans explicitly communicate with AI through conversation (e.g., chatbots, virtual assistants). When AI is embedded in the environment, it functions as part of the KB environment itself. The epistemic authority dimension refers to the degree to which AI is positioned or assumes epistemic authority. According to Jäger (2025, p. 1), epistemic authority is the authority we ascribe to people “in virtue of their favorable relation to epistemic goods such as true belief, rational credence, knowledge, or understanding”. It involves giving “special epistemic weight” to someone’s views. Though large language models (LLMs) do not possess true expertise or knowledge in the epistemological sense that they don’t have genuine understanding, beliefs, or direct access to reality (Bender & Koller, 2020; Mancoridis et al., 2025; Mitchell & Krakauer, 2023), AI systems are frequently anthropomorphized and positioned with varying degrees of epistemic authority in educational contexts (Jose et al., 2025). In this typology, we organized AI’s epistemic authority into high, shared/equal, and low. The AI’s outputs are given great epistemic weight, withz its assertions treated as authoritative to an extent. This configuration reflects what Tan  (2024) describes as “learning from AI”. In other words, AI plays the role of a more knowledgeable other in this configuration, such as tutors, experts, and guide.

Collaborative interaction (conversational, shared epistemic authority) In this configuration, AI engages learners via conversation as a co-constructive partner with shared epistemic authority, supporting psychological safety and joint knowledge construction, including roles as companion, partner, consultant, facilitator, or broker with shared epistemic authority with learners. This configuration aligns with Tan’s (2024) notion of “ learning with AI ”. Such positioning of AI as companion, partner, consultant, or broker, with shared epistemic authority differs from the directive interaction configuration, where AI assumes more authority.

Exploratory interaction (conversational, low epistemic authority) In this configuration, AI engages learners via conversation and introduces alternative voices and perspectives without assuming epistemic authority by playing the roles of group members (Bereiter & Scardamalia, KF) or other characters/persona (Huang, Chen, KF).

Diagnostic environment (embedded, high epistemic authority) In this configuration, AI are integrated directly into the KB environment (e.g. authoring functionality in software like Knowledge Forum) and are positioned with more epistemic authority to formatively assess, diagnose, and provide feedback on both individual contributions and community knowledge advancement. Reflective environment (embedded, shared epistemic authority) This configuration represents AI systems that are embedded into the KB environment with shared epistemic authority between humans and AI. Rather than providing assessments predefined by certain criteria, these systems create interactive spaces that require human interpretation and decision-making. The AI-enhanced interactive and concurrent analytics systems are designed to move beyond Scardamalia and Bereiter’s (KF) critique of AI offering “summarization [that] provides a simple accounting of topics and issues in the discourse... tending to close Experimental environment (embedded, low epistemic authority) This configuration transcends the conventional notions of “assistance” or “partnership” to position AI as part of the epistemic infrastructure proposed by Chen (2025) — not as an authority or companion, but as a responsive space that augments human creative and conceptual work. Instead of asking what AI cando for KB, this approach asks how AI can expand what students cando with ideas. Building on the sandbox metaphor  of knowledge creation, As Tsuji (KF) explains, “AI could be idea-and text-building space for personal (and perhaps also shared) reflection and experiment through remixing, prototyping, testing, and refining ideas and texts to support ideational writing in Knowledge Building.” The collaborative dialogue documented in this short paper reveals that the intersection of Knowledge Building and generative AI is not merely a matter of technological integration but represents a fundamental reconceptualization of what it means to engage in knowledge building in the AI age.  One of the main contributions of this collaborative dialogue highlighted in this short paper is the typological framework that maps AI role by interface type (conversational vs. embedded) and epistemic authority levels  (high, shared, low), creating various configurations. We thus propose a design principle that suggests that AI-powered KB environments should allow users to fluidly move between these roles based on pedagogical needs and objectives. Effective design should support learners in dynamically coordinating different AI roles for Knowledge Building. Rather than fixing AI in predetermined roles, effective design provides learners with agency to dynamically position AI along the typological framework as their knowledge work requires, and their capacity of productive human-AI collaboration develops. We acknowledge the limitations of our work, for instance the potential bias induced by the authors conducting the thematic analysis of data the production of which they were actively involved in. The  derived insights further originate from a pool of participants with the majority being researchers with  a learning science other backgrounds, teachers, administrators, policy-makers, and learners, and by analyzing cross-cultural contexts.
