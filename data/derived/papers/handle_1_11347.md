# A Socio-Cultural Approach to Personalization in Education: Case Studies in Empowering Learners as Knowers with AI

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In this conceptual design paper, we outline a novel socio-cultural approach to personalization that values diverse ways of knowing, learner agency, and co-construction of learning outcomes as an alternative to system-driven adaptation. We present two illustrative cases of AI agents developed to probe the viability of this approach. The TRANSLATE Buddy facilitates collaborative translation activities in multilingual classrooms aimed at empowering students as linguistic knowers and fostering metalinguistic engagement. The Scholar’s Table fosters a dialogue between post-secondary students and academic voices to promote active meaning-making of disciplinary texts. Both agents showed notable strength in supporting emergent learning directions, but variability in honoring multiplicity / relationality of meaning and positioning student-as-knower. Implications and future work are discussed.

(Re)Positioning personalization in education Efforts to personalize learning for individual stud ents have a long history of development in educatio n, with evidence of improvements in students’ learning outcomes over a one-size-fits-all model dating back over a century (Thorndike, 1911). Earlier approaches focused on teachers’ tailoring of content, pace, and feedback for learners, while recent work has focused on technology-driven approaches for personalizing learning at scale (Walkington & Bernacki, 2020). Technology-driven personalization is either taken to subsume adaptivity (adjusting instruction to changes in students’ knowledge over time) or complement it through responsiveness to each student’s interests, affect, motivation, needs and goals (Shemshack & Sp ector, 2020). Reg ardless, such conceptualizations center both knowledge about the student and decision-making as to how learning should proceed in the technology. Thus, while traditional personalization  approaches embody some aspects of learner-centered pedagogy, such as responsiveness to students’ “knowledge, skills, attitudes, and beliefs ” (Bransford et al., 2000, p 133), adaptation schemes based on a uniform conceptualization of mastery can limit learner agency (McCombs & Whisler, 1997), overlook the fluid ways students think and engage the world (Li & Wise, 2025) and limit opportunities for critical, self-directed learning which help diverse students thrive (Watters, 2021). In this conceptual design paper, we address these shortcomi ngs by proposing a socio-cultural approach to personalization that aims to embrace learners’ d iverse ways of knowing and empower their agentic me aningmaking. We present two case studies to probe the ap proach’s viability, while also introducing an alternat ive epistemological framework for learner-AI interaction that moves beyond the AI-as-expert paradigm. A socio-cultural approach to personalization and epistemological shifts From a socio-cultural perspective, knowledge is considered not to be fixed or universa l, but co-constructed and context-dependent (Vygotsky, 1978; Rogoff, 2003). This view casts the learner not as a passive recipi ent but as an active knower, engaged in self-directed, culturally mediated meaning-making that is deeply informed by their unique experiences and identity, and rich with emergent and multiple meanings (Cole, 1996; Nasir & Hand, 2006). Traditionally, personalization has been linked to an individualistic orientation (Watters, 2021), which may appear misaligned with this culturally embedded, sociohistorical stance on learning and  knowing. However, recent advances in generative AI, when mobilized carefully, offer promising opportunities to bridge this divi de. AIdriven conversational agents powered by large language models have the potential to engage learners in dialogic sensemaking that surfaces student interpretations based on their existing knowledge, actively responds to student reasoning, and bring s varied perspective s to the conversation  (Mollick & Mollick, 2023). While traditional personalization supported these activities to some extent,  recognizing and building on the multiple, relationa l ways that students engage in meaning making is difficult within a scripted, system-driven paradigm. Generative AI dramatically lowers the barriers to engag ing with the varied and potentially unexpected, pathways of  learner sensemaking, thus enabling both emergent directions of learning process and co-construction of outcomes. Such use of Generative AI is distinct from the common AI-as-expert paradigm on multiple epistemological dimensions. Multiplicity of Meaning: Recognizing that knowledge is not singular or static (Greeno, Collins & Resnick, 1996), a socio-cultural approach to personalization values the div erse interpretations and understandings of phenomena that can exist. Thus, rather than helping  a learner to arrive at a single, predefined unders tanding, this stance emphasizes navigating and embracing multiple, often culturally grounded, meanings (Warren et al., 2020). Relationality of Meaning:  From a socio-cultural perspective, knowledge is inherently relat ional, with meaning influenced by a learner's identity, history, and co ntext (Gutiérrez & Rogoff, 2003). Personalization thus aims to support learners in constructing knowledge in ways that are deeply connected to their own experiences and cultural background, recognizing the importance of learners' relationshi p to what is learned as a core source of meaning (González et al., 2005). Student as Knower: Following from the points above, learners are seen as active agents in the construction of knowledge whose persp ectives are integral to the learning process (Wetsc h et al., 2009). Personalization is thus not something done for or to them, but a process that recognizes their authority and expertise within their learning journey (Miller et al., 2018). Emergent Destination of Learning:  A socio-cultural approach to personalization intentionally allows the goals and direction of learning to evolve based on the learner's engagement and discoveries (Nasir & Hand, 2006). Th is aligns with the core of culturally sustaining pe dagogy, which emphasizes honoring students' unique cultural resources and insights, viewing their evolving contributions as essential to shaping a responsive and meaningful learning journey (Paris & Alim, 2014). In the remainder of this paper, we present two design cases of AI agents developed using this framing in order to probe the viability of the approach. The f irst addresses the learning of English by emergent bilingual middle schoolers while the second focuses on suppor ting post-secondary students in engaging with disciplinary texts. Each offers a compelling alternative to rele vant AI-as-expert tools in the domain: for case 1, language learning AI-tutors such as found in Duolingo, and for case 2, paper summarization tools such as NotebookLM. Case 1: The TRANSLATE Buddy TRANSLATE is a curriculum designed for teaching middle-grade English reading comprehension in multilingual classrooms, using translingual pedagogies that strategically use multilingual students’ full linguistic repertoire— including knowledge of languages other than  English—and metalinguistic knowledge (García & Wei, 2014) a s resources for text understanding (Jiménez et al. 20 15). Grounded in socio-cultural and dynamic bilingual lenses (García & Wei, 2014), the TRANSLATE curriculum empowers educators to tap into students’  diverse linguistic resources, even when they do not share all their students' languages. One key activity in the curriculum is collaborative translation. In this activity, students work in pairs or groups to translate parts of culturally relevant English texts (e.g., young adult novels featuring i mmigrant stories) into their shared home language. Teachers scaffold students' metalinguistic noticing and thinking about language features as tools for constructing meaning, by asking language-general inquir y questions. Collaborative translation activities in TRANSLATE classrooms position students as experts in their ow n languages and cultures as well as provide structu red opportunities to build shared understanding of text using their home language (White et al., 2024). These valuable learning experiences are unavailable to students who are the sole speakers of their home  language, a common reality in today’s diverse classrooms, motivating the development of TRANSLATE Buddy. We built the Buddy AI agent by providing ChatGPT 4o with custom system instructions and a curated knowledge base of translingual  theory texts. Applying persona prompting  techniques (White et al., 2023), Buddy was instructed to act as an inquisitive and supportive peer translation partner, following a key set of co nversation moves. These moves target the previously discussed pedagogical goals of collaborative translation and enact the four epistemological stances described above as fol lows: Engaging with the  multiplicity of meaning possible in language use, Buddy is told to prioritize collaborative exploration of  the spirit of the focal text over word-forword translation. As such, Buddy is explicitly inst ructed to produce multiple translations and to refr ain from seeking a single “best” translation (see Table 1). Instead, it is prompted to focus on how and why translations may differ and use this as a gateway for exploring the multiple ways that language conveys meaning. The relationality of meaning underlying text comprehension approaches rejects the claim t hat the textual meaning depends  solely on its lexical elements (Kintsch, 1988). Rather, Buddy was prompte d to pose probing questions that  invite students to draw on their lived experiences and culturally-rooted knowledge to make local meaning of the anchor texts selected for translation. Importantly, as students collaborate with Buddy to co-construct possible translations, a key interaction goal of Buddy is to constantly position students as knowers, uplifting students’ cross-linguistic noticing, linguistic expertise, and intellectual cu riosity as the primary factors that drive the emergent destination of the collaborative translation. To achieve this g oal, Buddy was instructed to (i) always ask students to share their translation before offering its own; (ii) use open-ended questions to invite students’ metalinguistic noticing; (iii) actively build on students’ noticing of word choice, syntactic construction, and discursive meaning when they compare and negotiate possible translations; and  (iv) employ inquisitive clarification prompts as in vitations for students to articulate their linguistic understanding and reasons for their translation decisions. Internal testing of Buddy by multiple members of the research team, using diverse anchor texts and home languages including Spanish, Mandarin, Korean, and Turkish, reveal ed strengths and limitations of the current prototype across the four epistemological dimensions. Overall, Buddy appeared to honor multiplicity by framing its own translation as an “alternative version for comparison”  and encouraging students to consider “the nuance/feeling you want to convey ” (Figure 1a). This afforded  emergent meaning-making opportunities during which the student and Buddy negotiated  and built on each other’s ideas. For example, we re gularly observed Buddy inquiring about users’ noticings or opinions in the translation process (F igure 1a) as well as instances of co-construction where Buddy actively engaged with and expanded on users’ perspectives: “Your choice  of 没有机会 really emphasizes that external factor—how others didn’t give her the opportunity to explain.” However, the challenge of moving beyond an entrenched notion  of a single “best” answer remains: Buddy often use d absolute language to affirm users’ choices (e.g., “That makes 学过 the perfect choice!”) implying a translation hierarchy antithetical to TRANSLATE curriculum's core values. In addition, we saw  room for improvement in positioning students as knowers as Buddy sometimes preemptively highlighted differences  in tran slations, limiting opportunities for students to make and share their own observations, a key aspect of TRANSLATE curriculum’s aim to foster their ownership and enga gement in metalinguistic meaning-making. Moreover, users asking for the meaning of English words to aid translation  tended to shift  Buddy into more of a tutoring role, overshadowing their home language expertise and their opportunities to engage in language construction. Finally, although Buddy responded usefully to cultural elements that highly engaged users brought to the conversation, it did not provide many invitations to make such relational text-to-self connections. For instance, Lillis et al.  (2016) contend that disciplinary discourses are historically saturated and contested. The recent development of  AI-assisted writing has presented new inquiries and implications regarding students’ academic literacy and compositional skills (Nguyen et al., 2024). In the context of academic reading, students  seldom utilize AI beyond summarizing papers (Liu e t al., 2024), but a socio-cultural approach necessitates a fuller engagement with literature that includes active meaningmaking (Gavelek & Bresnahan, 2014). In fact, college faculty often expect students to grasp complex texts, relate prior knowledge, and make connections across topics  (Clark et al., 2002). The critical need for reading support for such meaning-making at the postsecondary level motivates our development of The Scholar’s Table (TST). We built the TST AI agent by providing ChatGPT 4o with custom sy stem instructions to facilitate a “dinner party” between a learner and two simulated scholars (designated as such, see Figure 1b) who di scuss the reading in light of their own research and perspect ives. TS T’s design  reflects a socio-cultural approach to engagement with disciplinary texts and the four epistemological stances in multiple ways. First, we designed TST to reflect multiplicity of meaning by providing instructions to select scholars who ca n offer different ways to position the text within broader cultural and acade mic contexts (particularly considering perspectives underrepresented in fields the paper originates from / has had impact on), making visible the heteroglossic nature of texts (Table 1). Additionally, engaging with relationality of meaning, TST was instructed to support learners in crafting their own take to engage with the paper in relation to their personal or academic pursuits. Positioning students as knowers (and the scholars as powerful allies / resources for the students’ meaningful engagement with the text), TST was prompted to strategically end turns of talk with invitations for learners to elaborate, expand or contest the ideas shared. Lastly, instead of adheri ng to a set of predefined learning goals for a pape r, TST was prompted to invite students to drive their learning trajectory through dynamic co-construction and direction of the conversation (emergent destinations of learning). For instance, interaction is scripted to begin w ith scholars’ inquiries about how the ideas presented in the paper might connect to the student’s own interests and background. Internal testing of TST by multiple members of the research team to engage in article-based dialogue produced mixed success with respect to multiplicity of meaning. Although the tool was designed to present two different scholarly views, the agent brought in quite similar perspectives on most occasions. At times it also engaged in evaluative language such as “You’re getting to the heart of it!” implying a universal and single correct answer that the user was striving to discover. A key concern in creating simulated scholars was the possibility that TST might over-essentialize, stereotype, or misrepresent scholars'  perspectives; while this requires careful ongoing evaluation, our team’s initial uses did not find any notable examples of this nor that of AI hallucinations. One concern noted however was that conversations of ten drifted away from specific engagement with the academic text at hand into more general discussion of the ideas within it. TST’s behavior with respect to relationality of meaning were more promising: TST consistently asked students about th e application of the content to their own scholarship (e.g., “what might that look like in your field? ”) and presented each scholar’s views as a way rather than the way to look at a text. In early stages of TST development, we found a tendency of the scholars to pontificate and dominate conversati ons. With our goal to foster a dialogic experience, we re fined the prompt to create more space for the student  through balanced turn-taking and succinct responses. The final agent did better both  in making space for student talk and positioning the student as knower but also guided the conversation strongly, inviting this knowledge in quite specific directions: “I’d love to hear more about how you think assessment systems could truly support diverse learning identities. What would a system that respects these identities look like to you?”  Still, some emergent destinations of learning  were supported by the use of open-ended questions such as: “What’s your take?” While such questions by scholars were not intended to be coercive, we recognize that a learner’s identity (particularly pertaining to expe rience and expertise) might shape how they either comply with or challenge TST’s probing, warranting further testing and design iterations.

## Overall discussion and conclusions

The primary aim of this conceptual design paper was to outline a novel socio-cultural approach to personalization, offering an alternative to system-driven adaptation and the AI-as-expert paradigm. We sought to create two AI agents that would honor diverse, relational ways of knowing, recognize students as knowers and co-construct the direction of learning with students. Our development and initial use of t he TRANSLATE Buddy and The Scholar’s Table reveal both the challenges of the non-deterministic nature of system prompting  and the “jagged frontier” of generative AI capabilities  (Dell'Acqua et al., 2023) where certain goals are simple to achieve while others much more challenging. Both agents showed notable strengths in supporting emergent learning directions, something that has traditionally been quite difficult for learning technologies (Watters, 2021). However, they differed in the extent to which they supported multiplicity and relationality of meaning: Buddy excelled at holding multiple translations in play but rarely invited students to make text-to-self connections; TST consistently asked students how textual ideas connected to their inter ests but often introduced simulated scholars with very similar views. Both agents demonstrated elements of valuing students’ knowledge but also a tendency to act as  an authoritative knowledge provider. Some aspects of these limitations can be addressed through additional prompting in the next design iteration, but others may be tied to the specific pedagogical framing of each case. In addition, it will be important to examine how the ways users engage with the agents affect conversation trajectories. Informally, we noticed users who positioned themselves as knowledge holder s and the AI agent as a tool were more apt to receive responses recognizing them as an agentic knower, while when users engaged less confidently, the agents reverted to an AI-as-expert telling mode. This raises important concerns from an equity perspective as those who most need support to see themselves as knowers are least likely to receive it. We will explore this and other agent interaction dynamics in our next phase of work run ning user studies that look at patterns of use by students with varying profiles and backgrounds. In conclusion, this conceptual design paper present s a n initial framing of the design space for  a socio-cultural approach  to personalization  and demonstrat ion of  its viability. This approach  offers an alternative to the educational paradigm o f AI-as-expert and offers a broader framing of personalization than traditional approaches—not simply as an adjustment of pace or content but as a transformative practice that honors learners’ cultural context, agency, and evolving understanding.
