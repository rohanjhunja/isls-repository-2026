# Visualizing Local Data: Facilitator as Technology Conduit when using ArcGIS StoryMaps for Collaborative Map Building

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Within a collaborative map-building experience, the six participants, all women between the ages of 45 and 85, chose a hands-off approach to ArcGIS StoryMap technology, instead insisting the facilitator act as a technology conduit. This choice, which contradicts commonly held ideas about the importance of hands-on technology design, produced no discernable negative impact in participant’s sense of agency or engagement. Rather, participants report that avoiding direct engagement with technology enhanced their design experience.

## Introduction and background

In this study I aimed to understand, in a community  learning context, how ArcGIS StoryMaps might support participants to build a visual, spatial narrative that could act as a community data resource. Geographers have termed these sorts of sp atial narratives  deep map s. Within geography, a deep map “interweaves physica l geography and scientific analysis with biography, f olklore, narrative, text, memories, emotions, stori es, oral histories, and so much more to contribute to a rich er, deeper mapping of space and place” (Harris, 201 5, p.39). Theoretical framing Planning this project, I drew on the methods of asset-based collaborative co-design (Wong-Villacres et al., 2021). I relied on emerging conceptualizations of critical data sci ence perspectives and practices to explore  what valid approaches to data might look like (Kitchin & Lauri ault, 2014; Matuk et al., 2020; Wilkerson & Polman,  2020; Clegg et al., 2020), aiming to design a community learning space that supported conversations where “careful consideration of the contexts in which data is situated and why that matters” were supported in ways that allowed exploration of how people might “apply insights from data to real-world decisions” (Clegg et al., 2020, p. 2).

## Methods and analysis

I engaged a small (population of 6,366 in 2022) rural town along the mid-Atlantic coast of the United States. Six participants were recruited via coordination with a community partner, a pastor, and through snowball recruitment. All six participants self-identified as white women between the ages of 45 and 85. All reported living in the area for 10+ years. Participants chose a pseudonym to represent themselves  in all reporting of this research. I held a total of six design sessions lasting 2.5 hours in the early evening. Sessions followed the five stages of design: Empathize, Define, Ideate, Prototype, and Test/Iterate (Dam & Siang, 2021). Findings derive from analyzing four data sources: 15 hours of video and audio of the design sessions, design artifacts collected throughout the design experience, 7 hours of  researcher reflection  audio, and 3 hours  of post-experience member check interviews. Design session and member check interview analysis involved focused and inductive coding (Saldaña, 2021).

## Findings

Participants provided all waypoint components for e very location on the map. A waypoint was composed o f a geographic location, title, text, and image. The ArcGIS StoryMap web application had a well-developed user interface that allowed for multiple people to build  waypoints in the map. However, it became apparent early in the design sessions that, despite direct access to the web application both in and outside of the design sessions [all participants reported having a personal computer at  home], participants were disinterested in directly engaging with map-building. Participants instead directed me, as the facilitator, to create specific waypoints using text and images they collected or produced and digitally shared-primarily through email, text, and, less commonly, a shared Google doc). This division of labor was named directly during the midpoint of the  experience, when, in design session three, Daphne labeled my role in the map-building group as the ‘researcher’ while Classy articulated the role she and her fellow participants fulfilled as providing the “ideas.” Daphne: That's right! You are the researcher.

Heather: I am the researcher. (Heather laughing) I've taken a lot of classes! Classy: We're the idea people!

By assuming the role of technology conduit, I created a weak translational distance, or a hig h level of “indirectness between the user’s physical actions and the actions in the virtual space” (Shneiderman et al., 2017). However, my analysis of participant perspectives and behavior within the design sessions indicated that this weak translational distance did not hinder participants in clearly understanding how map waypoints were con structed, nor did it impact the strong sense of agency participants reported while designing the map, or the strong sense of pride and ownership they reported holding for the f inished map. This was confirmed by Eileen, Jane, Ka y, and Sallymearle during a member check interview. Jane utilized the metaphor of making a book to describe her role as a participant within the mapping group. Participants provided the content of the book, while she characterized my role of technology conduit as analogous to mechanical manufacturing.  Jane shared, “ The content was the building, the technology was just, okay, let's glue these pages together. It's like wri ting a book. You create the book, but you send it to a publisher, just to get it printed.” The group strongly rejected the idea that the lack of  hands-on encounter with the mapping technology might result in their experiencing less engagement or association with their design map (Saffer, 2010).  Eileen reduced the idea of encountering the technology directly to the menial act of typing, while Jane shared that if she had been asked to learn how to navigate the ArcGIS StoryMap web application, in addition to making all  the design decisions and providing all the content for their map, she would not have continued with the experience. Eileen: The idea was that we would be more engaged if we di d click, click, click. [Eileen makes gestures with her hands like she is typing on a keyboard.] But I think we felt engaged even without click, click, click.

Jane: Yeah, absolutely. Because you would have lost me  [from being part of the mapping experience] in a heartbeat on that! [engaging directly with the ArcGIS StoryMa p web application]

## Discussion

By taking on the role of technology conduit, I free d participants from the demands of new technology (e.g., engaging directly with the ArcGIS StoryMap web application), allowing participants to focus solely on generating waypoint content. A weak translational distance did not appear to impact the strong sense of agency  participants exhibited while designing the map nor the strong sense of pride and ownership they rep orted holding for the finished map. Participants were able to focus creatively and critically, with expressiveness and generativeness, on their personally held and community held knowledge and the process of storying that knowledge. In this way, the design of the experience supported critical data pr actices as participants explored more freely what l ocal knowledge might be thought of as data, as well as h ow storying that local knowledge could be a valid a pproach to data collection.
