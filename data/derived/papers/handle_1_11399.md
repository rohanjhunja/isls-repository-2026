# Modeling the Knowledge Underlying Student Engagement in Theory-Building Practices

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Science practices are a critical component of science education. Theory-building practices such as modeling lie at the heart of the scientific enterprise. To develop instructional activities that engage students in theory-building practices and assessments that track their development of expertise, it is necessary to understand the knowledge underlying students’ engagement in theory building. This paper presents a framework for modeling this knowledge, called the epistemic systems framework (ESF). Grounded in the knowledge in pieces (KiP) perspective, the ESF is designed to produce high-precision models of the structure, dynamics, and development of the knowledge students use to construct and refine theoretical artifacts, including models and explanations. The paper outlines the framework’s theoretical foundations and unpacks its major components.

## Introduction

Science practices have become a central focus of science education, reflecting the perspective that students should construct the knowledge of science through its epis temic practices (Duschl, 2008;  Schwarz et al., 2017). In the U.S. context, science standards emphasize theory-building practices, including modeling and explanation (NGSS Lead States). It is important to design science ins truction that engages students in a range of theory-building practices, to understand the resources they bring to their engagement, and to assess their development of practices over time. Toward these aims, it is necessary to understand the knowledge that students both draw on and develop through their participation in theory-building activities (Fo rd, 2008). Descriptions of this knowledge should be capable of producing high-resolution models of the knowledge students leverage during theory-building activities, which can explain how such knowledge is refined into more formal, scientific practices. Importantly, such models have the power to illuminate how students’ everyday  ways of knowing and making sense of the world are productive, both for in-the-moment sense-making, and as seeds for developing the knowledge r equired for participation in more formal, scientific practices.

While scientific theory building includes a number of practices, modeling has been a consistent focus of science education research (Gouvea & Passmore, 2017; Lehrer et al., 2008). Scholars have proposed a number of frameworks for characterizing the cognitive underpinnings of students’ participation in modeling activities, which characterize students’ knowledge about models and modeling and their knowledge for modeling. Most scholarship in science education has focused on students’ knowl edge of models and mo deling processes (Nicolau & Constantinou, 2014). Schwarz and White (2005) intro duced metamodeling knowledge  to refer to this kind of knowledge, and described it as including both knowl edge about the nature of models and knowledge about  the nature of modeling processes. Motivated by the grow ing interest in science practices, work has focused  on characterizing th e knowledge students leverage to participate in pra ctices during classroom inquiry activities (Kelly et al., 1998). Researchers agree that it is impo rtant to pay attention to what students do  in the context of classroom science activities to gain insight into the knowledge underlying their actions. Sandoval (2005) used the term practical epistemologies to refer to the knowledge students draw on to parti cipate in epistemic practices in the science classroom. This knowledge consists of s tudents’ beliefs regarding their own knowledge cons truction in school science, including beliefs about the natu re of knowledge, the methods of knowledge productio n, and criteria for judging knowledge. Berland et al. (2016)  proposed a framework for organizing students’ prac tical epistemologies called the Epistemologies in Practice  (EIP) framework. The EIP framework views students’ epistemologies as networks of understandings that are applied based on the needs of the sense-making context at hand. The framework includes students’ knowledge-construction goals and their understandings of how to engage in achieving those goals. The latter are referred to as epistemic considerations. These are ideas about the nature of the knowledge product they are constructing, its level of generality, how it can be justified, and what audience will use it.  Some scholars have characterized cognitive strategies  students demonstrate and develop through modeling activities. To characterize students’ enga gement in computational modeling, Stratford et al. (1998) introduced the cognitive strategies for modeling framework  (CSM), which included  five strategies: analyzing, relational reasoning, synthesizing, testing/debuggi ng, and explaining. Krist et al. (2019) proposed epistemic heuristics that guided students’ mechanistic reasoning during their construction of models of mechanisms underlying phenomena. Building on literature on mechanistic reasoning, they proposed three epistemic heuristics: thinking across scalar levels, identifying and unpa cking relevant factors, and linking to coordinate r elationships over time and/or space.

This brief literature  review indicates a range of perspectives on the knowledge underlying students’ participation in modeling practices, with scholars identifying ontologically distinct components. Whil e productive, the frameworks have several limitations. First, they lack coherence with respect to one another. Each framework describes a different kind of knowledge that students might draw on to engage in modeling, suggesting that each framework alone is incomplete. While it i s probable that students draw on knowledge featured in each of the frameworks, the frameworks are not designed to be integrated into a unified whole. Second, the frameworks have limited explanatory power. While all describe the composition, and some, the structure, of knowledge, none of the existing frameworks are designed to model the dynamics of that knowledge in use (i.e., how the knowledge facilitates students’ engagement in modeling), or t he development of that knowledge  (i.e., how students develop expertise with modeling practices over time). Third, the existing frameworks are not tuned for constructing highprecision models, as their components are of a some what larger grain size. This means they are not capable of capturing the subtle differences between the structure, dynamics, and development of different students’ modeling knowledge. Finally, the existing frameworks are all designed with respect to normative images of science, drawn from either scholarship on the nature of science or  classroom science inquiry. This makes it possible for researchers and educators to miss students’ potentially productive ways of thinking and making sense of the world that don’t necessarily look or sound like normative science. The present paper proposes a framework for modeling the knowledge underlying students’ enactment of theory-building practices, including modeling. It integrat es a variety of different kinds of knowledge, inclu ding knowledge related to the knowledge proposed in the frameworks discussed above. The framework is designed to model the structure, dynamics, and development of the knowledge used by students to engage in theory-building practices. The framework is tuned for high-precision descriptions of  networks of tiny pieces of knowledge, the moment-by-moment dynamics of its use, and the piecemeal shift s through which it changes. In the remainder of the paper, I introduce the theoretical foundations for the framework, followed by a description of it. Theoretical foundations Epistemic games Collins and Ferguson (1993) characterized the knowl edge-building processes of scientists and other scholars  as epistemic games. Scientists play epistemic games to produce epistemic artifacts, which address particular research questions. For example, the periodic table of the e lements is an epistemic artifact that addresses the  research question: how do the chemical elements compare to one another in terms of structure and properties? A scientist plays an epistemic game by filling out an epistemic form, or t emplate. In the case of the periodic table, the underlying form is a cross-product matrix. This form is ideal for structuring the comparison of elements along different dimensions. The moves used by the scientist to fill out the form correspo nd with the constraints of the form. To build the periodic table, Mendeleev needed  to identify the known chemical elements, determine structural characteristics against which they could be compared, identify the properties of each element, and then organize the elements in the matrix in terms of their characteristic stru cture and properties. In this work, I view theory-building practices as particular kinds of epistemic  games, which lead to the development of epistemic artifacts that are theoretical in nature.

Knowledge in pieces The knowledge used by an individual to engage in ep istemic games is viewed through the lens of knowledge in pieces (KiP; diSessa, 1993). Knowledge in pieces is a heuristic epistemological framework that models knowledge as a complex system of many different kinds of elements, which are drawn on in networks depending on the sensemaking demands of a given context. Learning occurs through a piecemeal process of reorganizatio n and refinement of the knowledge system. In this way, Ki P views naive knowledge elements as resources for the construction of more expert knowledge (Hammer, 2000). This makes KiP an asset-based perspective (Smith et al., 1994). The KiP agenda is focused on characterizing the productivity of students’ ways of thinking and making sense of the world. A long-term goal of the KiP program is to build computatio nally explicit models of human knowledge and learning. KiP seeks to build these mo dels grounded in the data, with an attitude of cont inuous improvement. Towards this goal, researchers have proposed a n umber of different kinds of knowledge elements and systems. Knowledge elements include phenomenological primitives (p-prims; diSessa, 1993), explanatory primitives (e-prims; Kapon & diSessa, 2012), and epistemological resources (Hammer & Elby, 2012). Knowledge systems include coordination classes (diSessa & Sherin, 1998) and strategy systems (Levin, 2018). I introduce several of these below, which are helpful to understanding the framework that I propose. Knowledge elements Phenomenological primitives (p-prims; diSessa,1993) are intuitive elements of know ledge that account for an individual’s sense of satisfaction or puzzlement in  response to physical phenomena. For example, an in dividual pushing a couch across a floor might expect the couch to move faster if they push harder. In this case, they might be drawing on the p-prim “more effort leads to more result.” P-prims are phenomenological in that they are both used to predict/explain physical phenomena, and that they are abstracted from individuals’ experience of physical phenomena. They are primitive in that they are self-explanatory, meaning explanations tend to bottom ou t at pprims. P-prims are also primitive in analogy with a computat ional metaphor. While coding primitive s are the smallest units of code out of which a larger program can be built, p-prims are assumed to be the smallest units of thought out of which larger patterns of reasoning c an be assembled. P-prims are assumed to have non-linguistic encodings, meaning t hey are inarticulable intuitions.  Explanatory primitives (e-prims; Kapon & diSessa, 2012) are a more general category to which p-prims belong. They are knowledge elements that func tion to explain phenomena of an individual’s experience. In addition to intuitive elements such as p-prims, e-prims also include knowledge elements that are linguistically encoded,  such as ideas learned in school. Epistemological resources (Hammer & Elby, 2012) are a category of knowledge e lements, which individuals have for making sense of knowledge-its nature, origins, and status. They are not necessarily primitive, as the term resource typically refers to chunks of knowledge of any grain size. Epistemological resources include ideas like “knowledge is discovered by scientists,” and “teachers have access to true knowledge.” Knowledge structures While p-prims, e-prims, and epistemological resources are particular  kinds of knowledge elements, the coordination class is a particular kind of knowledge structure (diSessa & Sherin, 1998). Knowledge s tructures are larger organizational structures that include k nowledge elements. The coordination class is a know ledge structure that was de veloped to model expert knowledge of concepts of measurable  quantities such as force, velocity, and acceleration. Coordination classes ha ve two major components: a perceptual component  and a cognitive component. The perceptual component consists of physiologica l means (readout strategies) by which individuals extract information from the world of their experience. The cognitive component consists of a network of knowledge elements (inferential net) that individuals both use to determine what knowledge is worth extracting (via their readout strategies) and to make sense of the extracted information. The knowledge network may include p-prims, e-prims, epistemological resources, or other kinds of knowledge elements. Epistemic systems framework Structure and function The epistemic systems framework (ESF) was developed to model the knowledge individuals dr aw on to engage in theory-building practices, or epistemic games. It emulates the basic structure of the coordination cl ass, which features perceptual and cognitive components guiding the extraction of information and its transformation into knowledge.

Perceptual component The perceptual component of the ESF consists of the  physiological processes that extract information f rom the world along with the extracted information. Perceptual extraction strategies facilitate the individual’s extraction of raw information from the world through the vario us sensory channels (e.g., sight, hearing, touch). Perceptual strategies are directed to attend to particular sou rces of information in the world, based on an indiv idual’s information needs and their expectations about how to effectively meet those. Extractions are the information drawn from the environment by an individual via their perceptual extraction strategies. Extractions are essentially perceptions, information that the mind/body comprehends but has not yet interpreted. Extractions are the raw data that the individual makes sense of and organizes through the moves of their epistemic game, using the knowledge elements within the cognitive component of their epistemic system. Cognitive component The cognitive component consists of the knowledge t he individual draws on to both determine what infor mation to extract from the world, and to transform that information into theoretical knowledge embodied in an epistemic artifact. I call this network of k nowledge the epistemic net. It consists of orienting, operating, and substantial elements. These three categories are proposed as classes of primitive elements that are analogous to e-prims. Like e-prims, they are small, self-contained elements that serve particular functions in cognition. They  can be either linguistically or intuitively encoded  and may have their origins in either informal social in teractions or formal schooling. Orienting elements are pieces of knowledge that orient an individual to their theory-building task. They may include epistemological resources (Hammer & Elby, 2012), such as knowledge of the entry conditions for a particular epistemic game, knowledge of the target form for a particular game, knowledge of the constr aints of the corresponding form, and basic moves  of the game. For example, when Mendeleev thought ab out comparing the known chemical elements along several dimensions, he might have recognized the opportunity to play a crossproduct matrix game. He might have known about the nature and constraints of  the cross-product matrix form, and how to go about filling it out. Operating elements  are pieces of knowledge that facilitate an individu al’s enactment of cognitive moves within an epistemic ga me. They may be likened to procedural knowledge (Anderson, 1996), in that they execute the cognitive moves of which the game is composed. Mendeleev may have drawn on operating elements such as identifying the  elements of a particular class, determining charac teristics against which they might be compared, identifying the properties of each element, and organizing the elements in the matrix in terms of  their characteristic structure and properties.  Substantial elements are pieces of knowledge that make up the explanatory, conceptual, or descri ptive knowledge used in the epistemic game. Substantial elements depend on the phenomena under study and are therefore highly context dependent. E-prims and p-prims used in the play of an epistemic game would fall in to the category of substantial elements. To arrange  elements in the periodic table, Mendeleev needed substantial  knowledge such as the atomic weight and  electronegativity of particular elements.

Dynamics and development The knowledge elements of the epistemic net work to gether synergistically and in coordination with perceptual extraction strategies to facilitate an individual’s  enactment of an epistemic game move. Each move’s enactment is viewed as an emergent phenomenon resulting from the interplay of these elements of the complex epistemic system. An individual’s enactment of an epistemic  game move is associated with a specific configurati on of perceptual strategies and epistemic net. This configuration is referred to a s an epistemic move projection. The superposition of epistemic move projections over th e course of an  individual’s epistemic game is referred to as an epistemic game projection. The dynamics of an individual’s epistemic system during their construction of an epistemic artifact can be understood by modeling th e epistemic projection for each move. This shows wh ich components work together synergistically to facilit ate the individual’s enactment of each move. A temp oral decomposition showing the epistemic projection for each move in sequence illuminates the dynamics of t he system across the game. By comparing epistemic game projections for the same individual playing the same kind of game at different points in time, we can track h ow the individual’s epistemic system develops. In s cience education research, this might look like comparing a student’s epistemic game projection for a particular kind of epistemic game at the start of the year with a proj ection from the middle of the year  and one from the end of the school year or comparing game projections across multiple years.

## Discussion

The present work introduced the epistemic systems f ramework, which can be used to model the knowledge an individual draws on to engage in scientific theory-building practices, including modeling. The present work joins the efforts of scholars within the science educatio n community, who have sought to describe the knowle dge underlying student engagement in scientific theory-building practices. Importantly, the ESF integrates  different kinds of knowledge, including knowledge related to the knowledge in the frameworks discussed in the introduction (Berland et al., 2016; Krist et al., 2019; Schwarz & White, 2005). The ESF attempts to provide greater explanatory power and precision by supporting the development of moment-by-moment models of the dynamics of knowledge used to facilitate student engagement in theory building, and by supporting the development of finegrained models of change in the knowledge system ov er time. Furthermore, the ESF is not designed to lo ok for knowledge that corresponds with aspects of normativ e science, but rather, to attend to the productive ways of thinking and making sense of the world that student s demonstrate in their classroom theory-building activities. Finally, understanding the knowledge students draw on and develop through their engagement in theory-building activities through the lens of the ESF can help res earchers determine how to design instructional acti vities that best support students’ learning of and through theo ry-building practices.  The ESF furthermore makes a contribution to the KiP framework, by specifying a knowled ge system for individuals’ engagement in scientific theory building practices.

The ESF is a first approximation framework that can  be used to characterize student engagement in scientific theory-building practices. The framework was inspired by epistemic forms and games  (Collins & Ferguson, 1993), and knowledge in pieces  (diSessa & Sherin, 1998; Levin, 2018). Th e major limitation of the framework is that it has been developed in coordination with analyses of relatively few data sets. As the framework is applied to rich process data of students engaged in different theor y-building activities in different content domains, shortcomings of the framework will be revealed, and it will be refined in response.
