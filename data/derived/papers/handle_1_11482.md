# Charting the Learning Sciences Neuroverse: Theorizing and Building Neurodiversity-Affirming STEM Education

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
The neurodiversity paradigm, originating from the autistic community, is reshaping the scope and approach to research on human cognition and sociality across fields, from psychology to medicine. Neurodiversity calls for a valuing of neurological diversity, critiquing deficit-oriented framings of minoritized neurotypes such as autism and dyslexia. This symposium challenges the learning sciences to reckon with this emerging paradigm’s implications for the core theory, approaches, and aims of the field, focusing on possibilities within STEM education research.

## Overview and objectives

“Neurodiversity” denotes the diversity in neurocogn itive functioning amongst humans. Neurodivergent pe ople are those whose neurocognitive function diverges fr om dominant societal norms, such as those with ADHD, autism, and dyslexia (Walker, 2021). A neurod iversity research orientation regards human variation as a form of biodiversity rather than impairment to “normal” fun ction (Chapman, 2021). Neurodivergent learners face epistemic injustice (Legault et al., 2021) and systematic barriers at every level of education (Clouder et al., 2020, Manalili, 2021). Despite commitments to fostering e quitable learning opportunities across numerous axe s of humandiversity, including cultural, racial, linguistic, and socioeconomic diversity (i.e., Gutiérrez & Jurow, 2016; Solomon et al., 2022), and a history of engaging wi th anti-deficit approaches to disability (Lewis, 2017; McDermott, 1993), the learning sciences field has n ot yet substantially engaged with emerging neurodiv ersity discourse. We propose that neurodiversity is ethically and conceptually central to the study of learning, warranting expanded attention across work in the learning sciences. In the cognitive sciences, neurodiversity-informed work has begun to spark revised theories a cross domains from sociality to cognition (Manalili et al., 2023). For example, research with neurodivergent participants complicates documented cognitive load effects such as the redundancy effect (Le Cunff et al., 2024). The objective of this symposium is to bring together emerging neu rodiversity-oriented work in the learning sciences, towards developing a more robust framework for how neurodiversity stands to impact the core theory and practices of our field. Beginning with the Science, Technology, Engi neering, and Mathematics (STEM) fields, we examine neurodivergent ways of engaging, thinking, and knowing, and how these might be better recognized and fostered within education. We take the domain of STEM education  as a paradigmatic case reflecting needed discourse across the learning sciences.

Following this year’s conference theme of “educatin g for world-making,” the work in this symposium positions neurodivergent people, both learners and researchers, as agents in designing more inclusive tools, practices, and environments, towards imagining more  equitable futures for teaching, learning, and lear ning sciences research. The authors embrace neurodivergence as a site of potential new forms of Community Cultural Wealth (Menzies): each paper centers systemically underrecognized strengths that neurodivergent people bring to STEM, from the visual and intuitive mathematical thinking of dyslexic mathematicians (Lambert & Harris) to the divergent thinking of ADHD engineering students (Ak demir et al.) to sensory-neurodivergent students’ “stimming” as multimodal embodied exploration of mathematics (Tancredi) to autistic students’ problem-solving and analytical thinking in computer science (Bonnet te). In so doing, we disrupt normative models of le arning, including assumed developmental progressions such a s progressing beyond the sensorimotor stage, or mas tering basics before progressing to complex ideas in mathe matics. We envision forms of instructional design, teaching, and assessment that are more responsive to the stre ngths of a neurodiverse student population, attendi ng to and building from neurodivergent practices.

What can a neurodiversity lens offer?

Given the history of asset-based and critical orientations to disability in the learning sciences, what new purchase might neurodiversity offer our field? The researche rs in this symposium leverage neurodiversity to thi nk across neuro-marginalized populations. Each paper addresses a different subset of neurodivergent populations; together, they raise a set of broader considerations for neuroqueering STEM education. By focusing systematically on those who fall outside the bounds of neuro-passing, we candevelop models, tools, and theory t hat better explains and supports the true neurodiversity of learners.

Towards a neurodiversity approach in the learning sciences Building from neurodivergent students’ strengths, t he authors in this symposium analyze and imagine ne w possibilities for STEM education that fosters learn ing and belonging, from pedagogy (Lambert & Harris, Tancredi), to systems of assessment and instruction al support (Akdemir et al., Bonnette). In dialogue with both critical learning sciences theory and foundational and emerging work on cognition and development, the  authors shine a light on the neuro-ableist underpinnings of how STEM learning is conce ptualized and cultivated, and begin the work of imagining systems and pedagogies that could pave a more equitable, neuro-inclusive future. Looking across our perspectives, we invite reflection in the field with some provisional next steps:

1. Reconsider the implicit neurotypical underpinnings of extant models of cognition, engagement,

participation, and development.  This entails reconsidering models rooted in the stu dy of neurotypical learners. What implicit assumptions, existing model s, and developmental trajectories should be revised to be more pluralistic? Are any challenged by focusing on neurodivergent populations?

2. Expand work with neurodivergent populations across learning sciences domains. Siloing neurodivergent

participants to Special Education research is doing a disservice to the study of all topics.

3. Study neurodivergent identity as it intersections w ith other marginalized identities.   Neurodivergent

marginalization is not separate from other systems of power and privilege studied in the learning sciences, and as such, these must be considered in integration. Neuro-ableism gets drawn upon to legitimize, compound, and reinforce other sy stems of power. Reciprocally, learning sciences’ perspectives on identity, relationships, and commun ication can broaden beyond the historical focus on white male enactments of neurodiversity in STEM.

4. Co-design neuro-affirming teaching tools and practices. The practice of design-based research in the

learning sciences field offers a means to theorize and build neuro-inclusive STEM education. Neurodiversity highlights the importance of centering neurodivergent voices. Reframing neurodiversity through community cultural wealth: Enactments and expansions Crystal M. Menzies

## Introduction and background

Community Cultural Wealth (CCW) theory has transfor med how critical researchers conceptualize assets w ithin marginalized communities by challenging deficit perspectives and recognizing alternative forms of capital (Yosso, 2005). Simultaneously, asset-based approaches toneurodiversity have emerged, highligh ting strengths among neurodivergent groups like pattern recognition, creativity, and innovative problem-solving (Hall et al., 2024; van Gorp, 2022). However, the intersection of CCW theor y and neurodiversity remains unexplored. This conce ptual paper examines how neurodivergent individuals may e nact existing forms of CCW and proposes considering cognitive differences as potential new forms of cultural wealth. Community cultural wealth Yosso (2005) posited that marginalized communities have knowledges and skills that are not widely reco gnized in research or utilized in practice called communit y cultural wealth. There are six forms of capital r ecognized within Yosso’s conceptual framework. Aspirational capital is the ability to maintain h igh aspirations despite challenging circumstances. Familial capital include s knowledge acquired through kinship networks. Ling uistic capital encompasses the multilingual and multimodal  communication skills extant in marginalized communities. Navigational capital represents the skills necessar y to navigate social institutions, particularly tho se that perpetually disadvantage marginalized communities. Resistant capital entails the ability to challenge negative stereotypes through the ownership of counter identities. Lastly, social capital refers to the networks of people who can support and guide minoritized students. Exploring neurodiversity within the CCW framework provides fertile conceptual ground for reframing the assets of neurodiverse populations. These differences include:

2. Ability to hyperfocus on areas of interest

3. Novel approaches to problem decomposition

4. Alternative information processing styles

5. Unique sensory processing and integration

These cognitive differences represent accumulated knowledge and cap abilities that are transmitted through neurodivergent communities, provide unique ways of knowing and navigating the world, often go unrecognized by dominant institutions, and generate valuable but undervalued perspectives and solutions.

## Implications

This theoretical expansion has significant implicat ions for learning sciences. It suggests ways that l earning environments might be redesigned to better support and benefit from neurodivergent ways of knowing. Ra ther than viewing hyperfocus as a deficit to be corrected, educators might recognize it as a valuable capability that can enhance learning. Similarly, alternative problem-solving approaches often dismissed as "wrong" might  be reframed as valuable demonstrations  of different ways of knowing that can enrich classr oom discourse (Hall et al., 2024).

Future research should examine how neurodivergent e nactments of CCW manifest across different educational contexts and explore whether cognitive differences themselves warrant theoretical expansio n of CCW. Additionally, work is needed to understand how  neurodivergent expressions of cultural wealth inte rsect with other forms of CCW, particularly for learners with multiple marginalized identities. This work could enhance our understanding of neurodiversity while enriching  learning sciences' frameworks around diversity, eq uity, and inclusion.

Ecosystems of belonging: Teaching assistants’ influence on neurodivergent computer science students’ sense of belonging Rachel Bonnette Supporting neurodivergent students in Computer Science (CS) requires an understanding of the contextual factors that influence their learning. Many neurodivergent students gravitate toward CS due to their skills in  creative problem-solving and analytical thinking (Russel et al., 2019; Wei et al., 2014). However, despite these strengths, these neurodivergent students often struggle to complete their degrees and experience challenges in feeling a sense of belonging (Syharat et al., 2023). Identifying th e role of relationships to learning within CS courses is c rucial for creating a more inclusive and supportive environment. In the U.S., CS programs often teach large, lecturebased courses in programming and mathematics, where  instructors are supported by severa l teaching assistants (TAs). TAs typically provide grading assistance and help fill gaps in students’ understanding through office hours or one-on-one tutoring (Mirza et al., 2019). TAs lack the ped agogical training and experience of instructors, bu t interface more frequently with students than instructors  (Austin, 2002; Zaman, 2023). For neurodivergent students, the relationships they form with instruct ors, TAs, and peers are vital to their sense of bel onging, which influences their mastery of content and ability to self-advocate (Vaccaro et al., 2015). Given that neurodi vergent students interact frequently with TAs, who often ha ve a degree of power and influence over students’ s ense of belonging, the relationship they have with TAs play s a significant role in their academic experiences (Kepple & Coble, 2020). Intersectionality likewise helps to i dentify how contextual factors like the primarily w hite, cisgendered male student population in such programs c an further complicate things. Queer, non-white, and female neurodivergent students are less likely to have dia gnoses for accommodations, while also being more li kely to feel out of place and struggle to establish relationships with peers and instructors (Ardaleanu et al., 2024; Rainey et al., 2018).

This exploratory study used thematic analysis withi n the sense of belonging framework presented by Vaccaro et al. (2015) to investigate how neurodivergent students’ interactions with TAs impact their experiences in a CS program. We conducted semi-structured interviews with three self-identifying neurodivergent undergraduate CS students from an urban, northeaste rn U.S. R1 university in Spring 2023, a queer and b lack student, an international student, and a third quee r and white, all of whom identified as aut istic in addition to experiencing various comorbidities (e.g., dyslexia,  depression). Each student participated in two inte rviews, beginning with a written reflection question: "How has being neurodivergent in your Computer Science program shaped your experiences?" These students reported more frequent direct and pr oblematic interactions with TAs than instructors. Several barriers were identified, including perceived attitudes toward students’ efforts to self-advocate, perceived beliefs about students' abilities to s ucceed, and the overall quality of the TA-student relationship. Students often felt confused by TA responses to questions. Students felt that there were not enough TAs to provide sufficient and timely support. Some students also reported feeling  mocked or rebuked for seeking help and reported fe eling rejected by TAs for asking questions. These negativ e experiences heightened students' fear of discrimi nation, already at greater risk because of students’ genders, races, and ethnicities. Neurodiversity is a valuable lens for addressing ch allenges in program design and pedagogy because it asks how students’ experiences are similar rather t handifferent; clarity and sensitivity from TAs wou ld support all students. These findings underscore the importa nce of considering the relationships that influence  student learning and the role of TAs in students’ belonging  in STEM programs, where students are more likely t o be excluded because of race or gender. Notably, students here expressed a strong desire for more awareness training for TAs, or hiring neurodivergent TAs who might be better equipped to understand their challenges. We encourage leveraging the learning sciences to better understa nd neurodiversity as a fact of student populations bearing implications for a need for change at the instructional level, rather than a deficit-focused problem to be addressed at the individual level throu gh additional student work. To that end, deeper exp loration into TA-student interactions could provide significant answers abou t the role learning sciences can play in neurodiver gent inclusion in STEM.

Neurodiversity as a challenge toneurotypical mathematical assumptions Rachel Lambert, Edmund Harriss The connections between neurodiversity and mathemat ics has been an open secret in the mathematical community, as it is not unusual for research mathem aticians to identify as neurodivergent. Yet normati ve and deficit-focused assumptions about the mathematical capacity  of neurodivergent children means that students in special education are often given fewer opportuniti es to engage in mathematical thinking and problem s olving. For some neurodivergent students, the so-called “basic” skills can be challenging wh ile more complex mathematics might be more accessible and engaging. For students with dyslexia, for example, difficulti es with declarative and procedural memory might mean that m emorizing the multiplication facts might be particu larly challenging (De Clerc q-Quaegebeur et al., 2018). Students in special educa tion are often given rote, repetitive procedural instruction and expectations (through in dividual goals called Individual Education Plans in  the US). We describe this situation, and how research in the  learning sciences could contribute to better under standing complex patterns in development through individual differences, as well as complex patterns of strengths that can be leveraged in math teaching and learning.

We will describe the results of a research project in which we co-researched the life stories and mathematical thinking of five dyslexic mathematicia ns (including Dr. Harriss) (Lambert & Harriss, 2022). Neurodiversity encourages researchers to conceptualize cognitive differences as natural forms of humandiversity with unique sets of challenges and strengths. Groun ding in this framework, we analyzed the experiences  of five research mathematicians with dyslexia as told throu gh personal narratives to find common strengths and challenges for dyslexic thinkers at the highest lev el of mathematics. We used thematic narrative analy sis to find commonalities across the narratives, coming to cons ensus as a team about our themes. We identified 4 t hemes:

(1) highly visual and intuitive ways of mathematical thinking, (2)  issues with language and translation between

forms, (3) issues with memorization of mathematical  facts and procedures, and (4) resilience as a stre ngth of dyslexia that matters in mathematics. We use our participants’ insights to explore how neurodiversit y, a view of cognitive disability developed by and for neurodive rse people, could expand opportunities for research. We call for mathematics educators to consider the strengths  and challenges of dyslexic l earners, as well as valuing expertise from insider perspectives. We also will d escribe how mathematical art and play can be a rout e to engagement and learning through our recent projects. Our findings support moving away from linear lear ning trajectories, as well as providing new insights about multimodali ty and mathematical thinking. One participant described “thinking in space,” which suggests non-verbal forms of intuition in mathematics that could  be investigated in the learning sciences.

Creative potential of neurodiverse engineering students: Insights from the Engineering Creativity Assessment Tool (ECAT) Zeynep G Akdemir-Beveridge, Arash Esmaili Zaghi, Connie Syharat Assessing creative potential among neurodiverse eng ineering students is pivotal for fostering innovati on and inclusivity within engineering education (Chrysocho ou et al., 2022). Neurodivergent students, including t hose with ADHD, often exhibit unique strengths such as divergent thinking and exceptional risk-taking abilities (Zaghi et al., 2016). However, the traditional deficit model of engineering education—focused on remediating perceived weaknesses—has been criticized for being inadequate and potent ially harmful toneurodivergent learners (e.g., Syharat et al., 2023).

To address this, strength-based frameworks have been integrated into engineer ing education programs. These frameworks emphasize leveraging neurodivergent students' strengths, including their creative abilities, and advocate for creativity-driven assessments over traditional standardized testing, as they ma y be better suited to highlighting neurodiverse students’ creative potential within engineering contexts. Reflecting this paradigm shift, this work-in-progress study introduces the Engineering Creativit y Assessment Tool (ECAT), designed to comprehensively measure divergent thinking skills —such as fluency and originality —among neurodiverse engineering students.

Unlike traditional domain-general creativity measures such as the Torrance Te sts of Creative Thinking (TTCT), ECAT utilizes the Consensual Assessment Tec hnique (CAT). This method leverages the collective judgment of domain experts, widely regarded as the "gold standard" for assessing creative potential ac ross disciplines (Kaufman et al., 2008). Whereas previou s studies have explored engineering students’ creat ivity through various methods (Baer & McKool, 2014; Charyton et al., 2008), ECAT uniquely combines an evaluation of divergent thinking skills with expert judgment, offering a novel approach not previously applied in engineering. The assessment development process began with ident ifying and training experts on the components of divergent thinking and its application to evaluatin g sample engineering products. Calibration meetings  were conducted to refine the ECAT, address experts’ feed back, and resolve initial ambiguities in the tool’s implementation. Currently, these trained experts ar e independently assessing 199 engineering products — presented as pictures and videos —produced by engineering students at different level s of their higher education in a public institution. The student participants, predominantly individuals with ADHD, represent a sp ectrum of neurodiverse profiles.

Our previous analyses relying on domain-general creativity measures revealed a weak but sig nificant correlation between students' adult creativity and verbal divergent thinking skills (r =.175, p <.05). To validate ECAT’s ability to capture creativity wi thin the engineering context, our study will compar e participants’ ECAT scores with their existing verbal (M = 112.15, SD = 17.67) and figural  (M = 99.51, SD = 13.38) TTCT scores as well. This comparison aims to highlight the distinc tiveness of ECAT in as sessing creative potential specific to engineering.

Once the psychometric properties of ECAT are valida ted, the tool may be implemented as a training resource for engineering faculty. Through workshops and training sessions, faculty will be introduced to the core principles of divergent thinking and how these skills can be fostered within engineering education. The goal is to help educators shift away from traditional deficit-oriented assessment practices and adopt methods tha t support and nurture the creative potential of neurodiverse engineering students. Neurodivergent embodied STEM learning: Stimming as an epistemic and interactional resource Sofia Tancredi Neurodivergent people often stim, engaging in repetitive body movements such as rocking and tapping. Whereas this phenomenon was initially named with respect to  autistic people, discourse on stimming has since b roadened to consider stimming among people with ADHD, people  with anxiety, and the general population. There is  an apparent tension between how stimming has been historically approached in education research and in reflections on the phenomenological experiences of neurodivergent people. In education research, stimming has largely been treated as a distraction from learning. The stakes of pressure for neurodivergent students to curb or mask their stimming are heightened by differential readings of learners’ bodies in institutional contexts, such as racialization (Annamma, 2017; Vickery et al., 2024). In contrast to dominant education perspectives, stimming is gen erally described by neurodivergent people as a functional process that supports self-regulation, exploration of the environment, and communication (Nolan & McBride, 2015) that it takes cognitive resources to suppress (Charlton et al., 2021).

Meanwhile, a growing body of embodiment research in the learning sciences demonstrates the centrality and potential of bodily activity for learning STEM concepts (i.e., Alibali & Nathan, 2012). The embodi ed cognition paradigm offers a new lens on stimming as  part of embodied cognitive activity (Tancredi & Abrahamson, 2024). From this perspective, stimming is theorized to reflect and regulate cognitive –affective system dynamics within the physical and cultural en vironment, for example by organizing or backgr ounding sensory stimuli, and to offer a prospective enactiv e substrate for complex actions, concepts, and comm unication, for example by introducing a sensorimotor structure congruent with thinking such as rhythmic tapping to consider groups of numbers. This perspective resonates with neurodivergent testimony of stimming’s functionality. Given this perspective, we might imagine a more inc lusive future for STEM instruction wherein neurodivergent practices such as stimming are not only allowed, but actively incorporated as a part of the learning experience. I propose a set of design heurist ics formulated and tested through the design-based research development of a first stim-centric instructional tool: Balance Board Math. Thi s tool explicitly establishes the common stim form of rocking as a means to explore m athematical concepts in the domain of functions and graphing, implemented with neurodiverse K-12 learners (see Tancredi, 2024). I draw examples o f how instructional design can incorporate affordances fo r stimming to fulfill regulatory, exploratory, and communicative functions within mathematical practices (Table 2). In fact, sensory profiles cut across diagnostic categories (Little et al., 2017), and while more rare sensory profiles might have a higher incidence within neurodivergent populations, within categories, sensory profiles may in fact be opposite from each other. A neurodiversi ty-centered approach enables centering and mapping sensory neurodivergence across diagnostic categories. Furth ermore, it invites broader neuroqueering (Walker, 2021) of the sensory environments we invite and construct fo r all learners, subverting neuronormativity. Such w ork is critical not only for developing tools that better serve neurodivergent children, but also to equitabl y implement visions of inclusive education (Erevelles, 2000).

Stimming is one example of how neurodivergent embodiment differs from neurotypical embodiment. As the learning sciences explore the embodied nature of cognition and seek to foster embodied learning, it is critical to recognize the diversity of sensory and bodily ex perience learners bring to bear. Embracing neurodiv erse embodiment challenges a range of norms in our field, including how we recognize and foster attentivene ss, conceptual thinking, and communication (Chen, 2024;  Tancredi et al., 2022). Balance B oard Math offers a first foray into how we might move beyond implicitly cent ering neurotypical embodiment in STEM instructional design; future co-design work is needed to recognize and build from the sensory and cultural variety of embodied experience amon g neurodivergent people. In sum, the intersection o f embodied cognition and neurodiversity compels a revisioning of how we read and cultivate learners’ bodily and cognitive activity in learning  spaces. It suggests that the stakes of attuning to and designing for diverse embodied experiences are the very efficacy of our instructional tools.
