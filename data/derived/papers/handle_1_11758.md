# Space and Time as Elements to Design With in Engineering for Sustainability

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Engineering co-design projects have great potential for developing sustainable solutions to global environmental and social crises. Rooted in sociocultural and sociomaterial theories of learning and activity, we examined a group of engineers, architects, and community members who gathered in rural Tanzania to co-design a sustainable roof tile. Using tools from microethnography and interaction analysis, we demonstrate how the successful co-design of the roof tile required collaboration between individuals (from different disciplinary, linguistic, and cultural backgrounds) and materials. This collaboration illuminated how the spatial and temporal trajectories of one material, rice husks, was critical for the design of the roof tile. This research highlights the central importance of local knowledge, systems thinking, and spatiotemporal reasoning for sustainability-focused engineering co-design.

## Introduction

This year, ISLS 2025 asks us to reflect on educational endeavors which envision and enact sustainable s olutions to global environmental and social crises. The Unit ed Nations describes sustainability as “development  which meets the needs of the present without compromising  the ability of future generations to meet their ow n needs” (Brundtland, 1987). While many scholars, practitioners, and or ganizers have critiqued this definition (Cayley, 2017), it is a useful starting point for our work. We b elieve engineering and engineering education can en gage people in sustainable development (Ashford, 2004; Byrne et al., 2010); however, we have identified two limits in engineering education research and practice that do not fully align with these sustainability goals: First, most engineering education research takes pl ace within the STEM pathway (Cannady et al, 2014) and focuses on those becoming professional engineer s: K-12 students, undergraduate students, or graduate students (Borrego & Bernhard, 2011). Far less research has examined co-design efforts where professional engineers or engineers-in-training collaborate with the public to design solu tions for pressing issues in society (e.g., the D-lab; Smith & Adams, 2022). This creates a power imbalance where the worldvi ews, learning, and activity of engineers is centered, and the worldviews, learning, and activity of the general public (who ostensibly benefit from engineers’ work) is marginalized. For engineering and engineering education to be socially sustainable (and just), we must also investigate when and how the general public shapes engineering practice. Second, most engineering education research has conceptualized design practice as a cognitive and social enterprise (e.g., Baral, 2013; Stevens & Hall, 1998). This is quite d ifferent from disciplines like science, technology, and society (STS) studies which foregro und the material aspects of engineering work to pro vide a more balanced account of the relationship between people and the physical world (e.g., Law, 1987). When tangible materials and material space are backgrounded in the engineering design process,  it becomes easier to forget the complex entanglements between humans, materials, spaces, and times. For engineering and engineering education to be environmentally and economically sustainable, we must consider with equal respect the materials that are recruited in the design process, where they come from, where they go, and how they are valued along the way. In this manuscript, we discuss our work in an ongoi ng research-practice partnership (RPP, Coburn & Penuel, 2016) which aims to realign engineering and engineering education wi th commitments to social, economic, and environmental sustainability. We inve stigated a group of people – engineering professors, engineering and architecture students, and local co mmunity members – as the y worked together to design a sustainable roofing material in the rural Tanzanian context. In line with the two concerns outlined above, we ask:

1. How was engineering practice distributed across par ticipants, some of whom identified as professional

engineers or architects and others who did not?

2. How did the materials recruited for designing the roof tile help shape engineering practice?

## Social, material, and spatiotemporal perspectives on engineering

To examine how people participate in engineering for s ustainable development, we: (a) ground ourselves in sociocultural (Lave & Wenger, 1991) and sociomaterial (Law, 1991) theories of learning; and (b) extend these theories in both the spatial and temporal dimensions.

Sociocultural theories understand learning to be a phenomenon which stretches beyond the mind of an individual and includes the social relationships that people form, the practices they participate in, and the identities that emerge as tools for sensemakin g (Lave & Wenger, 1991; Rogoff 2003). Engineering education research rooted in sociocultural perspective have examined i nteractions between experts and novices (Dixon & Jo hnson, 2011), the role of tools and symbols in engineering pra ctice (Stevens & Hall, 1998), and the co-construction of ethics (Gupta et al., 2022). Central to these studies are a focus on practices, identities, and changing participation within broader social structures. Sociocultural per spectives inform our focus on group dynamics, rathe r than individuals and their mental efforts to frame problems and design solutions. Sociomaterial theories understand that materials themselves exert tremendous agency over humans, our actions, and our abilities to make sense of the wor ld (Law, 1991). Engineering education research info rmed by sociomaterial perspectives demonstrates how enginee rs engage in dialectical conversations with the material world, rather than rhetorical conversations about the material world (Cunningham & Kelly, 2017; Manzini & Cau, 1989). That is, people converse with materials, learn a bout their properties, and attempt to work with or modify their properties for some expressed purpose. Sometimes, materials yield to our desires and become what we need them to be. Other times, people yield to the material and change the approach to engineering. For example, Gravel and Svhila (2023) describe how middle school stud ents talk with  digital sensors and construction materials throughout the process of design. Once a material has revealed its physical properties to a student, the student can work with these materials as they are (being materials), find new ways to work with materials (doing materials), or modify materials to work in new ways (becoming materials) (Shotter, 2006). Sociomaterial perspectives inform our focus on interactions between people and the materials they recruit for their designs. Building on sociocultural and sociomaterial perspec tives, we propose that sustainability-oriented engineering also requires detailed attention to space and time as elements to design with. As the UN definition indicates, sustainability requires people to reason  simultaneously about current and future needs. A m aterial, design, or technology is not inherently sustainable, but rather is only sustainable if it can foster social, economic, and environmental wellbeing now as well as in the future (McDonough & Braungart, 2010). For example, the sustainability of bamboo depends upon its current social, spatial, and temporal properties (e.g., cultural uses, water requirements, nutritional needs, acreage, time-to-harvest, transportation distance, and labor requirements) as well as its impact to future social, spatial, and temporal systems (possible soil degradation, the probability of becoming invasive, waste-recovery decisions about its disposal, post-use and competing cultural uses). Therefore, those who engage in sustainability-focused engineering must engage in dialectical conversations with materials as they are, as they have been, and as they will become. That is, people must reason about and design with the historic, current, and prospective social, physical, and spatiotempora l properties of a material. In our own data, this t ype of reasoning appeared when research teams identified a  material to use in a design, learned about the com peting cultural uses of the material, discussed the locations and timescales through which the materials become available, and imagined possible ‘downstream’ effects of using the material. When the se spatial and temporal aspects of a material are taken into consideration, engineering design can “[meet] the needs of the present without compromising the needs of the future” (Brundtland, 1987).

## Data collection and analysis

This study took place over the course of six days i n July 2024, during which the roof tile team met, i dentified materials needed to design a roof tile, acquired th ese materials, and began the co-design process. To study this group, we collected videos and photographs and wrote field notes. Videos focused on moments when participants were discussing roof tile design or engaging in phy sical manipulation of materials. Photographs were u sed to complement the video record. The first author wrote  field notes to describe the activities of the day and discuss any analytical insights. 33 hours of video, over 700 photographs, and 45 pages of field notes were collected. As a reminder, we set out to understand two aspects  of sustainability-oriented engineering: (a) how engineering practice is distributed amongst groups of people; and (b) how the spatial and temporal trajectories of materials shape practice. To investigate these aspects, we drew on approaches from microethnography (LeBaron,

2012) and interaction analysis (Jordan & Henderson, 1995). First, we identified any video footage or photographs

which focused on the roof tile team. We reviewed these video recordings and created content logs to describe who was in the recording, what activities were taking place, and what materials were being used. Second, we reviewed these content logs and flagged any activity which i nvolved discussions about materials. Third, we tran scribed these moments to capture participants’ talk, gesture, and references to the environment. Fourth, we analyzed these transcripts by paying close attention to how the ro of tile team talked about the materials they intend ed to use in their design, where these materials came from, and the social purposes or values of these materials. These analytical steps brought into focus many conversations which built towards a completed prototype of a sustainable roof tile. In this manuscript, we focus on conversations from the first day when part icipants discussed materials they needed to make a sustainable roof tile and identified where to find them. These conversations demonstrate how the roof tile team worked together to reason about the where materials come from and where materials go to engage in sustainable design. In the findings, we re-tell this story with special attention towards the materials and their spatial and temporal trajectories. Designing with the space-time trajectories of materials In this focal case, we begin by providing some back ground information on the sustainable roof tiles, i ncluding their purpose, legacies, and initial design. Then, we construct our case in two episodes: First, we de scribe a brief conversation where participants determined the task  for the first day: sourcing rice husks. Second, we explain a rupture in the design process, where Asha taught Gwen and Harriet about competing uses of rice husks in Kasisa. ## Background: Designing a sustainable roofing material in Kasisa

There are two primary approaches to building a low-cost roof in rural western Tanzania: thatched or sh eet metal roofs (Figure 2a-b). Thatched roofs are the traditional approach to roofing. Thatching requires people to g row, cut, dry, and bundle grass; then, layer and tie the  bundles into a cohesive surface using locally grow n sisal fibers as the fastening agent. These bundles are attached to a frame, which comprises the roof. Grass thatched roofs are sustainable in that they can be made by skilled lab orers with locally grown and renewable materials; however, they are permeable to moisture and insects and must  be replaced every 6-10 years (Odeyale, Adekunle, 2008, Mariarty & Svare, 1976). Makuti thatched roofs, an alternative to grass th atched roofs, are built with palm leaf fronds and are more durable to moisture and weather ing. However, this roof-type is more prevalent in coastal regions, and less typical in Western Tanzania. Thro ughout sub-Saharan Africa sheet metal roofs are the most common contemporary approach to roofing. Advantages include the long li fespan and potential for re-use, the light weight of the material, reduced structural ne eds, and the expanded material span-simplifying the complex roof-purlin system of a grass or tiled roof structure. F inally, rolled corrugated sheets are relatively eas y to transport. On the other hand, sheet metal has high embodied carbon from manufacturing processes, is made from a non-renewable resource with ensuing extraction impacts, and has a h igh carbon footprint associated with its transportation to rural places, such as western Tanzania (Seidlein et al., 2019). Members of the RP P, in collaboration with local community members, identified a need for a new sustainable roofing material which could be manufac tured locally and develop community livelihood opportunities. In the previous year, Jessie and Katrina (psuedonym, a former student from the RPP) worked with the community to investigate potential materials, ideally from local agricultural waste, which could be used to make roof tiles. Gwen and Harriet were asked to plan and enact the next phase of research and development by engaging in a co-design process with Theodora, Hannah, and Asha (i.e., the roof tile team). The team had four tasks: (1) source materials to make the roof tile mixture; (2)  create the mixture; (3) use the mixture to create a series of panels; and (4) test the panels (Figure 2c). As a n ote for the reader, we refer to these creations as ‘panels’ rather than roof tiles, because they are still at an early stage in the broader development of a roofing material. For testing, the team needed to explore material workability and  assess aspects – such as constructability, reproducibility, consistency, resilience to weathering, and cost – as feedback for the ongoing design process. In the episodes that follow, we present data and analysis from the first day when participants were trying to figure out where to find rice husks. Throughout the analysis, we frequently mention other tasks that had yet to begin-designing the mixture, creating panels, and testing. ## Discussion

This case is one of many where novice engineers and  community members worked together, reasoned about the spatial and temporal trajectories of materials, and  designed sustainable solutions for local needs. We briefly narrate these entanglements  from the vantage point of rice husks: Before arrivi ng in Kasisa, rice husks were considered by Gwen and Harriet to be a waste product with no use, cost, or value. This caused the two novice designers to believe that rice husks were a free material which could easily be recruited for their sustainable roof tile design. However, once in Kasisa, the spatial and temporal trajectories of rice husks revealed themselves: Jessie taught Gwen and Harriet that they must always inquire into the various uses of a material, even if a material is a cast off  ‘waste product’ from another system. By understanding the social uses of a material, one can understand the material’s value, and therefore, its role in a broader sociomaterial system. Asha taught Gwen and Harriet that rice husks have a rich life after being cast off fr om rice production. They are saved, stored in sacks, and burnt as a fuel source. Sometimes this fuel source is used in the home as domestic fuel. More commonly it is sold and used in another process: the production of earth bricks for construction. In conclusion, the sustainability and design of the roof tile depended deeply on the life history o f rice husks. In what remains, we discuss practical implications for sustainability-oriented co-design practice and theoretical implications for studies of learning.

## Implications for sustainability-oriented co-design practice

This case demonstrates how engineering design work is distributed amongst the four individuals, regardless of their disciplinary and cultural backgrounds. Although co-design values multiple ways of knowing and horizontal power relationships (Grogan, 2021), the distribution of engineering practice is not always equal.  In the above episodes, Gwen and Harriet referred to Jessie for decisions about the research and design process (1.01-09; 4.0104), and referred to Asha for information about sourcing, transporting, and purchasing materials (2.01-12; 3.0110). Furthermore, Theodora and Hannah were not yet involved in either discussion about rice husks  or about the research and design process (although, later in the project they would become crucial to the work). This dynamic creates a relationship where professionals are valued for their engineering and design knowledge and community members are valued for their context knowledge. At multiple points in the broader project, Jessie tried to subvert these relationships. She often re-directed students (like Gwen and Harriet) away from talking exclusively with engineering professionals (like herself and others on site) and towards discussions with community members, like Theodora, Hannah, and Asha. Jessie’s efforts were successful in fits and starts, even if students often regressed to their original behaviors. We still consider Jessie’s actions as successful and important both for the ir longer term impact on the students as future engineers, but also for the growing confidence of the commun ity, who tend to downplay their role as design-experts and context-experts. We recommend continued meta-discussions about the co-design process as necessary for students and community members to learn new ways of collaborating.

## Implications for studies of learning

In this paper, we argue that engaging in sustainabilty-oriented engineering work requires a reasoning with the spatial and temporal trajectories of materials. To be successful in designing a roof tile that is sustainable, the roof tile team had to reveal their assumptions about ric e husks and work together to better understand the material’s complex spatial and temporal trajectory. This works builds on learning sciences and STS research which discusses the entanglement of materiality and activity (Manzini & Cau, 1989; Gravel & Svhila, 2021; Shotter, 2006) as well as the role of assumptions in reasoning and learning (Gupta et al., 2022). In our case, we treat the roof tile team as a close d system. However, the reasoning and activity of th e roof tile team has implications for other RPP projects as well as the broader community of Kasisa. While the roof tile team currently needs only a  small amount of rice husks, the intention behind th is project is to provide a sustainable roofing option, other than thatched or sheet metal roofs. When this design becomes successful, making roof tiles will require larger quantities of rice husks, and potentially compete with other uses of rice husks (a fuel source for firing bricks). In tandem, the compressed earth blocks team (another project within the RPP) is working to design a construction material that can be made without firing, reduc ing carbon emissions. The intention is to offer a sustainable building material which could reduce the need for clay fired bricks. When this design becomes successful, the need for clay fired brick (and rice husks as a fuel source), will (theoretically) lessen. To summarize, a network is beginning to form in which reasoning and activity related to the development of a sustainable roofing material has implications for the carbon emissions of a (seemingly) separate practice – the construction of compressed earth blocks. This integrated systemic perspective on knowing and learning is not new (Fiksel et al., 2014), however, its application to research on engineering education is novel and promising.
