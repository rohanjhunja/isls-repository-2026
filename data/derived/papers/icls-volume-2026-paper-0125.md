# Teachers’ Expansive Explanations of Environmental Justice Issues

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study documents the efforts of science teachers to expand how they teach environmental and climate phenomen a to include social systems and structures as drivers of change. Through expansive practices, teachers candisrupt normative, science-only ways of teaching the climate crisis and environmental degradation by integrating political, cultural, and justice dimensions of socioecological issues. Through a lens of transdisciplinary teaching and epistemic diversity, this study analyzes the explanations and diagrammatic models teachers created while planning instructional units. Analysis identifies components and strategies for blending scientific and sociopolitical dimensions of environmental justice issues. This study contributes expansive practice s of ambitious and equitable science teaching during times of changing climate. ## Problem statement

Science teaching in the United States is increasing organized around students making sense of the world around them. However, the world is disrupted by processes that science, by itself, cannot explain or solve. Students often lack ways of thinking about how social, political, and ethical conditions cause problems and may blame individual life choices for socioecological issues rather than question problematic conditions  (Windschitl, 2023). Science teachers need strategies to expand the boundaries of their disciplines and practices to integrate the  sociopolitical causes of socioecological crises.

This study explores how science teachers use expansive scientific practices (Wagh et al., 2021; MoralesDoyle, 2024) to teach environmental degradation and climate change as driven b y sociopolitical processes. Specifically, we investigate the expansive nature of explanations and models that teachers created while planning units organized around environmental or climate injustices. We organize analysis around answering two questions: 1) What are the scientific and sociopolitical components of teachers’ explanations and models of environmental and climate phenomena? And 2) How did teachers blend scientific and so ciopolitical dimensions of phenomena?

Frameworks Practices of scientific models and explanations A core dimension of contemporary science teaching is that students learn disciplinary concepts by engaging in authentic scientific practices. Windschitl and colleagues (2018)  advanced the impactful approach of ambitious science teaching that advocates for phenomenon-driven instruction to support students in explaining the natural world. Gapless explanations of phenomena, or a full causal story that uses unobservable or theoretical components to explain observable events, are suggested as part of both teachers’ planning and students’ learning. MoralesDoyle (2024) in his framework for organizing instruction around social justice science issues advocates that teachers “embrace the gaps” in an explanation because science alone is inadequate for explaining socioecological issues (p. 80).

Scientific models are sets of representations, rules, and reasoning structures that allow one to generate predictions and explanations of how and why a phenomenon behaves the way it does  (Schwarz & White, 2005). Wagh and colleagues (2021)  and Morales-Doyle (2024) advocate for expanding  scientific modeling to include diverse epistemic resources that support students in sensemaking about their world. Environmental degradation is explained and addressed with inter-and-transdisciplinary knowledge and practices, and  productive interdisciplinary engagement requires “understanding and working across different kinds of knowledge” (Markauskaite et al., 2024, p. 234).

To  construct a more aut hentic understanding of socioecological issues, transdisciplinary knowledge and thinking is necessary, and this trans-disciplinarity can come from blending scientific and sociopolitical dimensions of those issues in explanations and models. We operationalize blended explanations and models as mixing canonical scientific concepts with  social 1109 conditions and social structures so that they combine to holistically describe socioecological issues. We represent the idea of blending scientific and sociopolitical dimension s as part of a spectrum of science teaching practices shown in Figure 1, ranging from less to more expansive. On the left, the least expansive version, is teaching science through rote memorization. Next is a narrow alignment with the Next Generation Science Standards (NGSS). These standards do include the anthropogenic  origin of environmental and climate harm, but they are not interdisciplinary; the science-only view in the NGSS is silent of the sociopolitical nature of environmental issues. Next is the approach we refer to as blending  sociopolitical dimensions which integrates social conditions and structures as mechanisms of change in environmental and climate systems. Blending represents an inter-andtransdisciplinary approach that prioritizes addressing issues and problems over disciplinary  core ideas (Takeuchi et al., 2020) and acknowledges that disciplinary learning is inextricable linked to social life (McKinney de Royston & Sengupta-Irving, 2019). Moving to the right, the most expansive version, is  what we call blending power dynamics with scientific dimensions; this end of the spectrum aligns with Morales Doyle’s (2024) articulation of social justice science issues in which teachers not only including sociopolitical dimensions of issues but  center the inequitable power dynamics that undergird those sociopolitical systems. The participants were five secondary science teachers who participated in a semester-long course on teaching climate rejuvenation, resilience, and justice which we will call TEACH-CRRJ. Heather was the designer and instructor of TEACH-CRRJ and Elena is a science teacher. Data sources collected during TEACH-CRRJ for this study are artifacts created or submitted by the participants: a) curriculum they designed during the course through revising a unit they had previously taught and

b) a narrative action plan they wrote to accompany that curriculum. During TEACH-CRRJ, one four-hour class

session was dedicated to the practice of blended explanations and models. During this class, Heather scaffold ed nature of all scientific knowledge and the transdisciplinary nature of phenomena that are relevant to students’ lives. Next teachers were supported by Heather  and their classmates in developing expansive explanations and models for the anchoring phenomena  of their units  by filling some of the gaps through blending. Across the semester, we worked to historicize and contextualize how social, political, and cultural forces are driving mechanisms of change in planetary processes and inequities and injustices in  these forces (Gandolfi, 2025). We analyzed the explanations and models created by teachers with an a priori coding framework that organized the major components of environmental phenomena. The coding scheme started with generic categories to accommodate for the different phenomena explained by each  teacher. First, we used grammatical coding analyze the anchoring phenomenon as solution-focused, problem-focused, global or local), 2) mechanism s (to analyze what is driving change in the phenomenon, including scientific components, sociopolitical components, blending), and 3) connections between students’ lives and the phenomenon (student agency and lived experience). Second, we moved to the specifics of each phenomenon by writing analytical notes about the components in each category which opened an opportunity to interpret data with grounded theory (Charmaz, 2006). Based on MoralesDoyle’s (2024) framework to “embrace the gaps” our analysis was rooted in a perspective of epistemic diversity. As described by Agarwal and Sengupta-Irving (2019), epistemic diversity refers to heterogeneity in knowing and doing science which includes perspectives, meaning, practices, and values of sensemaking that go beyond the canon of Western knowledge that have been traditionally marginalized in schools. Specifically, we looked for how teachers integrated social, political, cultural, and justice dimensions of a phenomenon to place each teachers’ work on the spectrum shown in Figure 1.

We present findings as cases  for three teachers (all names are pseudonyms)  with illustrative examples of their explanations and models to show their strategies for blending scientific and sociopolitical dimensions. 1110 Lenyx: Blending consumer behavior for fast fashion with the greenhouse effect The unit that Lenyx revised was for middle school student s to explore the ecological and climate consequences of fast fashion. The unit was anchored in the solution of bioplastics as an alternative to fossil fuel-based textiles to decrease carbon emissions the lifecycle of  clothing and thereby mitigating climate change. Lenxy wrote an explanation and developed a model that had different foci. The explanation had a mixed focus, primarily explaining the problems stemming from fast fashion consumer culture including greenhouse gas emissions, water pollution, microplastics po llutions, health  problems for workers  and communities, and waste in landfills and oceans. These problems are ecological and social, as well as justice oriented because of inequitable exposure risk; specifically she wrote, “Workers in factories, often in developing countries, are exposed to these toxins, resulting in serious health risks.” She explained regulation as a solution to limit pollution. In contrast, her model was focused on the solution of bioplastics as the raw material for clothing. She developed a template to compare a t-shirt made with traditional fossil fuel-based processes to a t-shirt made from bioplastics. This solution is technical, involving a reduction in the reliance on fossil fuels and resulting decrease in emissions. The problem and solution were global scale and Lenxy did not include dimensions relevant to students’ lives or agency. Lenxy used both science concepts and social conditions as causal mechanisms in her explanation and model. The science concepts include the greenhouse effect  and conservation of matter and energy. Socially, mechanisms of the problem included deregulation of industrial practices, consumer behavior, economic systems, and globalization. She blended these mechanisms by positioning social conditions as drivers of change in physical systems. For example, in her explanation she wrote,  “Increased demand for fast fashion, driven by consumer culture, results in higher emissions, exacerbating global warming. The more frequently people buy and discard clothing, the more energy-intensive the supply chain becomes, contributing to anthropogenic rise in global temperatures” and “The practices of mass production, overconsumption, and the prioritization of profit over sustainability directly lead to increased emissions, pollution, waste, and degradation of natural resources, all of which contribute to global warming and climate change.” In these excerpts, Lenxy named specific social conditions as playing an active role in climate systems and environmental processes. She blended knowledge of cultural patterns of behavior and economic patterns of disposability with the mechanisms of the disrupt ed greenhouse effect to explain the role of fast fashion in causing climate change. Amber: Blending capitalism and urbanization with habitat fragmentation The unit that Amber revised was for high school biology and focused on the ecology concept of carrying capacity. The anchoring phenomenon was land-bridges over roads as a mechanism of increasing the population of mountain lions in response to habitat destruction. Amber wrote an explanation and developed a model that had different foci. Her explanation was a mixed focus including problems of habitat fragmentation and biodiversity loss as well as the technical solution of a land-bridge over a freeway.  The model, in contrast, was problem focused using a template of before, during, and after in which students would model three time periods of escalating habitat fragmentation. These were all at the local scale. Amber did not include dimensions relevant to students’ daily lives or agency.

Amber’s explanation included scientific and social mechanisms. In terms of science, she included a range of disciplinary core ideas from the NGSS related to ecosystem interactions that explain density dependent factors on population. Socially, she positioned urbanization and capitalisms as drivers of change, specifically explaining how these conditions devalue ecosystems in ways that make habitat destruction  possible. She blended these mechanisms in her explanation by writing, “[Freeway construction] was possible because the landscape itself had no monetary value within our capitalist system, so it cost nothing to destroy, and it seemed like people would only benefit from its destruction.” Amber’s model and explanation blended capitalism and urbanization with habitat loss by describing that  “capitalism causes people to destroy the ecosystem to make money” and “urbanization causes roads and homes to be built in order to make people’s lives more convenient.”  Through this blending, Amber implicates the values embedded in capitalism and urbanization as drivers of habitat and biodiversity loss. Alicia: Blending combustion chemistry and fire management The unit that Alicia revised was for a high school  chemistry course. Her unit explored combustion reactions in a variety of contexts to engage students in explaining how three combustion related phenomena-zombie fires in the Arctic, urban wildfires, and landfill fires  – are related to climate change.  Alicia’s explanation had a mixed focus and a mixed scale, looking at problems and solutions of fires at local and global scales (she did not develop a model). Globally, the unit launched with studying the causes of zombie fires in the Artic as a positive feedback loop of melting permafrost, methane emissions, and global warming. She explained this phenomenon by writing, “with warmer climate, peat is exposed as permafrost melts, allowing it to combust.” The unit explored a series of 1111 solutions from Project Drawdown (Foley et al., 2020)  to reduce emissions and slow this feedback loop. A t an intermediate scale, the unit explored urban wildfire and urban structure fires as examples of combustion reactions. Alicia linked these phenomena to a range of social problems including extreme heat and heat-related illness. The solutions here were technical, like prescribed burns, and social, including civil lawsuits. This portion of the unit included justice-oriented case studies about how the city’s district attorney who was working to “pursue justice by prosecuting” businesses responsible for waste in urban communities that increased the risk of urban fires. The third phenomenon was fires in landfills which could be addressed technically through waste management. With landfills Alicia introduced another justice dimension, specifically the siting of hazardous waste facilities in low-income communities of color and the lack of regulation enforcement in these communities. Alicia’s unit was relevant to students’ lives and community; the unit engaged students ’ place-based knowledge and experiences by having students investigate  fire management strategies in their own communities and by integrating case studies of local events that students were familiar with.  She intentionally organized the unit to move from the global to local scale and planned opportunities to transfer their learning across scales. For example, she wrote that students would “connect Artic fires to global and local patterns by examining the causes and effects of fires in other carbon sinks and considering if local landfills would also be considered a positive feedback loop for fires.” We identified  blending of scientific and sociopolitical dimensions in the solution that Alicia explained at the local scale for urban fires and landfill fires.  For example, she wrote  that students would evaluate “prescribed burns, forest management, hazardous materials collection stations and recycling as strategies for drawdown, assessing how each impacts carbon storage and reduces future fire risk.” In this example,  Alicia explained Drawdown-based mitigation as linked to land use and waste management practices.  Through this blending, A licia links combustion reactions across scales and context to decision-making in political and economic systems.

## Implications

The teachers in this study  wrote expansive explanations of climate or environmental  phenomena by blending canonical scientific concepts with social conditions and structures to describe socioecological issues. They wrote expansive explanations that filled some of the gaps of a science-only explanation by linking specific actions in political, economic, cultural, or ethical realms of social life  to degrading or rejuvenating  changes in biogeochemical systems. Interestingly the teachers’ expansive explanations  of solutions all focused on public-sphere actions rather than individual actions, diverging from the norm in climate change education (Kranz et al., 2022). This focus on collective action acknowledges the systems-level nature of solutions  and the need to transformative relationships to address the climate crisis.  These findings show how blending can be a transdisciplinary science teaching practice that can  prepare students to understand and engage with the world around them.

Our analysis places the teachers’ practice on the spectrum shown in Figure 1, specifically as “blend sociopolitical dimensions.” P ower dynamics were largely excluded from  teachers’ stories of causal change; therefore they did not reach the most expansive version of practice on the spectrum. We notice three areas of epistemic diversity that represent gaps in their work. First, teachers ascribed agency uniformly to humans rather thandifferentiating agency for emissions based on power and privilege  (Clark, 2024). Second, teachers erased Indigenous communities and their contributions to knowledge systems. And third, they neglected local contextual details of a community.  Just as Markauskaite and colleagues (2024)  described how complex interdisciplinary problems can become “runaway objects” (Engstrom, 2007) that feel overwhelming and out of control to students, these categories of epistemically diverse knowledge may have been too overwhelming for the teachers to handle in explaining their phenomenon. These three categories are linked to ethical reasoning about relationships between humans and the natural world (Anderson, 2020), representing an area of future development for the teachers. We identify gaps  not as a critique but as opportunity spaces for  moving towards more expansive explanations of phenomena. Science t eachers and researchers  in this study and beyond need support  in expanding our own “epistemic horizons” (Agarwal & Sengupta-Irving, 2019) to explain the power dynamics and aspects of relational justice in phenomena.

The significance of this research is our effort to concretize the concept of an expansive explanation  and model of environmental justice phenomena. While this is a small exploratory case study that cannot be generalized to the population of science teachers, our findings spark questions that can inform research and practice on science teacher learning and professional devel opment. First, how do teachers balance disciplinary and transdisciplinary components of phenomena in teaching socioecological issues? The primary affordance of blending transdisciplinary dimensions in this study is that teachers were able to legi timize and privilege sensemaking resources that are traditionally excluded  or marginalized from science instruction (Sengupta-Irving et al., 2021). Second, how can teachers move from epistemic diversity to epistemic heterogeneity in their instruction? As 1112 reflected by limited inclusion of students’  lives and agency in  their explanations, teachers need support in responding to the heterogeneity of their students’ experiences. Third are Morales-Doyles’ (2024) questions about the inherent limitations of gapless explanations as a tool for teaching environmental  and climate justice phenomena. The teachers in this study were unsatisfied with teaching a NGSS-aligned, science-only explanation but did not have strategies to fully integrate the power dynamics relevant to environmental and climate injustices. A blending approach to e pistemically diverse, transdisciplinary science instruction represents  a n intermediary approach to helping students understand how and why socioecological systems work in times of changing climate.
