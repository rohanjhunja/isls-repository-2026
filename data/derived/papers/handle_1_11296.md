# Who Will Benefit From AIGC：The Intentions to Use Artificial lntelligence Generated Content among Students in Higher Education

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study explores the role of Artificial Intelligence Generated Content (AIGC) in undergraduates' learning and research, and its growing significance in higher education. Against this backdrop, understanding students' attitudes, behaviors, and intentions towards AIGC can better guide their learning under intelligent technology for high-quality development. Therefore, based on the Theory of Planned Behavior, this paper surveyed 336 Chinese undergraduates. The results revealed that: (1) Students hold a positive attitude toward AIGC, perceive behavioral control, and intend to use it, although some lack peer and institutional support. (2) Among users, attitudes toward AIGC, subjective norms, and perceived control each positively impact the intention to use it, with attitude and subjective norms as the strongest predictors. (3) For non-users, attitudes and subjective norms influence behavioral intentions only through perceived control, which fully mediates their effect on intentions. The study provides valuable insights for enhancing college students' application of AIGC.

## Introduction

The swift advancement of technology has ushered in the era of Artificial Intelligence Generated Content (AIGC) (used interchangeably with Generative AI, or GAI), which is increasingly being integrated into the educational sector (Carroll & Borycz, 2024). This integration is transforming learning experiences and educational paradigms, with AIGC offering personalized, immediate, and int eractive learning resources through intelligent con tent generation, enhancing engagement, interactivity, and learning efficiency (Van Laar et al., 2020). AIGC is used in various educational applications, s uch as teaching assistance, personalized learning pathways, and intelligent assessment  (Ma & Wang, 2024), improving educational outcomes. It analyzes student data for literature review and data mining, providing tailored resource recommendations and self-directed learning tools, empowering students with control over their learning.However, the integration of  AIGC technology also presents potential drawbacks. The complexity of AIG C algorithms and models can lead to misunderstandin gs or misuse, introducing errors or biases into the learning process. Overreliance on AIGC may hinder the development of cognitive and learning skills  (Jonathan M. Golding et al., 2024), and the influx of updated information could be distracting. There are also concerns about the c ollection and storage of personal information, whic h may compromise privacy and security.

As AIGC evolves, it is crucial for students to adapt tonew techno logical advancements to remain competitive (Borge et al., 2024). Educators must teach not only professional knowledge but also the operational principles and innovative applications of AIGC technology, fosteri ng independent learning and creative thinking.  In this transformative educational landscape, college stude nts, recognized as the most dynamic and innovative demographic, are pivotal users and beneficiaries of  AIGC technology (Mehmet Firat, 2023). However, there is ambiguity regarding their intentions to adopt AIGC technology, which affects its implementation in education. The adoption of technology is influenced by both co gnitive abilities and non-cognitive factors such as attitudes and social su pport. Therefore, it is essential to guide college students in developing positive intentions towards AIGC technology and to actively use it to enhance t heir learning and innovation capacities. This study addresses the factors that influence college studen ts' adoption of AIGC technology and aims to underst and their intentions to integrate this technology into their educational  experiences. Accordingly, this study addresses three pivotal research questions: RQ1：What is the attitude of undergraduate students towards AIGC RQ2: What factors could predict learners’ intention using AIGC RQ3: What should we do to help learners’ better use AIGC in their learning

## Literature review

In order to understand the use of AIGC at present, we investigated the current studies from the follow ing two aspects.

Research on the application of AIGC among college students There is considerable variability in college studen ts’ attitudes toward learning and using AIGC tools. Cha n et al.(2023) examined students' perspectives on AIGC technologies in higher education, revealing that while students acknowledge the potential of these tools to support  personalized learning, and enhance research skills, they also express concerns about accuracy, ethical issues, and the possible impacts on personal gr owth. Conversely, research by Habib et al.

(2024) indicated that students who used AIGC tools to as sist in completing projects experienced enhanced

creativity, such as generating more ideas and adopting different perspectives on issues. Delcker et al. Understanding stude nts' attitudes toward learning and using  AIGC tools can therefore support more effective int egration of these tools, promoting the further development of students' acad emic skills and abilities.  Currently, some experts are conducting research on how students can correctly utilize AIGC. Cui (2025) aims to enhance students' awareness and application abilities of AIGC by improving their perceived technological acceptance. Stornaiuolo et al. In summary, in recent years, AIGC, as an emerging t echnology, has attracted high attention from the academic community. Currently, research on AIGC in the fie ld of education is primarily theoretical, focusing on the  opportunities and challenges AIGC presents to vari ous industries. It also delves into the operational mechanisms and dissemination logic of AIGC. Therefore, this paper will study college students' attitudes, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control in the lear ning process towards AIGC technology, understand the factors affecting their behavioral intentions towards AIGC technology, and provide guidance for educators and decision-makers to better guide college students in using AIGC technology. Strategies for promoting the application of AIGC among college students AIGC presents both opportunities and challenges for  higher education. In instructional contexts, AIGC plays a pivotal role. Chiu (2024) describes AIGC as AI technology capable of generati ng new content, ranging from educational resources such as text and images to synthetic data, including code. The generation of these resources in the teaching process can help educators save sub stantial time and alleviate teaching pressures. In pedagogic al methods, Shi et al. The emergence of tools like ChatGPT enables a more personalized and flexible learning experience.  For instance, ChatGPT can employ the "Socratic met hod," guiding students through discussion, Q&A, and debate to foster autonomous inquiry. In the era of artificial intelligence, AIGC has a t ransformative potential in the field of educational assessment (Chiu, 2024). Traditional assessment methods in tea ching have relied heavily on teachers ’ capabilities and are constrained by human resources. AIGC can alter this landscape. Moorhouse et al. (2023) propose reconfiguring these limitations in traditio nal educational assessment, offering innovative app roaches to evaluate students' multifaceted performance, custom ize learning plans, and improve educational effecti veness. Research suggests that AIGC can provide not only personalized learning resources bu t also assist university students with tasks such as essay writing and exams, which increases the risk of academic dishonesty a nd makes detection challenging (Mehmet Firat, 2023). Chaudhry et al. (2023) examined ChatGPT’s performance in handling assignments and the effectiveness of plagiarism det ection tools, underscoring the need to reevaluate a ssessment methods in light of AIGC tools. Consequently, AIGC has led to shifts in student learning approaches, necessitating corresponding adjustments in teacher evaluation methods to account for AIGC’s impact.

## Methodology

Research model and hypothesis development The Theory of Reasoned Action (TRA), proposed by Fishbe in and Ajzen (1975) posits that an individual's behavioral intention is jointly driven by  attitude (positive or negative evaluations of the behavior) and subjective norm (perceived social pressure), which directly influen ces actual behavior through intention. However, TRA assumes that behavior is entirely volitional and fails to account for external environmental or resource constraints, such as skills, time, or technological conditions—a limitation that may weaken its explanatory power in complex behavioral contexts. To optimize predictions of tec hnology adoption behavior, Davis (1989) developed the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM)  based on the TRA framework. TAM deconstructs attitu des into technology-specific dimensions:  perceived usefulness  (the degree to which technology enhances performanc e) and perceived ease of use  (the effort required to use the technology), while downplaying the role of subjective norms to focus on users' individual cognition of te chnological value. Despite its widespread applicati on in information systems research, TAM has been criticiz ed for overlooking non-technological external factors, such as resource availability and socio-cultural pressures.

To address these theoretical gaps, Ajzen (1991) further extended TRA by proposing the  Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB), which introduces the  perceived behavioral control variable (an individual's assessment of their ability to execute the behavior and the av ailability of external supportive conditions). This  refinement modifies the behavioral intention formula to:  attitude + subjective norm + perceived behavioral c ontrol. The breakthrough of TPB lies in its incorporation of bo th TRA's cognitive and social dimensions and its qu antitative analysis of behavioral control capacity (e.g., skil ls, resources, environmental barriers). Consequentl y, TPB provides a more comprehensive framework for explain ing non-volitional behaviors (e.g., actions constrained by external limitations). By integrating perceived beh avioral control, TPB not only captures subjective m otivations but also quantifies practical feasibility, thereby enhancing its applicability to scenarios requiring the analysis of both internal intentions and external constraints (e.g., technology adoption in resource-limited educational settings). This study focuses on the implications o f TPB in the context of college students' intention s to adopt AIGC.This study's theoretical model is illustrated in Figure 1. In the Theory of Planned Behavior, Ajzen (2001) mention that cognition and affect, as two important components of attitude (ATT), are the stimulating factors of behavioral intention. College students' cognitive and affective evaluations of AIGC technology will direc tly affect their attitude and behavioral intention.  Subjective norms (SN) refer to an individual's perception of important others' approval or disapproval of specific behaviors. In the use of AIGC technology by college students, they may be influenced by classmates, friends, and the institution, forming subjective norms about AIGC technology (LaMastro, 2001). This positive subjective norms may strengthen students’ intention to use AIGC, driven by social pressure, group influence, and the desire to align with significant others  (Wang et al., 2024). Although TPB has been widely applied in studying technology acceptance behaviors, it faces certain limitations (Venkatesh & Davis, 2000). For instance, TPB relies heavily on attitudes, norms, and perceived control, but overlo oks external technological factors and user experie nce. In Ajzen's model, perceived behavioral control (PBC) c onsists of control beliefs, which are the external control beliefs of personal perceived control over key reso urces, and self-efficacy (Bandura et al., 1980), which refers to an individual's subjective evaluation and understanding of their capabilities during the specific implementation of a behavior, that is, the degree to which they grasp  the completion of the behavior. The higher the con fidence college students have in their abilities and resource control i n using AIGC technology, the more positive their intention to use the technology will be. Therefore, this study proposes the following hypotheses: H1: Undergraduates' attitude positively influences their intention to adopt AIGC. H2: Undergraduates' subjective norm positively influences their intention to adopt AIGC. H3: Undergraduates' perceived behavioral control positively influences their intention to adopt AIGC. Questionnaire The survey instrument for data collection consists of three parts. The first part gathers demographic information of higher education students, including gender, academic year, major, whether they have used AIGC tools, and the types of AIGC tools they have used. The second part employs a 6-point Likert scale to measure participants' attitudes towards AIGC, perceived norms, perceived behavioral control, intention to use and expe cted outcomes (for non-users), and experienced outcomes (for users) across  five dimensions. Responses range from 'strongly disagree' to 'strongly agree' and are adapted from previous research. Higher scores on this instrument  indicate a more positive perception towards adopting AIGC tool s. All data were collected through an online surv ey. The third part includes open-ended questions, aiming to gain in-depth insights into Undergraduates' expectations and suggestions for how higher education institutions can better support their use of AIGC.

## Participants

The participants of this study  involved 396 Chinese undergraduates, with data from  336 retained after excluding 60 for failing lie detectors, rapid responses, or highly similar answers. Regarding technology usage, 77.4% of the students had prior experience with AIGC tools, while 22.6% had no such experience. Among those familiar with AIGC tools, ChatGPT, Wenxin Yiyan, and iFlytek Spar k were the most prevalent, with usage rates of 71.9 %, 47.7%, and 26.9% respectively. The sample comprised 45.2% male and 54.8% female students. The year of study distribution included 24.7% freshmen, 24.1% sophomo res, 21.4% juniors, and 29.8% seniors. In terms of academic disciplines, 41.9% were from the humanitie s, 39.9% from science and engineering, and 18.2% fr om interdisciplinary fields. Geographically, 44.3% were from the eastern region, 37.5% from the central r egion, and 18.2% from the western region.

## Conclusion

The target of this study is to answer the question of who will benefit from AIGC. To achieve this goal, we investigated college students' behavioral intention s towards AIGC technology and found that students g enerally exhibit positive intentions, indicating a willingness to integrate AIGC into their educational experience. However, there is a notable gap between the high expectation s of non-users and the actual experience of users, suggestin g that the integration of AIGC into educational envir onments may no t yet fulfill all anticipated benefits, possibly due to a lack of tailored educational content or fu nctionalities within AIGC tools. For students who h ave used AIGC, their attitudes and subjective norms, influenced by personal experience, can predict their intentions, while non-users' intentions are primarily shaped by their perceptions of the skills, resources, and opportunities required to use AIGC technology. Demographic variables such as gender, major, and regional differences do not significantly impac t attitudes or intentions towards AIGC, indicating widespread acceptance across various student profiles, yet also suggesting that specific  student needs may be overlooked. Students' concern s about the accuracy of AIGC tools  and their desire for more complex functionalities h ighlight current limitations and the potential for improving learning outcomes through technological advancements (Barrett & Pack, 2023).
