# Introducing Authentic Datasets Into the Biology Classroom: Teachers’ Considerations When Designing Digital Instruction Units

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Introducing datasets into the science classroom may facilitate developing scientific practices and epistemic thinking. However, authentic datasets are rarely used in high school, and little is known about biology teachers’ knowledge considerations regarding dataset-driven instruction. Here we investigated these knowledge considerations in high-school biology teachers. Forty teachers designed dataset-driven instruction units and completed open-ended questionnaires during a professional development workshop. Our analysis, based on the PISA framework of scientific literacy, indicated that although epistemic considerations were the most prominent of teachers' perceived benefits of dataset-driven instruction, they were less pronounced than content and procedural considerations in the instruction units that they designed. This suggests that although teachers recognize the value of epistemic thinking and see authentic datasets as a means to facilitate scientific literacy, further effort is needed to assist teachers in designing instruction units which better reflect these goals.

## Introduction

Biology as a discipline has a long history of engaging with large datasets (Leonelli, 2014). Using authentic datasets in the science classroom enables students and teachers to engage in, and assist in developing scientific practices (Kjelvik & Schultheis, 2019; Wyner, 2013). However, most current use of data in the biology classroom is highly simplified, as authentic datasets are messy and com plicated. Indeed, selection and curation of dataset s require expertise and application of domain knowledge (Duncan et al., 2018). This notable gap between biology as a databased science and high-school biology could be mediated by the biology teacher. However, little is known about teachers’ perceptions of the challenges and benefits of data-driven instruction, or their design considerations, in general and in biology in particular. Therefore, we examined the knowledge considerations expressed in biology teachers’ views of data-driven instruction, and in the goals that they set when designing data-driven activities tailored to their students. Our research focuses on teachers’ epistemic considerations in using and designing datadriven instruction, and offers insights that can be  further used to design better learning materials, teachers’ professional development (PD) programs and teacher education. This may be a step toward promoting the use of authentic datasets in the high-school biology classroom, thus contributing to students’ scientific data literacy.

## Theoretical framework

Promoting scientific data literacy by using authentic data and the teacher’s role To gain scientific literacy, one should understand not only basic scientific information, but also the  process by which science is carried out, and its epistemic bac kbone (NRC, 2012). Here we followed the OECD PISA framework (OECD, 2019), which defines scientific literacy as the ability to participate in science-related issues. This framework defines three components of scientif ic literacy: (a) content knowledge – knowledge of t he theoretical concepts and ideas of the discipline; (b) procedural knowledge – knowledge of the standard procedures that underlie the methods and practices used to est ablish scientific knowledge; and (c) epistemic know ledge – understanding the rationale of scientific inquiry, and how scientific knowledge is derived; and the degree to which such knowledge is justified by evidence or theoretical explanations. The competency-based perspective described in the PISA framework also recognizes an affective element in the student's display of these competencies (OECD, 2019).

The recent strategic vision proposal for the PISA 2024 framework anchors the importance of data literacy, and states that understanding global challenges, su ch as pandemics, requires evaluating statistical da ta and probabilistic thinking (OECD, 2020). Data literacy can be developed by asking students to use authentic datasets, establish and justify interpretations, and consider potential sources of error, such as limited data (Osborne, 2014). Use of authentic datasets in the science classroom provides a broader range of science practices in which students and teachers can engage, and assists in developing these competencies (Kjelvik & Schultheis, 2019; Wyner, 2013), while applying biological knowledge (Yarden & Levko vich, 2021, p. 72). Although datasets do not permit  the full scope of scientific inquiry, because students do not collect the data first-hand, datasets can pr ovide a rich, ICLS2022 Proceedings 227 © ISLS multifaceted set of information that requires highl y complex reasoning (Chinn & Malhotra, 2002), vario us research questions, and data-analysis approaches (W yner, 2013). To support data literacy among student s, teachers must first be aware of its importance, gain familiarity with data-rich environments and be able to mediate these practices to their students. Previous researc h has shown that curriculum materials alone, withou t teachermediated norms that enable scientific sense-making and epistemic talk, are not sufficient to promote s tudents’ understanding of scientific knowledge (e.g., Berland et al., 2016). Thus, effectively teaching scientific reasoning requires holding a body of epistemic knowledge (Kind & Osborne, 2017) and applying epistemic considerations (Ke & Schwarz, 2019). When designing data-rich activities for their students, teachers use a variety of goals and considerations. Understanding teachers’ considerations in general, and epistemic considerations in par ticular, is important because they shed light on what teachers notice, value, and see as important for students to learn (Ariely et al., 2021). Therefore, here we focus on teachers’ epistemic considerations regarding the use and design of datadriven activities for their students. Science teachers’ PD Implementing instructional approaches that involve authentic scientific practices and technologies requires first introducing them to the teachers. Providing teachers with opportunities to experience the technology first-hand, to observe its pedagogical benefits and gain an awareness of its associated challenges may affect their approach to applying the technology in their classroom (Dorfman et al., 2019; Mishra et al., 2019). Referring s pecifically to working with authentic data, research has shown that following PD courses, teachers changed their v iews toward using authentic data and toward the way they use data in their classes. For example, following a PD course which included the implementation of a data-rich ec ology curriculum, ninth-grade biology teachers were  more positively disposed to including authentic scientific data in their lesson plans (Wyner, 2013). Another recent study showed that at the end of a PD course, teachers were able to integrate computational thinking into their teaching, emphasizing the important role of teachers as co-designers of activities for their students (Wu et al., 2020). PD courses in which teachers design and adapt instructional units can support different modes of use by the teachers, while meeting their instructional goals and their students’ needs (Brown, 2011). Supporting teachers' design process in the form of co-design can enhance teacher understanding of the technology being used, foster professional growth and improve outcomes in the classroom (Peel et al., 2020). Even in the context of integrating advanced educational technology, co-design processe s that involve teachers have been shown to be a use ful approach to designing solutions that address teache rs’ needs, and to gaining the teachers’ trust (Hols tein et al., 2019).

## The context of the study

Description of PD workshop In light of the above, a PD workshop was designed t o help high-school biology teachers incorporate aut hentic digital datasets into their instruction, and to ide ntify their knowledge considerations toward using d atasets and while designing digital instruction units. We followed the Eylon and Bagno (2006) three-stage model of teachers’ PD. In the first stage— teachers as learners, they experience data-driven instruction units as students. In the second stage—teachers as curriculum developers, they design an instruction unit based on their own pedagogical expertise, consultations with experts and critique by their peers. In the third stage—reflection, teachers report on their designed units and reflect on their design process (Eylon & Bagno, 2006). Similarly, in the current workshop, teachers first experienced an online data-driven ac tivity as students. Then they were presented with a uthentic taxonomy datasets. Based on those datasets, while relying on their pedagogical expertise, the teachers developed different instruction units. Following the workshop, teachers’ views and considerations of data-driven instruction were assessed through an open-ended questionnaire and their written instruction units (Figure 1). The workshop was designed and taught by the researchers and took place during the summer break (July–August 2020), in the context of the activities carried out by the National Center for High-School Biology Teachers in Israel. Due to the restrictions following the COVID-19 outbreak, the PD workshop was designed and carried out as a 30-hour online workshop, using the Personalized Teaching and Learning (PeTeL) platform. As a result, the rate of regi stration and participation was higher than expected (n = 52), and participants included teachers who do not usu ally participate in the face-to-face activities of the National Center for High-School Biology Teachers in Israel due to distance and time considerations. In addition, the COVID-19 restrictions created a need for teachers t o access online activities for their students, which may also have contributed to the relatively high number of participants (Sun et al., 2020).

However, 85% of the teachers who completed all of the assignments (n = 34/40) chose to use one of the four ready-made datasets (or subsets of those datasets) for their instruction units.

## Research questions

Teachers have a key role in promoting data literacy and epistemic thinking in the biology classroom. Since little is known about high-school biology teachers’ views regarding dataset-driven instruction, we sought to answer the following research questions: (1) How are high-school biology teachers’ knowledge considerations expressed in their perceptions of the challenges and benefits of  using authentic datasets in their classroom? (2) H ow are knowledge considerations in general, and epistemic considerations in particular, expressed in teachers ’ datadriven instruction units?

## Participants

Participants were high-school biology teachers in the above-described PD workshop focusing on dataset-driven instruction. Of the 52 teachers who started the workshop, 40 completed all of the assignments. Herein, we present the findings based on those 40 teachers. All of the teachers teach biology in high schools across Israel, and have teaching experience ranging from 1–44 years (avg 13.7 ± 9). Only eight teachers had previous experience working or teaching with datasets. The teachers voluntarily  chose to participate in the PD workshop and in the accompanying research.

## Data collection and analysis

Teachers’ views and considerations regarding the use of authentic datasets in the classroom were assessed by:

a) An open-ended questionnaire, which asked the teache rs to “describe the challenges and benefits of

dataset-driven instruction (i) for you as a teacher  and (ii) for your students.” The questionnaires we re voluntarily filled at the closing session on the la st day of the workshop. Each participant wrote free ly, with a total of 66 benefits and 58 challenges.

b) Written instruction units: during the workshop, the  teachers designed dataset-driven digital instruction

units, and documented their design considerations. Due to space limitations, in the current paper we focus on the design considerations expressed in their teaching goals (a total of 140 considerations). The data from the questionnaires and instruction units were analyzed according to the PISA framework (2019). In addition to these predetermined categories, we identified subcategories using the deductive–inductive procedure approach (Srnka & Koeszegi, 2007). Two sc ience-education researchers independently analyzed the ICLS2022 Proceedings 229 © ISLS data, and the categorization was discussed until an  acceptance rate of 100% between researchers was re ached. Pearson's chi-square test was performed to assess whether the distribution of the teachers’ perceived benefits and design considerations were consistent. A case study  was chosen from the instruction units. A comprehen sive analysis of that case study is beyond the scope of the current paper and therefore, it is presented he re only to demonstrate the translation of epistemic considerat ions into an instructional sequence. To provide a r ich illustration, we chose the instruction units with the most detailed epistemic considerations.

## Results

The challenges of using authentic datasets in the biology classroom In the open-ended questionnaire, teachers related to different challenges of data-driven instruction, with a total of 58 references. The knowledge considerations expressed in their answers are summarized in Table 1, in the order of their appearance in the PISA framework (OECD, 2019). Teachers' responses included considerations along the four dimensions: teaching content, procedural knowledge, epistemic knowledge, and affective aspects (coherent with the PISA framework—OECD, 2019). Eight teachers  related to conflicts between teaching the required content knowledge and teaching with datasets, as the latte r is often time-consuming. Some mentioned that contemplating the datasets may distract the students from the content knowledge. Most of the answers (n = 28/58, 48%) related to procedural challenges. They referred to difficulties derived from working with technology, which are not specific to working with datasets (n = 11, 19%), such as getting used to a new platform, user interface of the website, remembering a new password, etc. The rest of the procedural considerations (n = 17, 29%) related to data-driven instruction, such as the size of the da tabases potentially hindering students' orientation or operation throughout the activity. Some teachers stated the n eed for a lot of preparation work on their part, i. e., finding a quality dataset, editing, and cleaning it for their students. Ten teachers (17%) related to the epistemic challenges of dataset-driven instruction, such as its "messy" nature, and questioned the reliability of authentic data, as well as of detaching the database from the way in which the data were collected, making the data meaningles s for the students. Finally, twelve teachers also s tated the affective influences of the large datasets, as bein g intimidating and/or frustrating, and potentially hindering students’ approaching or completing their tasks (Table 1). "A new learning environment that is different from what the students are used to" 8 Teachers’ knowledge considerations expressed in their instruction units To answer the second research question, how knowledge considerations in general, and epistemic considerations in particular, are expressed in teachers’ data-driven instruction units, we analyzed the instruction units according to the PISA framework (OECD, 2019). In the current paper, we focus on the considerations expressed in their stated teaching goals. Out of a total of 140 considerations, 31 focused on biological content knowledge, such as “The students will learn about the characteristics and risks of radon gas.” The majority focused on procedural knowledge (n = 75/140). The teachers’ aim was for their students to “identify the scientific inquiry components” or “identify measurements that deviate from the trend of most of the data and will explain the anomalies and how to analyze general trends despite the anomalous exa mples.” Epistemic considerations (n = 20/140) included references such as “the students will gain knowledge regarding science and scientific research: critical thinking, research planning, problem definition and using suitable graphs” or “Students will recognize and understand the issue of dataset reliability.” All but one of the t eachers who highlighted epistemic design considerat ions also addressed procedural knowledge. Only six design considerations took into account affect and engagement aspects (“Creating a ‘sense of discovery’ of ecological rules,” “Emphasize the individual's commitment to the population in following the rules”). To further investigate ho w knowledge considerations are expressed in teacher s’ datadriven instruction units (research question 2), we explored the extent to which the knowledge considerations stated ICLS2022 Proceedings 231 © ISLS by teachers as benefits of data-driven instruction were manifested in their units. Although all knowle dge components were expressed as considerations in both the perceived benefits and the instruction units, there was a significant difference (p <.001) between the distribution of those knowledge considerations in each case. Pearson chi-square test revealed a significant difference between knowledge considerations in teachers’ perceived benefits and instruction units (X2(4, n = 206) = 38.63, p <.001). Figure 2 illustrates 40 teachers' general  considerations (blue) and their actual designing considerations (orange) as expressed in the dataset-driven instruction units. When generally discussing data-driven instruction, the most prominent consideration was epistemic knowledge (36% of considerations, Figure 2). However, when designing units in practice, 54% of the considerations relate d to procedural knowledge, 28% to content knowledge, and epistemic considerations dropped to 14%. ## Discussion and implications

In this study, we provide an analysis of knowledge considerations expressed in biology teachers’ perceptions of the challenges and benefits of dataset-driven instruction, as well as of their considerations when deciding on their design of a dataset-driven instruction unit. The co nclusions of this study leverage the PISA framework  for scientific literacy, and further refine it by identifying subcategories, in the context of scientific data literacy. Our conclusions have implications for the use of datase ts for biology teaching and for science teacher education, particularly as science teachers face the challenge of engaging in debates around the epistemic nature of science, for which dataset instruction can serve as one of many possible instructional strategies. Our analysis shows that although some teachers perceived dataset-driven instruction as a possible way of teaching content knowledge, content considerations were the least pronounced of the knowledge components when they considered the benefits and challenges of dataset-driven instruction. This may be because the teachers perceive dataset-driven instruction as conflicting with the curriculum. Indeed, it has been shown that  when designing non-mandatory curriculum instruction units, most science teachers' decisions are related to contextual ICLS2022 Proceedings 232 © ISLS factors, such as work overload (Kaplan Mintz et al., 2021). However, when designing their instruction units, content considerations were expressed significantly more often in the teachers' goals. The teachers found ways to connect the databases to the curriculum, possibly minimizing the aforementioned conflict. Procedural considerations made up a major fraction of the perceived benefits and challenges. Teachers often related to dataset-driven instruction as beneficial for teaching and practicing scientific practices and inquiry (both guided and open), which are a part of the mandatory Israeli biology curriculum. Perhaps that contributed to procedural considerations being the most pronounced  component in the instruction units, considering th e influence of their connection to the curriculum on teachers' practice (Kaplan Mintz et al., 2021). Chi nn and Malhotra (2002) stated that database tasks have the potential to simulate most features of authentic reasoning in the context of interpreting existing data. They stu died database-driven activities in which students c onducted investigations using a database containing information about dozens of birds (Reiser et al., 2001). However, they concluded that those database tasks do not allow students to design and carry out their own studies and do not permit new investigations. Our results indicated th at our much larger datasets (thousands of cases) ma y allow teachers to prompt their students to ask novel research questions, and seek answers in the dataset. As teacher AS explained, the volume of the data enables the students to become “inquirers, [who] should decide what is relevant and right for them.” The procedural challenges were  both dataset-specific and related to technology in  general, and were attributed similarly to students and teach ers. The teachers addressed these in their instruct ion units, providing scaffolds and leading their students step by step through the datasets, as demonstrated in the case study. As for epistemic considerations, the most popular benefit of authentic dataset-driven instruction referred to the opportunity afforded to teach epistemic knowledge, exposing students “to how research is really done, when the graphs are imperfect and require critical think ing." The epistemic considerations were also demons trated through the case study. Teachers’ epistemic conside rations while designing dataset-driven instruction indicated that they: (a) recognize the value of epistemic thi nking for students’ scientific literacy and (b) see  authentic datasets as means of teaching epistemic thinking.

However, in the instruction units, the fraction of epistemic considerations decreased significantly, becoming the least pronounced amongst the knowledge components. One possible explanation for this gap is that epistemic knowledge (although declared as important) is not expressed in practice, in the biology curriculum. As discussed above, the curriculum affects teachers' p ractices. Another possible explanation lies in the challenges. As teachers noted, there are epistemic-related chal lenges in dataset-driven instruction, such as expla ining the messy nature and reliability of the data, and data-collection procedures (which are not always availab le to teachers). Overcoming these challenges is even hard er when procedural-related challenges are still ver y pronounced, as our analysis revealed. Research has suggested that there is a hierarchy for the use of knowledge components during learning, according to which procedural knowledge is needed to promote epistemic knowledge (Levkovich & Yarden, 2021). Therefore, it is possib le that because the teachers are new to dataset-dri ven instruction, they still struggle with the procedural knowledge involved in engaging with datasets; although they did see authentic datasets as a means of teaching e pistemic thinking, they are still limited in their ability to incorporate these ideas into practice. The gap betw een teachers' knowledge considerations in the perce ived benefits and in the units they designed is not surprising. Research has shown the difficulty in inducing in-service teachers to change, and in their translating new approaches and ideas into practice (reviewed in Luft & Hewson, 2014). The PD workshop presented here was only 30 hours long, and although the participating teachers already deemed dataset instruction as valuable (as they chose to participate in the workshop), it could only have a limited effect on teacher change. Nevertheless, increasing instructors’ access to high-quality, data-intensive  resources, alongside the necessary training to effectively imp lement them, might increase the use of authentic da ta in the science classroom (Kjelvik & Schultheis, 2019). Further effort is needed to assist teachers in designing instruction units that better represent these goals. For example, future PD courses might explicitly discuss the way epistemic knowledge and considerations can be expressed in dataset-driven instruction.
