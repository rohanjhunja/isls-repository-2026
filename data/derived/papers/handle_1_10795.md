# Can Relational Ethics Offer Radical Hope for Teacher Learning?

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In working toward justice, teachers encounter many dilemmas that are not just pedagogical or political; they are ethical. This paper proposes that relational ethics, in contrast to traditional and rational ethics, are particularly well-suited to understanding teachers’ ethical learning in complex sociopolitical contexts. Given the hegemonic nature of U.S. public schools, however, are the visionary aspirations of relational ethics even useful for teachers? By thinking with theory, this paper explores what relational ethics can offer for finding radical hope in teachers’ extant ethical learning.

## Introduction

Although histories of educational injustices are al ways intertwined with histories of resistance, cont estation, and innovation toward justice, public schooling in the United States remains defined by the regular re-production of powered relations that regularly re-produce injustice. Because the systems of schooling and the systems in which schools are embedded often seem —and sometimes are —designed to thwart justice at every turn, t eachers frequently find their efforts toward social justice stymied (e.g., Esmonde, 2014). Consequently, teachers routinely encounter ethical dilemmas where they do not have a ll the information they would like to have before a cting, where the information they do have might suggest co ntradictory actions, where the possible outcomes of  their actions are ambiguous, and yet where they have to act. What teachers do in these situations matters, and therefore teachers’ ethical learning matters.

Consider the case of a skillful veteran Algebra 1 t eacher named Franck. One of his students, Roberto, rarely speaks in small group conversations, and Franck believes that he “tries hard” but “doesn’t have the tools to participate.” Because Franck sees part icipation as tied to learning, he wants Roberto to contribute to his group more actively but also wonders whether “impos[ing] it on him” respects Roberto’s agency. In a conversation with colleagues, Franck names some strategies he has tri ed and asks what else he cando. Franck's dilemma i s a pedagogical dilemma because what Roberto is learning in math cl ass is at stake. A pedagogical approach to this dilemma might recommend additional interventions th at Franck can try, such as establishing different participation structures or modifying the assignment; in fact, this is what Franck’s colleagues do when he asks for advice, and they consider the affordances of variou s pedagogical practices in this particular situatio n for this particular student. Franck’s dilemma, however, is also political, because there are stakes to constructing a Latino boy from recent migrant roots, who has been classified as needing an IEP, as being a lesser-than learner in a math classroom, and also stakes to “forcing him to do something he doesn’t want to.” A political approach might trace those stakes and connect them to broader sociopolit ical narratives about who is smart, or who is mathe matical, who gets to tell other people what to do, or even w hich migrants deserve access to which re sources. Framing Franck's dilemma as an ethical dilemma accounts for the pedagogical and the politi cal and also asks: what responsibilities does Franck have and to whom, and how should he navigate competing responsibilities? Scholars in the learning sciences have pursued axiological innovations that expand and tra nsform how ethics are conceptualized and enacted in the resear ch and practice of learning (e.g., Bang et al., 201 6; Espinoza, 2021; Meixi, 2022; Vossoughi et al., 2021), catalyzing a reimagining of what is possible and what is necessa ry for justice. This work, however, has rarely been done in traditi onal public schools, which we know to be assimilative and hegemonic spaces. Existing frameworks point out many ways that school s are flawed and many ways that teachers fall short of lofty ideals, so my aim in turning to ethics is not to find yet another way to illustrate shortcomings. Instead, I inquire as to how attending to ethics can illuminate the presence of and opportunities for radical hope that “help[s] us imagine and realize m ore humanizing, just, and equitable forms of engage ment and relationships with each other” (The Politics of Learning Collective, 2017, p. 94). If we take teacher learning to be changes in participation and practices (Lave & Weng er, 1991), how can examining teacher learning throu gh relational ethics reveal examples of and possibilit ies for disrupting, contesting, and creating anew? To explore this question, I first briefly outline what I mean by relational ethics, and then consider what framing ethi cs as relational might offer to understanding a specific case of mathematics teacher learning drawn from empirical data. Relational approaches to ethics Discussions of ethics in the U.S., including discussions of teachers’ professional ethics, typically rely on three traditions: 1)  virtue ethics, which evaluate the rightness or goodness of actions based on their adherence to virtues such as fairness, courage, or honesty; 2) deontological ethics, which foreground the congruen ce of actions with often-universal rules or principles about how to act or w hat is good; or 3) consequentialist ethics, which suggest that right or good actions should be determined bas ed on their outcomes. These approaches to ethics ar e often critiqued for, among other things, their assumption of individual rational actors; their reliance on rules, principles, or virtues derived from limited— mostly white, male, educated, and European— perspectives; and their inability to account for non-ideal situations where outcomes cannot be known  (e.g., Birhane, 2021; Jaggar, 2015). Nevertheless, they remain the most common ways of conceptualizing ethics in and of teaching, despite abundant evidence that they are insufficiently complex for parsing the actual practice of teaching (Sabbagh, 2009). Instead of relying on rational perspectives, questi ons of “ought” or “should” can also be addressed relationally. Ethics is discussed as relational in many Indigenous traditions, Black feminist philosophies, feminist materialisms, and disability studies (Meynell & Paron, 2021); here, I focus on the subse t of these traditions that share three premises. First, everyone is always and  already in relation with human and more-than-human others; (Barad, 2007). Second, these already-existing relations are inextricable from histories of power in relations that were in motion long before any individual existed (Butler, 2005). Third, these powered relations are sustained and reproduced through interaction, which means that th ere are always opportunities to challenge and trans form the relational status quo (Esmonde & Booker, 2017). From these premises, ques tions of ethics become questions of ethical relations. In other words, instead of askin g whether individuals’ actions are “right” or “good ” based on universal virtues, principles, or rules (rational f ramings of ethics), relationa l ethics ask: what kinds of relations are being enacted? Relational frames are particularly well-suited for understanding teachers’ ethical learning in complex sociopolitical contexts for three reasons: 1) how they shift whose perspectives are centered; 2) their expansive view of ethical actors; and 3) their stance towards possibilities for justice.

## Whose perspectives are centered

The learning sciences have aimed to expand whose knowledge is considered legitimate or mathematical  or scientific by honoring the experiences of historically margina lized students. Given the inextricability of epistemology, ontology, and ethics (Barad, 2007), r elational ethics provides a similar opportunity to broaden whose ways of determining what should be are taken as valid in conceptualizing ethics in math education. Ethical framings that are concerned with relations that make possible more liberatory and decolonial futures are common in the work of scholars writing from feminist, Indigenous, and Black feminist traditions (e.g., Bang, 2017; Meynell & Paron, 2021) and who see, for example, the everyd ay work of teaching and learning as “a collective p raxis of rewriting the narratives that constitute us” (Maynard et al., 2021, p. 140). Although commitments to social justice can be considered through many ethical theories (e.g., treating justi ce as a virtue), relational ethics emphasizes the dynamic and modest nature of interactors doing what they can from where they are (Barad, 2007; Sho twell,

2016) rather than the actions of heroic individuals trying to bend the moral arc of the universe.

Broadening ethical actors A focus on relations also allows enables accounting for the more-than-human in examining ethics, contesting the anthropocentrism that has long dominated educationa l research (Bang, 2017; de Freitas & Sinclair, 2013). For example, Franck and Roberto are entangled not just with each  other but also with histories, discourses, and materialities of racial capitalism, gentrification,  global migration currents and national immigration  policies, the theft of Indigenous land, the cultures and practice s of math education, and the patterns of U.S. math education reform. Relational ethics recognizes teachers’ and students’ participation in these entanglements. It also asks, among other questions, what it might mean for mathe matics to be treated as an actor and no t just a context in teachers’ ethical learning (Chen, 2023). For example, what kinds of relations between tea chers and mathematics or between students and mathematics are being made possible by teachers’ choices? Generative possibilities for justice Relational ethics privilege the interpretations of t hose who have historically been marginalized (Colli ns, 2000; Harding, 1993). Although traditional social justice approaches and relational ethics both critically analyze existing power relations and possib ilities for change, relational ethics are humble in  that they refrain from claiming unequivocal definitions of justice. Instead, relational ethics endorse the proliferation of diverse and heterogeneous possibilities for ethical relations rather than a s ingle way of determining what is right or good (Boylan, 2016; Meixi, 2022). This is also a way of honoring hetero geneous perspectives, especially of those whose int ellectual traditions have historically been dismissed (Bang, 2017). To be clear, this is not a relativistic stance; “not everything deserves a future” (Shotwell, 2016, p. 1 55). What is excluded matters as much as what is include d (Giraud, 2019), and as Vea and colleagues (2023) not e, acknowledging diverse meaning-making practices only expands possibilities when dehumanizing or fascist positions are excluded. Thinking with theory Relational ethics, as a lens for making sense of the pursuit of more humanizing and just futures, presents ambitious aspirations for teachers working in schools that are multilayered sites for the reproduction of powered relations (e.g., Sengupta-Irving, 2021). Are relational ethics useful, then, or do they merely reiterate the complex challenges of striving for justice? To think through this question, I borrow Jackson and Mazzei’s (2012) method of thinking with theory and the dilemma that Franck poses about Roberto, described above, which was drawn from video and observational data collected as part of a larger design-based research-practice partnership studying mathematics teachers’ learning (Horn & Garner, 2022). This dilemma arose as Franck and his colleagues —all experienced Algebra 1 teachers in one of the largest school dis tricts in the U nited States—watched several video clips from their own classrooms. Chosen by the researcher in r esponse to teachers’ requests, these clips showed h ow each teacher moved to include an excluded student in sma ll group work. Their conversation  about the videoclips was also videotaped and transcribed, and I conducted several rounds of inductive coding of the transcript and memoed about teachers’ meaning-making around their responsibilities, what they per ceived as tensions or contradictions, and their rationales. Considering relational ethics as a frame for this dilemma attuned me to the relationships and futures that could be made possib le—or that teachers predicted would be made possible —by the different ways that teachers suggested that Roberto could participate when Franck assigned small group work in class. ## Discussion

This paper considers the possibilities that relatio nal ethics offer for examining teachers’ ethical le arning, or how they participate in, produce, transform, and ethica l practices. By re-centering whose perspectives count in determining what is ethical, broadening who can be treated as ethical, and expanding possibilities for what is considered ethical, relational ethics offer insight that may otherwise be overlooked into how relational futures are built through interactions among teachers, students, and mathematics. Relational ethics does not necessarily take a stance on whether Proposal 1 or Proposal 6 is “better” for Roberto or which proposal might be the “more ethical” choice for Franck to make as a teacher. It is worth noting, however, that a straightforward pedagogical or political approach to this dilemma might suggest prioritizing  Roberto’s learning of mathematics and his status a s a mathematics learner as steps toward equity —toward equitable access to mathematics, or equitable participation in mathematics —over his membership in community. Therefore, using relational ethics has the potent ial to expand teachers’ understanding of what is at stake in ethical dilemmas. Furthermore, it offers radical hope by encouraging the excavation of humanizing ethical potentialities  that already exist, and that teachers are already discussing, despite the ethically-compromising conditions of working in hegemonic systems of schooling.
