# The Role of Epistemic Vexations in Elementary Preservice Teachers’ Sense-Making about Responsive Science Teaching

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Preservice science teachers (PSTs) often experience vexation when ideas and practices do not fit with their current ways of thinking, as this lack of fit is accompanied by emotions such as confusion or frustration. This study focuses on PSTs’ vexations that emerged as PSTs were positioned as sense-makers during scientific explorations in the science method class. This cross-case study revealed that PSTs experienced epistemic and pedagogical vexations as they engaged in scientific explorations supported by responsive teaching strategies. The PSTs’ experience of epistemic vexations was a precursor for resolving pedagogical vexations and was necessary for their learning and feeling of accomplishment in resolving vexations. This discovery of their resources and science capabilities motivated them to embrace responsive pedagogies for future teaching. This study also speaks to PSTs’ perception of power dynamics in the schooling system, and these perceptions shaped their embrace of responsive pedagogies for their future teaching.

## Introduction

The current vision of science learning relies on dr awing on students’ ideas and experiences to support  them in wrestling with why and how questions to figure out some aspect of the natural world (NGSS, NGSS Lead States, 2013). This approach to learning is based on the re cognition that students come to classrooms with resources (meaning their prior knowledge and experiences) tha t can be useful in this sensemaking process. If students are to be active agents in this sensemaking, it is impo rtant to leverage these resources in that sensemaki ng (Engle & Conant, 2002). Given this, science learning environments should be designed to create opportunities for students to speak up and make connections between their ideas and the scientific concepts they encounter in the classroom. Responsive teaching is an approach to science instr uction that foregrounds, takes up, and builds from students’ resources as a cornerstone of instruction (Robertson et al., 2016; Robertson & Richards, 2017). It also promot es the inclusion of a wide range of ideas and emphasizes the need for students to take ownership of their learning as they draw on their resources to build explanations during sensemaking (Engle & Conant, 2002; Hammer et al., 2012).

One of the ways to fos ter PSTs’ consideration of responsive teaching practices invo lves modeling the responsive stance that the PSTs are encouraged to enact  (e.g., Jaber, 2021; Robertson & Atkins Elliot, 2020; Robertson & Richards, 2017). This modeling positions PSTs as sense makers and provides them sp ace and time to explore, recognize their struggles, and build explanations  for themselves and peers-ultimately creating an environment where classroom members  collectively make sense. Research into responsive teacher education suggests that PSTs can learn to draw on their resources and capabiliti es for doing science, providing them the opportunity to feel accomplishment (Jaber, 2021). This experience can support PSTs in embracing such struggles as a part of learning and allow them to explore how  they can fit student-centered pe dagogies in their context (Allen & Heredia, 2021).

A growing body of research suggests that PSTs often need help understanding  and enacting studentcentered pedagogies. These struggles to understand  are referred to as vexations (Jaber, 2021; Radoff, 2017; Robertson & Atkins Elliot, 2020). When the notion of vexations is applied to understanding teacher learning from an asset-based perspective, research suggests that these vexations, instead of being something to avoid, can serve as critical resources for sensemaking, as they may trigger or sustain sensemaking (Gray et al., 2021; Odden & Russ, 2017; Radoff, 2017; Robertson & Atkins Elliot, 2020). Whi le much of this vexation research has emerged in secondary and post-secondary science classrooms, more needs to be  known about the vexations experienced by elementary PSTs.  Thus, the question we explored in th is research is: What is the interplay between the epistemic and pedagogical vexations that emerged for elementary preservice teachers in a science method course?

## Theoretical framework

This research is grounded in lenses of sensemaking and vexation together and draws on Odden and Russ’s (2018) sensemaking model. They describe sensemaking as “a dynamic process of building or revising an explanat ion to ‘figure things out ’ – to ascertain the mechanism underlying a phenomenon to resolve a gap or inconsistency in one’s understanding ” (p. 191-192). Sensemaking is understood as both a cognitive process and a discourse practice (Odden & Russ, 2018).  Sensemaking occurs in a classroom when learners communicate their resources and explanations, iteratively build explanations, and refine them individually or collectively. Vexations emerge as a part of students’ broader sensemaking efforts.  They emerge  when something does not  fit their thinking, causing the PSTs to raise questions or work to draw  on their resources, incorporating past experiences  or knowledge in their explanations (Odden & Russ, 2017, 2019; Robertson & Richards, 2017). Many scholars (e.g., Spillane et al.,  2002; Jaber, 2021) speak to the need to frame vexations as worthy of exploration and to see them as leverage for teachers’ sensemaking about new pedagogies. In this work, we use this framework as we examine PSTs’ sensemaking effo rts in a science methods course to explore the vexa tions that emerged in these efforts and to document how these vexations interacted to shape their learning..

## Methods

The data were drawn from a science methods course i n an elementary education degree program in which elementary PSTs engaged in a series of scientific investigations, studies of video cases of elementary students’ grappling to understand natural phenomena, and activities centered on responsive teaching pedagogies. The instructor approached the science method class by employing responsive moves such as eliciting PSTs’ ideas as they worked together to create explanations of natural phenomen a; that is, they were engaged in sensemaking about phenomena. As they worked to develop and refine explanations, the course instructor set norms for building PSTs’ explanations by using their language (rather than textbook definitions) and leaving PSTs with  lingering vexations between classes (~2 days) to provide them space to wrestle with their emerging ideas. Thus, vexations played a role in the more extensive sensemaking process, as vexations were understood t o motivate and propel students’ sensemaking efforts. By scrutinizing PSTs’ sensemaking during the course, we identified the vexations PSTs experienced and examined their influence on other aspects of their learning in the course.

## Participants

Using a cross-case study approach, we focused on the experiences of three PSTs, Harper (a black woman), Oakley (a white woman), and Yasmin (a Latina woman) (Pseudonyms). The three participants were selected for this study as they openly shared their views, questions, and feelings, making them particularly informative participants. The gestures (e.g., facial and body) of these participants were particularly animated, providing cues to their thinking. Data and analysis Data were collected during 15 weeks of the semester, including classroom recordings, classroom artifacts  (e.g., discussion board posts and exit tickets), and PST  interviews. To explore the interplay between epistemic and pedagogical vexations, we examined all the data sources to search for data  indicating the PSTs were engaged in sensemaking (i.e., the active attempt to answer unresolved questions).  Within these sensemaking data, we coded for specific moments when the PSTs problematized something that did not make sense to them (i.e., raised a question,  expressed uncertainty and ambiguity) and displayed affect (i.e., confusion, frustration, discomfort, or excitement) in their efforts (Allen & Penuel, 2015; Jaber, 2021; Radoff et al., 2019; Robertson & Atkins Elliott, 2020; Robertson & Richards, 2017).  After identifying  PSTs’ vexations, we searched for  data related to those vexations across all data sources. For example, when it came to developing an explanation why a particular change was an example of a chemical reaction, Yasmin was frustrated and expressed her vexation  as follows: “Oh my gosh, like I feel like I know this, but I just don't know how to explain it, or I don't know how to explain it without using scientific terms.” The instructor recognized her vexation and encouraged her to build her explanation. We then searched the discussion board data for the discussion of her epistemic vexation and noted how she connected this effort to her thinking and that of her student s (“I am finding how effective it is for me to first ex plain my thinking or share my ideas before I am given the right answer,” “I feel much better about my ability to guide student thinking without giving them the answer.”)

## Findings

The findings of this study  affirm the value of vexations  in sensemaking and  speak to the power of making vexations a central substance of responsive science teaching.  We found that the PSTs experienced two different but connected kinds of vexations: epistemic and pedagogical. Epistemic vexations are well aligned with Radoff’s

(2017) description that students “grappling with disciplinary uncertainty displayed a particular form of epistemic

effect that can signal discomfort” (p.36). The PSTs’ epistemic vexations (e.g., the relationship between heat and pressure in closed systems, factors influencing the speed of falling objects, etc.) and their experience working through these vexations paved the way for a second type of vexation, pedagogical vexations (e.g. when to address students' “wrong” ideas, when and how to introduce academic vocabulary, etc.). These vexations often emerged as the PSTs considered implementing responsive teaching in the ir classroom. Similar to epistemic vexations, pedagogical vexations speak to the PSTs’ discomfort and skepticism. Here, though, the target of this discomfort and skepticism is not their understanding of science but their understanding of responsive science teaching. Across three participants, Harper and Yasmin’s sensemaking process was different from Oakley's. With the support of the responsive pedagogies of the ins tructor, Harper, and Yasmin were found to recognize  their epistemic vexations (e.g., what makes up density, how does one know if a reaction is chemical or phys ical) and to strive to seek relevant, everyday examples to help make sense of the phenomena. Propelled by their vexations, Harper and Yasmin engaged themselves in resolving those vexations by finding everyday connections. Thus, their efforts to resolve their vexations prompted their broader sensemaking. During these explorations, they realized they were given the space and time in the class to articulate their vexations and discuss their ideas using their familiar everyday language.  They recognized that this pattern differed greatly from their past science learning experiences, which were starkly different. This juxtaposition of their past and current science learning allowed them to realize the affordances of responsive teaching.  As they experienced a sense of accomplishment in their science sensemaking in the course, they became willing to learn more about why and how to be responsive to their students’  resources. This, in turn, surfaced  some pedagogical vexations (e.g., how to deal with students' contradictory ideas in discussion and how to identify productive ideas to lead responsive discussion). But, as they worked to resolve their epistemic vexations as a part of their sensemaking in science, their pedagogical vexations faded, decreasing in importance. This occurred when they realized that their students could successfully call on their resources for sensemaking, similar to their experiences in science method class. In contrast, our third participant, Oakley, avoided working through her epistemic vexations by herself; instead, she sought answers to her questions from the instructor (e.g., “Is weight volume?”, “So, does adding salt make pasta boil faster?”). Oakley was fixed on obtaining the “right answer” instead of co nstructing a productive explanation. This fixation hindered her realization of the utility of her resources, blocking her from experiencing a feeling of accomplishment in her learning. Thus, Oakley did not find teaching science in a responsive  manner effective. Her pedagogical vexations  became more pronounced and remained unresolved, as she explained that she “might not have enough time to teach responsively,” and she failed to understand how pushing students t o construct an explanation and tailoring instruction based on students’ emerging ideas  could be productive in an educational system where testing is prioritized. In this way, we can understand Oakley to be compell ed by what she perceives to be a n authority (the school norms around science) as pr oviding the “right” answer instead of centralizing students' ideas to teach science.

## Discussion and conclusion

We sought to document the interplay of epistemic and pedagogical vexations in  PST learning about responsive teaching. Our findings suggest that when PSTs were positioned as sense makers in science, they discovered their resources and experienced a feeling of accomplishment about t heir efforts, as seen in Harper and Yasmin’s case. Their science learning experience allowed them to make sense of this kind of pedagogical opportunit y (Allen & Penuel, 2015).  However, w hen PSTs rejected this positioning of themselves as sense  makers, showing a prioritization of a “right answer” from an authorit y over ideas constructed by herself and her peers, as we saw in Oakley’s case, this prevented them from seeing the utility of supporting students in drawing on their resources to construct an explanation. This blocked the experience of accomplishment in their sensemaking and the particular joy and excitement that come  from a successful resolution of their vexations. Perhaps because of this omission, Oakley failed to recognize the utility of responsive teaching in supporting her students’ learning.  Thus, the lack of experience working through her epistemic vexation precluded the resolution of her pedagogical vexations about responsive teaching.

Responsive teaching is a powerful tool for creating an inclusive and just science lea rning environment, privileging students’ diverse ideas and fostering students’ agency in their learning. As illustrated in this st udy, however, students ’ identities and educational histories shape how they  react to this instruction. Harper and Yasmin, two women of color who recognized that they had b een underserved in their past science learning experiences, took up agency in their learning and considered res ponsive pedagogies in their future. In contrast, Oakley, a white student who experienced success in traditional education patterns, resisted taking up messages of responsive teaching and defended her traditional and systematically endorsed views of sc ience teaching (Bailey, 2017). This difference across the cases and Oakley’s resistance to wrestling through epistemic vexations in class made the influence of privileged identity visible. Being favored by and familiar with the school system from a place of privilege, Oakley’s views on science teaching and learning w ere challenged in the methods course. Similar to findings by Robertson and Atkins-Elliot (2020), Oakley did not take up messages of responsive teaching because she thought that responsive teaching put “learning right ideas” and “students’ success” at risk (p. 745). Her past schooling experiences supported her in developing a view that equates success in standardized tests with student learning, something that caused her to persist in traditional forms of instruction -- delivering correct answers immediately and positioning teachers as knowledge authorities. Because she did not find the responsive instructional moves employed in the class useful for her or her learning, she remained hesitant to consider them as a teacher (Robertson &Atkins-Elliot, 2020; Robertson & Richards, 2017).
