# The Affective Life of Whiteness: Researcher Reflections on Professional Learning Interactions

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper examines the racialized dynamics of teacher learning by attending to moments of tension, hesitation, and affective response in professional learning conversations in which race is explicitly named. Whiteness shapes the discursive and emotional terrain of teacher learning spaces – what feels safe, risky, or sayable. Whiteness exists at every level of conversation – often without being explicitly named, and is maintained through everyday interactions. We analyze moments where racial discourse faltered, shifted or was redirected, revealing how whiteness at work operates affectively, particularly through avoidance and discomfort. Our analysis positions research affect and identity as analytical tools, treating reflexivity as central to understanding the construction of whiteness at work in professional learning contexts. Objectives Research in mathematics education has documented how the dominant culture of school mathematics is structured by exclusion and racialized power (Louie, 2017) and functions as a white institutional space that privileges particular ways of knowing and being (Battey & Leyva, 2016). This culture is reified through hierarchical constructions of mathematical ability and through valuing objectivity, abstraction, and efficiency over personal, affective, or meaning‑making dimensions of learning. Classroom practices and discourses routinely (re)produce marginalization, constraining students’ opportunities to engage meaningfully with mathematics and to develop positive mathematical identities (Louie, 2017). Theoretical perspectives Whiteness at work We aim to understand whiteness interactionally and affectively in our critical exploration of instructional coaching interactions with experienced mathematics teachers. Whiteness functions as a social and racial position from which to make sense of the world, shaping both discourse and perception (e.g., Leonardo, 2009). Whiteness exists at every level of conversation and is maintained through everyday interactions. Yoon (2012), for example, presents a micro-interactional analysis of how whiteness was “at wo rk” in routine interactions among teachers, documenting discursive strategies, such as de-racialization, redirection, and the centering of white comfort that shaped teachers’ beliefs, intentions, and actions. This kind of racial positioning is often sustai ned through race evasiveness. Bonilla-Silva (2006) identifies four frames of race evasiveness that contribute to the invisibility of witness and the abdication of responsibility for racial inequality. Whiteness also shapes the affective dynamics of conversation (Matias, 2016), structuring who feels entitled to speak, who (and what ideas) are considered legitimate, and how emotional tension is distributed, absorbed, or deflected. Dilemmatic space We define dilemmas as “conflict-filled situations that require choices because competing, highly prized values cannot be fully satisfied” (Cuban, 1992, p. 6). Suh et al. (2023) similarly emphasize that dilemmas arise from the coexistence of conflicting commitments and values in justice-oriented educational work. For researchers engaged in conversations with teachers, these dilemmas emerge at an intersection of competing values: maintaining rapport versus pushing for more critical analysis and honoring participants’ perspectives versus challenging problematic framings. We conceptualize researchers’ positioning within conversations with teachers as research dilemmatic spaces. Researcher dilemmatic spaces are social constructions resulting from both the structu ral conditions and relational dynamics within researcher-participant interactions, where dilemmas around positionality and power emerge (Fransson & Grannäs, 2013). Specifically for our analysis, we explore our roles in the co-(de)construction of whiteness at work within professional learning contexts, drawing upon our affective experiences as a resource for understanding these dynamics. The role of affect and emotion 1847 We conceptualize affect not as an individual emotion or reaction, but as a collective, pre-personal (or nonconscious) force that moves through and shapes interactions, discourse, and possibility (Deleuze & Guattari, 1987; Massumi, 2002). Affect circulates across bodies, objects, and discourse, shaping what becomes sayable, doable, or thinkable in a given moment. In our analysis, we treat affective experiences (e.g., hesitation, urgency, discomfort) as meaningful indicators of where whiteness was at work, and as openings for understanding the ethical and political terrain of professional learning. We understand these affective moments as sites where whiteness is enacted, negotiated, and sometimes unsettled (Zembylas, 2025).

We draw from a multi-year research-practice partnership between university researchers, teachers, and a professional development organization in a large urban school district in the Western United States. This partnership focuses on learning of experienced  secondary mathematics teachers in urban schools as they work toward enacting equitable and ambitious mathematics instruction. We worked with 12 experienced high school mathematics teachers using a video-based coaching cycle and longitudinal interviews to support reflection. Of the 12 teachers, we focused our analysis on interactions with two focal teachers: one in which race was explicitly named and discussed, and another in which race was notably absent despite its potential relevance to the conversation (summarized in Table 1). We drew on critical and interpretative interaction analysis (Philip & Sengupta, 2021) to examine these moments, attending to both discursive content and affective dynamics. In both episodes, we carried with us prior experiences of unresolved racial discourse that shaped how we entered subsequent interactions. With Clark, earlier conversations had circled around racial dynamics without naming them directly, which eventually led to a more intentional effort to name race. By contrast, racialized exclusion was visible in video shared during Sofia’s debrief but remained unaddressed at the time and was only taken up in a subsequent conversation.

Clark: From hesitation to felt responsibility As a research assistant, I (Karen) partnered with Clark and his two colleagues. Over two years of data collection, I spent time in Clark’s classroom observing and facilitating instructional coaching sessions. After data collection ended, Clark and I contin ued informal Zoom conversations to discuss ongoing questions he was grappling with. As a biracial East Asian/Caucasian researcher, I felt the complexity of engaging with an older white male AP Calculus teacher and reflected on how my intersecting identitie s positioned me in relation to his authority. This episode took place during an informal Zoom conversation where we discussed Bartell’s (2011) article on care, race, and power. In earlier conversations, Clark often critiqued structural harms in schooling b ut tended to speak in color-blind terms, rarely naming race explicitly. Elizabeth and I chose this article to open space for deeper engagement with race, culture, and power.

When initially asked about the article, Clark lamented that, “school math can be oppressive and maybe it’s even designed to be oppressive” which spoke to “probably the political aspect of the care.” I felt a sense of agitation and discomfort. This pattern gnaws at me in my own life: the way I catch myself circling a thing, orbiting it with careful language instead of landing directly, naming the thing the thing. Sitting there, listening to Clark, I felt that same frustration turned outward. I wanted to hear him name his students: their races, their cultures, the 1848 day-to-day experiences that informed his perspective. Instead, we stayed suspended in theory. Please say what you actually mean. Elizabeth pressed Clark for clarification: “are things that are really specific to you and to your kids, things that you might say in this hypothetical talk?” However, Clark continued to reflect in non-specific ways, circling the idea that math could be oppressive without naming who was impacted or how oppression might occur along racial lines.

A moment of moral action As the conversation continued,  my hands moved frantically across the keyboard, firing off message after message to Elizabeth in the Zoom chat. How do I show Clark I’m listening (really listening!) while simultaneously signaling to Elizabeth that something different needs to happen? The messages piled up, one urgent line after another. We were both sensing it: Clark was talking about race without naming it. The persistent silence around race was becoming increasingly uncomfortable for me. How long could we let this continue? The silence had become its own presence in the conversation.

At this moment, I felt pulled to intervene. Normally, I try to build and connect to what teachers are saying but in this moment, I felt a moral imperative to build on Clark’s thoughts, but also try to take the conversation in a different direction and resi st deracialized conversation (Yoon, 2012). I found a lull to speak: “No, it actually makes a ton of sense to me. I feel like a lot of the things you're speaking to really resonate in the care piece when they're thinking about particularly race.” I referenced my own experience as a third-grade teacher and asked, “How have you guys thought about that?” In this turn, I named what I thought he was circling, drawing on my experience to open space for and share the emotional labor of naming race. The pause: Affective shifts and ruptures Clark paused for ten seconds – on zoom this feels like an eternity. This silence simultaneously felt thick with both tension and possibility. The palpable shift in energy and the discursive flow of conversation immediately halted. After my question, Clark immediately put his hand to his face and paused. When Clark finally spoke, his response was marked by continued hesitation: “I mean yeah (10 seconds) I don’t know. I think (12 seconds)...” The silences stretched between us, creating space for something new to emerge. When Clark continued, something had shifted: “I don’t know the right words... I would like the words, by the way [...] I would be led to believe that somehow I have to bring in Mexican culture into my classroom, meaning routines, but also artif acts from culture.” For the first time, he named his students’ ethnicities and spoke more specifically about the tensions he was navigating. After race had been explicitly introduced to the conversation, Clark began to name things more specifically: his students were “Mexican American, predominantly Latino,” he referenced “white culture” and grappled with questions about who benefits from this “grand design of oppression.” Rather than invoking abstract liberalism (Bonilla-Silva, 2006), Clark began to name r acial and cultural dynamics, opening space for more situated pedagogical reflection.

Sofia: Racial dynamics, affect, and the limits of discomfort Sofia Fontana, a Latina woman, taught in the same district as Clark. I (Elizabeth) developed a working relationship with her through data collection, PD meetings, and classroom observations. As a white researcher, I was attuned to the power dynamics at play and how my position shaped our interactions. The analysis focuses on two interactions: a video-based debrief and a follow-up exit interview. The debrief centered on student communication and included a clip where one of the few Black girls in the class, Dee, appeared excluded. Hesitation and holding back In preparing for the debrief, I felt ethically obligated to name the racialized exclusion – in part because of my own white racial identity and my ongoing reckoning with how I have participated in and benefited from race-evasive practices in the past. I also wanted to center Dee’s experience and the harm she may have experienced as one of the only Black girls in the class (Joseph, 2021). Before watching the clip of Dee’s group, Sofia explained the group consisted of three close friends and Dee, who “doesn't really know anybody” and is "quiet." She added, “I expect Mariana and Alex working together, Jaylen contributing when he has to... and then Dee just sitting in the back. Her stuff will be complete, she just won't be – ” I interjected, “She’s working the whole time.” Sofia affirmed, but reiterated Dee’s non-participation. Sofia attributed Dee’s silence to an inherent, individual disposition rather than socially produced within the group dynamic, naturalizing racialized exclusion. After the clip, I asked for Sofia's reaction. She responded, “How can I make or have groups that don't feel fully comfortable with each other still be able to engage...?” Sofia’s framing here emphasized student comfort or social ease and shifted the focus to improving group dynamics away from Dee’s exclusion. I felt my conviction 1849 or boldness slip away. Watching the video recording again, guilt and shame gnawed at me as Dee and her experience was discursively rendered invisible.

We went on to discuss participation structures and Sofia admitted, “I think [Dee] is one that has slipped through.” However, this brief moment of recognition was not taken up. Reflecting on this conversation, hearing Sofia describe the invisibilization of Dee, and the way I gloss over that, stung. These moments were affective enactments of whiteness, sustained through interactional quieting (Yoon, 2012). Returning, reframing, and redirecting After a year, I returned to the clip in Sofia’s exit interview. With [Karen], I planned to raise the racial dynamics more directly. Sofia watched and said, “She also was struggling... not that she didn't want to engage.” She reflected, “How can I make help ing each other a norm? Because right now, it didn't even come to them to help her.” Again, her focus was structural. I tried to name race more explicitly: “I can remember similar moments... and how that exclusion... could happen along more racial and gendered lines... I’m curious what you're thinking.” Sofia didn’t take it up, and instead pivoted: “What is coming to mind is my students with IEPs...” The shift was immediate and unmistakable – a quiet, affective rejection of the racial frame I had offered. In that moment, Sofia’s pivot could be understood through Bonilla-Silva’s (2006) framework of colorblind racism, particularly minimization and naturalization. By shifting the lens to IEPs, she framed Dee’s exclusion as an individual or structural learning challenge, rather than a racially charged interactional one. This move preserved a sense of pedagogical neutrality and deflected any emotional charge that race would bring. It felt like the air had been sucked out of the room. I was stunned – not just by what was said, but by how clearly race had been evaded. I felt a sharp pause in my body, an emotional halt. In that instant, I knew she didn’t want to go there. My resolve to pursue the thread gave way to a sense of resignation. I let it go, not because I thought it wasn’t important, but because I didn’t know how to move forward in that moment without pushing past a boundary that had just clearly been drawn. My hesitation, my desire to preserve relational ease, and my instinct to defer: these were all expressions of whiteness at work. Whiteness was enacted and sustained through subtle interactional moves – hesitation, redirection, and deferral – that shaped what felt sayable in each conversation. At times, it was disrupted through affective rupture, where discomfort opened space for racial specificity, but these moments were fragile and contingent. This paper contributes to scholarship examining the affective and relational dimensions of racial discourse in professional learning contexts. We analyze moments where racial discourse faltered, shifted or was redirected, revealing how whiteness at work operates affectively, particularly through avoidance and discomfort. Our analysis positions research affect and identity as analytical tools, treating reflexivity as central to understanding the construction of whiteness at work in professional learning contexts. As researchers, we found ourselves deeply interwoven in these dynamics: our feelings, silences, discursive moves and positionalities shaped what was possible in each conversation.

By foregrounding researcher dilemmatic spaces where competing structural forces and relational dynamics collide, we ultimately argue that as researchers, we need to be attuned to the complex, affective terrain in which whiteness at work is perpetuated or contested. We challenge the field to consider not just whether race is named, but how, when, by whom, and how our affective experiences can act as valuable resources for the way we engage with teachers. We push back against simple solutions like ‘just be more explicit about race.’ Our analysis invites others to consider not just whether race is named, but how, when, and by whom – and with what affective consequences.
