# From Knowledge Transfer to Thinking Design: A Practical Study on the Optimization of Problem-Driven Teaching Pointing to the Cultivation of Scientific Inquiry Abilities

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
The development of scientific inquiry abilities is not only essential for students to thrive but also a fundamental skill that drives innovation and discovery in science. This study proposes a framework grounded in design thinking to optimize problem-driven teaching and develop scientific inquiry abilities. The framework has four key features: project-based, subproblem linkage, multi-context, and practice-focused. A quasi-experimental study in a high school science classroom evaluated its effectiveness. Preliminary findings indicate that it successfully enhances students’ scientific inquiry abilities, particularly in formulating questions, generating hypotheses, designing experiments, and drawing conclusions. Additionally, the framework significantly boosts students’ motivation and interest in learning, further supporting the improvement of their scientific inquiry abilities.

## Introduction

Inquiry is the true essence of learning science and  the primary way scientists discover and innovate  (Kuo et al., 2015). Research has shown that scientific inquiry not only promotes deeper understanding and better application of scientific knowledge, but also has a positive impact on a variety of aspects, including technical skills, problemsolving skills, critical thinking, and self-efficacy (Frederiksen & White, 1998; Liu & Wang, 2022; Schlatter et al., 2020; Sutiani, 2021; Wu et al., 2021). Furthermore, scientific inquiry is crucial for making decisions about health and wellbeing at personal, social, and ecological l evels (Oliver et al.,2021). Meanwhile, w ith the rapid development of the new generation of technologi es represented by the Internet of Things, cloud comput ing, chatbots and other technologies, digital informatio n and knowledge have become key factors of p roduction, convergence of innovation and technolog y have become the key driving force for development,  and real-world problems and challenges are becoming increasingly complex and profound (Zhu & Dai, 2024). These changes are pushing education into a new era of digital transformation and increasing th e demands on  competence development. In this context, the development of scientific inqu iry abilities is not only essential for students to thrive but alsonecessary to drive innovation and discovery in science (Schwartz et al., 2021). At the K12 level, scientific inquiry abilit ies can be developed through science teaching.  For example, Jenice et al. (2013) designed inquiry-based teaching activities using task strings to encourage students to actively search for evidence and reason correctly in theory classes. Yanto et al. (2019) applied a three-tier inquiry model (structured, teacher-guided, and free inquiry) to theory teaching.  Killpack et al. (2020) integrated instructor-led scaffolding and inquiry activities into lab classes. Reiser et al. (2021) propose ‘the storyline units’ instructional model. Gillies and Rafter (2020) designed lessons based on the 5E instructional model around unit core concepts, embedding teacher explanations and student experiments. However, the development of scientific inquiry abilities varies with the teaching mode, often leading to sup erficial understanding of inquiry-generated knowledge, superficial application of inquiry practice, and un even development across ability dimensions (Gillies  & Rafter, 2020; Killpack et al., 2020; Suwono et al., 2023).  Therefore, exploring a more effective teaching meth od is urgently needed to address these challenges and enhance scientific inquiry ability development. Based on design thinking, this study proposes a fra mework to optimize problem-driven teaching. It integrates theoretical teaching with experimental i nquiry, teaching students both how to think and how  to apply knowledge to enhance scientific inquiry abilit ies. To investigate its effect, we conducted a quasi-experiment in a high school science classroom.

## Conceptual framework

Thinking is the intrinsic driver of learning behavi or. Learning that lacks thinking activities merely amounts to low-order and repetitive training. The activation of thinking primarily depends on the  problems and tasks set by teachers. Learners conduct investigations based on these tasks, thereby achieving synergistic enhancem ent of inquiry thinking and practice. Based on this idea, this study introduces the conce pt of design thinking (Goldman & Kabayadondo,2016; Pusca & Northwood, 2018)  and constructs the framework for optimizing the practice of problem-driven teaching as shown in Figure 1.  The concept focuses on the design of the thinking p rocess rather than the knowledge itself, guiding students from se lf-discovery of the “what” to the proper exploration o f the “why”, and then to the active formulation of “how t o solve”, leading to high-quality solutions. Specifically, the teacher guides students to deconstruct the complex core issue in class, identifying the requisite prior knowledge and the new knowledge to be learned. And then they formulate subproblems and explore solutions together. When the initial solution fails to correctly answer the subproblem, students experience cognitive conflict.  The teacher then accurately analyzes the subproblem, teaches new knowledge, and guides students to per ceive the new information. Students process their thinking, re-formulate the solution, and verify it through factual materials and inquiry activities. Once validation is complete, th e subproblem is resolved, and teachers and students further analyze and solve new subproblems, ultimately aiming to solve the complex core issue. Based on the concept of design thinking, we propose a framework for optimizing the practice of problemdriven teaching to cultivate scientific inquiry abi lities in science subjects  (Figure 1). The framework integrates theoretical teaching with inquiry activities to facilitate a more flexib le inquiry process. It comprises two main components: pre-course preparation and teaching practice.  In the pre-course preparation stage, teachers first strengthen the logical connections between knowledg e, construct a conceptual network for the lessons’ content, outline the main concepts and sub-concepts. Next, they create real-world contexts closely related to the conceptual network. Finally, they formulate a complex core issue based on the conceptual network and the selected context. In the teaching practice stage, teachers start with theoretical teaching, introduces the core issue with a real context, and guide students to deconstruct the issue. Subsequently, teacher s construct the teaching logic of “raising a subproblem-learning new knowledge-solving the subproblem-raising a new subproblem”, guide students to carry out practical inquiry tasks from a practical perspective, progressively expand their knowledge base, and internalize their understanding in the solution of the subproblem chain, ultimately forming a solution pointing to the core issue. Unlike Reiser et al.  (2021), our framework aims to balance student autonomy with disciplinary systematicity by preemptively disassembling the cor e issue into a logical chai n of subproblems and embedding disciplinary content (e.g., scientific concepts and experimental methods) into the problem-solving process. This method reduces implementation difficulty for teachers whil e ensuring the integrity of the knowledge system, making it particularly suitable for teaching scenar ios that require coverage of fixed curriculum stand ards. In contrast, their study emphasizes “knowledge  on demand” and may be more suitable for open-ended inquiry scenarios.

Figure 1 The Framework for Optimizing the Practice of Problem-Driven Teaching The framework has four main features:  project-based, subproblem linkage, multi-context, and practicefocused. “Project-based” means that the design of teaching is based on a sys tematic project with a core issue. “Subproblem linkage” means that the solution to the core issue depends on logical and practical subproblems. “Multi-context” means that the design o f subproblems should be based  on the core problem context and incorporate multiple contexts that promote students ’ connections and understanding.  “Practice-focused” means that the ultimate goal is to guide student-oriented practice and promote students ’ deep understanding of new knowledge.

Guided by the framework, we conducted a quasi-experiment in a real classroom setting. We assessed the students’ scientific inquiry abilities using the scientific i nquiry ability questionnaire before and after the intervention. The pre-test and post-test data were analyzed to evaluate the improvement in students' scientific inquiry abilities, thereby assessing the framework’s practical effectiveness.

## Participants

In this study, two equal level parallel classes of students in the first grade of F high school in S city were selected as the research subjects of the quasi-experimental study. There were 31 students in the e xperimental group and 35 students in the control group. The mean scores o f the two groups ’ science course grades on the most recent large-scale test were 73.1 and 73.6 (out of a total of 100 points), respectively, and were not significantly different (p>0.05).

## Procedure

The module “Molecular Composition of Cells” from the Huke edition of the Chinese high school biology textbook was selected as the learning material. It covers th e properties and identification of proteins, sugars, and lipids. Based on this learning material, both the experimental and control groups underwent 2 lessons of theoretical instruction and 1 lesson of laboratory experimentat ion (as shown in Figure 2). Both groups were taught by the same teacher. The control group was taught using the regular lecture mode. The relevant knowledge was divided into theory and experiment lessons, which were comp leted in stages. Finally, students were guided to complete the validation experiment to identify whether a sub stance belongs to proteins, sugars, or fats.  The experimental group was taught using the framework for optimizing the practice of problem-driven teaching. In the preparation stage, the teacher identified the core issue “Are sugar-free beverages really healthy?”, which was centered on the relevant knowledge. During the teaching process, the teacher supported the deconstruction of the core issue with a chain of sub problems, such as “What are common nutrients?”, “Are sugar-free beverages really sugar-free?” and “How can I test for sugars in beverages?”, so that learners can learn the properties of common nutrients in the process of solving the above chained subproblems, a nd then understand the principles of their identifi cation. Finally, based on the deconstruction of the core issue and newly acquired knowledge, students collaboratively designed and validated experimental schemes targeti ng the core issue  through group discussions and inquiry experiments. At the end of the lessons, the teacher facilitated a review of the complex issue-solving process and encouraged students to reflect on and share their experiences and insights gained from the scientific inquiry. ## Data collection and analysis

This study u sed the scientific inquiry ability questionnaire for both pre-test and post-test to collect quantitative data. The questionnaire included four scales: the scientific inquiry ability scale, the learning motivation scale, the learning i nterest scale, and  the learning e ngagement scale. Each scale was rated using a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). The scientific inquiry ability scale was adapted from the scientific inquiry ability a ssessment scale developed by  Feng (2023). The scale consist ed of seven dimensions: asking questions, making hypot heses, designing experiments, implementing protocols, anal yzing data, drawing conclusions, and reflecting and communicating, with a total of 21 questions. It demonstrated good reliability, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.72. The learning motivation scale was adapted from the learning motivation scale developed by Hwang et al. (2013), with a total of 4 questions. It demonstrated good reliability, with a Cronbach’s alpha value of 0.76. The learning engagement scale was adapted from the learning engagement scale developed by Fredricks et al. (2004) and consists of 4 questions. It demonstrated good reliability with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.73. The learning interest scale was adapted from the science learning interest scale developed by Li and Shi (2024), with a total of 3 questions. And it demonstrated good reliability, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.87. At the same time, we conducted brief interviews with students in the experimental group about teaching effectiveness and learning experience. The qualitative data collected were then organized and used pri marily to provide additional clarification for the quantitative data.

## Results

Before the experiment,  we administered a pre-test using the scientific inquiry ability questionn aire to both the experimental and control groups and conducted an in dependent-samples t-test using SPSS 26.  The results indicated no significant differences between the tw o groups in terms of scientific inquiry abilit ies, learning motivation, learning engagement, and learning interest. After the experiment, we administered a post-test using the same questionnaire to both groups and performed another independent-samples t-test on the post-test data.  The results, shown in Table 1, revealed significant differences in scientific inquiry abili ties between the groups, with the experimental group outperforming the control group (p <.05). Specifically, the two groups showed significant differences in the four dimensions of question posing, hypothesis making, experiment designing and conclusion drawing. The largest difference was in conclusion drawing (Δexp -con = 0.58), followed by hypothesis making (Δexp -con = 0.50), experiment designing (Δexp -con = 0.50), and question posing (Δexp -con = 0.43).  However, no significant differences were found in plan conducting, data analyzing, and communicating. Regarding non-cognitive factors, the results showed no significant difference between the two groups in learning engagement, while significant differences were observed in learning motivation and learning interest. ## Discussion

Based on design thinking, this study proposes a pra ctical framework for problem-driven teaching  to cultivate scientific inquiry abilit ies in high school science classrooms. Preliminary results indicate that the framework effectively enhances students’ scientific inquiry abilities especially in questioning, hypothesizing, experimenting, and concluding. Students in the experimental group gradually mastered the correct way of scientific in quiry and learned to deconstruct the core problem  from the shallowest to the deepest level, accurately match required knowledge, form reasonable hypotheses, and design feasible experiments. With the course progressing, the logical chain of subproblems continuously strengthened their investigative thinking, enabling  them to analyze, reason, and deduce solutions more effectively. As one student remarked, “I can now analyze complex pro blems and propose solutions more easily under the chain.” Meanwhile, the analysis of non-cognitive factors indicates that the framework effe ctively enhances students’ learning motivation and interest. By making abstract knowledge relevant through real-world contexts, encouraging active participation instead of passive listening and replacing confirmatory experiments with inquirybased experiments, the framework highlights the characteristics of the  science curriculum. It not only stimulates the learning motivation and interest of students in  the experimental group but also encourages their a ctive engagement in scientific inquiry, which contributes to the development of scientific inquiry abilities.  As one student remarked in an interview, “Problem-driven teaching makes science class more engaging a nd reduces my reluctance to participate in solving complex issues.” However, this study has limitations, including a small sample size and a short-term teaching intervention, which introduce instability in the effect on scientific inquiry abilities. Future studies will address these limitations by increasing the sample size, extending the intervention term, and incorporating more diverse evaluation methods to explore the long-term impact of the framework for optimizing the practice of problem-driven teaching.
