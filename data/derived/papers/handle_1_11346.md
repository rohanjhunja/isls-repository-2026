# Nest-building as World-building: Exploring Multispecies Sensemaking and Aesthetic Practices Among Two Youth Participating in a STEAM Summer Program

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study explores multispecies sensemaking and aesthetic practices of youth participating in a STEAM summer program. The program focused on ecological concepts and cartographic arts and was implemented in a rural, Latina/o/x agricultural community in the Inland Northwest. We share our early insights by presenting a case of multispecies sensemaking by two youth, Rafa and Arturo, as they build a nest for pet rocks they created. In this activity, designed to engage youth in resource ecology, the participants created pet rocks using paint markers and then used GoPro cameras to document finding food, water, and shelter for their rocks. Our video-based analysis traces Rafa and Arturo constructing a nest, with a focus on the emerging themes of seeking, friendship, and care-taking. The analysis aims to disrupt dominant solutionist narratives around crisis by centering the subtleties, resistances, and resiliencies embedded in youth’s everyday engagement with the more-than-humanduring times of precarity.

## Introduction

The climate crisis and its effects, from economic p erilousness to social unrest, is one of the largest  challenges of our time (UN, 2021, 2023). Human-driven land use is responsible for a 70-percent decrease in global animal populations and has displaced indigenous peoples from ancestral lands and compromised their relationships with the more-than-human (Bronen et al., 2018). Continued forest fragm entation has increased the frequency of zoonotic pathogens and multispecies infectious dise ases. These effects are often treated as problems t hat can be solved by scientific and technological innovations (Steffen et al., 2015), distilling the complexity o f crisis into “one-way solutionism” steeped in uniform narratives of w hiteness that ignore “wider social relation s…and interdependence” (Milan, 2020, p. 5; Morozov, 2013). In STEAM education, this dominant perspective, developed and cemented over time, results in missin g the complexities of crisis and the sensemaking yo uth practice everyday as they live in precarity.  However, there are varying ways of living in crisis  that can complement, contradict, or complicate the dominant narrative. In an effort to expand STEAM education beyond techn o-solutionism, in this study, we explore the multispecies sensemaking and aesthetic practices of  youth participating in a STEAM summer program. The program focused on ecological concepts and the cart ographic arts and was implemented in a rural, Latina/o/x agricultural community in the Inland Northwest. We share our early insights from this program by analyzing how two youth collaboratively build a habitat (a nest) for painted rock creatures they created during the program, attending to the rich multispecies sensemaking and aesthetic practices these youth engage in. Living and making in crisis: Multispecies sensemaking and aesthetic practices Everyday understandings of multispecies assemblages  in times of crisis can help youth and larger socie ty make sense of change (Kelly et al., 2021). We understand  multispecies sensemaking  as the everyday practice of interacting with and giving meaning to our webs of interconnection with the more-than-human. Multispecies sensemaking includes feeling, moving, and engaging with the social, cultural, and material dimensions of human and more-than-human interactions (Saba Fisher, Kelton et al., 2024). It is a pervasive aspect of everyday life, such as taking care of pets or deciding whether to put on bug spray. Multispecies sensemaking candevelop individually or collectively and can include aff ective dimensions such as feeling sadness when the neighborhood trees are being cut down to make room for power lines or appr eciating spring wildflowers on a family walk (Learn ing in Places, 2018). Sometimes multispecies sensemaking i ncludes speculative or  imaginative dimensions, such as inventing or engaging with fictional creatures or making predictions about species adaptations to climate change. We view attending to youth’s multispecies sensemaking as a way of attuning ourselves to young people’s practices for living in and with change. We can also see what  matters to learners as they make sense of change. Making sense of multispecies connections —understanding how these connections weave into our everyday lives—can help us cope and find resiliency in times of uncert ainty. It can help us work towards future well-being for all communities, human or more-than (Bang, 2016).

In this study, we theorize multispecies sensemaking as intertwined with youth’s aesthetic practices. We understand aesthetic practices  as learners’ material, representational, and relati onal choices, as well as the emotional, somatic, and political meanings attribut ed to those choices (Saba Fisher, Pautzke et al., 2 024). We draw on scholarship that views aesthetics as a ubiquitous aspect of everyday life that is inextricable from politics (Bečevića & Dahlsted, 2022; Kelton & Nemirovsky, 2023). In attending to aesthetic practices, we aim to expand approaches to STEAM education research by moving be yond simply adding the arts to curriculum and conte nt (Halverson & Peppler, 2018). We seek to attune to the details of yout h’s everyday aesthetic choices as social practice, from deciding what color to draw a squirrel to dancing like a bee in the breakfast line at school. Viewing learning as fundam entally a social practice, situated within activity  among participants and their environment (Lave & Wenger, 1991), we theorize aesthetic practices as playing a crucial role in the learning process. We also posit that attending to youth’s everyday aesthetic practices can help learning scientists disrupt dominant solutionist narratives around crisis by centering the subtletie s, resistances, and resiliencies embedded in youth’ s everyday engagement with the more-than-humanduring times of precarity. There is d eep complexity in these daily enactments. Understanding that these practices are powerful means that we can attend to learners’ expe riences during crisis, and not just white-techno-solutionism to solve a scientific problem (Milan, 2 020; Morozov, 2013). We can learn from youth what the larger climate conversation is missing as they cope, thrive, and live in times of precarity.

## Study context: Nest-building during Cartographic Creatures summer program

We co-designed and implemented a twenty-hour curriculum —Cartographic Creatures —that integrated ecological science, animal behavior, and cartograph ic art at an affordable housing complex serving Lat ina/o/x families. These families have been disproportionately affected by the impacts of crisis, from the COVID-19 pandemic to the climat e crisis, and face systemic injustices in their schooling, mobilities  (Marrun & Rodriguez-Campo, 2023), and health (Martínez et al., 2021). They also are participating in rich sensemaking about crisis that is overlooked by dominant problem-solving narrati ves steeped in traditional perspectives of science inquiry (Medin & Bang, 2014; Oziewicz, 2022). Participants in the program i ncluded youth between the ages of 5 and

14. Research design, data collection, and data analysis are informed by grounded theory (Corbin & Strauss, 2015),

interaction analysis (Erickson, 1992; 1996; 2004; Jordan & Henderson, 1995), design-based research (Cobb et al., 2003; Collins et al., 2004), arts-based visual research (Leavy, 2017), and ethnography (Emerson et al., 2011). We collected data on youth activity as it occurred in context for three weeks in July 2024, and asked: (1) How do youth engage in multispecies sensemaking in the con text of cartographic art activities?, and (2) How d o youth develop and exhibit aesthetic practices in the cont ext of cartographic art activities? Here we examine  one video episode in which  two youth, Rafa and Arturo, build a nest for their pet rocks they created using paint markers on found rocks. The activity, designed to engage youth in STEM resource ecology concepts, involved youth creating pet rocks using paint markers and then using GoPro cameras to document their journey of finding food, water, and shelter for their creatures. Rafa and Arturo find, deconstruct, and re-build a nest for their two creatures in this data excerpt.

Emerging analysis: Seeking, friendship, and care-taking Analyses are ongoing and currently centering around  three dimensions of Rafa’s and Arturo’s multispeci es sensemaking and aesthetic practices: seeking, friendship, and care-taking. Seeking Rafa and Arturo begin the activity by searching for the resources thei r pet rocks need: food, water, and shelter. They ask what their creatures eat (e.g., Rafa asks “Do parrots eat acor ns?”) and how to find these resources for their creatures. Arturo quick ly finds acorns for food: “little acorns…little acorns…let’s get him  some acorns” (video transcript, 18 July 2024). They then come across a pile of pine cones and rocks on the ground (Figure 1) and begin to use it as a place for their pet rocks.  They start to pull apart pieces of the nest, removing pine con es and some rocks, as they make it a place for their c reatures to thrive in. Yet, as they comb their hous ing center’s playground and play field, Arturo asks, “Now where the heck are we supposed to find wa ter?” (video transcript, 18 July 2024). This remains a constant question as they modify and build the nest. They travel to other parts of the housing complex to find new rocks and materials  for their nest, but have yet to find water. Rafa a nd Arturo are making sense of what their creatures need, and how to  care for them. When hitting a problem, they contin ue to move and to build a shelter for their creatures to thrive. Aesthetically, they are building a nest (e.g., finding rocks and putting them in specific areas of the nest). They also practice resiliency through constructing and making together in the context of resource constraints.

Figure 1 Finding and Deconstructing the Nest While Videotaping Activity (line drawing of video still by an author) Friendship Aesthetic practices as relational work appears in this episode as the friendship between Rafa and Arturo. As they work together on building a nest for their pet rock s, they regularly attend to their own relationship,  including the span of complex nest construction to simply kissing  their cameras together. For example, early in the episode, Arturo redirects Rafa to help find the resources th eir creatures need. Rafa is running and shouting on  the community center playground, and rather than join in, Arturo says “We’re not supposed to play around with them [the GoPros], we’re supposed to find stuff with the m…we have to find food, water, or shelter” (video t ranscript, 18 July 2024). By redirecting Rafa to the task at hand, Arturo brings Rafa into his space and activity consensually and collaboratively. When finding resources, Arturo invites Rafa i n: “Come!” and “I mean, this can be both of our shelters” (video transcript, 18 July 2024). As the episode progresses, their friendship does, too. They become two bodies sharing one nest for their creatures, and co-making decisions about how to build and what to sha re with the outside world. For example, at one point R afa find something in the environment (not visible in video data) to share with Arturo, but whispers with  the intent of the GoPro not picking up the conversa tion, “don’t say anything, don’t say anything” (video transcript, 18 July 2024). Arturo at first loudly asks, “huh?” to this direction, but quickly remains silent. Rafa also identifies a rock from a prior interaction between the two youth and shows it to Arturo. Arturo responds “Yo! Look at this!” and Rafa asks if they should “keep it” or “ask to paint on it” (video transcript, 18 July 2024). Often, rather than show their work or  found materials, Rafa and Artuto choose to make it their own material, even turning their c ameras black with their hands and whispering to kee p their relational work private: Rafa:  Wait! Cut the scenes! Cut the scenes!

Arturo:  Why?

Rafa:  Let’s go! (video transcript, 18 July 2024) Care-taking Multispecies care-taking emerges as an aesthetic practice in this epi sode. By building a nest, the two youth are caring for their pet rocks. They are providing shel ter and a home, and carefully curate the make-up of the nest over time. What begins as a found pile of rocks and  pine cones is attentively disassembled and reconst ructed to make a nest (see Figure 2) –a world–specifically for their creatures. As they do this, they show each other their creatures, giving them names and descriptions throu gh the adjectives they use. For example, Rafa asks Arturo to show him his pet rock for the camera. As Arturo sho ws it, Rafa spends the time to turn the creature's face to the camera: Rafa:  Can I see?...Can I see it? Can I see your face [Art uro’s creature’s face]? Can I see your face? Can I see his face?...I want to see the face. Show your camera the face… Arturo:  It’s ugly, right?

Rafa:  No, it’s cute. (video transcript, 18 July 2024). Arturo calls his pet “ugly”, but Rafa counters, saying “no, it’s cute.” This care and attention to their multispecies connections allows the two youth to imagine their creatures with them, living in the future. Additionally, there is care-taking in the act of recording their experiences. Rafa aesthetically guides the audience along the journey of building–both nest and world–through moments of fun (e.g., putting googly eyes on the GoPro camera as it moves through space with him) and checking in with the au dience at moments in the episode (e.g., calling the  audience “guys”). Care-taking is a practice of both multispecies stewardsh ip for resources, like shelter, and a practice of multispecies survival through joy.

Figure 2 The Final Reconstructed Nest with Arturo’s Pet Rock Resting Inside (line drawing of video still by an author) Further explorations As youth built this nest for their pet rocks, they also built a wor ld and affective atmosphere (Anderson, 2009; Giffero, 2014) of hope. The program continued to me et during severe wildfires in the inland Northwest,  and the community was impacted by unhealthy air quality fro m wildfire smoke. We view building shelter for thei r creatures in the context of this crisis as a form o f everyday resilience, one that is inextricable fro m the aesthetic practices related to seeking materials, building fr iendship, and enacting care for the more-than-human. Building shelter also aesthetically constructs a future worl d in which Rafa and Arturo’s pets can come home, and a future construction that the two youth can return to. What  other practices allow for multispecies connec tions during times of precarity? What further creativity makes f uture worlds out of crisis? Seeking resources, buil ding friendship, and care-taking are aesthetic practices, guiding us through precarity to keep moving, collaborating, and caring for human and more-than-human in times of global crisis.
