# Solo vs. Collaborative Programming? Cognitive and Affective Outcomes, Problem Design and Solving Patterns in a Game-Based Learning Environment

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study explores how individual and collaborative game-based learning environments for computer science (CS) affect learning outcomes and patterns in problem-design and problem-solving. A significant difference between individual and collaborative programming was found in computational thinking skills, but not in attitudes toward CS. Log data analysis further revealed similar but distinct patterns in problem-design and problem-solving between groups. We discuss the implications of these findings and suggest directions for future research.

Over the past decade, computer science (CS) education has gained considerable attention from practitioners and researchers worldwide and prompted research into the impact of instructional approaches such as collaborative learning (Vourletsis & Politis, 2022). However, whether collaborative or individual learning in CS can effectively develop computational thinking (CT) skills remains inconclusive (Umapathy & Ritzhaupt, 2017). While extensive literature supports the positive impact of collaborative learning on students' cognitive and affective outcomes compared to individual learning, there are inconsistent findings regarding its influence on students' motivation and programming performance (e.g., Zhong & Li, 2019). These inconsistencies highlight the need for a deeper understanding of the impact collaborative and individual learning have on CS education. We sought to answer two research questions: RQ1. What are the differences in computational thinking skill development and attitudes toward computer science between students who engage in individual versus collaborative gameplay? RQ2. How do problem-design and solving patterns vary between students engaged in an individual or collaborative game-based learning environment?

A web-based coding game This study used a web-based coding game developed by the authors using Unity3D. The objective of the game is to activate a portal with an avatar, by stacking coding blocks to program the avatar’s movements and actions. This coding game offers both problem-design and problem-solving opportunities. Students candesign and create their own problems by adjusting the size of the game world, making terrain within the game world, and strategically placing their avatar, the portal, and  other optional objects (e.g., monsters and items) to make the problems more challenging. This problem-design process also involves stacking coding blocks to program objects in the environment. Once a problem is successfully designed, it is shared online, allowing others to solve the problem or remix it (i.e., modify the shared level). Problem-solving requires students to thoughtfully stack coding blocks to move an avatar to a portal (Figure 1).

Because of an odd number of participants, one participant engaged in learning individually and was not included in further analysis. The rest of the students were paired up into ten  same-gender groups and worked together. Before and after the intervention, students completed pre and posttest measures of CT skills and attitudes toward CS. To measure CT skills, we employed an existing instrument (El-Hamamsy et al., 2022) with three add itional questions related to in-game function blocks, totaling 28 questions.  For attitudes toward CS, we used an established 16-item closed-ended survey (Kwon et al. 2021). Comprehensive data logging allowed us to track students’ engagement with the game. Excluding five students who did not complete either the pretest or posttest instruments, we used paired-sample ttests and separate one-way ANCOVAs to answer each research question. RQ1. Group differences in CT skill test and attitudes toward CS Comparing pretest and posttest across the entire sample, we found significant differences in both CT skill score (t(36) = 7.582, p <.001) and attitudes (t(36) = 5.876, p =.001). We also found, using a one-way ANCOVA controlling for pretest scores,  a significant difference in CT skill posttest scores (F(1, 34) = 4.38, p  =.044) by instructional condition (i.e., individual or collaborative), with the individual  players demonstrating greater improvement. However, the effect size was small (η 2 =.051). There was no difference in attitudes by instructional conditions, controlling for students’ pre-attitudes (F(1, 34) =.02, p =.9). RQ2. Problem-design and problem-solving patterns varying by groups Our analysis of the collected log data showed that both individual and collaborative groups exhibited similar levels of activity in problem-design and problem-solving. For instance, during the six-week intervention, individual players created 5.5 problems on average, and collaborative teams created 5.6 problems. Similarly, individual players solved 15.5 problems on average whereas collaborative teams solved 18.78 problems. However, a notable difference was observed in the time spent on problem design: individual players spent 794 seconds (SD = 252) on average to design a problem while collaborative-players spent 604 seconds (SD = 175), showing a statistically significant difference (t = 2.145, p =.04).

The significant enhancements in both CT skill scores and attitudes across the entire sample indicate that a game-based learning environment can effectively develop students’ CT skills and promote their attitudes, irrespective of whether students work alone  or in groups. Despite the small effect size, a significant difference in CT skill development between groups suggests that individual learning may be more beneficial than collaborative learning in certain contexts —for instance, in our case, a game-based learning environment designed to support young students develop CT skills by offering a predefined game goal (i.e., activating a portal by moving an avatar) and a debugging system (i.e., checking if coding blocks work or not). This may stem from the reduction of potential challenges (e.g., off-task behavior, disagreements)  associated with pair interactions, leading to a more focused learning experience that seems to be related to a greater time spent on problem design by individual-players. Future research should include a larger group of participants and continue to explore distinct engagement patterns by groups.
