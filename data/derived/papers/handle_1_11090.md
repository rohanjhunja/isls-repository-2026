## Introduction

Historically, STEM is a social construct and privil eges certain ways of knowing over others, as the standard for STEM is rooted in the academic experiences of wealthy White males (Morton et. al., 2019). As STEM disciplines have been continuously expanding within the domain of what is considered STEM and who does STEM, there is a need to redefine STEM to be inclusive both ethnically and culturally. One lens to view STEM through to contribute to redefining STEM is identity. Gee (2000) contextualizes human i dentity based on the role they take up in specific domains and how they are perceived in them. Carlone and Johnson (2007) expand on Gee’s (2000) work and use an identity lens to inform how margina lized populations experience success in STEM, there fore developing a science identity construct. I argue that identity is a social construct that plays a major role in how a person perceives themselves; STEM identity is a soc ially based identity grounded in the extent to whic h individuals see themselves and are accepted as a me mber of a STEM discipline or field (Kim et. al., 20 18). Through analyzing the  d evelopment of STEM identities within individuals that have been historically marginalized, we allow th e voices of these communities  to contribute to shifting and redefining what STEM is and who participates in it, calling for a demographically inclusive and holis tic definition. Combining Bronfenbrenner’s Ecological Systems theory (1979), Tajfel & Turner’s Social Identity theory (1979), and Projective Reflection theory (201 4), I  propose the development of a STEM Identity Ecosystem  framework, highlighting the interpersonal and intrapersonal interactions  of historically marginalized populations  as they navigate through and develop STEM identities.

Theoretical foundations Bronfenbrenner’s Development Ecology model identifi es four environmental systems: The Microsystem, the Mesosystem, the Exosystem, and the Macrosystem. Eac h of these systems are a part of an individual’s id entity regarding the role(s) they play within each system in relation to the people  and environments they interact with. Role is defined as how an individual reacts and responds in a relative environment, opposed to who a person may be generally, without the influence of an environme nt (Bronfenbrenner, 1979). Using Bronfenbrenner’s model, I highlight the social interpersonal interactions between individuals, their environments,  and their respective counterparts in each system that influence an individual’s role. Projective Reflection (PR) identity exploration theory framewo rk measures change in identity over different periods of a students’ learning experiences (Foster, 2014). From the PR identity exploration theory, there are four constructs, and the process is broken down into three stages beginning at starting self, moving on to role possible self, and ending in new self. I focus on the three stages within my proposed framework, emphasizing the intrapersonal communication an individual has impacting their decisions while engaging in STEM environments. Social Identity theory (Tajfel & Turner, 1979)  is used to situate the idea of STEM identity as a social construct. Social identity is who a person identifies as in relation to a group  (Tajfel & Turner, 1979). An individual’s social identity  undergoes a simultaneous process  involving social interactions (interpersonal), and their personal thoughts which are influencing and being influenced through while engaging in these interactions (intrapersonal).

These three theories inform my proposed theoretical  framework of a STEM Identity Ecosystem  (see Figure 1).

The linking state between starting self and new self is role possible self  and is within the domain of the exosystem and macro system; role possible self encompasses cultural factors and contextual/environ mental factors. Identity is iterative through these processes.

## Discussion

STEM has a historical foundation that excludes the experiences of marginalized communities and because of this, participation in STEM by these communities has been  limited. Through ecological analyzation of margina lized individuals, we can begin to understand the social factors that have impacted their perceptions of STE M, influencing their STEM identities. Magnification on these factors from marginalized communities can help contribute to the ongoing conversation of who does STEM and what counts as STEM. STEM is a social construct (Morton et al., 2019), therefore viewing identity through a social lens is necessary; I believe that social influences are not only a major factor in how the individual views and participates in STEM, but also contributes majorly to their STEM identities.
