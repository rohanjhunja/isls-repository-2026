# Climate Emotions in a Pre-service Teacher Course on Climate Change

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Emotions and their role in climate change education (CCE) is a small but growing area of research. A case study was conducted with pre-service teachers in a course on climate change and their emotional processing throughout the course. Findings highlight that they had emotional growth over the course related to building a classroom community and a relationship with the more-than-human world, as well as emotional interest increasing due to relevant course content.

## Introduction

Climate change is a growing issue in our world that  threatens many facets of life, humans and more-than-human physical health (United Nations, n.d.), human menta l health (Clayton et al., 2021), and socioeconomic development, especially in developing countries (Bu ggy & McGlynn, 2014). Climate adaptation is necessa ry to help mitigate the effects and impacts of climate ch ange (Buggy & McGlynn, 2014). As humans, this inclu des emotions and affective adaptation (Verlie, 2019). A ffective adaptation is the effectiv e processing of emotions, such as the emotions regarding climate change (Verlie, 2019). Education is critical to developing these adapti ve strategies (Buggy & McGlynn, 2014). Educators need to be aware of the emotions associated with climate change (Finnegan, 2023) and develop their own emotional skills and knowledge (Hemsley, 2022), as affective adaptation is a crucial part of climate change education (Cantell et al., 2019; Verlie, 2019). Climate emotions are defined as any emotions experi enced related to climate change  (Pihkala, 2022). While a recent synthesis of environmental education literature concludes that addressing emotions in educational spaces is needed (Olsen et al., 2024), more research is needed on emotions in climate change education, specifically for pre-service teachers (Hufnagel, 2015). The following research question guided this study:

• In what ways did pre-service teachers experience climate emotions over t he course of the term in an

undergraduate course on climate change?

## Theoretical framework: Emotions as social and relational

Emotions hold and bind a social body together (Coll ins, 1990); therefore, emotions surrounding climate change can be seen as a social phenomenon. Looking through  the lens of emotions as social is especially criti cal in the sense of climate change as it is a human-caused issue, and its mitigation is dependent on the created relationships and the emotions experienced.

## Setting and participants

Participants were students taking an introductory course on climate change designed for pre-service teachers at a large public research university in the northeaster n United States. Twenty-four out of twenty-five students provided consent to participate in the study.

## Data collection and analysis

Data sources included focal group interviews (n=5), field memos (n=22), and class assignments (n=24). The initial analytic strategy used in this case study was inductive. The analytic technique taken was one of pattern matching with a segue into explanation building as a more sp ecific form of pattern matching (Yin, 2018). A comb ination of a priori and in vivo codes were used. A priori codes were used to start to ensure alignment with conceptual and theoretical frameworks; however, in vivo coding was used to stay true to the pre-service teachers’ perspectives as the language surrounding emotions varies and is nuanced. Coding was done by the first and second authors, with an 80% intercoder reliability or 100% consensus cod ing achieved (Saldaña, 2016). Two main rounds of co ding were conducted in alignment with Saldaña (2016). Fo r the first round of co ding, a combination of simultaneous coding with emotions and values coding was used. Pattern coding was used during the second cycle to chunk data from the first coding cycle into smaller categories to identify emergent themes and explanations (Saldaña, 2016). Patterns were identified by looking at the entire coded data set.

## Findings

Students’ experiences and climate emotions were grouped into four themes. All names used are pseudonyms. Emotional growth over time While not as noticeable at the beginning of the cou rse, students’ climate emotions started to become m ore prominent after learning about climate change in th e first few weeks of the course; their emotions flu ctuated throughout the term. Uncertainty and overwhelm, experienced after learning more about climate chang e, played a large role in their emotional experience during t he course. These emotions also exacerbated their wo rry and concern about climate change. Students experienced anger, frustration, disbelief, and betrayal due to the perceived lack of action by others in general but expressed h ope in the possible future actions by humans, especially future students, as well as their own future action steps,  which include teaching about climate change in the ir future classrooms.

Classroom community Students frequently mentioned that they experienced relief when they talked to others in the class and discovered that others had similar emotions surrounding climat e change.  When students were away from the classroom community and/or when others didn’t share the same emotions as them in the classroom, students felt it  was harder to process their own emotions. Students need support in the form of community and connection, specifically surrounding climate change emotions.

Relationship to the more-than-human world Most students experienced more ‘positive’ emotions, such as feeling relaxed, calm, happy, and joyful when doing outside-of-class activities designed to connect to nature, inc luding a mindfulness walk and nature journaling. These activities and the emotions they fe lt during the activity influence d their perspectives on education in the form of them wanting to incorporate these activitie s into their future classrooms a nd helped them feel more hopeful about climate change.

Relevance Students expressed more emotional interest in clima te change when the topics of discussion were more p ersonal to their lives. Some examples of this are when the course content highlights a climate change issue in an area where a student has heritage from and/or family in the area. Students experienced excitement, hope, and empowerment when the aspects of the course were relevant to their lives and their future careers as well, such as when discussing how to implement pedagogical strate gies in their future classrooms, planning and ex ecuting presentations on climate change issues, and demonst rating lessons for their future students, as well a s discussing and understanding what action steps they can take to help mitigate climate change.

## Discussion and conclusion

Similar to Hufnagel (2015), our findings suggest th at both ‘positive’ and ‘negative’ emotional express ions are valuable in science learning settings. Processing ‘ negative’ emotions helped pre-service teachers move from a perspective of disengagement and/or overwhelm to on e of concern and willingness to take action. The fi ndings suggest that the ‘positive’ emotions experienced by  the pre-service teachers are due to the connec tions that are fostered by the educational strategies used in the course and that the pre-service teachers benefit from climate change instruction that focuses on emotions and emo tional processing. This can lead and support pre-service teachers to work towards developing affective adaptation (Verlie, 2019) surrounding climate change. Our findings also highlight the critical importance of relationships in climate change education, both in the form of community in the classroom and in the form of building and/or fostering a connection to the more-than-human world. Recommendations for pre-service teacher education Recommendations for pre-service teacher education include incorporating emotional processing activities into the curriculum surrounding climate chang e and designing courses and experiences to foster positiv e relationships between students, their peers, and their instructors. We also recommend including course activities and learning outdoors to foster a connection between the pre-service teachers and the more-than-human world/nature.
