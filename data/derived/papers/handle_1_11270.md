# Emotion Regulation During Failure-driven Learning

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Fifty-one ninth-grade students self-reported whether and what forms of emotion regulation they pursued during suboptimal idea generation for a math task targeting a yet-to-be-learned topic. Results showed that the majority of students did not try to actively change or manage their emotions. Among those that did, regulation strategies were predominantly cognitively-focused or involved taking an emotionally detached perspective to focus on problem-solving processes. Strategies where students acknowledged and worked with emotions without discrediting their experience were rarer. Furthermore, the awareness of failure critically impacted emotional reactions. Findings hold implications for increasing awareness of useful emotion regulation strategies in the service of improving emotional resilience in classrooms.

## Introduction

Failure-driven problem-solving is an emotionally-charged learning process (Sinha, 2022). Although learning sciences scholarship (e.g., Lamnina & Chase, 2019; Savelson & Muldner, 2023; Wang et al., 2024) has begun systematically study ing the complementary role of positively and negatively  valenced emotions in driving learning through failure, very little is known about whether and how students regulate these emotions. To persist through challenging learning situations, “it is not enough to be smart and hardworking – to have the added edge for success, students must also be able to understa nd and manage emotions to succeed at school ” (MacCann et al., 2020, p.174). To provide students with differentiated support for emotion re gulation, it is imperative to first understand how they naturally tap into their emotions as resources during failure. That is precisely the goal of the present work. Drawing on a broad and inclusive set of strategies typically studied and validated in the emotion regulation domain (Weidman & Kross, 2021), we measured how secondary school students  change or manage their emotions when generating suboptimal ideas  during a math task targeting a yet-to-be-learned concept. We further assessed whether students’ metacognition ab out failing affected their positive and negative af fect experienced during this task. Taken together, this work is a step towards our long-term goal of building scaffolds to support sensemaking with emotions during learning situations with high failure likelihood.

## Theoretical background

A key psychological construct underlying the presen t investigation is emotion regulation (Koole, 2009; Gross, 2015). It refers to how individuals can influence which em otions they have, when they have them and how they experience and express them. Several evidence-backed strategies to manage naturally-occurring emotions have been reported in the literature (e.g., Weidman & Kross, 2021). One such strategy is cognitive reappraisal, which involves changing how one appraises a task to alter its emotional significance, e.g., a challenging problem can be reframed as an opportunity to learn something new  and improve. Emotion regulation can further take th e more commonplace form of decreasing negatively valenced emotions and increasing positive ones, or, less fre quently the counter-intuitive form of deliberately increasing negativel y valenced emotions and decreasing positive ones (Tamir, 2016; Naragon-Gainey et al., 2017). Ultimately, an important educational goal when teac hing emotion regulation skills is to assist students with deploy ing (experiencing or expressing) emotions  in learning contexts where the functional purpose of those emotions matc hes with the situational demands, instead of habitually judging emotions as good or bad depending solely on their valence (Willroth et al., 2023). Failure-driven problem solving, which inevitably results in confusion, frustration, anger and shame (Sinha, 2022), provides a ripe context to study how students appraise the usefulness of (a nd manage) such unpleasurable emotions – e.g., do they distance themselves from such emotions? do they try to suppress them? do they reframe its instrumental quality? After all, it is not always better to experience more pleasurable and less unpleasurable emotions for academic and emotional well-being (Grant & Schwartz, 2011; Ford & Mauss, 2014; Wang et al., 2024).

When students engage in problem-solving tasks without contingent accuracy feedback, exemplified via learning designs like productive failure  (Kapur, 2014), their perceptions of success or failure can influen ce emotional responses. Depending on failure attributions to ability or effort (Dweck & Yeager, 2019), students may experience negative or positive affect respectively  (1). On the other hand, being unsure about whether one  has succeeded or failed may lead to emotional uncertainty (Morris et al., 2022), which can serve as a double-edged sword – while it can trigger awareness of knowledge gaps and spark hope and curiosity to know the right answer, uncertainty can also breed self-doubt, inadequacy and self-questioning. Taken together, the metacognitive ability to (un)-successfully judge performance may impact emotional reactions and the motivation to persist and learn.

## Participants

After obtaining informed consent from students and their parents, N = 51 upper secondary school students (grade 9, age 14-15, 70.6% male) from a specialized independent school in Si ngapore participated in the study. Their mathematics prior knowledge (max 100), as assessed by the most recent standardized annual national examination was M = 74.81 (SD = 10.24). The study was approved by the university ethics commission (IRB-2023-1040).

## Study design

Drawing on prior work (Sinha, 2022), a ll participants started with a 5-minute pre-questionnaire on individual differences that affect learning from failure – on a five-point Likert scale, everyone rated their (i) learning goal orientation (8 items, 𝛼 = 0.78, e.g., “the opportunity to do challenging work is important to me”, “I try hard to improve on my past performance ”), and (ii) attitude towards mistakes (8 items, 𝛼 = 0.86, e.g., “when I make mistakes, I am afraid that others look down upon me ”, “I believe it is smart to avoid making mistakes duri ng learning”). Subsequently, participants individually engaged in a 25-minute productive failure problem-solving task adapted from Kapur (2014), involving the generation of multiple solutions to quantify the consistency of two soccer players based on their goal-scoring records. Because standard deviation (the targeted learning concept) had not been formally taught, we assumed that the major ity of participants would be novices to the topic. After the problem-solving phase, participants spent another hour over two days receiving a teacher-led lecture on standard deviation and completing a posttest, following the design principles of productive failure (Sinha & Kapur, 2021a). Four novel data points relevant to the present analysis were c ollected in between the problem-solving and lecture phases – (i) subjective experiences of positive and negative affect, assessed via the PANA S scale on a five-point Likert scale (Watson et al., 1988), (ii) whether participants tried to change or manage how they were feeling when working through the math task  (e.g., trying to feel less negative (less anxious o r frustrated), trying to feel more positive (more happy or amused), or ev en trying to feel more negative or less positive), and if yes, (iii) one or more emotion regulation strategies that they used (see figure 1). Additionally, to tap into participants’ metacognition about failing, we provoked reflection on whether  they had succeeded or failed at the math task, along with descriptive accounts of their process of coming up with multiple solutions.

## Research questions

RQ1a: How frequent is spontaneous emotion regulation during failure-driven learning? RQ1b: What is the distribution of different emotion regulation strategies during failure-driven learning? RQ2: How does participants’ metacognition about failing impact their perceived positive and negative affect? Analysis plan To answer RQ1a and RQ1b, we tabulated response frequencies to analyze the presence of emo tion regulation strategies, contingent on whether participants repo rted engaging with their emotions during the math t ask. To answer RQ2, we used ANOVAs with Tukey post-hoc comparisons to assess how participants’ positive and negative affect differed among those who retrospectively thought they succeeded at the task, failed, or were  not sure about their performance. Cohen’s d was used to assess the practical significance of our results.

## Results

Manipulation checks Participants completed the math task as expected by  creating multiple solutions (M = 2.82, SD = 1.40, max 7). These solutions did not just involve (i) analytical application of prior knowledge such as central tendencies and graphing (e.g., “I tried to use my past knowledge on mean, median and mode to guide me through the question. I also applied some common sense to understand the qu estion and answer logically ”, “Link back to what I have learnt in lower secondary: bar charts, histograms, e.g., think about real life application I have seen before: stock market graph, e.g., try to find ways to visualize the data/quantify con sistency”), but also creatively explored (ii) other possibilities despite participants sometimes being unsure of their accuracy (e.g., “I thought about the factors that affect consistency like the differences in goa l numbers between 2 consecutive years. I then have a lot more possibilities to explore with this new set of data of difference in goal numbers between 2 consecutive  years”, “I thought of different ways that consistency might be  interpreted and just walked through each method systematically”). No participant, except for one, generated the canonical standard de viation solution, suggesting a high failure rate. Taken together, our study was successful in creating an authentic failure-driven learning setup. Emotion regulation during failure (RQ1a, RQ1b) Despite the prevalence of self-reported positive (M = 2.55, SE = 0.10) and negative affect (M = 1.33, SE = 0.06), only 41.2% of the participants (n = 21) acknowledged that they actively tried to change or manage what they were feeling. The majority of participants (n = 30) seemed not to engage with their experienced emotions, suggesti ng that spontaneous emotion regulation is infrequent during failure-driven problem-solving (RQ1a). For participants who attempted to regulate emotions during the math task, their strategy distribution is reported in Figure 1 (RQ 1b). The three most commonly used regulation strategies comprised reinterpreting the task to reduce its emotional impact (cognitive reappraisal), focusing attention on the problem-solving process without judgment (cognitive concentration), and taking an emotionally detached perspective on the learning situation (distancing). These strategies highlight a reliance on cognitive control (mental processes) to manage emotional responses, which despite being effective in certain situatio ns, may not always incorporate emotional awareness and acceptance. Some participants also reported actively taking  actions to improve the quality of the problem-solving experience so as to alter its emotional impact (situation modification) along with attempts to find meaning in the suboptimal idea generation process despite experiencing discomfort (reconstrual). These strategies showcase greater agency that can empower participants to influence their emotional experience. Figure 1 Frequencies of Emotion Regulation Strategies Used by N = 21 (out of 51) Participants during the Math Task. Inhibiting emotional reactions (suppression) and deploying attention away from the emotionally-charged problem-solving process  (distraction) were less frequent  in our data sample. These relatively maladaptive regulation strategies may result in missed opportunities to reflect on the underlying emotional cause by diverting focus away from the learning experience  (Gross, 2015). Finally, behaviorally and/or physically expressing emotions outwardly (venting, behavioral expression) was scarce.  Our classroom observation  memos did not record any such instances either. Although these strategies can provide temporary r elief, they may hinder longterm learning from emotions. Taken together, these results suggest participants deployed a suite of emotion regulation strategies, sometimes multiple of them in tandem to make problem-solving progress. Metacognition about failure (RQ2) Our results for RQ2 showed opposing trends for affect distributions among participants who thought they succeeded (n = 13), failed (n = 7) or were unsure (n = 30) about their performance (2). Descriptively, positive affect was lowest for those who perceived they failed at the task (M = 2.31, SE = 0.11) and highest for those who felt they succeeded (M = 2.68, SE = 0.23), with scores of those unsure about their accuracy falling in-between (M = 2.52, SE = 0.12). On the other hand, negative affect was descriptivel y highest for participants who perceived failure (M = 1.46, SE = 0.18) and lowest for those who perceived they had succeeded at the task (M = 1.22, SE = 0.18), again with scores of those unsure about their acc uracy falling in-between (M = 1.34, SE = 0.08). Across these pairwise comparisons (see figure 2), Cohen’s d ranged from 0.23 – 0.56, suggesting moderate effects. ## Discussion and conclusion

Learning sciences research has come a long way from conceiving emotions as “excellent examples of the fictional causes to which we commonly attribute behavior”  (Skinner, 1953, p. 160) to actively harnessing their potential in the learning process (e.g., Belland et al., 2013). At the same time, contempora ry pedagogies like productive failure (Sinha & Kapur, 2021a) debunk design myths that prioritize sparing learners the “costs and pain of faulty effort” (Bandura, 1986, p. 47) via guidance that leads to flawless behavior. Here, we use a failure-driven learning context to advance the scientific understanding of emotion regulation strategies deployed by secondary school students when engaging in an exploratory math task.  Our results suggest that spontaneous emotion regula tion is infrequent – when it does occur though, the most common forms involve reframing a difficult learning situation, focusing intently on the problem-solving process, or distancing oneself from the emo tional impact of mistakes. Depending on whether the reframing is geared toward s reducing negatively valenced emotions by putting a positive spin on discomfort, or, whether it seeks to acknowledge such emotions as a motivation to persevere and/or seek help, outcomes can vary – while the former discredits the  emotional experience, the latter can lead to heightened self-awareness and willingness to learn from mistakes (e.g., Leach & Cidam, 2015; Travis et al., 2020; Lench et al., 2024).  We also see the prevalence of some proactive and adaptive emotion regulation strategie s in our data such as situation modification and reconstrual that attempt to harness emotions as resources during the problem-solving process rather than isolating any task-relevant decision-making from their impact. Other emotion regulation strategies that involve behaviorally suppressing or expressing emotions outwardly were less commonly observed in our data, presumably to maintain formal classroom decorum during the study.

Our findings further demonstrate how the discrepancy between actual and perceived failure candifferentially affect emotional reactions during le arning. Underestimation of failure, despite putting  learners in a positive affective state, may lead to a false sense of mastery and delays in critically evaluating and adjusting task solutions, in turn breeding complacency (Sinha & Kapur, 2021b). On the other hand, performance uncertainty can amplify the emotional impact of perceived failure via feelings of inadequacy even if the actual performance is relatively strong (Morriss et al., 2022). Finally, accurate metacognitive judgments of failure can guide corrective actions and promote persistence despite inducing ne gative affect – caution must be taken, however, to facilitate avoidance of rumination and self-blame. Future replication of this work in primary and tertiary education settings is imperative to navigate the complex space of emotion regulation in failure-driven learning.
