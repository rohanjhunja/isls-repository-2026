# CoReTra: Supporting Learners in Identifying Personally Meaningful Contrasts among Readers’ Interpretations

**Conference:** ISLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Writing that involves personally reconstructing what one has read is essential for knowledge transformation, although university students often struggle to make meaningful connections between readings and their own ideas. Existing instructional approaches offer procedural guidance but often do not support deep, personally meaningful integration. To address this issue, we developed CoReTra (Collaborative Reflection through conTrast of interpretations), a web-based system designed to support learners in identifying and integrating personally meaningful contrasts. The system includes two views: the Decomposition View, which allows learners to extract content elements from readings and record notes as cards, and the Integration View, which enables learners to compare cards from different perspectives to refine their interpretations. The system uses large language model (LLM)–based semantic mining to adaptively suggest peer-created cards that represent diverse viewpoints. These features support learners in progressively revisiting and reconstructing their interpretations, promoting deeper reflection in CSCL contexts, while keeping contrast identification learner‑driven.

Writing that involves personally reconstructing what one has read has been conceptualized as a tool for knowledge transformation from a constructivist perspective (Bereiter & Scardamalia, 1987; Spivey, 1997). Such writing is particularly important in higher education, where students are expected to read critically and integrate what they read with their own ideas to produce original texts (MacArthur, 2022). However, university students often struggle to make meaningful connections between what they read and their prior knowledge, and they tend to rely on surface-level strategies (Du & List, 2020). Miyake et al. (2002) compared how university students and professional researchers integrated multiple sources and found that students tended to treat each article as independent, whereas researchers actively decomposed the articles into pieces and then reconstructed them into a new, integrated view. These findings suggest that students have difficulty relating and personally reconstructing information into a coherent, integrated explanation, emphasizing the need for effective support methods to facilitate this process.

Barzilai et al. (2018) conducted a systematic review of empirical studies on text integration support and found that the most frequent instructional practices were collaborative discussions, often combined with strategy instruction (e.g., explicit instruction of integration, modeling of integration), and the use of graphic organizers (e.g., tables, concept maps). These methods provided learners with procedural guidance on how to integrate texts and with frameworks for what to compare, thereby guiding collaborative practices in ways intended by teachers or researchers. In all effective interventions identified, students were taught integration processes, or how to integrate. This finding indicates that explicit instruction can play a major role in successful interventions. However, Boscolo et al. (2007) found that explicit instruction through modeling of good and poor literature reviews improved students’ organization and cohesion, but did not affect content integration. This instruction also reinforced students’ view of academic writing as impersonal and did not change their beliefs about the elaborative and transmission roles of writing. Nelson and King (2023) similarly noted that “form-and processoriented pedagogy” —approaches directed to textual form in written communication (e.g., arguments, literature reviews) while incorporating process-oriented elements such as modeling and collaborative practice—has improved many aspects of students’ writing in these genres, but integrative connections remain difficult for many writers, as illustrated by interventions such as Boscolo et al. (2007). For such integrative reading-and-writing tasks, collaborative learning offers an effective way to address this issue by allowing learners to externalize and compare differences in their ideas. In text comprehension, contrasts emerge in interpretations as a result of learners’ differing prior knowledge and beliefs (Kintsch, 1998; Bråten et al., 2011). Miyake (2013) characterizes this kind of socially mediated revision of understanding as conceptual change through collaboration, where collaboration and reflection serve as social mechanisms for r evising understanding, and where role exchange between expressing ideas and monitoring others supports the abstraction of understanding in classroom discussion. However, it is challenging for learners to identify, within classroom discourse, contrasting interpretations that contribute to reconstructing their own understanding. Large language models (LLMs) have the potential to serve as tools for semantic mining to support learners in exploring personally meaningful contrasts. Previous studies used distributional semantic models to generate semantic relationships based on learners’ input, rather than relying on fixed rules set by teachers. One early approach was Latent Semantic Analysis (LSA; Landauer & Dumais, 1997). For example, Wolfe et al. (1998) used LSA to estimate whether a text fell within a student’s “zone of learnability” by calculating the semantic distance between the learner’s prior knowledge and the text. Unlike LSA, which constructs a static semantic space from word co-occurrence statistics, LLM can generate context-dependent semantic representations dynamically through self-attention mechanisms. As a result, LLM can more precisely emulate contextual semantic differences—though still approximately—than static models. When used appropriately, LLM has the potential to enable a CSCL environment in which students adaptively explore personally meaningful contrasts based on their own writing, without being entirely dependent on externally provided forms. Building on this potential, we designed and developed CoReTra (Collaborative Reflection through conTrast of interpretations), an adaptive integration support system that uses LLM to support learners in identifying meaningful contrasts that they can personally relate to. The system supports learners in progressively revisiting their interpretive notes by contrasting them with those of others, using LLM-based semantic mining. In CoReTra, LLM is not used to generate answers—which could cause hallucinations or overreliance—but serves as a semantic-mining tool that helps learners identify contrasts among interpretations by suggesting potential perspectives. Thus, CoReTra uses LLM as an assistive tool for exploration, not as a decision-maker, ensuring that identifying contrasts remains a human-driven, reflective process. This system extends Tanaka et al. (2025a), which used LLM t o support identifying student–teacher contrasts, by enabling learners to compare interpretations with peers in a collaborative setting. CoReTra is primarily intended for university students and can be used in both formal and non-formal learning contexts. CoReTra has been piloted in an intensive graduate course on the learning sciences; an initial classroom account is reported in Tanaka et al. (2025b). CoReTra is a web-based system implemented in Next.js with a PostgreSQL backend and deployed on a managed PaaS (currently Railway). The first author developed the system end-to-end.

The system includes two main views: the “Decomposition View,” which breaks down reading materials into elements to create cards, and the “Integration View,” which enables users to compare cards with those of others. Learners can switch between these views using the toggle button at the top of the screen, depending on their reading and writing. In addition, CoReTra provides the “Integrative Report View,” which learners access via the “Write Integrative Report” button to write a provisional, revisable integrative report for the current assignment while revisiting the cards they created. CoReTra organizes activities in a workspace (e.g., a course project) and multiple assignments within it (e.g., class meetings), each with its own reading materials and a shared set of cards. Decomposition View: Extract sources into cards and take notes The Decomposition View was designed to support learners in identifying and extracting content elements from reading materials, creating cards as personal units for later comparison. Several reviews (Barzilai et al., 2018; Nelson & King, 2023) have noted that prior studies have found supporting learners in identifying content elements from reading materials and organizing or describing their relationships can effectively promote the creation of new connections. Based on these findings, the Decomposition View allows learners to extract text segments into cards and to write notes or annotations on each segment. In the Decomposition View, when learners select text in the left pane, a “Create quote card” button appears. Clicking this button generates a card in the right pane, prefilled with the selected text. Learners can add questions, partial summaries, or paraphrases to the card. Each card created from a quotation includes a button that allows learners to jump back to the corresponding location in the original text. The color of this button matches the tab color of the reading material.

In addition, guiding questions displayed at the bottom right of the screen help learners determine what to extract and record as notes. These questions are predefined for each material. By clicking the “Add current answer” button, learners can create a special card that records their provisional responses to the guiding questions at that moment. Because the system supports progressive revision of learners’ descriptions, they can record multiple responses to the same guiding question during the reading process. These cards can also be revisited in the “Integrative Report View” when learners write an integrative report for the current assignment. As cards are created in the Decomposition View, they become part of the assignment’s shared set of cards, which the Integration View later searches for comparison. Integration View: Compare cards from different perspectives to refine interpretations The Integration View was designed to support learners in reconstructing their interpretations through collaborative comparison with others. It includes a function that enables learners to compare the cards they extracted in the Decomposition View with those of others or with their own previous cards, supported by an LLM. In the Integration View, when learners click the “Compare with other cards” button, the system searches for cards created within the same assignment and suggests three candidate cards that may represent different perspectives. Each suggested card includes a brief explanation of why reviewing from the suggested perspective may be useful. Figure 2 shows the process by which the system generates these suggestions. This process is executed each time the user clicks the “Compare with other cards” button. As a result, even when the same base card is used, different candidate cards and explanations are generated with each execution. When a learner selects a suggestion to reference, clicking the “Use this as a reference” button generates a new card with links to the referenced card. By clicking the inserted link button, learners can access both the original base card and the referenced suggestion. Each suggestion can be reviewed alongside the recommended card and the explanation generated by the LLM, which describes the reason for its selection. The suggestion function is enabled only after at least three candidate cards are available. Because what constitutes a “meaningful” contrast is inherently subjective, suggestions are intentionally exploratory rather than “best match” recommendations: the system returns up to three candidate contrasts with brief rationales as cues Figure 2 LLM-supported suggestion process in the Integration View for reflection, not correctness claims. Learners may ignore unhelpful suggestions and re-run the function to explore alternative contrasts.

For LLM-based suggestion, the server sends only the textual content of the base card and candidate cards (quoted excerpts and learners’ notes) along with internal card IDs; it does not include user account identifiers (e.g., names or emails) in the prompt. Candidates are restricted to note-bearing cards created within the same assignment in the workspace (excluding deleted cards and the base card). To bound latency and cost, the candidate list is truncated to fit within a fixed token budget (default: 12k tokens for candidates, measured with tiktoken), removing older cards first until the budget is met.

Interactive demo In this interactive demo session, participants will use CoReTra to collaboratively read and interpret a short text familiar to the ISLS community. They can write their interpretations in the system and discuss them with other participants through the system interface.

Each participant will receive a temporary account created specifically for the session and may use the system independently on their own computer through a web browser (Chrome, Edge, or Safari recommended). Participants can create cards anonymously, linked to their session accounts, which are identified by randomly generated dummy names consisting of a color and an animal (e.g., BlueCat). All potentially discriminatory terms were excluded from the name set.

For the demo, we will prepare an initial pool of cards so that participants can try the suggestion feature. Participants can also open the “Integrative Report View” via the “Write Integrative Report” button to write an integrative report based on the cards they created.

The reading material used for this demonstration is an open-access article by Pea and Linn (2020), “Personal Perspectives on the Emergence of the Learning Sciences: 1970s–2005.” This material is used under the terms of the Creative Commons Attribution (CC BY 4.0) license, with appropriate citation of the original publication. We expect that reading and interpreting this text together will enable ISLS members to identify meaningful contrasts in their interpretations and engage in deeper discussions about issues central to the ISLS community.
