## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Given the manner in which climate change is intertwined with histories of racial-capitalism and colonialism and both historical and ongoing stories of community resistance, integrating the sociopolitical aspects of climate change with climate science is cr itical to learning about the subject as a whole. While a substantial body of literature is focused on how to engage students who are on the frontlines of climate injustice, here we ask how to meaningfully engage students of relative economic privilege in meaningful learning around climate and environmental justice. In this paper, we describe the design of a yearlong trajectory within a high school science class, meant to engage students in identifying and questioning dominant narratives within the culture o f a particular school, paying specific attention to dominant narratives that assume the beneficence of STEM and industry (Morales-Doyle, 2025).

Over the past decade, a growing number of scholars have called for climate change education (e.g. Iyer & Brett, 2024, Nxumalo & Montes, 2021) and science education more broadly (e.g. Morales-Doyle, 2017) to be justice centered. Recently, more scholars (e.g. Clark, 2024)  have reported examples of what justice-centered climate change teaching can look like in classroom settings. These examples are almost exclusively centered on what such teaching can look like in settings in which students are from communities who are highly impacted by the structures of oppression described in the abstract. We believe that this is important work, and that youth whose communities have been historically on the receiving end of environmental injustice and whose trajectories are vulnerable to the precarities of our changing climate, “have a right to be angry” (Davis & Schaeffer, 2019). However, we also ask what it might mean to engage privileged students in learning around climate justice. Specifically, we consider how we might engage students who, through their various privileges, are likely to be insulated from the worst aspects of climate change and environmental degradation in grappling with structural (rather than scientific) aspects of climate justice. We argue this is impor tant because through their various privileges and access points to power, these students are poised to reproduce problematic power dynamics that play into environmental and climate injustice as they become adults. Over the course of this paper we engage with the question of how to meaningfully engage economically privileged students in ideological questions of environmental and climate justice by: 1. Motivating the challenge and importance of centering justice in discussions of climate change and the environment with students of relative privilege and articulating why doing so may be different than teaching students from frontline, racially or economically marginalized communities. 2. Describing the school community in which Michael teaches and identifying some of the “dominant narratives” within the school community that we see as salient to climate change and environmental justice, 3. Describing a yearlong instructional trajectory meant to build student capacity to identify and critique dominant narratives and foster ideological broadening within classroom discussions.

## Theoretical framework

Pedagogy of the oppressed(ors?): Challenges in teaching privileged students about climate change and environmental injustice A substantial body of literature, including some of our own (e.g. Fitzmaurice & Wilkerson (2024)), points to Paulo Freire’s (1970) idea of “Praxis” as a grounding framework for engaging students in climate justice learning. But Freire’s ideas, as conveyed in the title of his book, “Pedagogy of the Oppressed,” are centered on disrupting knowledge hegemonies and giving voice and ultimately power,  to “the Oppressed,” and as described by Allen et al. (2009) and in this paper later on, critical pedagogies may m eet substantial resistance when applied to “Oppressor” or privileged students. This challenge is further complicated by current rhetoric within American politics that demonizes teachers for encouraging students to reckon with their own privilege in the context of structural injustices.

Many teachers teaching more privileged students, even those who are interested in engaging intellectually with structures of oppression may not necessarily be equipped to speak with students or facilitate learning that explicitly takes up questions of capitalism, race or other structures of oppression. As described in 2157 Philip (2011), teachers' expressions of ideological commitments may shift as they move through different settings. This is particularly true of teachers of privileged students, who may feel a strong tension between discussing the structural causes and ineq uitable impacts of climate change, and engaging in instruction that honors the lived experiences of their students, or not transgressing too far against the dominant narratives told within their school communities.

To illustrate this point, we tell the story of another teacher that Helen has worked with, who also works with students of relative privilege. After engaging in a workshop about Environmental Racism in our area facilitated by Helen, this teacher commented:  “I feel like … I can finally define White Supremacy in my own words and draw the link between White Supremacy and Environmental injustice. I have a lot of family members that don’t get it that I need to tell about this.” At the end of a yearlong professional development program meant to focus on questions of climate justice, Helen asked the same  teacher how she was bringing the ideas of environmental justice into her classroom. The teacher paused for a long moment and said, “Well my students live in [a neighborhood] where they don’t really experience environmental injustice,” and followed up by explaining that she did not teach about environmental injustice because she wanted her instruction to be centered in her students’ lived experience. This teacher engaged her students in a project that was in many ways phenomenal: in the amount of agency she facilitated, in exposing her students to ways through which to influence policy at the school district level. Yet, she was not able to relate ideas to her students that she felt so urgently that her own family members needed to know, because of her desire to center her students' lived experience in her instructional practice. In the design work in which we engage here, we recognize this tension and ask, how can we leverage students’ lived experiences or dominant narratives within school culture to build student capacity to engage with structures of oppression?

Developing students’ ability to identify and question dominant narratives and encouraging ideological broadening at the classroom level In their paper on ideological convergence within the context of an engineering class discussion on drone warfare, Philip et al. (2018) describe a classroom, full of engineering college students. Despite having been given, and being fairly well versed in re adings that represented a diverse set of ideologies, the students converged on ideological logics that valued American lives over other lives. The year-long design we describe below is designed with the hopes of halting such ideological convergence and encouraging ideological broadening at the classroom level. The societal discourse around climate justice and remediative actions is full of especially destructive narratives that pose as solutions (e.g. it is up to our individual actions as consumers to halt climate change). The instructional design we describe below is meant to support students in identifying and questioning dominant narratives, such as capitalist STEM-Corporations-as-savior narratives, but also those espoused by normative environmental movements.

We ask the questions above in the context of a teacher-researcher collaboration and reflection. Teacher-researcher conversations included formal interviews, teaching debriefs, co-planning conversations, and research discussions over a two year span. The yearlong design described below was implemented in the 2024-2025 school year at Washington High School where Michael has taught science for the past 8 years.

## Context and some dominant narratives at washington high school

We take up the question of what it means to engage students of privilege in the context of the AP Environmental Science at Washington High School taught by Michael. Washington High School is a comprehensive High School in Fremont Unified School District, located within the broader Silicon Valley region in the Bay Area of California. Students at the school come from a wide range of socioeconomic statuses and racial groups.  Overall, 23% of students at Washington receive free and reduced lunch, and the racial  demographics of the school are roughly: 50% Asian, 26% Latine, 13% white, 5% two or more races, 3% Black. While the school as a whole is racially, linguistically, and socio-economically diverse, students are segregated via tracking. East and South Asian students, as well as white students are over-represented in academically advanced classes, while Latine and 2158 Middle Eastern students are over-represented in remedial tracks. Many of the wealthier families work in the tech industry, while most of the lower income families work blue collar jobs. Michael estimates that his AP class is 50% South Asian, 25% East Asian, 12% Latine, and 12% white. Within the class there are students whose parents came to the United States on H1-B visas to work high-paying tech jobs, refugees fleeing Afghanistan and Syria, and students whose families have been in the area for  >100 years. ## Instructional design in the context of valleyview

In the sections below, we describe the yearlong design sequence through which Michael works to support his students’ in identifying and critically analyzing dominant narratives and broadening their ideologies. Due to formatting constraints, we describe the year in brief but give more detail about the first unit in Semester 2. Semester 1: Empiricism as a safe space for developing student capacity to critique dominant narratives Before teaching at Washington High School, Michael taught at another school in our region. The students at this other school were predominantly Black and Latine and many students’ homes were located close to and downwind of a refinery with a recent history of toxic releases. Students responded with interest to lessons about environmental racism. Michael felt that he was simply giving a name to a phenomena his students already knew about and took for granted. In contrast, in his first years at Washington, Michael found conversations about environmental justice more challenging. In response to engaging with case studies of environmental injustice, 2159 students shared that they wished Michael would just spend more time preparing for the AP Exam (Dominant Narratives #1 and #2).  Michael also received parental complaints that were forwarded to the principal; vocal parents were especially unappreciative of Michael’s critiques of capitalism and large corporations. In response, Michael shifted his approach away from engaging students with the social and political aspects of environmental and climate injustice at the beginning of the school year. However, STEM subjects and staff, and parents alike. Michael leverages the status of STEM methodology, using empiricism as a “safe space” with which to engage students in the practice of identifying and questioning dominant ideologies over the course of the first semester. Some learning activities (such as having students “fix” a broken CO 2 experiment fall within traditional inquiry-based science as epistemological learning paradigms (e.g. Hammer, 1994). Others, such as using ecological data to assess rules within and critique the merit of the AP standards serve a similar purpose but challenge Dominant Narrative #1 more directly. For example, students are asked to consider that a college board standard defining the Malthusian theory, which neither mentions inaccuracies nor its use in eugenics movements, requires students to challenge the idea (aligned with Dominant Narrative #3) that STEM is used for social good. ## context of climate change

Only after building students' skills in identifying and questioning dominant narratives within the “safe space of empiricism” over the course of the first semester, does Michael ask students to engage with climate change and environmental injustice as a social or political phenomenon. Michael first engages students in thinking about the role that capitalism plays in environmental issues through a unit on GMOs and their relationship to monopolies in American farming (Elsheikh, 2015). As part of this unit, students engage directly with the concept of an ideology. They map out lay arguments that they have heard and view others on social media, both for and against GMOs, and group the arguments by ideology, giving names to the ideologies they identify (e.g. pro-technical innovation, natural is better). Students also explore scientific data, and social and economic history related to GMOs and their use. Through this exploration, students find that most of these lay arguments are not grounded in fact (e.g. GMOs foo d products are not likely to cause cancer or be more nutritious). They also uncover which people and ecosystems benefit (e.g. large-scale agricultural corporations, pesticide companies) and are harmed (e.g. local ecosystems, farmworkers) by GMO farming. Th is unit allows students to engage with the idea of capitalism as a structure, without implicating themselves directly. It also give students an opportunity to engage with the idea that dominant narratives can be used by STEM corporations as a distractor from true harms, nudging them to question Dominant Narrative #4.

A subsequent unit, also related to agriculture, requires students to explicitly wrestle with capitalist and environmentalist worldviews. In the course of this investigation, students who favor capitalist practices have to wrestle with the concept that the current practices of industrial farming are inevitably destroying the soil and will only work in the short term. Students who favor environmentalism, on the other hand, have to wrestle with the social and economic pressure that enable unsustainable agricultural practices (e.g. burning down the rainforest for fertilizer). In the final unit of the year, students quantify the environmental impacts of power generation and other heavy industries, as well as environmental legislation. The goal of this lesson is to have students consider multiple ideologies in deciding what to quantify (e.g. CO2 output, standard of living). Michael’s goal here is to ask students to “add to” rather than “give up” capitalist ideologies. In the final days of the school year, students engage with the question of why it is that BP, an oil company, sponsored and promoted the idea of the individual carbon footprint (Solnit, 2021).  Through this mini unit, students engage deeply with and articulate rationales for and against Dominant Narrative #4.

The design which we describe is an iterative work in progress towards an answer to the question we pose, in Michael’s particular setting. It has gaps; for example, it does not strongly address the entanglements of racism and colonialism with capitalism tha t have caused and exacerbate climate injustice. We view this paper as an invitation for others to think with us about what it means to and how to go about engaging students of economic privilege in learning and taking action towards climate justice.
