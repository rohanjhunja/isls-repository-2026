# “It Goes Both Ways”: Envisioning Mutually Compassionate Relations between Teachers and Students

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Cultivating compassion has been shown to support teachers in caring for non-dominant youth in K-12 schools. While students often show compassion for other students, students’ skillful caring for teachers has remained relatively underexplored. Here, within an intergenerational co-design context with students and their teachers, we explore the pedagogical conditions that support students and teachers to surface and develop the conditions for mutual compassionate relations. Our findings demonstrate that, when used in tandem, a User Persona activity and Theater of the Oppressed Activity supported students and their teachers in adopting compassionate stances towards each others’ suffering, thereby creating the relatively unusual opportunity for teacher participants to vulnerably share their suffering incurred while trying to care for the students. We conclude by showing how cultivating mutual compassionate relations as a goal of co-design spaces can open up expansive objects, including systemic changes motivated by the interlinked suffering of intergenerational stakeholders.

## Introduction

Researchers’ understanding of caring relations in K-12 contexts has evolved over the years, particularl y as researchers have attended to the experiences and ne eds of non-dominant youth (McKinney De Royston et al., 2017; Valenzuela, 1999). These framings have precip itated a shift in understanding toward care as a co ntextdependent, politically-laden negotiation between the carer and the cared-for and more attention to non-dominantyouths’ needs that result from their suffering in K-12 contexts (Dumas, 2014; Garcia, 2019). Further, cultivating compassion has surfaced as a goal in teacher professional lear ning, to foster skillful expressions of politicized care for students (Potvin et al., 2023). Students, too, can express compassion for students, but attention to students’ skillful caring for teachers remains an underexplored topic in the learning sciences and in education research more broadly. As we continue to move away from paternali stic care, it is critical to cultivate authentic co mpassionate relations without both the student (usually presumed to be the one sufferi ng) and the teacher (usually presumed to be the one addressing the suffering) in collecti ve dialogue. However, historically institutionalize d power asymmetries internalized by both teachers and their students complicate these conversations.

In this paper, we sought to create conditions for m ore mutualistic care in a co-design space that was focused primarily on understanding the conditions needed for productive and equitable collaboration in classroom space. As part of a co-design workshop, we facilitated multiple activities with teachers and  students together, in which compassionate responses to teacher suffering emerged among students. And while not an intentiona l goal of our design, we sought to analyze how these responses emerged. We ask in this paper, what pedagogical supports in co-design contexts create opportunities for surfacing and developing the conditions for compassionate relations between teachers and their current students? Second, how do students and teachers negotiate their rol es within these co-design contexts, and what sorts of “expansive objec ts” (Bang & Vossoughi, 2016) emerge as a result of those negotiations?

The contributions of this paper are twofold. First,  we describe how a sequence of co-design activities— a user persona activity and a Theater of the Oppressed (Boal & McBride, 2020) activity—supported teachers and student participants in adopting compassionate stances towards the suffering of each other. Second, we show how student participants’ compassionate stances towa rds teachers in general, even teachers who they per ceived as being uncaring towards students, created the relatively unusual opportunity for teacher participants to vulnerably share their suffering incurred while trying to care for the students. This opened up discussions on creating systemic support for teachers interested in care within scho ols. Finally, we conclude by offering reflections o n the humanizing nature of the interactions and the poten tial implications of supporting compassionate respo nses to suffering for K-12 education more generally.

## Background

We situate this work in the space of ethical and re lational approaches to cooperative and participator y design where we forefront expansive subject-subject relations in the design of expansive object s (Bang & Vossoughi, 2016). In recent years, those approaches have been leveraged in various co-design efforts that guide students and teachers in re-imagining inequitable institutions, yielding new objects or products aimed at promoting equity, such as curriculum, technology,  and professional development (Chang et al., 2024; P enuel, 2019; Roschelle et al., 2010). Along these lines, recent approaches have at tuned researchers to how relationships themselves a re under negotiation within the dynamics of co-design spaces themselves (Bang & Vossoughi, 2016; Chang & Philip, in press). When stakeholders with existing relationships (e.g., teachers and their current students) gather in  the relative unfamiliarity of a co-design context—a temporary boundary practice (Akkerman & Bakker, 2011)—this relational negotiation has the potential to open up expansive objects within new worlds that differ from the status quo (Chang et al., 2024; Potvin et al., 2024).

The learning sciences has been engaged in several t ypes of relational re-imagining of K-12 schools, generating new ways of thinking about caring for students (McKinney De Royston, 2021; Krist, 2024). One way that researchers have sought to create caring relations is to support teachers in developing compassionate routines and practices in their schools that address everyda y forms of suffering there (Potvin et al., 2022), w here compassion is understood as “a sense of concern that arises when we are confronted with another’s suffering and feel motivated to see that suffering relieved” (Jin pa, 2015, p. xxii). Particularly within K-12 contexts, the focus of our study, scholars have demonstrated that suffe ring is particularly acute amongst non-dominant youth, who experience broader oppressive forces amplified into  the organizational context of schools  (Dumas, 201 4). Compassion involves more than generating awareness and empathetic concern for such suffering; it invol ves a movement toward action (Jinpa, 2016). Such actions can include  proposing new possibilities (Engestrom  et al.,

2012) and everyday resistance and restructuring act ivity (Gutierrez et al., 2019). In the co-design spaces that we

created, we hoped to support students and teachers in envisioning how K-12 schools could better support compassionate (and therefore more caring) relations between students and teachers. Like in many other Western organizational contexts,  surfacing compassion in schools can face many barriers. Organizations regularly “systematize, rat ionalize, routinize, and bureaucratize human action  in an attempt to strip away or control emotion that might  interfere with rationality” (Dutton et al., 2006, pg. 60). K-12 educational contexts are no different in that organ izational structures systematically work to rank and reward students and teachers on the basis of (for instance) purportedly objective standardized testing metrics (Au, 2013). Within these organizationally desirable activities,  one way that education researchers have come to un derstand compassion aligned with the goals of schooling is i n terms of the emotional labor the teachers take on  to support their students (Kariou et al., 2021). Teachers, perceiving in interaction some set of emotions (including suffering) from their students, shape their own responses and emotions to advance the learning of students. While emotional labor is not de facto undesirable, it has been connected to rising tea cher burnout (Kariou et al., 2021) as teachers contend with the competing demands of addressing pr essures to raise test scores, organizationally-sanctioned emotional management, and finally, personal interest in students’ holistic well-being in and out of the traditional classrooms (Bodenheimer & Shuster,  2020). Further,  teachers face burnout when they generate empathy f or students but are not able to act to alleviate their suffering, due to limited resources and organizational constraints. Outside of education, research on nursing has conceptualized moral distress as characterizing one’s desire to show compassion but has been inhibited by organizational policies or structures (Morley et al., 2017). To date, there has been little work exploring the mutual expression of compassion of students and teachers for one another. K-12 schools not only create barriers about how compassion is shown, but to whom that compassion is directed towards; there is relatively  little written about the role of students in creat ing compassionate classroom environments (see Johnston et al., 2022, for an exception) despite being perha ps the most direct witnesses to their teachers’ suffering.  We note that conversations about s uffering are distinct from conversations about positive affect (e.g., love, joy) which have been documented  to have been shared between students and adults (Zellers et al. 2014).

In the construction of co-design contexts to envision new possibilities for caring schools we conjectured that engaging intergenerational groups of teachers and their current students together would open up conversations of care that were not commonly found within formal academic contexts, where existing asymmetrical instructional routines tend to dominate (Buttimer, 2018). Our imm ediate goal had been to explore how caring schools might provide for a better context for more productive and equita ble collaboration within classrooms, as this was the wider focus of the project in which we were engaged  (see D’Mello et al., 2024). Through co-design, we sought to surface both teacher and student perspectives on care in classrooms and schools, knowing that they might bring different perspectives on care in schools (Soep, 2006). Zeldin et al. (2012) argue that conditions of reciprocity are crucial towards creating a mutual agenda, which can  benefit youth but also adults (particularly margin alized adults) to share successes and feel belonging (Ginwright, 2005). We also anticipated that creating such conditions would be challenging, because of existing power rel ations. Yip et al. (2017) demonstrated that initial intergenerational design interactions were often in itially unbalanced, with both youth and adults taking the l ead on design depending on the activity; only through a  gradual building up of trusting relationships and familiar design routines did those design interactions becom e more balanced. As we explore here, the activities  we designed to address power imbalances produced an unexpected but welcome result: the emergence of a stance of student compassion for their teachers, which in tur n, deepened teachers’ own reflection on their exper iences and activities of care.

## Case context

We present data from a paid, after school workshop held in Spring 2023. As a part of a large interdisc iplinary institute, the goal of the workshop was to bring students and teachers together to envision possibilit ies and limitations for AI in supporting collaboration in schooling. Held once a week over nine weeks after the end of the school day, it was held in the classroom of one of our teacher participants. The workshop engaged three teachers and 15 high-school aged youth (majority youth of color), all of  whom taught or studied in a public school in the California East Bay. While two of the youth participants had engaged in earlier iterations of this workshop held in previous years, the remainder of the participant s were first-time participants who had not experienced many other opportunities to envision expansive learning possibilities; in fact, one participant shared that he wished our co-design space had been held earlier in his high school career so he could have the language to share his difficult academic experience with others.

Teachers were recruited from an existing network of educators who were vocal with their administrators and colleagues about school decisions, practices, a nd policies that they perceived harmed students of color. To recruit students, we asked participant teachers to send a recruitment flier to students, emp hasizing that we hoped to reach a diverse group of students with varying l evels of affinity and skepticism towards the standa rd instructional practices of schooling. During the recruitment process for students, we continually asked the teachers if student applicants fit that criteria. All 15 par ticipant students had studied under participant teachers for at least two academic semesters. In the weeks leading up to and during the workshop, participant teachers were regularly consulted for, and in many cases, proposed key design activities including the one described later in this paper.

In this paper, we focus on the eighth session of th e workshop, which was significant because the two implemented activities were specifically designed f or students and teachers to step into each others’ shoes and enact each others’ classroom experiences, thereby c reating a space where participants could make visib le their compassion. Leading up to this eighth session, we h ad engaged the participants in several design activ ities that they described as being different from their typica l classroom interactions. These activities, over th e 7 weeks prior, were designed to gradually acclimate student s and teachers to designing with each other in a ba lanced manner (Yip et al., 2017). Weeks 1-3 explored participants’ experience within their existing schools and compared them to radical possibilities for education such as  the Black Panther Liberation Schools. During weeks  4-5, participants envisioned their ideal schools through  design activities using toys and Legos, and in wee ks 6-7, participants learned about AI and how it might be able to support learning within their ideal schools. During this time, we saw students and teachers incr easingly sharing proposals and intimate experiences with each other, even ones that might be seen as conventionally inappropriate (e.g., voicing complaints about one of the teacher participants’ colleagues). Teacher  and student participants regularly voiced their surprise about learning details about each others’ personal and academic experiences. Teacher participants reported to the first author that after the start of the workshop, they had noticed some student participants engage more proactively in their standard classroom activities outside the co-design context. The teacher participants attributed  this change in students’ behavior to the deep relationship buil ding happening in the co-design context in the earlier weeks of the workshops.

Up to our focal week 8, participants frequently mad e a distinction between “caring teachers” and “uncaring teachers.” The student participants were quick to note that the teacher participants were am ongst the caring teachers. Hoping to learn more about the  youths’ perceptions of an “uncaring teacher,” one o f our teacher participants Mr. Harper proposed a user persona activity. User personas, a common practice from User experience design where designers tell stories abou t potential users, are designed to engender empathy  through imaginative activity, but do not necessarily lend themselves to acting on that empathy. Amongst their peers, they constructed narratives about what defines a “caring teacher” versus an “uncaring teacher,” a distinction commonly made by participants up to this point in the workshop. Immediately after the User Persona activity, the participants engaged in a Theatro activity (Boal & McBride, 2020). In a Theatro forum, partici pants act out a pre-arranged script featuring an everyday injust ice, while the audience taps in and replaces the ac tors to experiment with different interventions. The initial Theatro script, which was created by the student participants pri or to the User Persona Activity, told the story of a student petitioning an uncaring teacher to round up their grades.

## Data analysis

After the Theatro Activity described above, one tea cher participant privately shared with facilitators  that they were brought tonear-tears by the students’ uncanny reproduction of the “uncaring” teacher’s behaviors. This vulnerable sharing motivated us to better understand the conditions that led to that response and also to understand how that response may have impacted later envisioning processes. We then began by transcribing video recordings from the workshop. Because we were interested in un derstanding how co-design activities created opportunities for care, we began by deductively coding the data for moments where participants adopted a caring stance towards either for oneself or another. We coded separately for a compassionate stance, which is “a sense of concern that arises when we are confronted with another’s suffer ing and feel motivated to see that suffering reliev ed” (Jinpa, 2015, p. xxii), and for skilled responses to those compassionate stances, which is “how one might translate those aspects of compassion into action that is constructive and beneficial to others and that creates conditions for care to develop more widely in specific communities and organizations” (Potvin et al., 2023). After the initial round of coding, we iteratively refined the codes. First, we separated the skilled responses into two categories, one that was directly enacted in the co-design space (e.g., expressed by one co-design participant to another) and one that was proposed (e.g., a co-design participant describing how they would respond to a compassionate moment). Second, in moments wher e a compassionate stance did not result in any skil led response, we carefully noted who participants deemed as being responsibl e for the experienced suffering, as this framed how a participant would potentially go about addressing  the suffering. Third, we observed that there was a shift in whose suffering was salient and thus wor thy of compassion; thus, we also coded each compass ionate stance for the intended recipient of the comp assion. At times, these stances were layered, e.g.,  teachers demonstrated self-compassion for their personal suffering that occurr ed while advocating and uplifting the suffering of students. These moments of common huma nity represent a particular kind of awareness (of one’s own suffering), one coupled with awareness of how t his kind of suffering is shared by others. Based on  these codes, we wrote analytical memos. Based on these memos, we developed an analytic framework shown in Figure 1, which draws connections between how a school context under design comes to shape the envisioning processes happening within the co-design context.

## Findings

All participant names referenced below are pseudonyms.

User Persona Activity In the first of our activities in week 8, the user persona activity, student and teacher participants each formed separate groups. In the teacher participant group, teacher participants, who at earlier points in the workshop identified themselves and were identified by studen ts as “caring teachers,” characterized caring teach ers by their “knowledge of content” and “foundations of care out side of work,” indicating the support and encourage ment those teachers receive from their families, peers, and colleagues. On the other hand, uncaring teachers were characterized by their academic practices, e.g., his identity is “centered in content. ## Discussion

Here, we have demonstrated how two sequential activities within a co-design context—an User Persona Activity and Theatro Activity—supported intergenerational participants in expressing compassionate stances towards each other and other school actors. We elaborate on the conceptual and empirical implications below. Conceptually, we contribute to the burgeoning theories of compassion in education, and specifically how K-12 organizational contexts structure opportunities for compassion between teachers and students. Our findings draw particular attention towards the mutual nature of compassion, and how unexpected moments of compassion snowball and deconstruct institutional barriers tha t obstruct opportunities to share compassionate sta nces. We connect this finding to the literature on compassio n deservingness, i.e., how individuals come to justify who is deserving of receiving help (Gross & Wronski, 2021). As Yu et al. (2023) argue, a bias about a particular groups’ intentions or qualities can guide decisions about d eservingness. In the context of our findings, we in ferred that teacher participants felt that “uncaring teachers” (and perhaps teachers in general) were undeserving of students’ compassion. Students’ compassion towards uncaring t eachers moved teacher participants, and later creat ed the context for teacher part icipants to share their own suffering incurred whil e advocating for students —suffering which they previously had not shared. While these i nteractions happened in the liminal space of a co-design contexts, our findings suggest that deservingness i n schools often creates opportunities for teachers showing compassion for students but not the other way aroun d; this unidirectional understanding of compassion is to the detriment of all. Theories of compassion (and relat edly, care) must carefully draw more explicit conne ctions between organizational contexts and the directionality of compassionate relations. Empirically, we build on Chang et al. (2024), who demonstrate that expansive objects may emerge from re-envisioning hierarchical relations in the context of schools. Going into the Theatro Activity, youth had already developed the DataBox tool, a student facing tool. In contrast, through the Theatro Activity, the mutu al understanding of compassion that had bubbled up pre viously in the User Persona activity set up more ex pansive uses for the DataBox, where both students and a hypothetical “uncaring” teacher, use it to advocate for their own suffering within their shared institutional context. We refer to this as a tool for supporting compass ion, a Compassionate Solidarity Tool, a tool that supports  students and teachers in seeking to address each o thers’ suffering, within and between different classroom contexts. These finding s also provide a starting point to understand the available resources that students ac cess in order to design organizational conditions t hat help to address teacher suffering. Student par ticipants primarily demonstrated intent to address their  teachers’ suffering by conceiving of economic and legal solutions (e.g., increasing teacher pay through supporting their o ngoing strike). While a powerful shift, future work must m ore deeply explore how to support youth in deep exploration of the organizational conditions of mutual compassion beyond these capitalistic framings. Overall, expanding the “object” of co-design to mutually compassionate relations between students and teachers helped to re-frame the transformative proposals (Gutierrez et al., 2019) envisioned in the co-design space. In our workshop space, teacher participants shared their invisible work of caring for students across sites of activity in schools, not to guilt students or to shut down the proposals of student participants, but because student participants like Emory invited compassionate practices into the  co-design context. Limited opportunities for mutual compassion serve to invisibilize the intra-organizational caring practices that already happen within and across classrooms. Our findings offer hope that, in  many cases, the seeds for caring schools have alre ady been planted in the agentic, silent efforts of caring te achers. Taken together, in our efforts to create th e conditions for expansive caring schools, educational spaces that normalize  compassionate stances across adult-youth hierarchy like the one described here can open the door to systemic change-making possibilities, guided and grounded by sweat and tears that are too often stowed away with the best of intentions.
