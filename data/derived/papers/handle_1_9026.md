# Centering Ethics in AI Education: Cutting Through the Controversy

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Current efforts teaching the ethical implications of AI neglect current dilemmas rooted in biases. We propose two design considerations for curriculum designers in this area: embedding ethics across the curriculum and foregrounding authentic problems facing the AI field. Our proposals are compared to a responsible AI curriculum we are helping build to explore the implications of each approach to ethics in AI.

## Introduction

As encounters with Artificial Intelligence (AI) subside from the surreal and remarkable to the ordinary and mundane, a new literacy is emerging. How are we to make sense of these modern marvels we interact with everyday, that shape our lives in powerful ways? AI technologies answer questions I pronounce in my kitchen, decide if I qualify for a credit card, and scan my face to access my phone. Yet the technology appears impervious to inspection, shielded in a black box, suggesting no questions need be asked. But the consequences of these ubiquitous technologies are not neutral nor fair, but biased and ethically fraught. To prepare a public to contend with the ramifications of our increasingly AI-driven world, education researchers have called for students to learn not only the technical knowledge and skills related to AI, but the societal implications (Zhang et al., 2021; Lao, 2021). In response, lessons on AI have been designed and implemented for children as young as 4 (Ali, Payne, Williams et al., 2020).

However, current efforts addressing the ethical dimensions of AI in education fall short of realizing their promise. These nascent endeavors focus on identifying stakeholders and their values to explain AI as socio-technical systems (DiPaolo et al., 2020), products of design agendas and biased datasets. But learners are presented with scenarios disconnected from real-world dilemmas— cats, dogs, and peanut butter and jelly sandwiches figure prominently (Ali, Payne, Williams et al., 2020)—and neglect how AI design decisions may cause harm. Our project intends to fill the gaps in this freshly poured foundation. As part of a team building a responsible AI curriculum for middle schoolers, we are envisioning a new set of principles centering ethical thinking in the design, teaching, and learning of AI in school. We propose ethics must be embedded, with authentic problems facing the AI field foregrounded. This poster begins to explore why ethical thinking must be woven into and emphasized throughout AI curricula, and how ethical dilemmas should be approached. Roots of Bias in AI Ruha Benjamin (2019) explains how “[w]ith emerging technologies we might assume that racial bias will be more scientifically rooted out. Yet, rather than challenging or overcoming the cycles of inequity, technical fixes too often reinforce and even deepen the status quo.” Indeed, it is the rising awareness of systemic bias, of unfair decisions made by humans making up an organization, that results in outsourcing organizational processes and practices to AI. In AI we trust, may the logic read. However, outsourcing decision-making that is historically biased to AI simply embeds the social bias into a technical artifact. This can be seen across time, whereby dehumanization caused by AI today echoes a legacy of oppression championed in the sciences. For example, an AI technology used in the U.S. criminal justice system to predict recidivism rates incorrectly flagged black defendants as likely to commit future crimes at rates nearly double that of white defendants (ProPublica, 2016). Bonilla-Silva & Zuberi (2008) remind us “that the founder of statistical analysis also develop[ing] a theory of White supremacy is not an accident. The founders developed statistical analysis to explain the racial inferiority of colonial and second-class citizens in the new imperial era” (p. 5). That is to say, scientific progress and technological innovation often act in tandem with logics of domination, particularly White Supremacy and anti-Black racism (Gould, 1996).

Teaching how image classification works should occur in a context connected to its use by industries and effects on consumers. Datasets using cats and dogs may be effective at teaching the technical aspects of image classification, but essentially ignores the sociological, and presents a problem of transfer for students expected to discuss ethical issues when their hands-on engagement revolves around cute animals. Our second design consideration,  embedding ethica l thinking content throughout the curriculum, intends to better reflect the AI-creation pipeline, where decisions with significant ethical consequences occur every step of the way (Suresh & Guttag, 2021). Learners need to be confronted with scenarios that allow them to leverage their prior knowledge to make meaning about how AI impacts their lives, with the goal of helping them understand how they can affect change in their communities. Currently, little research exists regarding the te aching and learning of AI and its inherent dilemmas. Based on their literature review, Long and Magerko (2020) identify ethics as one of 17 key competencies substantiating AI literacy. Based on their literature review of 150 documents, only two intersected AI with curriculum design for non-technical learners and ethics. Thus, ethics is an imperative component of a robust AI education, while a body of research that could help direct efforts integrating this competency into AI curricula remains almost nonexistent.

Applications: The case of RAICA curriculum Responsible Artificial Intelligence for Computational Action (RAICA) is a design-based research project to develop and research a project-based AI curriculum for middle school students in the U.S. and Dubai. Our overarching research question asks how can we transform students from AI consumers to conscientious AI contributors? A curriculum module spans 20-25 lessons, 50 minutes a piece. Students learn core AI concepts, such as supervised learning and natural language processing, by creating a technical solution (using simple coding tools such as Scratch) that leverage AI technologies (like image and audio classification). The curriculum designers struggle with the question of controversy—-i.e., How can we include real ethical dilemmas that might be controversial without damaging the sense of safety for teachers and students? For example, we adapted the activity featuring cats and dogs to explain image classification, but noted how detached from real world dilemmas this technology is implicated in: Black people being labeled gorillas or primates by Google and Facebook (Mac, 2021; BBC, 2015); deep neural networks detecting sexual orientation based on a subject's face (Wang & Konsinski, 2018); and military applications, such as Project Maven, which intended to identify people and objects in drone footage (Wakabayashi & Shane, 2018). The RAICA curriculum bookends ethical questions, s ans scaffolds between understanding bias with datasets featuring cats and dogs and what that can mean when applied to humans. We propose for future iterations to embody these two design considerations to present learners first and foremost with scenarios based on real-world dilemmas. We will then investigate the impact these proposed principles have on students’ grasp of AI’s ethical implications compared to the initial implementation. Considering transfer, we expect students to have a richer understanding of how AI shapes their world and how ethical considerations apply across different AI technologies.
