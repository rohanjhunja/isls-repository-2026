# Exploring Out-of-Tutor Interventions by Teachers in Supporting Student Learning with AI Tutors

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Learning analytics has embraced the complexities of modeling student learning and engagement in ecological settings like K12 classrooms. While analyzing AI tutor interaction logs remains common, there is a growing need to include events that happen outside the AI tutor. Studies indicate teachers play active roles when students use AI tutors, and teacher support tools improve learning outcomes. However, methodological challenges exist in studying how such out-of-tutor interventions in the classroom shape learning (e.g., no counterfactual data, ethical issues in collecting control data). We propose a simple reconceptualization of the problem with which we could use standard statistical approaches to quantify the relationship between out-of-tutor interventions and student learning in the AI tutor. We study how moments of teacher help relate to improvements in students' performance on the skill or concept they were helped with. Our analysis reveals that teacher help has a significantly positive effect.

## Introduction

AI tutoring systems are becoming increasingly common in K-12 classrooms, where teachers and AI systems work together to support student learning. Past studies suggest that teachers play an active role in such A I-enabled classrooms, moving from one student to another, providing individualized help (Schofield et al., 1994) and socioemotional support (van Leeuwen & Rummel, 2022), and helping them get out of an unproductive rut (Holstein & Aleven, 2022). Learning analytics research in these classrooms has traditionally focused on analyzing the log data of students' interaction with the AI tutor (Karumbaiah et al., 2018). Past studies have built complex computational models of several learning constructs such as stude nt cognition, affect, motivation, and self-regulated learning (Nye et al., 2017; Aleven et al., 2004). However, these models often overlook crucial events occurring outside the direct student-AI interaction – what we term "out-of-tutor events" – that may significantly influence learning outcomes. These include interactions with teachers, teaching assistants, and peers that shape how students engage with and learn from AI tutors.

To illustrate the importance of out-of-tutor events, consider help-seeking behaviors in AI-enabled classrooms (e.g., Aleven et al., 2004). Looking only at the interaction log data may give us an incomplete picture. Along with measures of students' hint usage in the AI tutors, we need to analyze students' help-seeking outside the AI tutor (e.g., seeking help from the teacher or a peer) to capture this learning construct better. Moreover, outof-tutor interactions are likely more common than curr ently acknowl edged in learning analytics research (Karumbaiah et al., 2021). Past research has report ed how AI tutors designed to be used one-to-one get used in diverse ways in practice (e.g., collaboratively with peers in some cultures; Ogan et al., 2012). Accordingly, there is a growing need for novel analysis methods that intentionally incorporate such realworld complexities in their modeling paradigms. For  example, if students are making good progress in t heir learning with an AI tutor, what in-the-moment teacher practices may be contributing to it? Like wise, if students are not improving in their learning with an AI tutor, could there be distracting out-of-tutor events that the teacher in the classroom can address? Answering these questions is important for several reasons. First, it helps us gain a better understanding of factors that affect student  learning in AI-enabled classrooms, including the cumulative effects of multiple learning activities, which can be surprisingly strong (Holstein et al., 2018). Second, it would help us identify and further support effective clas sroom practices and mitigate ineffective ones. For example, along with student analytics, teacher dashboards co uld include data on out-of-tutor events to provide data-driven evidence for  effective interventions. Third, it would facilitate  considering the role of out-of-tutor events in assessing the AI tutor's effectiveness in real-world contexts. Lastly, AI tutor design could be informed accordingly to work more synergistically with teach ers' practices and classroom environment. However, there are methodological challenges that make it difficult to study these out-of-tutor interventions with current exploratory analytics methods.

To this end, we propose a simple reconceptualization of the problem that affords using standard statistical approaches (e.g., t-test) to test hypotheses spontaneously. Specifically, we leverage process data (i.e., AI tutor log data) to measure changes to s tudents' learning after moments of the out-of-tutor intervention and compare it to students who didn't receive such intervention. We d emonstrate this approach for a specific out-of-tutor intervention, that is, teacher help giving. Past research suggests that teachers (and their short, one-on-one helping sessions with individual students; Holstein & Aleve n, 2022) have a substantial impact on student learn ing in classes that use AI-based tutors. Hence, we are interested in testing t he following hypothesis with our proposed methodology: teacher help is positively related to improvements in student performance. We use teacher help data collected through field observations by human annot ators ph ysically present in the classroom, which is then temporally mapped to students' interaction with the AI tutor. Our data comes from three classrooms taught by two teachers who differ in their help giving tendencies. We present a detailed discussion of our findings and their implications for research on out-of-tutor interventions. We discuss how such research could inform the design of AI tutors and teacher support tools that intentionally supports human-AI partnered teaching in K12 classrooms.

## Methods

In this paper, we analyze qualitative observations of teacher helping from three classrooms to analyze  how it relates to improvements in student learning with th e AI tutor. These data were previously collected in  a 2016 study (Holstein et al., 2017). Human  annotators physically present in the classroom used  the LookWhosTalking application to code the timestamped instances of te acher help and student hand raises. Teacher help is  coded in those instances when the teacher is actively assisting a student in the activity they are working on in the AI tutor. Note that two of these classes (hereon referred to as Taylor1 and Taylor2) are taught by one teacher (pseudonym: Taylor) and the third by a different teacher (pseudonym: Kusha). 48 middle school students across the three classes used Lynette (Long & Aleven, 2014), an algebra AI t utor, for 60 minutes to learn equation solving. The teachers didn't have access to any teacher tools or dashboards. Hence, their decision to help a student is mainly determined by their direct observations of student work with the AI tut or, while walking around to monitor student progres s and by students' requests for help, guidance, and feedback. ## Analysis and results

Our analysis tests the following hypothesis: teacher help is positivel y related to improvements in student performance. It is non-trivial to determine if teacher help helped their s tudents since this effect needs to be separated from the improvement students are already  having through their practice with the AI tutors. In other words, even if a student improves after receiving h elp from their teacher, it would be incorrect to co nclude that the improvement happened because of the teacher's assistance rather than the student's practice. In an ideal scenario, we would have data on the stu dents' learning trajectories with and without teach er help, and we could compare both and see if the teac her's involvement made a difference. Since that wou ld be impossible to get for the same skill (with the same starting point), we chose to compare the p erformance change of the student who received help to the rest of the  class. To simplify, we assume that a teacher can h elp only one student at a time, and that the rest of the class does not receive any assistance during this period. Unlike traditional classrooms, teacher help in classrooms with AI tuto rs tends to be one-to-one (Holstein, Schofield). Since all the students in this data set are working on the same problem sets, we take the average improvement of the rest of the class as a proxy for the case where a student did n ot receive teacher help for that skill around that point of the learning trajectory. We discuss a few limitations of these assumptions later.

Using this general idea, we go through each case of  teacher help and calculate the improvement to students after they receive help. We define improve ment as the change in a student's average correctne ss before and after teacher help, as calculated using the formula in Equation 1. For each case, we also compute the average improvement of the rest of the class before and after the time this student was helped. We only consider data from the same class and same session. Before and after h as no qualitative meaning for the rest of the class since there is no teacher intervention at that time, and that's precisely the point: we want to have a proxy for the improvement in learning from practicing with the AI tutor, without teacher assistance. Improvement= (After-Before)/(1-Before)                (1) We also assume that when teachers assist students with a particular problem, they are assisting them with a specific skill or concept (examples from this data include c ombining constant terms and computing quotient for constant to solve math equations) rather than problem-solving in general. Thus, when we calculate improve-ment, we only do so for the skill or concept for which the student received assistance. Most AI tutors tend to map each problem or activity to the concept that it is associated wi th, often to model student learning (Corbett & Anderson, 1 994). By synchronizing teacher help time with AI tutor log data time, we infer which concept the student was working on when they received the help. We discuss the implications of this assumption in the discussion section. In this analysis, we only consider those transactio ns that belong to students entering a response to a practice problem. We focus on the responses for the concept for which the student received help (Concept_Y). To calculate improvements for the helped students and the rest of the class, we need before and after for each. Hence, we categorize the relevant student responses in a single class session into four groups:

1. All responses to Concept_Y of the student who received help (Student_X) before receiving the help

2. All responses to Concept_Y of the student who received help (Student_X) after receiving the help

1. All responses to Concept_Y of all other students before Student_X received help

2. All responses to Concept_Y of all other students after Student_X received help

Once we have these four groups, we find average cor rectness in each and calculate improvements for the  helped student and the rest of the class using equation 1. We do this for each instance of teacher help, and we end up with two lists: one with improvements of helped students and one with corresponding i mprovements of the rest of the class. These lists are of size equal to the number of teacher help instances. For teacher help to be effective, students receiving teacher help should improve more than the  others. Hence, we expect the mean of the first lis t to be higher than the mean of the second. In the final st ep of the analysis, we compare the means of both li sts and perform a Welch’s t-test to see if the difference is statistically significant. Table 3 presents the results of our analysis. Irres pective of the performance measure used, we can see that the improvement in performance is statisticall y significantly higher in the case where the studen t received teacher help than the rest of the class that used the AI tutor to learn that concept at that time. These results support our hypothesis that a teacher’s active help-giving has a significant positive relationship with  students’ subsequently performing better in their class.

## Discussion

Learning analytics research is increasingly incorpo rating ways to model student learning in contextual ly rich, authentic settings like K12 classrooms. Although an alyzing student interaction logs from AI tutors con tinues to be a predominant practice, there is a growing need to include events that happen out side the AI tutor to fully understand how student learning is being shaped in real-world settings. There are several methodological challenges in systematically studying the roles of the multitude of out-of-tutor events happening in the physical classroom (e.g., no counterfactual data, ethical issues in collecting control data). To overcome some of these, we devise a new approach to collect data for the control group (i.e., those who didn’t experience the out-of-tutor event at that point in time) within the process log data from AI tutors. This allows us to conduct exploratory hypothesis testing on how multimodal proxies of out-of-tutor events from the physical classroom relate to student learning and engagement with the AI tutor. In this study, we use human observations of teacher help-giving as a proxy and test how this out-of-tutor teacher intervention relates to improvements in student learning. Our analysis reveals that teacher help has a significantly positive effect. We use this use case to demonstrate how the simple statistical approach proposed in the study could be used to explore any timestamped multimodal proxy of out-of-tutor events with respect to student engagement and learning in the AI tutor. There are several theoretical and practical significance to the methodology proposed in this paper. First, it affords new research directions to generate a mo re complex scientific understanding of how students  learn in AI-enabled classrooms in a relatively quicker way. Past approaches in studying out-of-tutor events relied mostly on collecting pre-and post-test scores and worked with a small set of variable s. While such approaches lead to more generalizable results, they are not suitable f or early explorat ory research that is needed to work with the large space of multimodal proxies of out-of-tutor events in contextually rich real-world settings such as K12 classrooms. Second, it contributes to the growing e vidence of the critical role teachers play in AI-enabled classrooms. Third, in a more practical sense, such research on out-of-tutor events could help identify ways to design better teacher support for orchestration of AI-enabled classrooms. Lastly, such methods could faci litate considering the role of ou t-of-tutor events in assessing the AI tutor’s effectiven ess in real-world contexts. Furthermore, AI tutor design could be informed to work more synergistically with teachers’ practices. There are several limitations to the exploratory ap proach presented in the paper. First, we make certa in assumptions in the method proposed, which needs to be tested further for its impact on the results.  For instance, we assume that when teachers assist students with a particular problem, they are assist ing them with a specific skill or concept rather than problem-solving in general. We assume that the concept bein g helped is the same as the one a student was working on in the AI tutor when they were helped by the teacher. In most cases, this shouldn't pose a problem since teachers often provide proacti ve help while monitoring (Schofield et al., 1994). Next, one methodological issue in studying out-of-tutor events that is yet to be addressed is the teacher selection effect. Is it possible that the large difference between helped and non-helped students we observe is due to a selection effect? So, perhaps the next step in this work should be to control for the before score.

In conclusion, our study tries to address the need for suitable quantitative approaches that would all ow us to explore the large variable space of out-of-tutor events in real-world settings. More broadly, the proposed approach has theoretical and practical  implications as it affords practitioners to analyze  rapid changes in their classroom pedagogical practices and researchers to test many hypotheses on how students learn in AI-enabled classrooms. Although not discussed here, there are other interesting research problems in the context of out-oftutor events (e.g., how do we predict student performance in the presence of out-of-tutor events).
