# Learning in Vicarious Failure: How Intermediate Knowledge Links Prior Knowledge Activation And Conceptual Knowledge

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In Vicarious Failure, students prepare for learning from subsequent instruction by studying other students’ erroneous solution attempts. This preparation activates prior knowledge, which is assumed to help students process the instruction, but it is unclear how it affects students’ learning from activating to building conceptual knowledge during instruction. We analyzed students’ learning by assessing their knowledge based on the number of conceptual features of a targeted concept at three points in time: during the preparation phase, in an intermediate test before instruction, and in a post-test after instruction. We hypothesized that the effect of students’ prior knowledge activation on their learning from instruction would be mediated by their intermediate knowledge, and expected that students’ knowledge of conceptual features would remain consistent from one phase to the next. While no mediation effect emerged, students’ knowledge mostly remained consistent from one point in time to the next.

## Introduction

Instructional designs that encourage students to de al with an unknown problem prior to receiving expli cit instruction have been shown to foster students’  conceptual knowledge (Loibl et al., 2017). In Vicar ious Failure (VF) settings, students study and analyze erroneous  solution attempts in an initial preparation phase and receive explicit instruction in a subsequent instruction phase (Kapur, 2014a). VF is modeled after Productive Failure (PF), an instructional setting in which students generate  solution att empts themselves in a preparation phase prior to instruction. Thus, in VF, students analyze solution attempts that  were either generated by other students prior to instruction or that resemble typical solution attempts students would g enerate in a PF setting (Hartmann et al., 2020; Kapur, 2014a). PF and VF settings have been f ound to be comparable with respect to their underlying cognitive processes during the preparation phase and learning outcomes after the instruction phase (Brand et al., 2021; Hartmann et al., 2021, 2022). We assume that dealing with erroneous solution attempts in both PF and VF prepares students for conceptual learning by activating prior knowledge. During this preparation phase, students likely build intermediate knowledge that helps them to process new information during the instruction phase more deeply (Loibl et al., in press). However, detailed insights into  the mechanism underlying prior knowledge activation and how it fosters students’ learning across the preparation and instruction  phase is still lacking, especially for VF settings.

Following Loibl and colleagues’ (in press) call to systematically examine underlying learning mechanism across the two phases via students’ intermediate knowledge, the present paper investigates students’ learning in VF in order to learn more about the preparatory mechanism of prior knowledge activation. In contrast to PF setti ngs, in which students generate varying  solution attempts  by themselves, VF settings open up the opportunity to investigate students’ preparation for learning in a more controlled manner, by providing all students with the same solution attempts to study (Loibl et al., in press). Specifically, we examine how students’ knowledge develops at three points in time across the VF phases, from the (1) activation of prior knowledge  during the preparation phase to (2) intermediate knowledge  in an intermediate test before instruction, and (3) conceptual knowledge in a post-test after instruction. At each of the three points in time, we assess students’ knowledge on the concept of variance according to the completeness of their knowledge, t hat is the number of conceptual features of the targeted concept that students mention. Preparation for subsequent learning through vicarious failure In VF, prior knowledge activation is assumed to be a vit al mechanism for preparing students to learn during subsequent instruction. To make sense of the solution attempts analyzed during VF, students need to activate their prior knowledge (Kapur & Bielazyc, 2012; Kapur, 2014a, 2014b). While the solution attempts are erroneous by themselves (Loibl et al., 2017), they often address partial concepts of the targeted solu tion (i.e., conceptual features). Accordingly, the prior knowledge activated by the students includes elements of the targeted concept. Which features of the targeted concept are addressed and thus activated depends on which solution attempts students receive. The activated prior knowledge is assumed to prepare stu dents for subsequent instruction (Loibl et al., 2017): It is likely to remain activated and  to be represented as initial, preliminary intermediate knowledge (Loibl et al., in press). While students lack the necessary knowledge to solve the given problem on their own and have therefore not yet acquired canonical knowledge, the preliminary intermediate knowledge helps them process the subsequent instruction more easily (Loibl et al., 2017, 2023). Previous studies have found an association between the breadth of conceptually different solution attempts and students’ subsequent learning (e.g., Kapur, 2015; Wiedmann et al., 2012), insofar as students who activated prior knowledge by generating their own solut ions in a broad manner seemed to be better prepared for learning from subsequent instruction. However, this evidence was only correlational in nature, and the studies did not investigate in detail how and which conceptual features students represented in their intermediate knowledge before instruction, or how this facilitated their learning during instr uction. Assuming that students’ prior knowledge activation is represented in their intermediate knowledge, those who activate broad prior knowledge will likely build more profound intermediate knowledge, including a broader range of concepts, which aligns better with subsequent instruction (Loibl et al., in press). As congruence between students’ prior knowledge and the targeted knowledge has been found to facilitate learning (Bein et al., 2015; Brod, 2021), students' intermediate knowledge should act as a bridge upon which students’ knowledge acquisition can buil d. Yet, congruence can only occur if students activate relevant knowledge: Kapur (2015)  found that students who generated solution attempts that were relevant to the concept to be learned outperformed students whose attempts were less relevant. Accordingly, one might assume that students are only prepared for learning from instruction if they activate prior knowledge that specifically relates to the targeted concept, for instance by addressing specific conceptual features. A popular VF task targets the mathematical concept of variance, which requires four conceptual features that together build the  concept to be learned. One of these features is the requirement  to include all given data points in order to assess the consistency of distri butions (e.g., Kapur, 2014a). When students activat e prior knowledge relevant to this conceptual feature (e.g., studying a solution attempt that visualizes the distribution of data using all data points), they represent knowledge of this feature in their intermediate knowledge, which  then helps them to understand the subsequent instruction on this feature (i.e., why all data points have to be inclu ded to assess consistency). The more conceptual features students address in their broad, but relevant, prior knowledge activation, the better their preparation for subsequent instruction due to a more profound intermediate knowledge targeting the specific features taught during the instruction. In a VF setting, a broad knowledge activation c ould be specifically manipulated by selecting solution attempts that together cover many different relevant conceptual features of the targeted concept, from the range of solution attempts that students would normally gene rate on their own (i.e., in a PF setting).

## Research questions and hypotheses

We expect ed that students who activated broad and relevant prior knowledge, which was represented in their intermediate knowledge before instruction, would be better prepared for learning. A large breadth of activation is achieved by studying solution attempts that cover several conceptual features of a targeted concept in a VF setting. Students’ intermediate knowledge is assumed to func tion as a bridge between the  prior knowledge activation during the preparation phase and learning during the instruction phase. In the present paper, we investigate students’ learning in the VF design focusing on the role of students’ intermediate knowledge for the relation between pri or knowledge activation and conceptual knowledge acquisition from instruction. We open up the following questions: (1) On a lowe r granularity,  does students’ intermediate knowledge mediate how their prior know ledge activation during the preparation phase facilitates their learning from instruction, and (2) on a higher granularity, do students build knowledge on conceptual features consistently from the preparation to the instructio n phase ? That is, do students show intermediate knowledge before instruction on the same features for which t hey activated prior knowledge during the preparation phase, and are features represented in intermediate knowledge learned better during the instruction phase? In the course of a larger  2x2 experimental study, w e manipulated a set of five solution attempts with regard to their coverage of conceptual features of the targeted concept (high versus low coverage). High coverage included all four conceptual features of the targeted concept of variance, entailing a broad activation of knowledge, whereas low coverage only included half of the conc eptual features, leading to a more limited knowledge activation. To assess students’ intermediate knowledge before i nstruction, we employed an intermediate knowledge test, which only half of the students received in order to control for the testing effect (Rowland, 2014). To test our research questions, we conducted two an alyses (see Figure 1). In analysis 1, we described students’ learning in a mediation model. We hypothe sized that students’ intermediate knowledge would mediate the effect of their prior knowledge activation on their conceptual knowledge after instruction (H1; indirec t mediation effect ab) when studying solution attempts that vary regarding  the coverage of conceptual features relevant to the targeted concept (high versus low coverage). In analysis 2, we examined students’ knowledge of conceptual features (present versus absent) at three points in time across the VF phases: during the preparation phase, in an intermediate test before instruction, and in a post-test after instruction.  We expected that students’ intermediate knowledge would show the same conceptual features that they h ad studied during the preparation phase (H2a) and that students would show the same, or more, conceptual features in a post-test after instruction compared to their intermediate knowledge (H2b).

## Participants and design

The data analyzed in the present paper are part of a larger study  with secondary school students. In the present analyses, we use the data from two out of four conditions that implemented an intermediate knowledge test (N = 83; age: M = 16.68; gender: 59% identified as female, 41% as male). These conditions differed in the coverage of conceptual features: low coverage (LoCov: n = 41) versus high coverage (HiCov: n = 42). The participants were randomly assigned to the conditions.

## Materials and procedure

The materials and procedure of the study are based on previous PF and VF studies and focused on teachi ng secondary school students the concept of variance  (e.g., Loibl & Rummel, 2014b; Kapur, 2015). The study was conducted in a classroom of an out-of-school lab over one day, divided into two learning phases: the preparation and instruction phase. Before the preparation phase, we assessed students’ incoming  characteristics, including their prior knowledge, mathematical ability, and mathemat ical self-concept. To assess prior knowledge, students completed a test on descriptive statistics, which tested prerequisites for the targeted conce pt of variance (e.g., calculating the mean or interpreting graphical repr esentations). To capture students’ mathematical ability, we recorded their two most recent grades in mathematics (Kapur et al., 2023). To assess students’ mathematical selfconcept, we administered a questionnaire including items on students’ beliefs about their mathematical skills and achievements, rated on a 6-point Likert scale (Rost & Sparfeld, 2002). These three student characteristics  have been frequently used as control variables in previous PF and VF studies, as they have been found to affect students’ learning (e.g., Kapur et al. 2023; Loibl & Rummel, 2014b). During the preparation phase, which included the experimental manipulation, students in the two conditions each studied a different set of erroneous solution attempts for 40  minutes, with the high coverage (HiCov) set showing a high number of conceptual features and the low coverage (L oCov) set showing a low number. Both sets comprised five solutions that were incomplete or erroneous by themselves: For instance, one solution attempt might display a conceptual feature such as showing deviat ions from the mean  but without revealing the canonical solution (i.e., the formula  of variance). Across all solution attempts, t he HiCov group received solution attempts that toge ther included all four conceptual features of the targeted solution, while the LoCov group received solution attempts that together included two features. After studying the solution attempts  and before instruction, the students underwent an intermediate knowledge test, in which they wrote  down how they would have solved the task. The intention was to measure the conceptual features,  previously activated during  the preparation phase, which were included in students’ intermediate knowledge. We expected students to write down solution attempts showing the conceptual features that they had previously studied. We coded the sum of described conceptual features (0-4 points) in the solution(s) as intermediate knowledge.

During the instruction phase of the study, students received teacher-led classroom instruction on the conceptual features of variance. Finally, they underwent a conceptual knowledge post-test measuring their general understanding of the targeted concept and their knowledge of each feature. Statistical analysis In the present paper, we analyzed students’ learning from prior knowledge activation during the preparation phase, via intermediate knowledge, to conceptual knowledge in the post-test using (1) a mediation analysis and (2) a descriptive analysis of students’ represented conceptual features during three points in time: during the preparation phase, in an intermediate test before instruction, and in a post-test after instruction. For the mediation analysis, we used a simple mediation model  with intermediate knowledge  as the mediator, condition as dichotomous independent variable (LoCov vs. HiCov group), and students ’ overall conceptual knowledge  after instruction as  dependent variable (Hayes, 20 22: model 4). We tested whether  the control variables prior knowledge, mathematical self-concept, and mathematical ability correlated significantly with the dependent variable and, if so, added them to the mediation model as covariates. The mediation analysis was conducted using the SPSS macro PROCESS (Hayes, 2022; mediation model 4, 5000 bootstrap samples, 95% bootstrap confidence intervals, alpha level = 5%).

For the analysis of students’ conceptual features, we coded features as absent (=0) or present (=1) at each point in time across the VF phases: during the preparation phase, in t he intermediate test before instruction, and in the conceptual post-test after instruction. To represent students’ activated prior knowledge, we counted the conceptual features that students had studied based  on the experimental manipulation  during the preparation phase. To measure students’ intermediate knowledge, we coded the features that students had written down in the intermediate test before instruction. For students’ conceptual knowledge of features after instruction, we coded the correctly described features in the conceptual post-test after instruction.

Using the 0/1 coding, we analyzed patterns of students’ learning for the respective successive points in time across the VF phases. That is, each of the fou r features was either present or absent  during the preparation phase (Prep: 1 or 0) and in the intermediate knowledge test (Inter: 1 or 0), yielding combinations of 0/1 codings for each of the features (Prep-Inter: 1&1, 0&0, …), and similarly from the intermediate test (Inter) to the post-test (Post) (Inter-Post). We then calculated the frequencies of each pattern (combinations of 1&1, 0&0, … for Prep-Inter and Inter-Post) across all four features, yieldi ng the number of times students had retained, gained or lost a conceptual feature from one point in time to the next (see Table 2 for pattern meanings).

## Discussion and conclusion

The present analys es investigated students’ learning in VF in two ways: first, using a mediation model of the effect of students’ prior knowledge activation, via intermediate knowledge, on their overall conceptual knowledge, and second, by analyzing the presence or absence of knowledge of conceptual features at three points in time across the VF  phases. We were unable to  confirm our mediation hypothesis: Students’ intermediate knowledge did not mediate the effect of students’ activated prior knowledge on their conceptual knowledge, nor did students’ breadth of prior knowledge affect stu dents’ intermediate knowledge significantly. However, in line with our expectation, the conceptual features shown in students’ intermediate knowledge  most frequently corresponded to the features  upon which they had previous ly activated prior knowledge. Nevertheless, a substantial proportion of  students did not show the  previously studied  features in the intermediate test  and regardless of activating prior knowledge with a low  or high coverage, students only showed int ermediate knowledge with a low coverage of less than two features. This failure of retention was especially dominant in the HiCov group and might explain the lack of difference in conceptual knowledge between conditions. The LoCov group might also have closed the gap by engaging in more sense-making and thus gaining features that they had not previously studied.

Since our data showed that some students retained f eatures from the preparation phase until the subsequent intermediate knowledge test more easily than others, the question arises of how and under which circumstances intermediate knowledge is formed thro ugh prior knowledge activation. We argued that stud ents’ intermediate knowledge is an outcome of their activation of prior knowledge when they make sense of the solution attempts (see Kapur & Bielazyc, 2012). But studying solution sets with a higher coverage of co nceptual features alone might not actually prepare students better fo r instruction. This corresponds to the findings of  Loibl and Rummel (2014b), who demonstrated that providing stu dents with guidance to generate higher-quality solution attempts (i.e., one solution that combines more conceptual features) did not improve their conceptual knowledge after instruction compared to a group without guidance. Rather, the depth of knowledge activation, which results from a more extensive knowledge activation, could be one potential influe nce facilitating retention of features and possibly learning. If students analyze and make sense of the solution attempts, they might form assumptions regarding which solution attempts may or may not help them to find the correct solution (also sometimes referred to as negative knowledge, see Gartmeier et al., 2008; Loibl et al., in press). Therefore, the more explicit and elaborated these assumptions are, the greater the d epth of students’ intermediate knowledge, and the m ore likely they may be to  represent conceptual features in their intermediate  knowledge. However, as we only expect students to fully understand the conceptual feature s of the targeted concept after instruction (Loibl et al. 2017), students’ knowledge activation and depth of interme diate knowledge likely varies a great deal, from very rudimentary to more explicit ideas  (see Loibl et al., in press). Moreover, students may find it more difficult to express rudimentary knowledge in an intermediate knowledge test, thus potentially obstructing knowledge that might have helped them to process the subsequent instruction. While some processes during the preparation phase, such as gaining an understanding of a task’s goal (e.g. describe consistency in data) have been found to be helpful for students’ preparation for learning (Brand et al., 2018), future research needs to conduct process analyses unravelling processes that might support students’ depth of prior knowledge activation as well as the formation of intermediate knowledge.

Regarding students’ learning from the intermediate test to the post-test after instruction, we did not find an effect of students’ intermediate knowledge on overall conceptual knowledge in our mediation model. However, the analysis of conceptual features provided further evidence of a link between knowledge in the intermediate test and knowledge in the  post-test after instruction. As hypothesized, the majori ty of students either kept the same knowledge or gained new knowledge after instruction. In a large proportion of these students, the conceptual features in the intermediate  test matched those in the  post-test: They either had knowledge of features at both points in time  or did not. The dominance of matched knowledge in  these consecutive points in time, especially regarding students with absent knowledge in both, underlines the importance of building intermediate knowledge to enable students to process the instruction and to learn. However, when examining the frequencies of each single pattern, it emerged that the likelihood of retaining a feature from int ermediate test to post-test after instruction was similar to the likelihood of losing a feature or gaining  a new feature. Accordingly, it remains unclear which intermediate knowledge leads to which pattern of lo sing, gaining or retaining features. Again, this suggests the need for  further process analyses of students’ preparation p rocesses and of  their intermediate knowledge. For instance, understanding students' reasoning for choosing the solution attempt(s) they presented in the intermediate knowledge test could help us to identify whether factors such as the depth of intermediate knowledge  affected their learning. Moreover, given that students in the H iCov condition more frequently lost knowledge of feature s across all consecutive points in time (i.e., Prep-Inter and Inter-Post), it appears that the students struggled to retain higher amounts of information in their working memory prior to explicit instruction and might thus require additional support.

Overall, our findings need to be considered in the light of some limitations. The small sample size may constitute one reason why we were unable to find the expected effects.  Moreover, the analyses of features were limited to descriptive analyses of our sample, which cannot necessarily be generalized and therefore need to be verified in future  studies. It further needs  to be considered that due to the experimental manip ulation, some patterns were bound to occur more or less frequently in the two conditions. Finally, the intermediate knowledge test might not have captured students’ more rudimentary knowledge of the conceptual features. While our study did not confirm a mediation effect of students’ prior knowledge activation on conceptual learning via intermediate knowledge, our findings do offer  new insights into students’ detailed learning at three points in time across the VF phases, as called for by Loibl and colleagues (in press). Students’ knowledge of conceptual features  was indeed connected across the consecutive p oints in time, with  the majority of students presenting a matching knowledge of features from one point in time to the next. Yet, students also differed widely in terms of their  patterns of gaining, retaining or losing knowledge.  The present findings raise important new questions such as: Might the depth – rather than the breadth – of prior knowledge activation  lead to better intermediate knowledge, and might the depth – rather than the breadth – of intermediate knowledge better prepare students for conceptual learning?
