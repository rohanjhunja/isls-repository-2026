# Cultivate a Research Mindset: How Do High School Students Understand and Approach Authentic Research?

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study investigates students’ understanding of good research as related to their own research experiences and future research interests. The participants were 12th-grade students (17-to-18-year-olds) who participated in the Innovative Solutions project supported by three teachers and a cluster of professional partners from community-based organizations. An individual interview was conducted at the end of the project focused on students’ research experiences and outcomes, their understanding of good research, their interests to do research in the future. Grounded theory analysis of the interview data revealed core facets of students’ epistemic understanding of good research including aims, criteria, and process. As a compelling theme, students embrace research as a progressive, ever-deepening process to uncover deeper problems as research progresses. Detailed analysis of two student cases elaborates on how students’ epistemic understandings connect with their research experiences and future research interests. The findings inform educational designs to cultivate authentic research experiences among students.

## Introduction

Recent research and education reforms highlight the need to help students develop authentic knowledge practices similar to those used by real-world researchers to construct knowledge and address complex issues (Cress et al., 2021; OECD, 2018). To address this need, educators explore opportuniti es to engage students in authentic research across disciplinary areas. Research in real-world settings is a systematic investigation that contributes to knowledge advancement, including problem finding,  collaborative problem solving,  idea development, testing and evaluation, and communication. In education, authentic research experiences refer to structured opportunities for learners to conduct research and produce new knowledge in ways that connect to or mirror real-world problems and practices (Rule, 2006). Developing a reflective mindset of research will lay a foundation that prepares students for college, careers, and citizenship in the rapidly changing world (Pendleton-Jullian & Brown, 2018). The existing literature on engaging students in res earch has mostly focused on college students through undergraduate research experiences (UREs). UREs are defined as student-centered, active learning practices that allow students to begin thinking, working, and communicating like scientists and researchers (Eagan et al., 2013; Linn et al., 2015). Most studies of UREs focus on ma king STEM research accessible and relevant to stude nts (Bangera & Brownell, 2014), so they can work as “doers” of science (Gentile et al., 2017). Students use scientific practices and research techniques t o solve realistic problems, including through colla boration and teamwork (Auchincloss et al., 2014; Gentile et al., 2017). R esearch shows that productive designs of UREs can l ead to positive outcomes, including understanding, confide nce, and awareness of r esearch as well as personal interests in STEM (Hanauer et al., 2016). A set of studies ha s emerged to examine college and postgraduate students’ conceptions of research (Bowers & Parameswaran, 201 3; Pitcher, 2013;  Stubb, 2014). Student conception of research has essential implications on their research practices (Bowers & Parameswaran, 2013). Further research needs to explore opportunities to cultivate authent ic research practices among K-12 students. As the literature suggests, educators often find it difficult  to formulate research problems or tasks  that are authentic to real-world communities and educationally meaningful to students (Magnussen & Hod, 2023). The existing practices often ask students to work on sm all, well-defined tasks following predefined procedures (see Bird et al., 2023), limiting student agency for engaging in open-ended research (Sadler et al., 2010). Such tasks may only give students a shallow and narrow view of  research. To develop a deeper approach to research experiences, the field needs deeper theory building  about how students learn to research and research to learn in authentic settings (Ahmand & AI-Thani, 2022; Auchincloss et al., 2014; Gentile et a l., 2017; Linn et al., 2015; Sadler et al., 2010).

As part of an ongoing design-based research project, we have been collaborating with a team of teachers and community partners to develop a collaborative e nvironment for high school students to engage in au thentic research experiences (Zhang et al., 2024). Such experiences aim to develop “research literacy,” which involves epistemic understandings of what research means and  how it works, personal and collaborative  engagement in research processes to address knowledge gaps and so lve problems in professional and personal settings,  and discursive practices to communicate research with various audiences and contribute to the larger social discourse (Zhang et al., 2024, 2025). We integrate theories of Knowledge Building (Scarda malia & Bereiter, 2006), community-based learning (Rogoff, 2014), and epistemic cognit ion (Barzilai & Chinn, 2018) to design and analyze student research experience s. The Knowledge Building (KB) theory offers a princ iple-based framework for cultivating knowledge-creating practices in schools. The KB principles highlight authentic problems and real ideas, collective responsibility for community know ledge advancement, and epistemic agency for continu al idea improvement (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2006). Students’ knowledge work in each classroom community is further situated in an open world of information and expert ise, so students have the opportunity to learn from  and build on the knowledge of other communities while using t heir knowledge to make a positive impact (Zhang et al., 2020). Students can participate in the ongoing ende avors of their communities, “learning by observing and pitching in” (Rogoff, 2014). Thus, our design of authentic research focuses on creating a collaborative community in which students take epistemic ag ency and collective responsibility for advancing sh ared knowledge while contributing to one another’s learning.  Students w ork with their peers and professional partners to i nvestigate authentic challenges facing the local or broader co mmunities and build knowledge that informs better s olutions. As the community advances knowledge together, membe rs develop their own personal knowledge and identit ies as learners and researchers. There are rich opportu nities for students to engage in metacognitive talk s and reflections focusing on the meaning and processes of  research. Such opportunities are expected to deepe n students’ epistemic cognition about research, which  refers to epistemic beliefs, understandings, and v alues regarding knowledge and knowing and ways of communicating and contributing (Barzilai & Chinn, 2018; Greene et al., 2018). Building on Chinn and colleagues (2014), we conjecture that epistemic cognition about research may include how people understand the aims of research in particular settings, the criteria used to evalua te research outputs, and epistemic processes used to carry out research and create knowledge products in light of the criteria. Epistemic cognition is important for students to na vigate the complex process of research in meaningfu l and adaptive ways beyond simply following predefined procedures for information search. Our prior research examined how students navigated the unfolding resea rch processes to address real-world challenges with the support of their teachers and community partners. Beyond hands-on work with data and tools, students participated in the iterative cycle s of research processes to formulate research directions based on authentic challenges proposed by community partners  and identified deeper issues as progress was made (Zhang et al., 2024). Building on the above finding, the current  study investigates students’ epistemic reflection on research. Our research question asks: How do high school students frame the nature of research and position their research engagement as they collaborate with their peers and community partners to address authentic challenges?

## Method

Classroom contexts This research took place at Tech Valley High School (TVHS) located in the Capital Region of New York State in the U.S. TVHS is a four-year regional public high school that is open to students from different  school districts. This school was established in early 2000 based on the principles of innovative, authentic, and studen t-centered learning. For the past three years, our research te am has been collaborating with TVHS to design authentic research opportunities for their students. Each year, a class of 12 th-grade students (17-to-18-year-olds) participated in the Innovative Solutions project su pported by the teachers specializing in English, ar ts/business alliance, and special education.

The Innovative Solutions project  kicked off in mid-October, when the representatives of community partners came to the classroom to present their “Business Pitches”: open-ended authentic challenges or problems that the organizations needed to address. For example, two staff from Red Cross presented the challenge: How do we, the Red Cross, create a social media campaign to attract high school students and recruit them as volunteers? The students indicated their preferences and the teachers formed teams based on the students’ interests, personality and other considerations. Each team then worked with one another and with their community partner for the next 8-9 weeks, carrying out various research activities. The students generated questions and initial ideas, received research technique training, research related literature, conducted surveys, and interviewed target participants and people in their local communities. The teams had in-person and/or online meetings and ongoing email exchanges with their community partners every week. They orga nized their research using planning documents, onli ne websites and databases of research papers, and othe r specific tools relevant to their research (e.g., digital design software). Short whole class meetings were held in the middle of the project to share progress and challenges, and to plan for their next steps. The teachers monitor the progress in th e classroom and provide guidance to address their needs, such as introducing experts to expand students’ research direction and rubrics to set up expectations for major works. As a research product, each team drafted a research repo rt. In early December, a public presentation was held with community partners, and the public. The teams presented their research and solutions, with the community partners serving as listeners and discussants. Data sources and analyses Within a larger design-based research project, we collected multiple data sources of processes and products to trace students’ research work in teams supported by their  teachers and community partners  (Zhang et al. 2024), the data sources for this paper were semi-structured student interviews conducted on completion of the Innovative Solution project.  The interview s solicited student reflection on their research expe rience, including how they managed and participated in the research process, what they had learned, and what kinds of support they received. The current study zoomed in  on three interview questions that focused on students’  epistemic reflection on research: (1) what counts as good research in your mind? (2) Looking back at your team’s research work  in this project, what were your most important research fin dings and outcomes? (3) Would you be interested in doing research as part of your future study, work, or lif e? If not, why? If yes, what areas of research are you inter ested in? In total,  23 students participated in the interview during th e first two iterations (2022-2024) of this project. Among them, ten students were selected bas ed on the principle of maximizing the richness and depth of understanding. Five students were selected from the first iteration (2022-2023), five from the second iteration (2023-2024). The interviews were transcribed and analyzed using a grounded theory approach (Strauss & Corbin, 1998). Before formal coding, two researchers piloted codin g on one interview to ensure consistency in their individual work. Then, we individually read and re-read transcriptions of 5 interviews, creat ing raw codes to capture key elements of students’ notions of resear ch, major findings and ideas about future research opportunities. After, we discuss those raw codes to make agreement. Then for axial coding, we co-review the raw codes and examples to identify thematic clusters and connections among three dimensions. A set of themes were developed to capture students’ reflective views of research, research outcomes, and future research in terests supported by students’ research products. To deepen the findings and illustrate students’ notions about research, their reflection on research findings and future research interests, we selected some representative excerpts from students’ interviews after we generated the themes. Furthermore, we delved in two students’ research works as “telling cases” (Mitchell, 1984), each showcasing a  research project conducted with peer students and a community partner. The two students were chosen for representativeness and their rich narratives. Their narratives could provide insights on the connections among three dimensions. Selection occurred during axial coding phase; the themes generated from their interviews are analytically salient.

## Findings

Our analysis of the  interview data identif ied major themes that describe students’ epistemic understandings of good research work in connection with their reflections on their research outcomes and future opportunities. Below we present the themes based on the interview questions, with a brief illustration of the themes through verbatim quotes from students' interview transcripts, followed by the analyses of two student cases. Students’ understanding of what counts as “good research” Theme 1: Identifying and addressing progressive questions As the first theme focusing on the process of research, students emphasized that good research involves identifying and uncovering progressively deeper problems as emerging directions for research. This theme was generated based on four open codes from students’ interviews. They are as follows: focusing on questions that are not easy to answer, unpacking the problems to solve, investigating evolving questions over time, and clarifying focal issues to “take the extra step ” to go deeper. In the Innovative Solutions project, the community partners identified challenging issues facing their organizations; in response the students played an active and agentic role in formulating their research questions and shaping their research processes. Several students commented that good research starts with questions that are not that st raightforward, so they need to explore different aspects of a problem and figure out what they need to know. Others noted that good research le ads tonew or “more of a specific question”. Several students emphasized having a basic understanding of what they needed to do and took extra steps to explore. In short, they all underlined the impor tance of seeking deeper questions or ideas as the research process unfolds. As William said, “You start with that basic search, or that question, tha t first question that you want to answer that's going to change so many times throughout the process. It's going to get partially answered and then you're going to realize, ok, this needs to be more of a specific question.” Theme 2: Deep work with high quality sources The second  theme, also focusing on the process of research,  highlighted that good research involves deep thoughtful work supported by high quality informati on sources, such as Google Scholar and people with related expertise. This theme indicate d students using their criteria for collecting and processing sources to guide their research process. This theme was generated based on four open codes f rom students’ interviews: using reliable sources, finding enough information (not overwhelming), getting people’s input for sources, and deep work with sources to answer  questions. Some of the students th ought that the source of research needs to be trustworthy, using google scholar to search. Some thought enough information is the criterion of good research. Others thought good research needs to share their information with  others and get their insightful input. Some emphas ized the process of searching sources: good research starts off with questions and searching sources through snowballing. Overall, they all focused on the importance of making constructive use of high-quality sources. Edward told us, “Good research in my mind is a reliable source to researc h...So, a reliable source that's like a very important thing. A reliable source will help you with your research and give you basically credibility of why is this a really good solution that you made.” Theme 3: Developing big understandings with coherence The third theme conveyed student understanding of the goal of research with a sense of the quality criteria (e.g., coherence, relevance). Students recognized that good research aims to rise above different sources of information to develop coherent understandings that lead to productive solutions to the focal problems. Four open codes helped generate this theme: generating answers to  questions informed by what you find, spending more time on developing “bigger understandings”, substantial reasoning and sensemaking, and searching for connection across data and sources. Some students th ought that good research requires finding information that is relevant to the research questions and eventually developing informed answers to the question s. A few other students underscored efforts to continually work toward the criteria of deeper understandings and “big ideas” that lead to a better product. And some thought good research is making sense of the results that they researched. Some noted research processes involve  working iteratively to make sense of ongoing research findings, building coherent understandings based on different sources of information, and addressing gaps. They all attach ed importance to the criteria of having a deeper understanding of resources and trying to make connection s between them. As is seen in Oscar’s interview, “Comparing all the data and seeing both these articles are talking about the same topic, but have different answers. And if you read and you go down through all the articles that they both went to, you'll find similarities. It's almost like people read that first article and they  interpret it differently, it was 5 different interpretations of it, that are all wordies and sounds differently but are saying the same thing. And going through that process over and over again, and just spending a long time on your computer just reading.” Theme 4: Engaging people The fourth theme focused on the criteria of research communication  to make the research work and findings engaging to the relevant audiences and stakeholders. Two open codes generate d this theme: organizing information in a way that engages audiences and relaying understandable information for mutual understanding. It is believed that good research includes organizi ng, refining, and presenting information to gain the audience’s attention. Other students emphasized communicating research information in a way that is easy to understand with enough details. These thoughts were integral to students’ planning of research meetings and presentations. As Emma has told us, “I think research is less about finding a ton of information. As it is taking that information and like being able to relay it simply, but also in a w ay that people understand. It also has details. That was the challenge of the project, we're not st atisticians or anything, but we had to take all this information and then spit it back out in a professional way. In a way that made sense.” Theme 5: Making contributions The last theme  about the aims of research focused on intellectual and social impact.  Students highlighted that good research makes positive contributions that  influence a field(s) or communities of people. Two open codes gave rise to this theme: generating “important stuff” and creating something new. Good research aims to address important issues and benefits  people, leading to  new knowledge and solutions to problems.  The students all recognized research as a way to make contributions. It is seen in Daniel’s interview, “I think it's cool, it's like impactful  early. It's about something important to know. …  W hen you're researching something that's super important, like using a game to teach climate change, like climate change is a super big issue. I think research in that is super awesome.”

## Student reflection on their research findings and outcomes

In the post-interview, students reflected on their important findings or outcomes achieved through the Innovative Solution project. The analysis of their responses identified three compelling themes. The first theme of the students’ research output focused on solving a problem or challenge. Their specific research outcomes involve understanding a research area and audience in order to solve the problem, unpacking the problem to figure out what they need to know,  developing a new solution to the problem, and developing convincing support for their solution in comparison to  alternative solutions. The second theme emphasized the impact of research on students themselves. It included engaging with people in research, growing public speaking skills, gaining research experience s and skills (e.g., survey), understanding specific area of research (e.g., marketing). The last theme highlighted the social impact and contribution of their research, which was important to people. Four open codes under this theme covered community engagement in using their  research product, contributing to addressing important issues such as climate change, public awareness and safe adoption of new technology, and designs of educational outreach. They took pride that their research  outcomes could have a positive impact on others.

Student interests in doing research as part of their future study, work or life The interview asked students about if/how they woul d like to do  research as part of their future study, work or life. Only one (out of ten) student preferred not to do r esearch but was open to the possibility of future research involvement.  Nine students were interested in doing research for two major reasons. ## Discussion

This study analyzed how the high school students (1 2th-graders) understood research as related to their re search experiences in the Innovative Solutions project. Ou r design of authentic research focused on creating a collaborative community in which students worked wi th their peers and professional partners t o investigate authentic challenges (Zhang et al., 2024). The desi gn principles emphasize student agency and collecti ve responsibility (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2006) for a dvancing research while collaborating with professi onal community partners. Essential to students’ epistemi c agency, the findings shed light on students’ epis temic understandings of what research means and how it wo rks in connection with their research experiences a nd interests. This study is among the first to examine high school students’ epistemic cognition of authentic research integral to their own learning experience. Their no tions of research align with the key components of epistemic cognition, such as understanding the aim, criteria and process of knowledge construction (Barzilai & Chinn, 2018; Chinn et al., 2014). Regarding each component, stud ents demonstrated epistemologically rich understand ings of research. First, students position the aim of research as developing coherent understandings that lead to productive solutions to the focal problems. Their criteria of research outcomes include cognitive coherence th at rises above diverse perspectives and sources, practical value i n solving problems, as well as social engagement and impact. The participants emphasized that good research need s to generate something ne w and important that addresses important issues and benefits people. As a compelli ng theme about the process of research, students embrace research as a progressive, ever-deepening process to uncover deeper problems as res earch progresses. As part of the research process, students need to make constru ctive use of high-quality sources. These key features are consistent with the findings of our study that used metaphor analysis to examine student notions of research at the same school site (Zhang et al., 2025). Their sophisticated understandings of the aim, criteria and process of research demonstrate the characteristics of epistem ic agency by which students take high-level responsibility for advancing the community’s knowledge beyond simply doing given tasks (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2006). The findings further elaborate on the connections between students’ understandings of research and their research outcomes and future research interest. Their reflection on what they had achieved through the Innovative Solutions project highlighted skill-based, idea-based, and value-based outcomes. As indicated in the finding, the variation in views and connection among views, refl ection of important outcomes and future opportuniti es has implications for the way students understand research relate to their agency and responsibility during the research process, their evalua tion of research outputs and their future research expectations. For instance, those students who consider good research is made up by making contributions tend to consider important stuff as their important outcomes and prefer to research important stuff that affects their chosen profession and the public. The two cases documented how students with various notions of research navigated their research process as they interacted with their community partners. The community partners identified multiple challenges authentic to their organizations, with a shared expectation that such challenges should be open-ended, leaving enough space for students to direct their paths of inquiry. Students took on active rol es to understand the challenges in specific context s (e.g. KDA, Earth Cells) and went through a progressive, “rabbit hole” process to identify specific problems and research goals for their team. While the existing practices to int egrate research experiences often ask students to f ollow predefined procedures to do some peripheral  tasks (Ahmand & AI-Thani, 2022; Bird et al., 2023; Sadler et al., 2010), the findings of this research suggest the po ssibility of having students take on more agency in positioning their research efforts, including defining research questions and goals, negotiating research plans with their team members and partners, and reflecting on their research processes for continual idea improvement. The findings shed light on new possibilities to ext end student knowledge building to solving authentic challenges in an open, community-based environment in high school settings. Based on the findings, our teacherresearcher team co-designed the third iter ation of this project. More detailed data analyses are conducted to examine students’ conception of research using mult iple data and connections among possible constituti on of epistemic cognition for supporting student deep engagement in research.
