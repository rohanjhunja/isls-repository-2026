# Coding Foundations, AI Futures: How Districts Built K-12 AI Capacity Through Collaborative Peer Networks

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Artificial intelligence (AI) education builds on core computational thinking (CT) practices such as abstraction, algorithms, and data. Yet most research to date over-emphasizes classroom tools rather than how schools can more systematically implement AI ef forts. This qualitative study examines how six U.S. school districts extended K-12 CT pathways to build AI capacity through peer-networks. Guided by Coburn’s theoretical framework, depth, spread, shift in ownership, and sustainability, we followed districts over one year as they navigated AI implementation. Data sources included monthly cohort recordings, district artifacts, district presentations, and a questionnaire. Findings indicated that districts leveraged existing CT practices f or depth, launching both AI/data science courses and integrative activities. Spread occurred through cross-district learning, policy development, and professional growth. The cohort catalyzed a shift in ownership through district-led AI efforts. Last, existing CT efforts coupled with peer-networks proved crucial for sustainability of scalable and coherent, district-driven AI education.

Computational thinking (CT) provides an essential foundation for understanding and engaging with artificial intelligence (AI). Core CT practices such as abstraction, automation, algorithmic thinking, modularization, and data analysis are not only relevant to computer science but also central to how AI systems are developed and interpreted (Mills et al., 2024). Rather than viewing “AI literacy” as an entirely new construct, schools are better positioned to understand AI and machine learning as an extension of their existing CT efforts (Grover, 2024). Preparing students for an AI-driven future requires more than basic computer science exposure; it demands a deeper integration of data science as the bridge between CT and AI (Grover, 2024). As AI literacy gains prominence in K-12 education (Casal-Otero et al., 2023; Mills et al., 2024; Ng et al., 2021), research continues to affirm that CT and data science skills are critical for equipping learners with the conceptual understanding necessary to navigate and shape AI technologies (NRC, 2026). School districts are central to sustaining and institutionalizing AI innovation. While a growing body of research has explored how AI tools are implemented at the classroom level (Lee & Kwon, 2024; Su et al., 2024), fewer studies have examined how school districts take up and scale AI-related initiatives. Prior studies around introducing computer science (Dunbar  et al., 202 6) and data science (National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, & Medicine, 2023) in schools emphasize that for curricular reforms to last, ownership must shift from external researchers to local educators. To examine how CT pathways can be leveraged to support AI integration at scale, we draw on Coburn’s (2003) framework for understanding scale through the dimensions of depth, spread, shift in ownership, and sustainability. This model allows us to analyze not only whether AI efforts are expanding, but also how deeply they are embedded into teaching practices, organizational routines, and district policies.

This article examines how six districts’ past efforts (2022 –2024) around creating integrative K-12 CT pathways set the stage for their collaborative work (2024– 2025) developing AI education initiatives across their schools. Through a year-long peer-network model, the six districts met monthly, culminating in a showcase of their respective AI initiatives. Leveraging the same peer-to-peer district network that helped them develop CT trajectories (Dunbar et al., in press), the six districts were part of a larger research-practice partnership (RPP), which emphasized reciprocity, trust, and collective capacity-building as essential for scaling and sustaining educational change in K-12 computing (Coburn & Penuel, 2016). Specifically, the RPP was strategically designed to sustain CT efforts by gradually transferring leadership responsibilities to district administrators and teachers (Dunbar et al., 2026).

The paper addresses the following research question: How do CT Pathways build the capacity, including depth, spread, shift in ownership, and  sustainability to support the development and implementation of AI initiatives?

407 Central to the debate over AI in K-12 schools has been the question of ownership (Fullan et al., 2024). While state and district policies stress “shared responsibility” for ethical usage and school-based documents highlight “best practices,” the question remains: who should teach with AI tools, and who should teach about AI conceptually? Unlike the “computer labs” of the 1980s (Cuban, 2001), AI is not easily relegated to a specific physical space or standalone class, especially at the K-8 level.

Much of districts’ adoption of AI has been driven by tools saturating the marketplace (Filiz et al., 2025). This is most evident with large language models such as ChatGPT, which have pushed English teachers to become de facto gatekeepers of AI use in schools (Diliberti et al., 2024). A RAND study confirmed this trend, noting that English teachers were nearly twice as likely as math teachers to report AI tool use (Kaufman et al., 2025). While humanities teachers may welcome this role, it also reflects overreliance on marketplace tools rather than intentional outcomes.

This issue is not new; technology adoption in schools has long been shaped by corporate marketing (Livingstone et al., 2024). Coburn’s (2003) framework offers an alternative, pointing to depth, spread, shift in ownership, and sustainability as criteria for durable integration.  Using this framework, this study examines how six diverse districts leveraged CT foundations to adopt AI initiatives, framing CT as cumulative competencies necessary to meaningfully engage with AI (Jacob et al., under review). Recent literature highlights computing and data literacy as fundamental to grasping machine learning and AI ethics (Grover, 2024; Touretzky et al., 2025; Weng et al., 2024). Thus, CT pathways provided scaffolds upon which AI learning could be layered.

## Theoretical framework

This study is guided by Coburn’s (2003) framework for rethinking scale, which emphasizes four interrelated dimensions of educational reform: depth, spread, shift in ownership, and sustainability. Rather than focusing only on the number of schools or districts adopting an innovation, this framework highlights how reforms are embedded in practice, extended across contexts, and maintained over time. It has been widely applied to examine large-scale changes in education including in computer science and computational thinking education (Dunbar et al., 2026; Farley-Ripple et al., 2018).

Depth refers to the extent to which reforms transform core beliefs, instructional practices, and organizational routines. For AI initiatives that are grounded in CT pathways, depth involves integrating AI concepts with established CT practices such as abstracti on, data analysis, and algorithmic reasoning. This dimension underscores the need to move beyond surface-level adoption of AI tools toward embedding AI literacy into pedagogy and student learning in durable ways (Coburn, 2003; Grover, 2024). Spread captures how reforms expand across schools, classrooms, and subject areas, as well as how norms and principles diffuse into district policies and routines. In scaling AI initiatives, spread requires extending CT-informed practices across grade levels and content areas, while leveraging cross-district networks to adapt approaches to diverse contexts. This process ensures that implementation is systemic rather than fragmented (Coburn, 2003; Cannata et al., 2017).

Shift in ownership highlights the transfer of responsibility for reform from external facilitators to local educators and administrators. For AI integration, this means districts and teachers, rather than external experts, take the lead in designing curricula, setting policies, and shaping professional learning. When reform ownership shifts to those closest to implementation, capacity is built locally and the likelihood of long-term impact increases (Coburn, 2003; Coburn & Penuel, 2016). Finally, sustainabi lity refers to the endurance of reforms over time despite leadership changes, competing demands, or resource constraints. Anchoring AI initiatives in strategic plans, curriculum standards, and professional development structures increases the probability that they will endure as integral elements of district computing pathways (Coburn, 2003; Fullan et al., 2024).

Data sources Data sources included an AI Cohort Questionnaire, district leader interviews, recordings of AI Cohort meetings, and meeting artifacts, including academic year-end culminating district presentations and shared resources on each district’s AI initiatives.

1. AI Cohort Questionnaire:  District leaders and administrators completed a brief online questionnaire

(approximately 10 minutes, administered via Google Forms) consisting of four open-ended prompts. The questions asked participants to a) describe AI initiatives launched in the past two years; b) reflect on how CT pathways laid groundwork for this work; c) consider the potential for integrated CT + AI pathways; and d) identify needed supports and resources. This instrument provided insight into districts’ self-reported perspectives on AI integration, highlighting perceived depth, spread, and sustainability (Coburn, 2003).

2. District Leader Interviews: We conducted semi-structured interviews with district leaders (60 minutes)

to capture reflections on CT pathway implementation, cohort participation, and emerging AI integration efforts, providing deeper insight into contextual factors influencing CT and AI integration.

3. Meeting Artifacts:  We collected artifacts from monthly AI cohort meetings, including agendas and

resource repositories, and collective meeting notes. These artifacts moved beyond self-report to capture concrete actions, such as piloting new AI courses, drafting policy, or developing professional learning, while also illustrating cross-district resource sharing and leadership shifts. Unlike most qualitativ e paper will report on district-led actions with respect to implementing AI initiatives.

4. Meeting Recordings:  Each monthly meeting (approximately 60 minutes in length) was recorded and

transcribed. Districts rotated facilitation on focal AI topics, enabling analysis of both district-led interventions and the ways leaders articulated CT as foundational to AI.

5. Culminating Presentations:  At the close of the academic year, each district delivered a 20-minute

culminating presentation showcasing its AI work to date. These presentations served both as verification of implemented initiatives and as a venue for district leaders to raise questions and articulate insights about future directions.

Taken together, these complementary data sources offered a multi-faceted view of how districts enacted AI initiatives. While the questionnaire foregrounded self-reported perspectives, artifacts, recordings, and culminating presentations allowed us to trace  implementation in practice and verify reported progress. Therefore, the qualitative findings present on district-led AI activities in-depth alongside participant perspectives. In alignment with Coburn’s (2003) framework, this combination enabled systemati c analysis of depth, spread, shifts in ownership, and sustainability across districts’ CT-to-AI pathways.

## Data analysis

Data analysis was guided by a deductive analytical approach (Azungah, 2018) grounded in Coburn’s (2003) framework for understanding educational scaling. We applied an a priori  coding scheme (Saldaña, 2014) based on the four dimensions of the framework (depth, spread, shift in ownership, and sustainability) to systematically examine district-level AI initiatives. Coding of the questionnaire and meeting recordings was conducted iteratively, beginning with the application of these primary deductive codes, followed by the development of second-level codes to generate a more granular analysis of how each dimension manifested across contexts. This two-tiered coding process enabled an in-depth interpretation of how districts scaled AI initiatives within the broader structure of existing CT pathways We conducted a content analysis (Hsieh & Shannon, 2005) of the meeting artifacts and culminating district presentations to examine how district documentation reflected AI implementation and the role of CT as foundational. Content analysis systematically examines textual and visual materials to identify recurring patterns, themes, and meanings. By organizing data into categories and coding units of analysis, it enables researchers to move beyond surface-level description and interpret how documents reflect u nderlying processes and practices (Hsieh & Shannon, 2005).

In our approach, we began with a priori  codes drawn from Coburn’s (2003) framework (e.g., depth, spread shift in ownership, sustainability) as well as CT/AI integration concepts, while remaining open to emergent codes that surfaced during analysis. Each artifact and presentation transcript was segmented into meaningful units (e.g., paragraphs, bullet points, slides). Through iterative coding cycles, we developed themes that captured the implemented strategies (e.g., AI pilot courses, cross-district resource sharing, leadership roles). By comparing across documents and presentations, the content analysis enabled verification of district claims and deeper insight into how AI efforts were concretized in practice. To enhance validity, we triangulated across multiple data sources, including interviews, meeting recordings, and artifacts, to surface themes and cross-verify findings (Miles et al., 2014).

We present our findings by addressing each of the Coburn’s four dimensions of rethinking scale, according to our central research question: How do CT Pathways build the capacity, including depth, spread, shift in ownership, and sustainability to support the development and implementation of AI initiatives? Depth: Embedding AI through established CT foundations The CT Pathway efforts built depth of capacity by deeply embedding computing competencies and mindsets into 410 school systems, which in turn paved the way for meaningful AI integration. This depth is evident in how AI initiatives are not superficial add-ons, but rather extensions of an existing culture of computational learning. For example, because CT concepts (su ch as algorithmic thinking, pattern recognition, data analysis) had been internalized by teachers and students over several years, these same concepts could be directly applied to understanding AI principles. One district leader explained their method for integrating CT and AI literacy curricula: We're looking to do a crosswalk of our AI curriculum, that we have our current computer science curriculum that we have at elementary, and then also do a crosswalk with CT. And so we want to come together with a comprehensive curriculum that we're going to use to empower kids with computer science, computational thinking, and AI. Initiatives such as these led to more practical guided implementation. In Iowa City, for instance, educators leveraged their understanding of algorithms from CT workshops to comprehend how large language models function upon sorting and recognizing data via pattern recognition, thereby increasing their comfort in delivering AI lessons. This prior capacity allowed districts to design deeper AI learning experiences, rather than limiting AI to a cursory introduction. Some districts explicitly set district-wide AI learning goals (e.g., defining key AI competencies that all graduates should have).

Building upon the foundation of their existing K-12 CT Pathways efforts, districts extended their computing curricula to include new AI and data science learning opportunities. Several districts launched new programs explicitly focused on AI or data science, using the established CT pathway as a K-8 "feeder" program. One district leader noted: That's the approach that everyone's going to take…. Rather than creating these things in silos, how can we marry them together? So it's a unified approach to building student capacity around just literacy in general. And AI is going to be a component of that. But also CT and CS. Districts altogether emphasized a unified approach integrating AI, CT, and CS in order to build overall student literacy, rather thandeveloping each of these in  isolation. In the case of Florida’s expansive Broward County Public Schools, the district expanded computer science across K-12 through districtwide pathways and professional learning aligned to computational thinking. This system-level infrastructure established the access, coherence, and student preparation needed to support scal able AI integration. Indian Prairie School District built on its existing computational thinking pathways to design a vertically aligned AI scope and sequence that integrated AI concepts into existing digital literacy and technology courses across K-8. This progression introduced foundational ideas in elementary grades and advances through middle school from understanding AI to engaging with and creating using machine learning.

In addition to these initiatives, all six districts continued to infuse AI topics into existing classes and grade-level units to enrich the established CT pathways.  As one district leader stated: We have been working to grow teacher and student understanding of AI following algorithms to provide outputs based on our prompts. In addition, our student lessons expand on the ways in which AI leans into patterns to assist in its predictions for outputs. Lastly, decomposition has been critical in helping staff better understand the ways to prompt Gen AI to obtain the desired outputs.

The districts regularly discussed embedding CT concepts into  AI initiatives to strengthen teacher and student understanding of AI as not just a tool but also as an applied use of computation. To this end, Kettle Moraine School District developed an AI literacy approach grounded in its AI Literacy draft based on district guidelines and aligned it with its broader Accessible Use Policy and Literacy Framework, establishing coherent guidance for responsible and instructional AI use across the district. Even very small districts within the cohort found ways to leverage CT foundations for AI.  In North Salem (a rural NY district with only two schools), rather than adding separate AI programs or electives, educators chose to embed AI into interdisciplinary projects. This approach built on their CT initiative’s emphasis on cross-curricular projects and ensured AI learning reached students without overburdening a small staff. In summary, the pre-existing CT pathways provided a structural and pedagogical foundation for AI initiatives, allowing districts to develop AI curricula that are coherent, cumulative, and integrated into the broader K-12 program rather than isolated tech novelties. 411 Spread: Extending AI initiatives across the district CT Pathways also built capacity for spread, meaning the ability to expand innovative practices system-wide across schools, grade levels, and subject areas. Through the CT initiative, districts established widespread awareness and buy-in for computing education, from the classroom to the school board. Many CT pathway projects engaged multiple schools and a broad range of stakeholders, normalizing the idea of district-wide technology integration. Spread was most manifestly evident in cross-curricular reach. The CT Pathways program encouraged integrating computing across disciplines (e.g., math, science, language arts), which helped to support districts with ready inroads to likewise embed AI content in various subjects (such as science data units and technolog y classes in middle school). Furthermore, district leaders have actively communicated about AI to staff and families, building on the outreach channels and champions developed during the CT initiative. Wisconsin’s Kettle Moraine, for example, reported increased outreach to families in their post-cohort interview: We are communicating to our district at large, parents becoming more aware that AI is not going away. We want to use it to support our teaching and learning in a safe and ethical way…I like the highlighting having some sort of newsletter or email blast that goes out highlighting a tool of the month…This is something we already own. You wanted to get into AI. Here's something you can use.

As a result, all six districts reported a growing district-wide awareness of AI’s importance in K-12, which is of course crucial for scale. Due, in part, to the groundwork laid by CT (which often involved policy changes such as counting CS as a graduation credit or adopting CS standards), districts found it easier to secure approval for AI-related expansions. For instance, IPSD’s new AI Task Force gained Board of Education buy-in for drafting an AI Education Strategic Plan, and this top-level endorsement helped legitimize AI work across the district. Similarly, Iowa City developed an AI champion cohort with the approval of the board. This past summer, that's where we really did develop the [AI] student curriculum. And that was a result of the board policy. We've started an AI champion cohort this year, and that's really something that we're leaning into… Still, participants acknowledged ongoing challenges to spread, such as ensuring all teachers are prepared for AI content and addressing unequal resources among schools. During monthly cohort meetings, district teams candidly shared obstacles. For example, they mentioned limited devices for AI experiments or varying teacher enthusiasm. Nevertheless, they also brainstormed solutions together at said meetings. The CT Pathways experience in solving implementation problems specific to each district’s context (such as scheduling CS classes or training elementary teachers) gave leaders a problem-solving mindset, rooted in large part with the recognition that many of them had quite similar challenges around distribution/spread. Shift in Ownership: Transitioning leadership to local stakeholders The cohort's growing capacity was also evident with the wider shift in ownership of AI initiatives over the academic year, increasingly moving from external purveyors (e.g., the companies making the AI tools) to local educators and administrators. Coburn (2016) emphasizes that enduring change in education requires transfer of knowledge and leadership to the implementers on the ground. The CT Pathway project was designed in a way that gradually increased districts’ responsibility for driving the work, and this transfer is now evident with the range of their respective K-12 AI initiatives.

Early in the CT effort, many districts relied nearly entirely on outside facilitation (e.g., from Digital Promise to support curricular design and professional development). Over time though, each district’s teams gained expertise and both co-designed and published/circulated their own respective CT Pathways. Now, with AI, those same individuals and teams took the helm. The formation of district-led AI committees exemplifies  this ownership. Instead of waiting for a state mandate (e.g., AI standards) or a vendor’s program, districts including IPSD and Broward proactively set up their own internal task forces. Moreover, within their peer network, the locus of expertise visibly shifted to the districts over the 2024-25 academic year.  AI cohort meetings that were initially facilitated by researchers evolved into district-led sessions, with participants from the districts setting the agenda and leading discussions on AI education. Multiple districts developed specialized expertise on the AI education topics they felt were most pressing.  Specific districts volunteered (or were nominated by peers) to lead cohort sessions on areas where these districts had developed expertise. For example, Kettle Moraine, Iowa City, and Indian Prairie co-led a session on crafting district-specific AI policies; Iowa City and KMSD (Kettle Moraine School District) guided the group on evaluating AI tools and associated ethics; and Talladega and Broward 412 organized a workshop on teacher professional development for AI, including “prompt engineering” techniques for educators.

This rotation and sharing of facilitation roles meant that multiple districts experienced being in the driver’s seat, teaching others what they had learned. Such practice signaled to everyone in the cohort that they were both experts and learners in this emerging field. One district leader wrote in the post-survey that the year's highlight had been “ Continued [cohort] meetings with others [districts] and discussing their implementation”, while another leader discussed the  “continued sharing of resources and ongoing support as we further develop our AI initiatives." This data suggests the cohorts experienced myriad benefits from the meetings by sharing their implementations and supporting others.  Over the eight-month academic year (2024-25), the peer network operationalized the collaborative improvement principles suggested by Cannata et al. (2017), demonstrating that when districts lead together, they can support and sustain complex reforms such as CT and AI integration far more efficiently than solely through top-down mandates.

Sustainability: Ensuring long-term support for AI pathways Finally, CT Pathways helped develop capacity for sustainability, helping ensure that AI initiatives are not fleeting experiments but have the capacity to be enduring parts of the education system. Several elements from the CT work contributed to this susta inability. First, as noted, districts put in place supportive organizational structures during the CT initiative: committees, working groups, and professional learning communities that have persisted and simply shifted focus to AI. By expanding existing leadership bodies or establishing new task forces, the districts amplified governance mechanisms that keep AI initiatives aligned with district priorities. These structures ensure that AI work is not just driven by one enthusiastic teacher but then largely lost across other classrooms and schools. Instead, it has formal oversight and champions at the district level, which is crucial for longevity and wider vision. Second, the CT Pathway efforts underscored the importance of policy and strategic alignment in scaling innovations. Many districts, by the end of the CT project, had adopted key policies such as recognizing CS coursework for graduation credit or embedding CT in their strategic plans and visions, such as Portraits of a Graduate. Building on that experience, they likewise approached AI with an eye toward policy support and strategic planning from the outset. One district leader stated in the year-end survey: “We created guidelines for use for teachers and students, followed by a pilot team of high school teachers, admin, and library media specialists.” Multiple districts likewise quickly identified the need for ethical AI use guidelines and began crafting Acceptable Use Policies specific to AI. Through the peer cohort, districts collaborated on model AI policies, producing reference documents and sharing drafts (e.g., Indian Prairie’s student AI use guidelines were shared across the network for multi-stage feedback). The act of codifying AI practices into policy helped institutionalize them, potentially increasing the likelihood that the initiatives will survive leadership turnovers or shifts in funding. Likewise, the incorporation of AI objectives into high-level district strategic plans anchored the work in the district’s long-term vision. With AI written into five-year plans and technology roadmaps, there is a mandate to continue supporting it through professional development, budget allocations, and curriculum time. Through distributed leadership and collective support, the cohort model ultimately fostered a culture in which districts saw themselves, rather than external experts, as the essential drivers of innovation. A key indicator of this cultural shift was visible at the conclusion of the project. District teams indicated across the board that they intended to continue the work on AI pathways regardless of whether external  facilitators would be involved. In all post-cohort interviews, the six districts plan to con tinue the AI cohort, with three districts seeing the need for more nuanced sub-cohorts over the 2025-26 academic year, including a specific focus on district policy, teacher PD, and tool accreditation/recommendation. By the end of the cohort project, distr ict leaders reported that ownership had transitioned to the districts themselves, which was a fairly stark transformation from the project’s start, when researchers led nearly all of the documentation and agenda-setting. One district leader noted: “Really the expertise and the knowledge is the room itself…I think that was the piece that was beneficial for allowing the opportunity for districts to take more ownership of the process as opposed to it being all on Digital Promise. ” This outcome underscores how the peer network cultivated not just specific outputs (such as curricula or policies) but an enduring mindset of innovation. The cohort’s influence is also seen in how districts are sustaining and spreading the work beyond the initial network. Several districts have presented their AI pathway progress at national conferences and in publications, further taking ownership of disseminating lessons learned. Digital Promise, as the network convener, amplified these district voices through its channels, but impor tantly the messages came from the districts themselves. The result suggests stronger AI initiatives within participating districts and points to the potential of peer-driven collaboration as a promising approach for sustaining innovation in K-12 education.

413 Our findings demonstrate that existing K-12 CT Pathways provided a foundation to extend AI initiatives with substantial depth, spread, shift in ownership, and early signs of sustainability. Because computational thinking practices and curricula were already deeply embedded in these districts, AI integration became a natural extension rather than a superficial add-on for their schools. District leaders and teachers leveraged their prior CT capacity to demystify AI concepts, creating more meaningful and pedagogically grounded AI learning experiences (which, in turn, felt less like “another new thing” for their teachers). At the same time, the collaborative peer network accelerated system-wide spread of AI innovations and fostered a culture of collective responsibility, empowering local educators as having crucial expertise and decision-making capabilities. While a growing body of research has examined AI tools in classroom settings, far less attention has been paid to implementation at the district level. This study addresses that gap by analyzing AI integration from a district-wide perspective. A district-l evel focus is critical for achieving scalable, systemic change, as districts act as conduits between policy and practice and can embed innovations into teaching practice, organizational routines, and broader policy. Given the current state of K-12 education nationally, we see this as no small insight. By concentrating on districts, we illuminate how AI initiatives can move beyond isolated pilot programs and become embedded in K-12 education systems. Given the current shuttering of multiple federal education programs, states are increasingly being tasked to fill in the gaps around policy, curricular models, and best practices in teacher PD in key areas such as AI in K-12 schools.  Being able to go to their own school districts as models of innovation would very well be a boon to state departments of education (SDE) and the cohort model could likewise see SDEs enact real capacity to scale efforts, accordingly. Of course, the study’s scope was limited to six districts over one year, which constrains the generalizability of the findings, particularly in terms of Coburn' last dimension around long-term sustainability. Furthermore, due to the nature of the RPP, districts developed their own definition of AI and selected their specific topics to focus on (see Study context). Developing a shared understanding would further help peer networks collaborate across initiatives. Future research should address these gaps by t racking district AI initiatives over multiple years to assess long-term outcomes and by examining how state-level policies influence local implementation efforts. Another limitation of this study is that districts were early adopters where AI implementation began with established policy foundations and guardrails. Findings may not generalize to districts still building policy capacity as a first step toward scalable, sustainable AI integration.

## Implications

Despite these limitations, these findings have broader implications for K-12 AI education, particularly as they relate to districts' and schools' past and ongoing efforts around CS and CT. First, the results underscore the importance of foundational CT and data literacy work as a springboard for AI learning. Districts that have invested in computing pathways are strongly positioned to introduce AI concepts as a coherent extension of existing computing curricula rather than as an unrelated add-on. Second, the success of the peer network approach illustrates the value of cross-district collaboration for capacity-building. Such networks accelerate shared learning, resource exchange, and collective problem-solving, leading to wider and more consistent adoption of AI initiatives. Finally, the clear shift toward local ownership in this project demonstrates that empowering district leaders and teachers to drive AI efforts is key to scalable and sustainable implementation. Taken together, these led efforts grounded in prior infrastructure, collaborative learning, and sustained local ownership. Scaling AI education in K-12 requires not just new tools but broader infrastructures that allow for technological innovation into existing systems of teaching and learning. Analysis across Coburn’s (2003) dimensions shows how districts built on CT pathways  to integrate AI, expand initiatives across contexts, and cultivate local leadership for sustained progress. The findings highlight the value of peer networks in fostering coherence and shared capacity for AI integration.
