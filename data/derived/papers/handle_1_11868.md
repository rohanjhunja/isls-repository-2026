# Supporting Preparation for Peer Tutoring with a Dialogue Planning Tool

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Peer tutoring has been widely recognized as an effective learning strategy, yet the reported modest effect sizes indicate room for improvement. Research indicates that unprepared peer tutors mostly rely on knowledge telling, while few studies have specifically focused on the preparation phase. To address this gap, we developed a dialogue planning tool that provides structured question-answer prompts combined with a network graph to support tutoring preparation. This paper reports an ongoing small-scale empirical study testing the tool in science tutoring by comparing dialogue-based with slide-based tutoring plans. Initial findings reveal that dialogue planning facilitates a higher number of question-answer turns and adaptive guidance and increases the frequency of evaluating-level cognition and metacognitive-level knowledge. Future research will leverage the tutoring plandatabase, incorporating Large Language Models, to provide feedback on developing tutoring plans, aiming to support peer tutors’ transition from knowledge-telling to knowledge-construction.

## Introduction

Peer tutoring has been extensively researched (e.g., Topping, 1996; Roscoe & Chi, 2007, 2008; Ros coe, 2014; Flores et al., 2024), with students collaborating i n pairs or groups where one serves as the tutor and  the other as the tutee receiving support, leveraging one of the benefits of peer relationships to enhance learning through shared language and mutual understanding among peers (Falc hikov, 2007; McLuckie & Topping, 2004; Abdelkarim & Abuiyada, 2016). We are studying peer tutoring from the learning-by-teaching perspective: Learning opportunities for the student in the tutor role. Theoretically, peer tutors’ learning gains stem fro m the dialog ue with tutees, through which explanations and questions emerge. Pe er tutors build new insights by consolidating conce pts and reasoning through complex issues, promoting reflect ive knowledge-building rather than knowledge telling (Roscoe & Chi, 2007, 2008). However, multiple meta-analyses report an average effect size of less than  0.5, indicating a relatively modest impact (Roscoe & Chi, 2007; Leung, 2019; Nickow et al., 2024). Subsequent research on enhancing peer tutoring suggests that even without actual tutoring, preparing for tutoring alone is an effective learning strategy, fostering a deep  comprehension of knowledge, conceptual connections, and anticipated explanations and reaso ning for tutoring (Fiorella & Mayer, 2013, 2014), a s well as benefiting the development of self-monitoring metacognitive skills (Topping et al., 2017). However, research on the preparation phase remains unsystematic and limited, normally treating it as a  preliminary fragment of actual tutoring. In our literature review on peer tutoring preparation and peer tutor training (e.g., Ginsburg-Block et al., 2006; Roscoe & Chi, 2008; Berghmans et al., 2013; T opping et al., 2017; Clements, 2022; Schill et al., 2024), Fiorella and Mayer (2013, 2014) empirically compared the distinct effects of preparation and actual tutoring on the learning outcomes of peer tutors (i.e., student teachers). However, their preparation group focused exclusively on studying a lesson with the expectation of delivering a brief lecture to explain the Doppler Effect (Fiorella & Mayer, 2013, 2014) without addressing specific tutoring planning. Tutoring planning is particularly challenging for n ovice tutors because it requires setting learning objectives, designing learning activities, checking  comprehension, and managing incidental factors suc h as error detection, time management, and emotional dynamics (McLuckie & Topping, 2004; Milkova, 2012). Besides, beginners are neither professional teachers nor have a deep understanding of the domain knowledge. Fortunately, research shows that peer tutoring preparation train ing—including skills in questioning, explanation, feedb ack, metacognitive strategies, scaffolded simulation, and structured interaction—equips tutors with greater adaptability in clarifying misunderstandings and solving complex problems (Ginsburg-Block et al., 2006; Roscoe & Chi, 2008; Topping et al., 2017). We build on the idea of Socr atic tutoring (Collins & Stevens, 1980), which invo lves not providing answers but engaging in questioning and reasoning to elicit deeper explanations. We expect that tutors who employ these strategies will obtain more learni ng gains. However, adaptive Socratic tutoring is very demanding. It requires preparation to achieve this  complex task (Berghmans et al., 2013). Hence, this study focuses on tutoring planning in the preparation phase.

We developed a dialogue planning tool to assist tutors in visualizing and t racking tutoring sequences. This paper reports an ongoing study, firstly introducing the design of a dialogue model and then conducting a small-N empirical study in the science domain to compare tutoring plans created using the planning tool against those made with presentation slide  decks. This study aims to determine whether the dialogue planning tool improves the quality of tutoring plans. The specific research question is: What are the differences between using slides and a planning tool to prepare for peer tutoring? Design of the dialogue planning tool The tutoring plan tool (tuPlan) builds on the five-step dialogue frame (Graesser et al., 1995): Tutor asks a question, tutee answers, tutor gives feedback, improves answer quality, and assesses tutee’s understanding. It further affords the Socratic dialogue approach: Posing a series of probing questions that lead learners to conclusions through their reasoning rather than providing direct answers. We ad apted the dialogue frame to support tutors in planning questions, anticipating correct and incorrect answers, assessing understanding, giving adaptive guidance feedback, and improving answer responses.

In the context of tutoring preparation, a student tutor employs this planning tool to prepare for a one-onone tutoring session for a future tutee. As illustrated in Figure 1, tuPlan provides tutoring prompts for formulating tutor questions (a) and crafting expected tutee ans wers (b). Additionally, the network graph (F igure 2b below) assists users in visualizing and tracking the sequence of the tutoring steps. We designed prompts for  questions (Figure 1a) and answers (1b) to support tutors in creating dialogue tutoring plans. Tutors were instructed to come up with multiple likely tutee answers for each question and to plan a response to each of these. For each tutor respons e, up to three levels of guidance could be specifie d: “Low guidance message,” “High guidance message,” and “Correction me ssage.” The data model is based on the hypothesis that cognitive and metacognitive strategies in tu toring—such as questioning, assessment, and explanation—enable tutors to convey domain knowledge creatively and reflect on their understanding (Roscoe & Chi, 2007). By reasoning through questions and correcting errors, tutors deepen their knowledge. Specifically, the “Response” and “Follow” options in the “Question” p rompts encourage tutors to expand their reasoning a nd reflection, enhancing metacognitive monitoring and knowledge building. The “Low Guidance” and “High Guidance” in the “Answer” prompts serve as adaptive scaffolding strategies, allowing tutors to provide varying levels of explanations and adapt their feedback fle xibly. Fields like “Correctness” and “Alert Message” support tutors in assessing misunderstandings promptly and engaging in metacognitive reflection.

## Method

The tutoring planning tool was evaluated in the sci ence domain through an experimental design with ten  college science majors randomly assigned to two groups: fiv e participants in the control group (CG) and five i n the experimental group (EG). The learning topic was about the water cycle system, specifically exploring how water moves between the hydrosphere, atmosphere, geosphere, and biosphere through visible and invisible processes. The learning-by-tutoring procedure consisted of two main steps (see Table 1). During the first hour, all students received identical instructions on tutorin g plans, studied provided materials, and were allowed to search for additional online resources. In the second hour, participants were asked to prepare a tutoring plan for a onehour tutoring session with a prospective peer tutee, potentially a  classmate from the same course. The key difference was that CG student tutors used slide decks to create their tutoring plans, while EG student tutors used the dialogue planning tool.

## Results

Table 3 summarizes the descriptive statistics for the slides and tuPlans across three variables. The results indicate significant individual differences, particularly in  the number of guidance levels, where extreme value s in the tuPlans (SD = 10.1, Max = 30, Min = 3) skew the distribution. Therefore, we consider the median to account for group differences. The median number of answers per  question is comparable between slides (1.7) and tuPlan s (1.8). By contrast, the median number of guidance levels (12) and question-answer turns (13) in tuPlans notably exceed those in slides (7 and 9, respectively).

In addition, tutor participants expressed positive perceptions of the tutoring preparation process. Fo r instance, one tutor remarked, “Editing wrong answer s and guidance messages helped deepen  my thinking and clarify misconceptions.” Concerns were raised, such as, “This was my first time using this tool  to design a plan, so it took time to get familiar with everything and a bit longer to input the content for each step.”

## Discussion

The first findings reveal that using the tuPlan too l to prepare for peer tutoring generates a higher n umber of question-answer turns, as well as a greater amount of guidance messages, including correctness assessment, alert message, low guidance, high guidance, and correction message, compared to using slides. Among those guidance messages, a higher occurrence of evaluating cogniti on and metacognitive knowledge emerges in tuPlans t han in slides. The prompts combined with a visualization of the dialogue sequence thus supported tutors in the planning of adaptive scaffolding —guidance designed to support building understanding  beyond current knowledge (Munshi et al., 2023). What fell short of expectations was the minimal increase in the number of answers and the cognitive level of analyzing. Moreover, notable individual differences emerged in guidance strategies. Our study is motivated by the lack of research on p eer tutor preparation. In one of the few studies we know of that asked peer tutors what they found hard  (Berghmans et al., 2013), the key challenge mentioned pertained to intervening: knowing when to intervene  and how to guide (asking questions and guiding) wa s experienced as difficult. Also reported as difficult was helping tutees when they got stuck and did not know how to proceed. For a peer tutor having neither deep do main nor pedagogical knowledge, the  only strategy for addressing these challenges is (better) preparation. This is a small contribution to inform how tutor preparation can be accomplished, in particular for the demandin g task of preparing for Socratic tutoring. As a rea listic comparison, we choose presentation software.

There are numerous limitations to this study, from the small-N comparison conducted within a specific science context to the fact that we were focusing exclusively on the preparation phase. We also did not make use of an additional capability of the tuPlan tool: All tutoring planning information is stored in a dat abase. This capability affords steps such as providing feedback  on the developing tutoring plan, for instance, by applying a rubric and/or by comparing multiple tutors’ plans. Exploiting and testing these capabilities, as well as the inclusion of Large Language Models, will be the subject of future work. Despite the limitations, we are encouraged by our initial findings and hope to succeed in moving peer tutors away from knowledge telling to knowledge construction, to their and their peers’ benefit.
