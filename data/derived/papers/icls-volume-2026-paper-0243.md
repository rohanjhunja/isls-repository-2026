The need and the goal Artificial Intelligence (AI) is reshaping the landscape of education, transforming how teachers design instruction, scaffold inquiry, and engage students in meaning-making. Generative AI tools, particularly large language models, are increasingly used as co-design partners in lesson planning, assessment development, and instructional communication. These technologies promise to enhance efficiency, creativity, and personalization in education, yet research suggests that their classroom adoption often reinfor ces existing instructional patterns rather than catalyzing transformation (Avraamidou, 2024; Bozkurt et al., 2024). Scholars have warned that the proliferation of AI in education is driven by commercial and technological agendas that risk reproducing colonial, epistemic, and environmental inequities rather thandemocratizing knowledge (Avraamidou, 2024; Carmi & Nakou, 2025). AI brings new opportunities for data-driven and interactive learning but also raises ethical and ecological concerns. Misinformation, algorithmic bias, and surveillance are well-documented (Bender et al., 2021; Pangrazio & Selwyn, 2022), and large-scale model training produces substantial energy use and emissions (Yuan et al., 2024; Varelas et al., 2025). These dynamics situate AI as both a pedagogical and environmental issue—requiring educators to weigh innovation against planetary impact (Bozkurt et al., 2024; Sperling et al., 2024). These tensions are heightened in climate and environmental education, which emphasizes systems thinking, civic reasoning, and ethical reflection (Sezen-Barrie et al., 2025). AI can assist teachers in analyzing data, visualizing systems, and modeling scenarios (Varelas et al., 2025), yet it often reproduces narrow framings of climate justice and overlooks local and community knowledge (Nguyen et al., 2025). This study investi gates how in-service teachers conceptualize and use AI in environmental and climate education—examining perceived benefits, challenges, and professional learning needs. This study will respond to the following research questions: (1) What are in-service teachers’ current uses of and perspectives on AI tools in their teaching, both generally and within climate and environmental education? (2) What benefits and challenges do teachers perceive when integrating AI into climate and environmental education, particularly in relation to student learning, data literacy, and ethical reasoning? And

(3) What forms of professional learning and support do teachers view as necessary to develop critical AI literacy

and to integrate AI tools ethically and sustainably in their classrooms?

## Conceptual framework

This study is grounded in the concept of AI literacy, extended through emerging work on critical AI literacy and socioecological learning frameworks  in climate and environmental education. AI literacy encompasses the knowledge, skills, and dispositions required to understand, use, and evaluate AI systems responsibly (Sperling et al., 2024; Veldhuis et al., 2024). Within educational contexts, it involves teachers’ ability to interpret how AI technologies function, apply them meaningfully in pedagogy, and assess their social and environmental implications. Foundational studies identify AI literacy as essential for  supporting teachers’ agency and reflective decision-making in an era of rapidly evolving digital tools (Bozkurt et al., 2024). However, as Avraamidou (2024) and Carmi and Nakou (2025) argue, technical proficiency alone is insufficient. Critical AI literacy calls for attention to the political, cultural, and ecological dimensions of AI. It frames AI not merely as a computational system but as a sociotechnical phenomenon that is embedded in infrastructures that shape power relations, 1697 knowledge hierarchies, and environmental sustainability. In the context of climate and environmental education, these critical orientations align with the field’s emphasis on place-based learning and socioecological systems thinking. Environmental education scholars conceptualize learning as situated within interdependent human and ecological systems, emphasizing that understanding climate change requires attending to relationships among local places, global systems, and lived experiences. (Bang et al., 2022; Pugh, 2019). When AI is introduced into these spaces, it mediates how environmental narratives are constructed, whose voices are amplified, and how learners interpret responsibility for the ecosystem.  Nguyen et al. (2025) show that generative AI’s depictions of climate justice often lack place-based sensitivity and omit Indigenous and community perspectives, reinforcing the need for educators to act as interpretive mediators. Building on these insights, this study conceptualizes critical environmental AI literacy  as an integrative framework combining technical, ethical, and ecological dimensions. It positions teachers as key agents in interpreting and shaping how AI is used to teach about the environment. By examining how teachers articulate these decisions, this study illuminates the conditions under which AI can contribute to reflective, participatory, and sustainable learning.

This study employs an explanatory sequential mixed-methods design (Creswell & Clark, 2017) to investigate how teachers engage with AI tools in environmental and climate education. The initial survey phase identifies broad trends and patterns through quantitative and qualitative online questions to capture teachers’ current perspectives and experiences with AI integration. The next phase will include case interviews to deepen understanding. This proposal reports on the first phase that is the pilot implementation of the survey.

## Participants

The participants in this study were in-service elementary and secondary school teachers across California, recruited through educator networks and professional development communities. They represented a range of subject areas and years of experience, prov iding a diverse sample of educators. The pilot survey included twelve in-service and preservice teachers representing upper elementary, middle, and high school levels. Most participants identified science as their primary subject area, including physics, c hemistry, biology, and earth science, while three specifically taught environmental or climate science and one taught social studies. Two participants at the elementary level taught multiple subjects, including one student teacher. Overall, two-thirds of the teachers (67%) reported using AI tools such as ChatGPT or other generative platforms to support their teaching, while one-third (33%) indicated that they did not use AI (Table 1). Three participants (one high school environmental science, one high school social studies, and one elementary school teacher) reported not using AI tools in their teaching, citing ethical hesitation, unfamiliarity, or philosophical misalignment. For exampl e, a high-school environmental-science teacher reflected a justiceoriented ethical stance: I am not yet comfortable using AI due to the way that I understand AI was originally trained. Finally, I am interested in humanizing education and I do not feel that using AI is in line with my understanding that education should be embodied. Such reflections reveal that teachers’ decisions to abstain are often value-driven, aligning with sustainability and humanistic frameworks in climate-justice education.

Perceived benefits of AI integration in environmental and climate education All nine AI-using teachers identified at least two benefits of integrating AI into environmental and climate education. The most frequently mentioned advantages were designing instructional materials efficiently (8 teachers), supporting students in exploring projections of climate solutions (7), finding and adapting instructional resources (6), analyzing large environmental data sets (5), providing immersive simulations (4), and offering personalized learning pathways (3). These patterns suggest that educators primarily view AI as a means to enhance instructional design, deepen scientific inquiry, and make complex environmental systems. Teachers’ qualitative responses illuminate how these perceived benefits manifest in practice. A high-school environmental science teacher explained, “Using AI to analyze pollution data and visualize it provides access to data that people would not normally have.” This comment highlights how AI tools enable students to interact with large-scale environmental data that would otherwise be inaccessible, expanding inquiry opportunities beyond traditional classroom resources. Similarly, another high-school biology teacher described how visualization tools deepened student understanding: “Students can visualize the impact of climate change on ecosystems and have deeper conversations about the cause and effect of human impact on the environment.” These examples illustrate how AI enhances conceptual understanding by connecting abstract scientific processes to tangible, data-driven representations. Other educators emphasized AI’s potential to promote inquiry and dialogue. One high-school teacher who co-designed AI chatbots observed, “It allows students to ask questions and get feedback in an authentic manner based on the questions they have. It helps them digest information they are seeing in real time.” This example reveals how AI can extend student agency by creating interactive and responsive learning environments, enabling learners to engage with environmental data in personally meaningful ways. Overall, teachers viewed AI as expanding student agency and access to environmental data while balancing efficiency. Their reflections illustrate an emerging critical AI literacy.

## Perceived limitations and ethical concerns

Across participants, teachers expressed a complex mix of limitations and concerns about integrating AI into environmental and climate education. Quantitatively, the most frequently cited challenges were inaccuracy or outdated information (8 teachers), biased resources or misinformation (6), ethical concerns such as fairness and academic integrity (6), data-privacy risks (5), and environmental impacts (4). While many educators recognized the potential of AI to streamline teaching and enhance inquiry, their c omments revealed an equally strong awareness of its epistemic, ethical, and pedagogical boundaries. 1699 Teachers articulated their concerns with a deep sense of professional responsibility. A high-school physics teacher who emphasized cross-referencing data described the difficulty of maintaining scientific accuracy, noting, “With ever-changing statistics and news, I try not to use AI when stating numerical data unless I cross-reference it.” This comment reflects an awareness that AI’s convenience must be balanced with the teacher’s role as a verifier and contextualizer of information. An early-career teacher also voiced concerns about transparency and control over AI’s informational sources. Other educators foregrounded environmental ethics.. A high-school environmental science teacher expressed discomfort with the sustainability costs of AI infrastructure, r emarking, “I am uncomfortable with the water and energy use as well as the environmental-justice issues associated with AI use”. Such reflections reveal a paradox within climate education: teachers are using AI to teach climate justice while remaining critical of the environmental toll of AI’s energy-intensive computational systems. Beyond epistemic and ethical challenges, several teachers identified pedagogical and social risks associated with AI integration. A high-school teacher who frequently used image-generation tools described the struggle to maintain instructional precision and accessibility, explaining, “Sometimes [AI outputs are] too difficult for students to understand, sometimes it’s too simple… I can’t get AI to remove certain text/images even when I prompt it. It is limited in its ability to adapt.” This comment suggests that AI systems often lack the contextual sensitivity teachers bring to curriculum design. Another middle-school science teacher warned of the potential erosion of collaborative classroom culture, stating, “Declining collaboration with classroom community due to overreliance on AI.” Such reflections emphasize the importance of maintaining humandialogue and co-construction of knowledge even in technologically mediated learning en vironments. These concerns reinforce the need to view teachers not as passive adopters of technology.

Professional learning needs Despite these concerns, all twelve participants identified the necessity of professional learning to foster ethical, informed, and creative engagement with AI. The most frequently desired supports included teacher workshops or demonstrations showing concrete classroom examples (7 teachers), communities of practice for peer sharing (5), training on evaluating bias and data accuracy  (5), guidance on ethical and environmental impacts  (4), and curricular resources aligned with NGSS and environmental-science standards (3). Teachers’ qualitative responses provided compelling insight into what effective professional development might look like. A high-school teacher who co-created AI chatbots emphasized the value of situated modeling, sharing, “Seeing how teachers in the classroom have used these tools, what best practice looks like, and how lessons can tackle these ideas within a progression will best support instruction.” This teacher’s reflection suggests that observing AI integration in real contexts can bridge theory and practice while promoting critical reflection. A high-school biology teacher echoed this need for ethically grounded learning: I would benefit from professional learning that focuses on how to integrate AI tools ethically and effectively… especially workshops or communities of practice that model classroom applications, address data accuracy and bias, and provide examples of student-centered projects. These calls for mentorship, demonstration, and ethical modeling converge on a vision of practice-based, reflective, and justice-oriented professional learning. This insight reframes AI fluency not as a technical ability alone but as a communicative and ethical practice, integral to both teacher design and student inquiry. This study extends emerging work on AI literacy and critical AI literacy (Avraamidou, 2024; Bozkurt et al., 2024; Pangrazio & Selwyn, 2022) into justice-oriented environmental education by examining how in-service teachers make sense of AI in environmental  and climate contexts. Findings show that teachers act as balancing AI’s potential for efficiency, visualization, and data analysis with concerns about bias, accuracy, and environmental impact. This dual stance represents an emerging form of critical environmental AI literacy, where educators interrogate how AI shapes environmental understanding, equity, and responsibility (Avraamidou, 2025; Nguyen et al., 2025). Teachers’ lived experiences and classroom innovations provide essential feedback for designing AI systems that enha nce, rather than replace, teacher judgment and community knowledge. Meaningful AI integration depends less on tool proficiency than on collaborative professional learning that cultivates ethical, contextually grounded, and justice-oriented practice. Linking AI literacy with environmental learning underscores the value of partnering with purpose, uniting technical and sociocultural expertise to design learning that is both innovative and reflective.
