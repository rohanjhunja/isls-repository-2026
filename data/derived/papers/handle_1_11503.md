# Computational Thinking in the Age of Artificial Intelligence: Charting Directions for Learning Sciences Research and Practice

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
The introduction of artificial intelligence and machine learning applications to the public has led to heated discussions about its potential impacts and risks for K-12 education. One particular challenge has been to decide what students should learn about these topics, and how this relates to computational thinking, which has served as an umbrella for introducing CS education in schools. We propose CT+, an expanded version of computational thinking,, to include artificial intelligence and machine learning technologies and outline different directions for future research in the learning sciences. We also discuss how these efforts can be informed by lessons learned from the last decade in designing instructional programs, integrating it with other subjects, and addressing issues of algorithmic bias and justice in teaching computing in schools.

## Introduction

Generative AI (GenAI) and machine learning (ML) are  transforming K-12 education (Long & Magerko, 2020; Touretzky et al., 2018) by providing assistance in learning and automatic feedback while also raising concerns about academic integrity, (meta -) cognitive laziness, and algorithmic bias (Bozkurt et al., 2 024). However, AI’s rapid deployment has outpaced educational guideline s, necessitating new approaches to its integration in CS and other classrooms (Grover, 2024). Integrating AI/ML into curricula requires dispelling misconceptions, ensuring equity, and fostering robust AI mental models in students. Over the years, computational thinking (CT)  has provided a vehicle to bring computational creation and problem solving into classrooms. Over time, CT conceptualizations broadened to include creative expression and social action (Grover & Pea, 2018). The need to expand CT to include socio-cultural and socio-political dimensions in addition to technical CT co ncepts and practices led Kafai and Proctor (2022) to cal l for the development of broader computational lite racies as part of CT learning in K-12 education with the overall goal of helping students move from a narrow technical engagement to include also contexts as well as “values, biases, and histories embedded  in computational technologies and cultures which run on computers” (p. 147).

Extending Computational Thinking for AI/ML (CT+) We contend that we now need “CT+”, a framework that extends traditional problem-solving to incorporate AI/ML ethics, data-driven reasoning, and critical engagement with AI t echnologies and tools. To achieve this goal, we believe learning sciences research needs to address the following key directions: First, traditional computational models rely on det erministic, logically verifiable algorithms, wherea s ML models operate through opaque, data-driven processes that optimize objectives based on statistical inference. Tedre et and colleagues (2021) introduced the conce pt of CT 2.0, distinguishing it from the original conceptualizations of  CT by highlighting its botto m-up, probabilistic nature. Unlike traditional progra mming, ML models are evaluated using test datasets rather than pre-defined outcomes. This shift demands that students develop novel mental models to understand and evalu ate ML-based systems. CT+ must encompass learning of both the old CT and CT 2.0, as well as concepts of statistical confidence and probabilistic success criteria. Future research should explore pedagogies that effectively scaffold these complex ideas for K-12 learners. Second, beyond the focus on new content, learning science research must emphasize ethical and justicecentered considerations in computational design to address the socio-political influence on —and impact of — AI/ML (Broussard, 2018). This includes tackling bia ses in data and ML training and deployment processe s, discriminatory AI practices, and real-world inequities. As students develop CT+ skills, they should critically examine data provenance, fairness, and societal impact. Culturally relevant pedagogies must integrate discussions on algorithmic fairness, privacy, and stakeholder p erspectives. Examples include algorithm auditing in  high school ML projects and critical inquiry into AI-generated content. Effective CT+ pedagogy interconnects cognitive, social, and ethical dimensions.

Third, we need to involve all stakeholders, includi ng students, teachers, and other relevant partners. Ensuring student identity development and empowerme nt requires equity-oriented, inclusive teaching strategies, particularly for groups historically marginalized i n computing education and careers. AI/ML education must be integrated into teacher preparation and address its disproportionate impact on underserved populations. Teachers play a critical role in CS education, necessitating  professional development through research-backed strategies like unplugged activities, subject connections, and  teacher communities (Grover et al., 2024). Co-designing curricula and supporting formative assessment are e ssential. Additionally, educating parents, administrators, and other stakeholders will help build a supportive AI/ML learning ecosystem in schools. Fourth, learning science research must examine inte gration of CT into STEM and other subjects. Weintrop and colleagues (2016) expanded CT for STEM  to include data practices, modeling, problem-solving, and systems thinking. CT+ will be a vital 21st-century skill with broad applicability, encouraging the integration of CS and AI/ML across subjects. Understanding data  is a key part of CT+, aiding analysis, visualization, and understanding of domain-specific phenomena. With the rise of big data, educ ation must emphasize data science skills like collection, cleaning, and analysis. Whi le AI/ML is data-driven, its applications still rely on algorithms that shape data selection.

Finally, learning science researchers should promot e CT+ through diverse, inclusive pedagogies that engage both teachers and students. Approaches like project-based, inquiry-based, and game-based learning help introduce computational problem-solving in accessible ways. Creating inclusive lear ning environments i s essential to broadening participation, especially a mong minoritized groups. Real-world contexts and accessible tools like Scratch, Snap!, and NetsBlox support CT+ learning. Developing more tools for K-12 learners will help build both technical and ethical AI/ML competencies.

## Conclusion

Our proposal for the learning sciences to forge a p ath forward for CT+ builds on the large body of research that defined and framed CT for K-12 CS and STEM education and expands it to include AI/ML technologies and issues. Defining and framing CT has been an ever-evolving journey as understanding of learning and c omputing has grown along with the fast-paced growth of CS. The introduction of AI/ML necessitates an expansion of what we mean by CT. In line with recent progress, we call for a stronger emphasis on critical and ethical considerations in developing CT+ competencies, while extending the technical concepts and practices to include new data-driven ML programming paradigms. We also recommend several  directions where efforts to promote CT should be focused, drawing on lessons learned from K-12 CS education d uring the last decades. Furthermore, we recommend that AI/ML topics and issues should not b e limited to CS classes but follow efforts that hav e introduced CT in STEM topics and across the curriculum. Realizing all goals and demands will require significant efforts and resources on the part of all involved in the education ecosystem. CT+ could well aid in providing some of the answers in this monumental, multi-faceted, emergent enterprise.
