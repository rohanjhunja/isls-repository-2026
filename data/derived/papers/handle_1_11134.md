# Toward Black Feminist Mathematics Pedagogies: The Influence of Positionalities

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
We seek to understand the influence of our positionalities during an equity audit of a reform-oriented secondary mathematics curriculum. In the equity audit, we empirically explored how to support humanizing and just teaching, particularly for Black girls. Drawing on intersectionality theory, we report on how our positionalities—namely as a Black woman, white man, and white woman—influenced our coding with the Black Feminist Mathematics Pedagogies framework (Joseph, 2021). Our data include our coding experiences and a transcript of an hour-long reflective dialogue on how our gendered and racialized experiences as students and teachers influenced our coding in the equity audit. We report on two themes that emerged in reflective dialogue, the savior complex and interest convergence, and connect these to our attempts to design against white supremacy culture in our curriculum equity audit. We conclude with insights about tensions between a truly reimagined mathematics education and making a difference for students now.

## Conceptual framework: Intersectionality

As we interrogate how our positionalities inform ou r understanding of how secondary mathematics curric ular materials can support Black Feminist Mathematics Pedagogies  (BlackFMP; Joseph, 2021), we draw on Crenshaw’s (1989) theory of intersectionality. The BlackFMP framework is also rooted in intersectional ity and connects research on ambitious mathematics instruct ion with dimensions of pedagogy that Joseph (2021) argues are essential for equity for Black girls: integrati on of the social with the academic; opportunities f or developing expansive, robust mathematics identities; and space to exercise critical consciousness and reclaim mathematics. Intersectionality underscores the idea that focusing on a single aspect of a person's identity, such as race, gender, or class, obscures the complex interplay of  different forms of discrimination and oppression. While intersectionality theory has broader implications t han merely how our identities (e.g., dis/ability, g ender, class) intersect to influence our perceptions of the world  and how others perceive us, for the purpose of ana lyzing how our backgrounds inform our recognition of BlackFMP, we concentrate on the intersecting facets of our identities in relation to power and marginalization. We all ha ve aspects of our identities that shape our lived e xperiences, whether we are conscious of them or not, including language, sexuality, gender, disability, fem ininities, masculinities, religion, culture, and class. Althou gh there are many more dimensions to our identities, these particular dimensions often indicate positions of p rivilege and oppression, with historically dominant  identity markers (e.g., male, heterosexual, white) bestowing unearned advantages, such as assumptions of competence. ## Context

Given our proximity to CPM, we first offer a descri ption of CPM as an organization and then describe o ur curriculum analysis as the context that seeded our question on how our positionalities inform our sens emaking around power and privilege within mathematics education. Mathematics Educational Program As a predominantly white institution, CPM recognize s they have “fallen short of encouraging people fro m historically oppressed social groups to join the em ployee candidate pool” (CPM Strategic Goal 1 for 20 26: Identify and disrupt inequities within our organiza tion and in mathematics education within our sphere  of influence). This shortcoming perpetuates the contin uation of centering whiteness within mathematics cu rricula. CPM recognizes the power it wields in the broader m athematics education community giv en its role in the mathematical experiences of roughly 700,000 students and their teachers. Curriculum Analysis We began analyzing Inspiring Connections Course 3 (8th Grade) to interrogate who is currently served and who can be served by CPM’s most recent efforts to develop mathematics curricula centered around tenets of culturally relevant teaching. Given the United States’ gendered and racialized school system, in our analysis, we constructed our codebook around Joseph’s (2021) BlackFMP framew ork. Building on Crenshaw’s (1989) theory of intersectionality, BlackFMP describes four dimensio ns of mathematics teaching that can make instructio n more affirming for Black  girls: ambitious mathematics instruction, academic and social integration, robust mathematics identities, and critical consciousness. In our analysis of the written mathematics curricul um using the BlackFMP intersectional lens, we asked, How do race, gender, class, and other social identities come together to influence the ways students might experience contexts of mathematical tasks? After coding several lessons, discussing the result s, resolving dilemmas, and modifying the codebook further, we developed sub-codes for each of the four dimensions of BlackFMP a nd established grades of each (i.e., problematic, emerging/weak, present, robu st; see Jasien et al., under review). We increasing ly reached a consensus on assigning sub-codes to curricular elements, yet our teaching expe riences and individual identities made reaching a consensus on the grades for each co de challenging. For example, while coding Door Ques tions as humanizing, we initially disagreed about whether teacher and student interactions, such as taking time to get to know students before entering the classroom, were c ommon practice amongst secondary mathematics teache rs across the United States. Practices for getting to  know students’ communities and interests beyond the ir mathematical expertise are still novel to many teac hers (e.g., WCNC, 2017), but are common humanizing practices to others. As we continued coding Inspiring Connections Course 3, we recognized additional instances in which our positionalities influenced our interpr etations of the written curriculum for creating mor e equitable experiences for all students, and for Black girls in particular.

## Research design

We draw on Milner’s (2007) process for conducting e ducational research as we unpack how our positional ities inform our understanding of how curricular materials can align with BlackFMP (Joseph, 2021): Researchers … engage in reflection together to think through what is happening in a particular research community, with race and culture placed at  the core. … The point is that researchers … may interpret an experience or an interaction in very different ways, depending on [their] life worlds, phenomenologically speaking. (Milner, 2007, p. 397) Collective reflection provides an opportunity for our coding team to unpack the tensions and disagreements in our coding as they relate to our positionalities.

Our collective reflection contributes to the learni ng sciences through our cultural interpretations of  our auto-biographical reflection. In this way, we engage in methods akin to collaborative autoethnography (Chang et al., 2016) by drawing on ethnographic methods to connect our e xperiences with broader cultural meanings. The collaborative nature of our work increases the trustworthiness and generalizability of our findings as our multiple perspectives and experiences complement and contrad ict each other, thus taming our individual perspect ives through multivocality (Hernandez et al., 2017).

Sources of evidence After coding two chapters of Inspiring Connections Course 3 using our BlackFMP (Joseph, 2021) codebook, we recorded an hour-long reflective conversation to make sense of how o ur experiences with power and privilege played out in our coding, and between us within the coding team. To guide our conversation, we adapted prompts from resources for supporting reflective teaching (i.e., Zeichner & Liston, 2014). Examples of our pro mpts include: What beliefs and assumptions become visible in my c oding? How do my racialized, gendered, classed, and other experiences as a mathematics learner and teacher affect my coding? Our conversation was semi-structured, which provided opportunities for each o f us to expand on and explore ideas as they were pr esented. The prompts were used to reignite the conversation as needed.

## Methods

After transcribing the Zoom video recording with transcription software (temi.com), we independently identified evocative moments in the transcript. Then, we synchronously and collectively themed the selected text (Saldaña, 2021), both orally and in comments through a shared  Google Doc of the transcript. This informal themin g and debriefing process encouraged us to respond to and extend the ideas shared amongst our team (e.g., com mon instances of assimilation and resistance to white supremacy culture). To support our investigation, we adapted two socio-historical questions from Boveda and Annamma’s

(2023) positioning framework:

● How are our professional situatedness, power dynami cs, and collaboration impacted by privilege and oppression?

● To what extent does the institution we are affiliat ed with hold power within mathematics education and influence our coding?

Our findings are partial answers to these questions. The transcript excerpts that we share have been e dited to remove filler words such as “like” and “um.”

## Findings

We realize that our “baselines” of expectations for  teaching are different because of our racialized a nd gendered teaching experiences (e.g., there are things that w ould look very good to Jasien and more basic to Ean es). In the reflective conversation on o ur relationships to power and privilege in our codi ng team, we noted two prominent areas where our positionalities foreground instances of resistance and assimilation to white supremacy culture. Savior complex and assimilation: “We’re not doing this to save Black girl s from white supremacy in math.” As soon as Eanes joined the coding team, we collect ively realized that our perceptions during curriculum coding were rooted in our experiences as teachers. It was only through critical self-and collective-reflection that we were able to recognize the perv asiveness of a savior complex in our teaching and h ow this influenced our coding. A savior complex is the false idea that external forc es are needed to “save the day.” This idea is learn ed without effort through enculturation into the pervasive whi te supre macy culture and is often reinscribed in teacher education through the romanticization of using “bes t practices” to “fix” students of color (Aronson, 2 017) by making them fit white definitions of success (i.e.,  assimilation). Many educational interventions  embody this ideology, failing to  consider the systemic causes that led to the need for intervention (Wilkes, 2022) and to center the needs and brilliance of (Black) students.

When interrogating our collective teaching tenures, we identified shared experiences of a savior complex despite holding different racial identities. For in stance, Eanes later shared, “I went into teaching w ith that exact same white savior complex as a Black person, and that was upsetting.” We recognized o ur unconscious desire to position ourselves as saviors given our relationshi ps to power as teachers; we viewed ourselves as abl e to assist our students in rising above their circumstances. Through our reflective interrogation of our relationships to power within mathematics education, we noticed how our ex periences in teacher preparation programs (includin g traditional programs [Eanes and Lolkus] and Teach F or America [Lolkus and Jasien]) led to this savior  complex as educators serving marginalized students.

In our coding, we saw the savior complex emerge in our coding of teacher notes that described growth mindset interventions (Dweck, 2016). Lolkus coded a  growth mindset intervention as a productive way to  foster equity (humanizing, present), but Eanes coded the i ntervention as problematic. As we debriefed this co ding disagreement as a team, we all came to see Eanes’ p oint that growth mindset interventions place the lo cus of the problem and of change within students rather than within the system. We came to realize that growth mindset and similar interventions perpetuate the idea that children, particularly Black children, need saving, because: [these interventions do] not interrupt assumptions… that Black learners are less successful than their peers because they do not work as hard as their peers [and instead] reinforce the assumption that Black people have an inferior work ethic. … framing intelligence in this way fails to either consider or address the systematic ways Black children are oppressed in school. (Wilkes, 2022, p. 34) Thus, we see tacit enactments of the savior complex  emerge even in our coding when we view interventions that aim to “fix” students (e.g., the ir mindsets and dispositions) rather than the syste m as productive and promoting equity.

When reflecting on our power as a team, we wondered how pervasive a savior complex is in mathematics education. Eanes had noted that: I realized that in teaching, that’s kind of all tha t we do. We assume, particularly for black students and poor students, we assume that their wa ys of learning is wrong and their ways of doing, we need, they need to be fixed.

This claim that “that’s kind of all we do” lead to an “aha” moment for Jasien. She began to question how endemic the savior complex is in teaching as a model of education-through-instruction (versus other possible models), and what can be done to unlearn the idea of fixing students within this model. This led to existential questions of what it would mean to teach if we fully eradicated the idea of fixing students, and how we could support such unlearning through curriculum. This prompted Lolkus to identif y tensions related to his role as a curriculum writer, particularly around relationships between the savio r complex, teachers’ need for affection from studen ts, and teachers’ felt need to maintain control. He wondered how curriculum can help teachers decenter themselves rather than cater to these things: I think teacher lust is a thing where teachers want  and need the affection of students and it’s, I don’t know, it’s just how much of that control is d esired by teachers and how much are we catering to that with what we’re writing as the [teacher notes]. … And are we successfully using study team teaching strategies tonegotiate away fr om that in ways that a teacher can pick up our materials and decenter themselves?

In sum, our reflective conversation on our position alities uncovered that all of us experienced savior  complex as teachers, which sparked our recognition of how “fix ing” students is embedded within the education syst em and our complicity in our current roles. We began to wo nder and brainstorm how we can ensure we do not unintentionally perpetuate this idea and instead mo ve toward centering students’ brilliance. Our refle ctive conversation made this explicit, and Eanes reframed the purpose of our analytic work succinctly: We’re not doing this to save black girls from white  supremacy in math. We’re doing this because we believe that everyone should have equal access to good mathematics regardless of race, class, and gender.

Interest convergence: “If white men aren’t going to agree with it, then what’s the point?” Our positionalities inform the ways that we exist i n relation to white supremacy culture. Though our e fforts in coding curriculum using BlackFMP are an attempt to push back against interest convergence (one symptom  of white supremacy culture), our reflecti ve conversation uncovered ways that we are complici t in maintaining hegemony in mathematics curricula. Interest convergence is the problematic reality that “racial equality and equity for people of color will be pursued and advanced when they converge with the interests, needs, expectations, and ideologies of Whites” (Milner, 2008, p. 333). Using BlackFMP to code CPM’s curriculum was an attempt to push back on interest convergence by centering Black gir ls, which can inherently advance equity for Black g irls in mathematics classrooms. However, in discussing our relationship to power and privilege as educators, w e found that, even in this attempt, we are still complicit in interest convergence. Our codebook’s code descriptions use the language o f “students” rather than “Black girls,” despite the fact that the codes originated from a framework tha t drew on research about Black girls’ experiences i n mathematics classrooms. This has the flavor of mu ch research on “ambitious instruction” in mathemati cs that promotes particular teaching practices as beneficia l for all students. We chose to use “students” in our code descriptions because we believe that centering thos e at the margins benefits those who are already cen tered. In this way, our use of “students” instead of “Black g irls” re-inscribed whiteness as we justified our use of a framework centering Black girls by claiming it was also good for all students, with all students tacitly pointing to white students. Our relationship to CPM, an organiz ation that makes mathematics curricula for students  of all races and ethnicities throughout the United States, surely informed our decision not to use the term “Black girls” in our codebook. The transcript below contains our conversation about our conflict with this decision. Lolkus:  I think that this comes out in BlackFMP and the way  that we’ve adjusted the code activity for all students … and removing the language around Black s tudents. But I do feel a little bit like, are we playing this game of, we need to show that it is go od for white students, affluent students, and in this power broker way of saying, look, it’s good for you, therefore BlackFMP is good for everybody, or it’s okay to engage in BlackFMP because you are not harmed in this instructional practice. … [Lolkus goes on to revisit others’ ideas.] Jasien:   I wanna pick up on this, what you were saying Mike [Lolkus], about, so we’re, we’re looking at our codebook, which we’ve developed from Dr. Joseph’s B lackFMP … And we also say, oh, we can see that this is good for white students and we’re gonna generalize that. Then if it’s good for Black girls plus white students, then it is good for everybody and we’re kind of okay. Lolkus:  I think the need to say that it’s good for white students is the thing that is gnawing at me. Jasien:    That resonates with me … I feel like part of the reason is because if you can’t make that justification, then … it’s gonna be picked up less and used in fewer classrooms. I think it is true that it is good for white students, but, the need to, to say that … the need to justify something that doesn’t really need to be justified. It should just be taken … for the value that it has in and of itself. Eanes:   And I think that’s just a function of whiteness and  white supremacy, right? … you have to add this asterisk on it to say that it’s okay for white peop le too … We always feel the need to put in these qualifiers of, okay, if this is good for Black girl s, this is gonna be good for white kids. What’s the point of putting in these qualifiers?

In this conversation, we articulated that, through centralizing the needs of white students for capita listic logics, we uphold the status quo of enacting “policy decisions around the curriculum and instruction in teacher education …[maintaining] the interests of White students at their core” (Milner, 2008, p. 337). Thus, our decision to expand beyond Black girls upholds a realized white hegemon y, in Lolkus’ words, a kind of “power brokering,” w ithout effectively pushing back on interest convergence. W e also questioned whether our logic of if it is good for Black girls and good for white students, then it is good for all students was faulty. There are so many different student groups with different needs. Are the needs of Sican gu Lakota boys living on a reservation the same as the needs of Black girls in Detroit? BlackFMP builds on Black  Feminist Thought (e.g., Collins, 2000), which tells us that individual Black girls’ mathematical experiences, opinions and perspectives, and ways of relating to the collective of Black womanhood are unique: Black women are indi viduals. It also builds on Critical Race Feminism (e.g., Evans-Winters & Love, 2015), which tells us that there is a historical context that situates Black girls’ individual experiences and creates some coherence across them. We wonder if we are perhaps extending the logics of Critical Race Feminism too far in our generalization, perhaps in a way that embodies interest convergence. We still argue that, because the needs of Black girls are so often excluded from mathematics spaces, attending to the dimensions of BlackFMP should be good for all students. Yet, o ur positionalities do not allow us to have full con fidence in this claim—we do not have a Lakota man on our coding team, for instance. And, as Philip et al. (2019) point out, any claim that something is good for all students (e.g., “best practices”) should be questioned since teaching requires responsiveness to the ever-changing needs of students as individuals. Even more, we consider how our claim that a curriculum that embodies the dimensions of BlackFMP is good for all students causes harm in that it “justifies something that doesn’t really need to be justified” but should instead be “taken for the value that i t has in and of itself” (Jasien). Eanes noted that,  in a capitalist society dominated by white people in general and white men in particular, we feel pressure to “add this asteri sk on it to say that it’s okay for white people too.” Eanes que stioned, “What’s the point of putting in these qualifiers?” At the conclusion of our conversation, she reframed her question: …when we talk about how math as a whole, mathematic s is a white space. […] Because it is placed under this white male lens, there has to be a quantifier. Because if white men aren’t going to agree with it, then what’s the point?

Eanes’s rhetorical question points toward the unfor tunate realities of one way to make a difference at  scale: we have to “play the game to change the game” (Gutiérr ez, 2009), in our case, by supporting the developme nt of curricular materials that meet the requirements for adoption lists and thus can make it into classrooms. Otherwise, “what’s the point?” Interest convergence is built into the system of mathematics education, and figuring out how to challenge this in our coding is complex work. Ou r positionalities, including our institutional affiliation wit h CPM, influence how we make sense of disrupting inequities in mathematics education.

## Discussion

Understanding how our identities and experiences in form our interpretive analyses is a significant yet  under-acknowledged issue in science in general and the le arning sciences in particular because people and th eir interactions constitute our data. Our positionaliti es inform how we make sense of power and privilege within mathematics education by making our experiences our baseline and standard, informing what we each can see as desirable and problematic. In this paper, we illustra ted how efforts to create more equitable curricula can perpetuate instruction that aims to fix students (e.g., growth mindset). We labeled this as a manifest ation of the savior complex and described our realizations about  how deeply embedded the ideas of fixing and saving  are in education. Intellectually, we understand that we ca n eradicate the savior complex by seeing students i n their full humanity, instead of seeing them as causes to save and fix. Yet, our power and privilege as people who  are successful within the current system result in a tendency to view our way—the white culture way—as the best or one right way. Countering this tendency in our rese arch requires more than intellectual knowledge: it requires ongoing collaborative reflection as an analysis team so that we can surface biases that creep into our coding. Only through such intentional work can we counter the id eology of whiteness that “operates to devalue, oppr ess, and discriminate those perceived as ‘less’” (Battey & Leyva, 2016). We also illustrated how centering Black girls while aiming to support the creation of curricular materials that are good for “all” students can lead to intere st convergence. Given our proximity to and influenc e in CPM, an organization with some power in the United States mathematics education system, we recognize our po tential for challenging the status quo and embracing discom fort as we work to foreground particularly marginal ized student populations and their ways of knowing in ou r curricular materials. It is critical that we push  back on interest convergence and  center marginalized groups. Our analysis indicates that even in a project that aims to center Black girls, our unconscious tendency is to subtly recenter white students. Our reflective conversation made the tension betwee n a reimagined mathematics education (Martin,

2021) and making a difference for students now (Jos eph, 2021) visceral: the savior complex is so perva sive that

it is built into our model of teaching-through-instruction and interest convergence is creeping in to our codebook because of capitalistic realities. We know that wor king towards a reimagined mathematics education req uires decentering ourselves and using our privilege to re center students. This idea is deceptively simple: o ur analysis indicates that decentering ourselves and white cult ure requires collaborative vigilance and ongoing re flection. Engaging in collective reflection (Milner, 2007) ga ve us a nuanced understanding of how our positional ities influence how we make sense of power and privilege within mathematics education and gave us insights into how to be more intentional in our resistance of white supremacy culture in our qualitative coding.
