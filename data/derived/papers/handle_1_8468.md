# Adaptation Principles to Foster Engagement and Equity in Project-Based Learning

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Teachers adapt science curriculum materials in planned and unplanned ways to address a variety of goals. This professional learning design seeks to support 18 elementary science teachers in biweekly professional learning (PL) sessions during COVID 19. They discuss and share their own adaptations for enhancing opportunities for productive disciplinary engagement and equity by collaboratively analyzing one another’s teaching artifacts. Findings from transcribed interviews, PLs, and video of teaching are synthesized toward five adaptation principles.

Studies show that science teachers can make use of teacher-facing materials to guide their teaching decisions and the sequence of learning activities. At the same time, teachers make in-the-moment decisions to adapt those materials and learning contexts. Teachers adapt lessons to scaffold student learning, differentiate instruction, and respond to student ideas and questions (Drake & Sherin, 2006). When te achers do not adapt curriculum by responding to stu dents’ questions and ideas, they may miss opportunities to  foster an equity-centered environment that makes a vailable cultural and linguistic intellectual resources. Sim ilarly, teachers can use in-the-moment feedback fro m students to make lessons more cognitively engaging for students (Chi, et al., 2018), such as fostering students’ shared construction of new ideas. Little is known about how to support teachers to make principled decisions related to pr oject-based learning (PBL) and adaptive practices that move tow ard goals for equitable practices and productive di sciplinary engagement in science.

PBL’s student-centered instructional approach presents a framework in which students generate artifacts that can take the form of actions (i.e., short play, soc ial action) or physical objects (i.e., bird feeder,  drawn model) to collaborate using disciplinary practices in engaging learning environments. The features of PBL include meaningful driving questions, collaboration, extended projects, orientation to standards-based goals, and community connection. Research suggests that positive results can be trac ed to fidelity to the PBL features, which is the de gree to which teachers support meaningful questions and collabora tion, shift to a student-centered approach, and cre ate authentic experiences (Grossman, et al., 2021).

Our collaborative professional learning (PL) design  (Swan, et al., 2014) responded to the challenges o f maintaining the integrity of PBL during virtual contexts, created by the urgency to adapt instruction. Teachers needed to address the learning goals of the designed PBL units but also adapt the lessons to foster engagement and equitable access, participation, and outcomes for learning. To this end, we welcomed adaptations as part of robust teaching and learning. The PLs were based on co-creation, leveraging, and reflecting on adaptations among communities of teachers and researchers. Hence, our PL design adhered to th ree commitments: 1) PBL features; 2) positioning te achers as experts; and 3) productive adaptive practices that support equity and engagement. During the PLs, all partners-teachers, researchers, and curriculum designers-shared their knowledge and expertise. The study offers insight into PBL environments and highlights adaptation potential for enhancing engagement and equity. Theoretical foundations We build on the theory of principled adaptations (Potvin, et al, 2021), in which the change in lesson enactment from the lesson-as-written deepens the PBL learning trajectory. We use affordance to describe opportunities for action for learning (van Lier, 2008), and how a lesson feature  can be adapted for specific purposes in situ. In t his view, every PBL feature presents myriad opportunities for learn ing, and so adaptation is the mobilization of a par ticular opportunity that can be realized. The context, incl uding aspects of the environment, participants, and  teachers’ instructional goals, determines the selection of a particular affordance of a feature. For example, a teacher may select ICLS2022 Proceedings 1289 © ISLS among affordances of the feature “artifact development.” Affordances include the opportunity for informal assessment, making a physical representation of thinking, or support for comparison of ideas across student groups. Teachers can be supported to adapt PBL lessons in ways that are principled, meet learning goals, and ultimately lead to increased engagement of students, including students from groups underrepresented in science. Student engagement and equity describe processes and outcomes for learning in a PBL classroom. Student engagement is a multidimensional, dynamic interplay among individuals' academic, cognitive, and social involvement in specific activities (Fredricks, et al., 2004). Chi and colleagues’ (2018) ICAP framework describes engagement as “pragmatically based on the absence or presence of o vert, observable behaviors, as well as whether the products contain information that went beyond the instructio nal materials (p.1791).” According to this framing, other latent forms of engagement may be present, but as teachers  can only respond to observable forms of engagement, this definition renders it productive for classroom use.  Observed evidence of engagement includes students exhibiting behaviors such as using disciplinary ideas and prac tices to gather evidence and construct new understa ndings, productive manipulation of materials, and interaction with others to solve problems. Equity in PBL classrooms is conceptualized as processes of access, participation, and outcomes of students from groups that are underrepresented in STEM, and advocacy for the inclusion of all students in the classroom (Tzou, et al. 2010). In this manuscript, we focus on the o bservable behaviors, interactions, and resources in  the classroom that support equitable participation and engagement in science practices. For example, evidence of equity might be a teacher providing opportunities for students to uti lize home and community resources and interactions where underrepresented students position themselves as knowers and doers of science (Miller, et al, 2021). This study sought to develop a set of guiding principles that could inform teachers’ adaptation of PBL features in ways that enhance equity and engagement of stude nts during COVID-19, with the purpose of extending these supports for adaptation in non-COVID-19 contexts. ## Methodology and data analysis

This research project is in the context of Multiple Literacies in Project-based Learning (ML-PBL; Miller & Krajcik, 2019), a 3rd-5th-grade coherent interdisciplinary c urriculum system for meeting the science standards in the USA, designed for face-to-face contexts. Participating teachers (n=18) taught grades 3-5 across two great l akes states and represented schools from a variety of regions, economies, and student demographics. During the study, in 2020-2021, schools shifted among fully virtual, face-to-face, and hybrid formats of instruction. Teachers participated in 14 PLs designed to forward three foundational constructs: 1) Deepening enactment and reflective practices of PBL; 2) Positioning teachers as experts, with collaboration among teachers and researchers as critical agents of learning; and

3) Creation of productive adaptive practices, meani ng that researchers encouraged teachers’ adaptation s (planned,

enacted, and reflected on) in the service of collectively realizing goals for learning. The collected data included transcribed teacher interviews (n= 36), video-taped observations and field notes of classroom teaching (n=154), and transcribed teacher PL sessions (n=14). Teacher interviews were semi-structured ICLS2022 Proceedings 1290 © ISLS and conducted three times across the school year (b eginning, middle and culmination of the academic ye ar). The biweekly, 2 hour, PL meetings, had three parts: 1) Teachers presented artifacts from prior adaptation to interrogate and refine the groups’ definition of equity and engagement; 2) They reflected on PBL features and their affordances;

3) They co-planned adaptations that would foster engagement and equity.

ML-PBL adaptation principles were designed through investigation of instances of student productive disciplinary engagement and equity processes, and r elated lesson events. Coding across data employed t hematic analysis using grounded coding (i.e., characterizing instances of criticality, accessing student intel lectual resources) and a priori coding (i.e., PBL features, ICAP Frame work). After identifying preliminary equity and eng agement instances in classroom videos, we generated codes a nd then looked across classrooms and similar contex ts to reach consensus. Next, we characterized the teacher adapt ations and related factors that may have contribute d to those processes. As some adaptation codes and features became salient for repeated and pronounced associations with equity and engagement, agreed-upon themes were created and  then triangulated with the PL transcription and te acher interviews to seek confirmatory and disconformity evidence. In disconfirmatory cases, the team returned to the original example to better understand the event; in confirmatory cases, themes were further supported.

## Findings

Findings from the project include five adaptation principles that can be used to enhance equity and engagement: Adapt features to 1) bring in and leverage students’ iden tities and intellectual resources from home; 2) pro vide studentcentered choice; 3) elicit different ideas and dive rse perspectives about the same event; 4) make use of different modalities; 5) disrupt the traditional classroom structures. The adaptation principles can guide the adaptation of PBL to support equitable science learning opportunities  and student engagement. We expand on the first ada ptation principle with evidence from videotaped observation s, transcription of PLs, and teacher interviews rel ated to enactment in the science classroom.

Adaptation Principle 1: Adapt features to bring in and leverage students’ identities and intellectual resources from home. Using and leveraging students’ identities and intellectual resources from home or community is actionable and can be used to guide adaptations. Equity and en gagement were associated with instances in which th e teacher created investigations that foster students’ intellectual and home resources. ## Conclusion and Discussion

Teachers, when supported with collaborative profess ional learning design, can adapt lessons based on f eature affordance, and maintain the coherence of the unit and learning trajectory. By focusing on features of PBL and their affordances for student engagement and equity, teac hers were able to create principled adaptations tha t deepened teacher expertise and student learning. Thus, we add to the field’s understanding of the benefits of welcoming teacherdriven adaptations that adhere to principles of learning environment design, while elevating the singular expertise that teachers can offer researchers.

This study provided a set of five adaptation princi ples that can be expanded and further iterated in f ace-toface and cross-disciplinary settings. Further resea rch is needed to investigate how principles can be introduced to teachers to promote sustained disciplinary learning on the part of students and promote shifts in practices for teachers. Additionally, teachers in this study were respondin g to extraordinary circumstances. More research is needed to understand how teachers might develop and utilize p rincipled adaptations in business-as-usual learning  contexts. Furthermore, research is needed to investigate whether use of adaptation principles can strengthen the main tenets of PBL and translate to student learning outcomes.
