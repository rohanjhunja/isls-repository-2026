# How Students' Mental Models of AI Shape Their Epistemic Cognition of AI -Generated Results: Source and Justification

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
The introduction of generative artificial intelligence (AI) has changed students ’ learning environments. Such rapid changes have the potential to exacerbate the post-truth challenges we face today. Despite the rapid increase in the use of AI in education, there remains a lack of research on how students reason about AI-generated results. Aligned with models of epistemic cognition, this study investigate d students’ mental models about AI and how such models influence their epistemic cognition about AI, particularly regarding source and justification. Results revealed that students’ epistemic reasoning varies depending on their mental models of AI. Students with a web-scraping model indicated that AI-generated results could be unreliable and superficially justified due to the source of information (i.e., the Internet). Students with a training-dataset model emphasized human intention in selecting training data and highlighted critical flaws in AI justification processes based on their understanding of how AI systems function.

Epistemic cognition, which refers to the cognitive processes by which people think about what they know, what knowledge is, how it can be used, and how they know what they know, has critical roles in navigating contemporary complex information environments (Greene et al., 2016). In this information landscape, people are increasingly vulnerable to post-truth challenges. People are likely to disregard truth or reliable ways of knowing by accepting misinformation without considering its source or distrusting providers of information (Chinn et al., 2011; 2020). The advent of generative artificial intelligence (AI)  has further intensified these challenges due to people's uncritical acceptance of information generated by AI. According to a nationally representative survey collecting perspectives from teens and young adults on AI, half of the young people have used generative AI in learning, and its proportion is considerably increasing (Hopelab et al., 2024; Pew Research Center, 2025). Despite the growing use of AI in learning, research on how students  reason about AI-generated information remains limited. The few studies that have examined people's perceptions of AI suggest that prior knowledge about AI may influence how individuals evaluate and reason about AI-generated results (Cukurova et al., 2020; Kapania et al., 2022). Because the AI's process of generating answers is largely opaque, which is often described as a 'black box,' users cannot directly observe how results are produced, making their mental models of AI central to how they evaluate those results. This study, therefore, examined how students' mental models of AI, particularly their understanding of how AI generates answers, shape their epistemic reasoning about AI-generated results, with a focus on source and justification.

Epistemic cognition Educational and psychological studies of epistemic cognition investigate lay people’s thoughts about questions of knowledge and justification. There are several models to theorize the construct of epistemic cognition, including developmental (Kuhn & Weinstock, 2002) and multidimensional models (Hofer & Pintrich, 1997).  Both models examine how individuals form beliefs about the nature of knowledge and the processes of knowing, albeit from different theoretical perspectives. In these models, epistemic beliefs are understood to consist of several independent dimensions.  Fo r example, Hofer and Pintrich (1997) reviewed articles that examined people’s epistemic beliefs and proposed that epistemic beliefs comprise four independent dimensions: certainty of knowledge, simplicity of knowledge, source of knowledge, and justification for knowing. In particular, the latter two dimensions, which will be the primary  focus of this study,  pertain to the nature of knowing, addressing the following questions: What is the source of knowledge, and how is it justified? Building on these foundational models, contemporary approaches to epistemic cognition have sought to integrate discipline-specific findings and broaden the scope of the construct.  Contemporary epistemic cognition models try to integrate findings in discipline studies. As a result, the epistemic cognition model in learning science, which emphasizes context and situativeness, has expanded across various disciplines, creating a better model to 1922 explain both the extent and mechanisms of the generalization of epistemic cognition (Sandoval et al., 2016). Additionally, unlike earlier studies on epistemic cognition, which limit their scope  to knowledge, recent models have expanded their focus to include not only  knowledge but also understanding and explanation. For example, Chinn and his colleagues (2014) suggested the AIR framework consists of three components: Aims and values, epistemic ideals, and reliable processes. In this framework, the focus extends beyond knowledge to include various epistemic ends, along with the criteria and processes by which those ends are pursued. Prior knowledge in epistemic cognition Many studies examining the role of prior knowledge in epistemic cognition consistently emphasize its critical importance. Research across v arious disciplines, including history and science, finds that an individual ’s knowledge in specific disciplines is closely linked to their cognitive processes (Bromme & Goldman, 2014; Chinn & Duncan, 2018). When individuals possess sufficient expertise to understand a document’s content, they are able to  apply knowledge-based validation to assess  the knowledge claims. However, in the absence of such expertise, individuals tend to rely more on the source of information than the content itself. According to Barzilai and colleagues’ (2014) bidirectional model, sourcing strategy, which involves evaluating the credibility of information sources, can help compensate for limited  discipline knowledge by supporting document evaluation. In this respect, people ’s general knowledge, which is not associated with a particular discipline, could  affect their  reasoning processes (Chinn & Duncan, 2018). For example, students' limited knowledge of how search engine results are ranked leads them to consult only the first few pages, potentially resulting in a biased understanding (Granka et al., 2004). Similarly, students' understanding of how AI generates answers, that is, their mental models of AI, may shape how they evaluate the source and justification of AI-generated results. Therefore, this study aim ed to examine how people's prior knowledge, particularly their conceptual understanding of AI (i.e., mental models of AI), affects their reasoning about the AI-generated results, in terms of source and justification.

## Participants

The participants in this study were recruited via a flyer containing IRB-approved recruitment  m aterials. The recruitment was limited to students who have AI experience in their learning. A total of eight students were recruited from a university in the Midwest. Three people majored in STEM fields (Computer Science, Computer Engineering, and Information Technology), and five people majored in non-STEM majors (Education, Biomedical Health, and Psychology). Two of the participants were graduate students, and t he others were undergraduates.

Interview protocols and procedures The interview protocol was designed to investigate students ’ AI knowledge and experience as well as their epistemic reasoning in terms of AI-generated responses. The interview protocol was developed and reviewed by the research team, including an experienced researcher in epistemic cognition. The eight interviews were conducted virtually through an online video conference platform. Interview lengths ranged from 39 minutes to 1 hour 5 minutes, with an average length of 49 minutes.

The interview in this study consisted of two parts. In the first part, participants were asked about their general AI experiences in learning and their prior knowledge of AI. To assess the latter, they were asked to explain the process of generating AI results in their own words. In the second part, participants were presented with the task. The task presented is a controversial issue regarding AI integration in education: Answer this question: “Should schools ban or encourage the integration of generative AI in learning? ” Such an ill-structured scenario helps reveal how people deal with the diversity and complexity of knowledge (Kuhn & Weinstock, 2002; Barzilai & Weinstock, 2015). Then, the AI-generated results were presented on the screen. The results outlined four reasons supporting and four reasons opposing AI integration in education, respectively, along with a proposed balanced approach that considered both the strengths and li mitations of AI in education.  When the participants finished reading the AI-generated results, they evaluated them and were asked to explain their thoughts regarding the source and justification aspects.

## Data analysis

Thematic analysis was performed to identify meaningful themes representing the phenomenon of interest through a systematic data coding process (Braun & Clarke, 2006). When it comes to coding, this study employed inductive coding, which initiated coding without an a priori list of codes. In line with the research question, relevant codable 1923 segments were identified and coded with an emphasis on the source and justification dimensions. To enhance coding consistency, inter-rater reliability procedures were employed during the initial coding phase. T wo researchers independently coded a subset of three interview transcripts, through which shared codes emerged. Based on the agreed-upon codes, the inter-coder reliability was established (k=0.82), and discrepancies in coding were discussed and resolved.

Two distinct mental models of AI Based on the interview question asking participants to explain how AI generates answers, the results revealed that students held two distinct models of AI functioning. Students with a web-scraping model of AI described AI as a system that searches, collects, and integrates information from the Internet. Although students were unable to specify the exact sources AI draws from, they commonly described AI-generated results as being scraped or collected from the web. In contrast, students with a training-dataset model of AI demonstrated a more elaborated understanding, recognizing two key aspects. First, they understood that AI is not trained solely on Internet data, but on a variety of curated sources through a process such as fine-tuning. Second, they articulated a probabilistic view of AI output generation, using terms such as "statistically" and "mathematical algorithm" to explain that AI responses are grounded in probability rather than semantic meaning. Unsurprisingly, students majoring in STEM fields demonstrated a comprehensive understanding of how AI works and its limitations, whereas non-STEM participants showed only a partial understanding. Interestingly, one of the non-STEM participants, despite having a major un related to AI, demonstrated extensive experience with and understanding of AI, showing an in-depth grasp of how AI operates that was on par with that of STEM majors. What is the source of AI results?

The Internet: An extensive but unreliable source of AI information. When it comes to the source of AI results, participants across both models  explicitly mentioned the Internet as a critical source of AI information. However, the two groups differed in how they conceptualized its role. Students with a web-scraping model of AI viewed the Internet as the primary and only source of AI results. They attributed AI's versatility to the vast and diverse range of information available online. At the same time, they also attributed inaccurate AI responses to the existence of unreliable information on the Internet.  These responses suggest that students with a web-scraping model conceptualize AI as functioning analogously to a search engine — searching, aggregating, and retrieving web-based content.

“(S1) I can't say it's like 100 percent reliable because it's obviously, like I said, I mentioned, like how I understand it. ChatGPT takes information that's on the Internet already,  and not everything that's on the Internet is correct. Like anyone can put things out on the Internet. So if it does pull something out of there, then you're at a risk of being misinformed. ” An AI model selectively trained on human-generated text and speech. In contrast, students with a training-dataset model of AI demonstrated a more nuanced understanding of AI's data sources. Even though they also identified the Internet as a primary source of information, they further highlighted the role of humandecisions in curating and selecting training data. Students with the training-dataset model explained that AI goes beyond simply collecting information online, as it is trained on deliberately curated datasets through a machine learning process. Consequently, they recognized that the selective, human-driven nature of training data can result in outputs that reflect bias or the intentions embedded in those datasets. “(S6) I know there was the whole thing with DeepSeek at one point, where it wouldn't spit out anything about T iananmen Square. So stuff can be definitely influenced by the programming and whatnot. So just being aware of any potential biases.” How are AI results justified?

A surface-level justification with explanations from unreliable sources. When asked whether the AI results were justified, most students expressed skepticism about whether AI-generated 1924 alongside its results, they nonetheless questioned the credibility and evidentiary basis of those explanations. Students with a web-scraping model of AI, in particular, questioned the reliability of AI-generated justifications, attributing this skepticism to their distrust of web-based content. Furthermore, they also mentioned that adequate justification would require AI to disclose the sources underlying its responses. “(S3) I think that it justified it gave you like a subtitle or something like it gave you like here's a benefit, and then it explained its benefit to give you more information … So yeah, I think that it, without looking at the credibility of these justifications, I think that the system does justify what it gives you.” The critical flaw in justification: A plausible sequence of mathematical algorithm results Whereas students with a web-scraping model described AI as solely collecting and aggregating information online, students with a training-dataset model recognized that probability underlies the process of generating AI results. From their perspective, AI does not truly comprehend the input prompt or formulate meaningful responses; instead, it selects and arranges the most statistically probable words based on algorithms and mathematical calculations. In other words, although the AI’s response appeared coherent  and plausible on the surface, these students remained skeptical that the AI-generated results were supported by factual evidence or human-like reasoning. “(S7) Honestly, I don't have confidence that it justifies its answers because I know that again, it's not researching, it's just kind of spinning out response algorithmically to whatever input question I'm asking it.” The findings of this study demonstrate that students' mental models of AI, which are rooted in their prior knowledge, shaped how they evaluated the source and justification of AI-generated results, highlighting the epistemic significance of AI understanding in learning contexts. When students were asked about the source and justification of AI-generated results, they responded based on their existing understanding of AI, regardless of its accuracy, suggesting that even misconceptions about AI can systematically shape epistemic reasoning.  This finding further supports previous research suggesting that prior knowledge is highly associated with individuals’ epistemic cognition (Samarapungavan et al., 2006; Chinn & Duncan, 2018; Duncan et al., 2018). Notably, students generally evaluated AI  responses as unreliable and lacking proper justification. However, the cognitive processes  behind their evaluations varied according to the  mental models grounded in their prior AI knowledge. Through the lens of Chinn and colleagues' (2014) AIR framework, the two mental models reflect qualitatively distinct epistemic profiles. For students holding the web-scraping model, their primary epistemic aim centered on verifying the accuracy of AI-generated results. To this end, they evaluated AI outputs based on the perceived reliability of their Internet as a source and suggested that source transparency on the part of AI would be necessary for more adequate evaluation. In contrast, students  with the training-dataset model articulated additional epistemic aims, including assessing whether AI-generated outputs were biased and whether human intentions had been embedded in the training process. To evaluate this, they referenced the transparency of humandecision-making in dataset curation and the underlying algorithms as key epistemic ideals, suggesting that any meaningful evaluation of AI-generated outputs must account for the probabilistic mechanisms through which those outputs are produced.

In addition, while previous studies have mainly focused on discipline knowledge in a specific domain (Richter et al., 2009; Thomm & Bromme, 2012), this study demonstrates that general knowledge can also play a role in shaping reasoning. As Chinn and Duncan  (2018) suggested, this result indicates that although laypeople without disciplinary knowledge may be unable to judge the validity of knowledge claims in controversial issues directly, their general knowledge pertinent to reasoning significantly influences their comprehensive understanding and reasoning about the issues. Thus, research on people's epistemic cognition should attend not only to their domain-specific expertise but also to their general knowledge that supports reasoning across contexts. In this regard, AI literacy, broadly defined as the ability to understand how AI systems work, recognize their limitations, and critically evaluate their outputs (Long & Magerko, 2020; Ng et al., 2021), can be understood as a form of general knowledge that shapes learners' epistemic cognition in AI-integrated learning environments. We argue that AI literacy functions as a trans-disciplinary form of general knowledge, insofar as it operates across diverse contexts wherever AI is encountered, rather than being confined to any single domain (cf. Long & Magerko, 2020; Ng et al., 2021).  The findings of this study suggest that fostering AI literacy may serve as an effective educational strategy for supporting more sophisticated epistemic reasoning about AI-generated content, regardless of students' disciplinary backgrounds.
