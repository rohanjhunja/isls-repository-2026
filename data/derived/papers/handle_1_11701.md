# Learning about Hidden Bias in Ambient Identity Cues through Seeing Visible Bias in Computer Vision AI

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This poster explores teacher learning about (a) equitable design of ambient identity cues for minoritized students and (b) equitable use of AI through seeing racism in/by AI. We analyzed data from a survey, class artifacts including image redesign using AI, and an artifact-based interview with three preservice teacher participants. Our participants began noticing racial inequity upon seeing bias in computer vision AI.

## Conceptual framework

Teaching is hard and includes many tasks and not enough time. Thus, it is easy to fall into the bad habit of adopting a minimally (maximally) (in)sufficient strategy for  assessing the needs and backgrounds of students. One such strategy is dysconscious racism, defined as attribu ting differences in opportunity, resources, wealth,  and socioeconomic standing among members of different racial and ethnic groups to events that happened in the past (King, 1991). While the denial of equal opportuniti es to members of marginalized racial and ethnic gro ups is ongoing, people who adopt a dyconscious racism stance often assume that one cannot change past events, i.e., that past events are fixed factors. When people attribute events to fixed factors, then they often will not see any potential for change and not exert further effort (Weiner, 1986). This can lead to the acquiescence that differences in opportunity, resources, wealth, and socioeconomi c standing among members of different racial and et hnic groups is the status quo, which one cannot change; rather, all one cando is to mitigate the harmful  effects of the status quo. But the theory of dysconsciousness posits the critical role of connecting inequity suffered by members of marginalized groups not just to history and lack  of opportunity, but also structural racism and oth er prejudice that enable such inequity to exist. A key element that undergirds support for such inequity is the question of who benefits from (a) the factors that lead to inequity and (b) the presence of inequity (King, 1991). The lens of dysconscious racism may also be applied to classroom design. For most, it is unobjectionable to place posters of famous professionals in classro oms to inspire students  (e.g., in mathematics classrooms one might find posters of Fermat and Fibonnacci, and in physics classrooms Newton and Einstein). Someone applying a dysconscious racism perspective would say that one needs to choose among the mathematicians and physicists who made the greatest impact, and that due to historical factors  that disadv antaged members of minoritized communities, most of the former happen to be White. This is of course false  in that it (a) ignores the critical contributions of Black, Latino(a), middle eastern, Asian, and indigenous scholars, and (b) promotes the acquiescence of historical inequity and the ongoing existence of such inequity. Computer vision AI  is the use of artificial intelligence to generate i mages and videos in response to prompts (Khan & Al-Habsi, 2020). It can generate alternative images that embed inclusive ambient identity cues. But to accomplish this, it is critical that preservice teachers (a) understand and are committe d to culturally sustaining instruction, and (b) understand the role of ambient identity cues in inviting or dissuading participation of people of color in STEM.

## Research questions

Our research questions were:

1. How does critical (dys)consciousness of participants evolve while learning about hidden bias in ambient

identity cues?

2. How does seeing visible bias in computer vision AI help participants recognize the ambient identity cues

that create sense of exclusion among students of color?

## Context and participants

This study includes data from three participants (Gabby, Kristen, and Maya; all pseudonyms) who were enrolled in a hybrid undergraduate course required for education majors. The instructional unit covered: 1) materiality of AI, 2) i nteraction with image generative AI, 3) ambient identity cues, 4) sociocultural theory of identity, 5) stereotypical conceptions in media, and 6) bias in computer vision AI.

## Data collection and analysis

Data sources were a pre-survey, artifacts, and a post-interview. The pre-survey contained 14 items on demographics and 13  items on perceptions and experiences with AI in general. Artifacts were participants’ responses to prompts provided in class. Artifact-based interviews were conducted after the unit ended. Two researchers collaboratively coded the data in N Vivo 14. The coding scheme included nodes on identity considerations, equity considerations, and noticing or reacting to various cues.

## Findings

Four major findings are:

1. Participants began noticing racial inequity upon seeing bias in computer vision AI

2. Participants exhibited more consciousness on racial inequity than gender inequity

3. Participants attempted to attribute racial discrimination in materials to unintentional errors of creators

4. Participants imaginatively resolved harm and tension from racial discrimination through imagined love

Participants were shown images from a book about a Black girl learning to program a robot to build a sandcastle while a White man was reading a computer  programming newspaper and a Black woman was sunbathing on a beach. Before seeing bias in computer vision AI, participants did not notice race or gender-based inequity. Then participants were taught about bias in compute r vision AI. For example, participants were taught about an AI passport photo AI system that rejected a Black woman’s photo because of AI’s misclassification of her mouth as open. Upon seeing racial bias in computer vision AI, all participants noticed ambient identity cues for racially minoritized groups.

Participants focused more on racial identities than  gender identities when asked to react to or change ambient identity cues. For example, Maya noticed racial inequity in the video in which a White man was the only scientist, and noted, “I would change the doctor to a Brown person so people can see no matter what, anyone cando whatever the other person cando ”. However, Maya was less critical about gender ine quity. When asked to recreate the book image using generative AI to impr ove ambient cues for a sense of belonging among fem ale students of color, she produced images with people of color, but assigned no role related to computer science or reading to the Black woman. Instead, she created a scene of the woman lying on the beach, as in the or iginal illustration, but with a kid because she would see that on a beach in reality: “for parents, adding different skin colors, and having the mom lay with the one kid as if I saw it on the beach”. As discussed earlier, participants recognized that racial discrimination is offensive and hurtful to racially minoritized people. However, despite the seriousness of racial discrimination they recognized in materials  and their action of replacing such discrimination with equitable ambient identity cues, Gabby and Kristen attempted to attribute such racial discrimination to unintentional errors that the material creators made. Gabby noticed the White man reading the computer science newspaper before other participants commented on problems with such inequitable ambient cues: “I was also gonna say  maybe if I'm understanding correctly, it's kind of offensive that the White male’s reading the newspaper and the African American girl is just like sitting there, I guess?”. Gabby expressed that  the illustrator may have wanted to show people doin g what they wanted to do: “It just kind of shows, I guess, their different personalities. He, instead of just completely relaxing, he's still kee ping up with stuff, and she's just enjoying her time”. When challenged, Gabby questioned herself and mentioned the possibility of racial bias but later during her interview she again exhibited her trust in good intentions of creators. Participants frequently mentioned and created a mixed-race family for Black and White characters in materials. For example, all participants hoped or imagined that the Black woman and the White man on the picture book were an interracial couple. Gabby said: Knowing if they're together would make a big change ’cause we talked about how this was kind of offensive because the White guy’s reading the newspaper, but I think if knowing if they were together, it wouldn't be as offensive because he's not doing it in a harmful way to prove something.

## Implications

Our findings showcase learnability of equity-centered approaches especially using bias in computer vision AI. Still, a key challenge participants faced was in considering ambient identity cues with respect to intersectionalities of identities. Further research i s needed to (a) fully understand how to combat racial dysconsciousness  among preservice teachers, and (b) to learn how to combat intersectionality and gender dysconsciouness.
