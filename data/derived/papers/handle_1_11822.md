# Grounding Mathematics Learning in Culturally Authentic Movement Practices: From Attentional Anchors in Balinese Dancing to Auxiliary Lines in Geometry Reasoning

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Solving geometry problems often requires generating auxiliary lines. Yet, learning to generate auxiliary lines is challenging, because it involves envisioning figural elements that by definition are not yet overtly available for students’ visual sensing or teachers’ direct indexing. Espousing an enactivist perspective, this design-based research project explores dance as a culturally authentic grounding context for geometrical reasoning. Learning new dance movements often elicits the spontaneous mental generation of attentional anchors, imaginary perceptual structures enabling dancers to perform complex coordinations. We conjectured that, given appropriate semiotic resources, dancers could objectify their tacit attentional anchors from dance practice as explicit auxiliary lines for geometry practice. We report on a pilot evaluation of GRiD, a low-cost resource for blending dance and geometry. Microgenetic analysis of a young Balinese dancer’s multimodal actions and utterances reveals the emergence of an interdisciplinary nexus of practice imbricating dance and mathematics and benefiting both discourses.

## Introduction

We report on emerging results from an ongoing desig n-based doctoral research project that is taking on s everal research problems, two at the level of a pedagogica l design framework (Abrahamson et al., 2020)  and one at the level of building activities targeting specific disciplinary subject matter:

• Framework-level research problems: (1) How might the heuristic principles of embodie d design,

heretofore implemented using advanced digital techn ologies in industrialized countries, impact the STEM learning of remote students with low/no access to interactive electronic platforms? (2) How might the heuristic principles of embodied design, hereto fore implemented in disciplinary contexts, such as mathematics classrooms, be situated in cultural–historically authentic practices?

• Activity-level research problem: In creating an embodied design for geometric reas oning, what

evolutionary capacities might we draw on? How would  we elicit those capacities? How would those capacities mobilize grounded mathematical discourse of the pedagogically targeted content? In the remainder of the introduction, we expand on these three problems to explain our motivation for engaging in this line of inquiry. Then the later se ctions of the paper present our solution to the act ivity-level research problem as offering proof-of-concept solutions to the two framework-level research problems. Embodied design: A need for low-cost and culturally situated framework expansions Embodied design is an evolving research paradigm integrating embodi ment theory, HCI technology innovation, and multimodal mixed methods to promote and investigate phenomena of teaching, l earning, and designing (Abrahamson et al., 2020). Scholars of embodiment have challenged the traditional mind-body dualist perspective, i.e., the “cognitivist” assumption that the mind is  epistemically separate from the body (Shapiro & Stolz, 2019). Instead, “4E” scholars—proponents of the notion that the mind is embodied, embedded, extended, and enacted— claim that the body, as well as its ecological nich e, are inherent to, even constitutive of the cognit ive system. Therefore, from an embodied cognition perspective, understanding and reasoning about the world necessa rily begins from experiences of acting on the world (Shapiro & Stolz, 2019). Through these actions, per embodiment ontology, mathematical entities come forth as pheno menologically present objects we grasp, transform, and transport (Abrahamson et al., 2020).

The theory of embodied cognition can be applied to pedagogical practice by designing conceptually meaningful sensorimotor problems whose perceptual s olutions ground the target content (Abrahamson, 201 4). Some examples specifically of the action-based genre of embodied design-based research work in the field of teaching and learning include: (1) Mathematics Imag ery Trainer activity tasks that challenge students to enact a target movement that maintains invariant some figur al, relational, or quantitative prop erty they thus first apprehend (Abrahamson, 2014); (2) Enacting Euclidean Transfor mation, where students learn translations, reflections, and rotation through their collaborati ve action on a tarped Cartesian coordinate system (Soto, 2022); and (3) Balance Board Math, where vestibular sensor y-seeking participants rock on a balance board to gen erate graphs with specific targeted properties  (Tancredi et al., 2021). These projects all sought to create opportunities for students to first encounter concepts through bodily engagement with the environment and only later to signify these experiences using disciplinary frames of reference (see also Palatnik et al., 2023). With an eye on equity, a key mission of embodied de sign is to enhance the accessibility of learning by embracing the variety of students’ sensory, motor, and neural capacities as well as their diverse cult ural epistemologies (Tancredi et al., 2021). In designing an educationa l product, embodied designers seek to develop a situated environment that invites students to eng age intuitively in attempting to perform a motor-control task. The task then becomes increasingly challenging, sti mulating students to shift their perception of the situation so as to enable them to move in a new way; these new p erceptions constitute epistemic pivots in the sense  that articulating these perceptual orientations multimod ally with available semiotic resources engages stu dents in conceptual discourse  (Abrahamson & Sánchez -García, 2016). When students reason about their different movement solution strategies, they coordinate new conceptual insight (Abrahamson et al., 2020). Advanced technology, such as augmented or virtual r eality (XR), bears affordances for actualizing embodied design, because these platforms’ immersive  affordances can enhance students’ sensorimotor experiences (Johnson-Glenberg, 2022). Yet whereas these digital solutions benefit stude nts in resourceful environments, they are liable to increase education al gaps for students in disadvantaged socio-economic circumstances (OECD, 2022). As embodied designers, can we address the need to  create low-cost technological educational artifacts that foster embodied learning despite digital scarcity? Consider the following anthropological perspective.

An important intellectual source for the theory of embodied design comes from the work of  Reed and Bril (1996) on infants’ early motor development. They coined th e phrase field of promoted action to capture the practice of adults’ explicit physical intervention,  within a dedicated physical space, that occasions opportunities for young children to develop coordinative capaciti es enabling the prospective performance of cultural ly valued skills. For e xample, a Bambara mother in Sub-Saharan Africa holds her infant upside-down by the ankle to scaffold its coordination of lower-and upper-body skeletomuscular units, fostering physical powe r and agility. The researchers found that infants and toddlers raised in fields of promoted action located in remote rural villages advanced significantly in their motor capacities as compared to their urban peers (Reed & Bril, 1996). Embodied-design draws on the cultural precedent of fields of  promoted action as a theoretical justification for engineering digital architectures where children solve assigned motor-control problems to develop a perceptual grounding for the mathematical concepts they are later to understand (Abrahamson & Trninic, 2015). Still, what might action-based embodied design be in non-digital environments? Moreover, whereas embodied design has thus drawn on a culturally authentic pra ctice to configure its general architecture, the framework ultimately implements its activities within a “cultural void,” in the sen se that students participate in the activities outside of any a priori meaningful context (Ba & Abrahamson, 2021). We speculated that presenting mathematics as augmenting existing cultural–historical practices could potentially ground the target skills more robustly within contextually authentic and personally relevant inte rgenerational discourse. Hence, we sought to create  a field of promoted action where the geometric notion of auxiliary lines would spring forth as a spontaneous elaboration on existing praxis.

Geometry learning: Toward an embodied design for grounding auxiliary lines A sweeping goal of all stakeholders in mathematics education is for students to understand the content  they are learning, not just follow rote procedures (Skemp, 1976). In other words, the hope is for students to engag e in the core mathematical practice of reasoning. In mainstream education, mathematical reasoning i s fostered through various classroom activities that require students to make generalizations and support claims regardin g logical– quantitative features of situations, such as the pa tterns or composition of spatially deployed objects  (Stylianides, 2008). Yet, these features of the situations in question, what the reasoning will be about, are not immediately obvious or available—they emerge for the learner through exploration, in vestigation, conjecture, and evaluation (Radford, 2003). In so doing, students tune their p erceptual attention toward the situations so as to mentally construct them in ways conducive to solving the pro blem at hand (Abrahamson & Trninic, 2015). Consider, for example, how the relation between geometrical shapes could be perceived in Figure 1. The dashed red lines in Figure 1 serve as auxiliary lines. Auxiliary lines are supplemental diagrammatic elements that a geometry problem solver introduces onto the shapes in question to facilitate the disce rnment of implicit figural properties and latent relations key to constructing a mathematical proof (Palatnik & Sigler, 2018). For example, auxiliary lines can help us appreciate  the structural premise for calculating the area of  a parallelogram by instead calculating the area of a rectangle sharing the same base and height (Figure 1a) or to realize that a hexagon is composed of two trapezoids (Figure 1b). Figure 1 The Conceptual Function of Auxiliary Lines (a) (b) The educational goal of geometric reasoning, howeve r, is for problem solver s to figure out themselves what supplementary markings would mobilize their geometric argumentation. And so, students eventually receive images without ready-made auxiliary lines. Yet, it is precisely this fir st stage of generating useful auxiliary lines ab nihilo (i.e., “out of nothing”) that students find difficult: We are asking students to attend to something t hat is not there, at least, not as a bonafide sensory stimulus. In other words, we are asking students to perceive something they cannot see. As such, we face a double referential and pedagogical conundrum: a teacher cannot, in principle, point to an auxiliary line that isn’t there, and, in any case, the teacher may prefer not to gesture (depict, trace, etc.) that line either, so as not to disclose its form, location, or logical affiliation to the entire display. How, thus, might these lines come forth for students? How might students learn to generate these lines themselves? In seeking to create a design for auxiliary lines, we join scholars who advocate for geometry educatio n that draws on students’ intuitive capacities  (Doorman et al., 2020). Yet, what naturalistic capacity might ground the construction of auxiliary lines?

• The first clue is found in evidence that dancers sp ontaneously generate imaginary figural elements to

support the enactment of a dance movement  (Clark & Ando, 2014). More broadly, Hutto and SánchezGarcía (2015), who draw on the enactivist paradigm, coined the p hrase attentional anchors  to characterize perceptual resources, often involving imaginary constructions, that tighten one’s grip on the domain of action. Indeed,  when embodied design-based researchers posed students with motor-control problems, the students spontaneously generated atte ntional anchors to enable the enactment of solution movements (Abrahamson & Sánchez-García, 2016).

• Moreover, Abrahamson and Shulman (2019)  explicitly compare mathematics and dance pedagogy t o

argue for the shared cognitive function of attentional anchors in solving movement problems across both disciplines. As such, design-based researchers have theoretical grounds for situ ating mathematics learning within dance practice.

• Further, Gerofsky (2011) found that students who experience “being” a concep t through kinesthetically

enacting it in whole-body mimetic movement are generally more creative i n problem-solving than their peers who report “seeing” the concept as a static display that is separate from their body.

• Furthermore, movement practitioners can represent t acit aspects of their performance skill using

externally inspectable semiotic resources  (Kirsh, 2010). As such, by overtly marking their co vert perceptual operations, as they solve a movement tas k, students could construct external representation s that serve as shareable objects of thought, thus supporting inter-and intra-personal communication.

• Finally, design-based researchers have characterized unique pedagogical affordances of collaborative

body-immersive activities for geometry learning  (Palatnik et al., 2023; Vogelstein et al., 2019). Yet, whereas these geometry designs have demonstrated th e spontaneous emergence of mathematical problem-solving, imagination, and discourse from students’ situated task-based interactions with educational resources, the studies did not address the enactivist grounding of these cognitive activit ies. Namely, can we design geometry activities where vit al aspects of formal disciplinary practice, such as building auxiliary constructions, emerge from stude nts’ spontaneous solutions to problems of sensorimotor engagement?

Considering these insights, we conjectured that students’ evolutionary capacity to generate attenti onal anchors as perceptual solutions to movement problems in dance practice could be tapped as a means of training them to construct auxiliary lines as solutions to g eometry problems in mathematical practi ce. Drawing on principles of action-based embodied design  (Abrahamson, 2014)  and the semiotic –cultural theory of objectification (Radford, 2003), the present project therefore conceptualizes the emergence of geometry discourse (i.e., discussing auxiliary constructions) as students’ semiotic objectification of their situated perceptual solutions to motor problems of enacting goal movements (i.e., their attentional anchors). In summary, our design-based research seeks to situate embodied activities for geometry learning within culturally authentic dance practices, using low-cost dedicated materials. We conjectured that, give n appropriate semiotic resources, the attentional anchors students spontaneously generate in learning to move in new ways (i.e., in solving motor-control problems) could be objectified as auxiliary  lines serving in solving geometry problems. As such, we are attempting to ground mathematical reasoning within dance practice. Geometry resources in dance (GRiD): Overview of a design solution for grounding mathematical reasoning in movement practice GRiD (see Figure 2) comprises a gridded mat, markers, sticker s, highlighters, straws, connectors, yarn, pipe cleaners, and elastic bands (graphic artwork: Made Ariawan). Students were invited to utilize the GRiD to solve our designed problems (for additional dancing-geometry GRiD activities, see Apsari & Abrahamson, 2024). In so doing, the researcher encourage d the students to utilize a variety of multimedia and  multimodal semiotic resources, including actual enactment, pantomime, gestures, speech, text, and drawings. ## Implementation and evaluation of GRiD: A case study

We implemented the design with 77 fifth-grade students in Bali and California (9-11 years old) who joined individual or group sessions in a 60-minute teaching experiment (Steffe & Thompson, 2000)  with the first author. The audio-video data collected during the study were analyzed  qualitatively using micro-genetic ethnography (Siegler, 2006) and elements of the ethnomethodological approach to  conversation analysis (Mondada, 2019) to take into account multimodal semiotic resources tha t participants presented during the problem-solving activity.

In this paper, we will present selected results and  findings of the activity with Anna (pseudonym), a 10-year-old Balinese dancer in traini ng, that we argue, exemplifies the pedagogical affo rdances of our. Specifically, we contend, GRiD offers students engaged in solving sensorimotor coordination problems opportunities to objectify their tacit perceptual operations in disciplinarily  normative forms, thus grounding geometry discourse in dance practice.

The researcher guided Anna (A) to practice a dance posture called Tapak Sirang P ada (henceforth, TSP), where the heels touch while the feet are rotated out, creating a 90° angle. The notion of a right angle as well as its standard Mesopotamian measure as 90º has ent ered Balinese culture and vernacular, so that some modern Balinese dance teachers might casually refer to the TSP aperture in those terms. However, little if anything is ever said regarding how the students might assess the ri ght-angled quality of their own postural form. Soon, Anna demonstrated how she usually performed and evaluated her posture (see Transcript 1). At this moment, GRiD has not yet been introduced—a carpet at the research site happened to feature various traditional forms, but these were never alluded to. It is important to appreciate that Anna was not aware of the researcher’s mathematical “ulterior motives”—all geometric discourse manifest in these excerpts was entirely spontaneous. ## Discussion

Embodiment perspectives conceptualize the core cogn itive work in mathematical learning as the explorat ive process of perceptually coordinating goal-oriented sensorimotor activity (Abrahamson & Sánchez-García, 2016). As such, the traditionally disparate scholarship ar ound mathematics and movement may engage in dialogu e that benefits both fields  (Abrahamson & Shulman, 2019). In this study, we des igned a gridded tarp (GRiD) as a semiotic resource for dancing students to objectify  their tacit attentional anchors in the form of geo metrical auxiliary constructions. Our empirical data suggest that the study participant indeed alternated between dance and geometry discursive registers grounded in shifting perceptions of the available media (on “keying” reg isters, see DeLiema et al., 2019).

Our culturally situated activity spontaneously elicited from the participant mathematical reasoning in her attempts to explain her dancing posture (Transcript 1). Once she had voluntarily constructed auxiliary lines, GRiD was presented as bearing potential semiotic resources for her to elaborate on her reasoning. Working with GRiD, Anna could then describe with greater precision the  imaginary auxiliary elements she had employed in d ancing, thus rendering her implicit routine transparent for  others to follow  (Transcript 2). For instance, Anna was eager to talk about the auxiliary elements (e.g., center point, lines) that she employed to adjust her feet’ s position per TSP. Whereas prior studies report that students experience difficulty generating auxiliary lines to solve geometry problems (Foster, 2023), our participant intuitively constructed auxiliary elements that proved effective geometric means of solving her movement task.

By situating geometry discourse in dance settings, our activity created a new nexus of practice—a domain of study created by a constellation of different pr actices connected by various relations  (Hui et al., 2017). This nexus allowed Anna and the researcher to establish a mutual understanding of the discourse, enabling them to employ rules, entities, and vocabularies across dance and mathematics without causing confusion. The data show how the designed environment of GRiD both elicited and enabled an individual to objectify their intuitively generated auxiliary lines to bridge the discourses of dance and geometry. Participating in the designed activity, Anna’s dance experience merged with the mathematical universe. Indeed, she became part of the mathematical model of her dance strategy (Freudenthal, 1978) and, vice versa, the mathematical model then enhanced her dancing. This ontological merging of geometry and dance construct ions as dynamic accessories —her geometric perception of dance —is evident in Anna’s utterances, such as “all the lines just come like this” or “this triangle thing always stays with my feet.” Similarly, Anna perceived her danceoriented attentional anchor as an angle-bisector auxiliary construction running forward from her TSP-formed heels (see Figure 3). Furthermore, Anna’s multimodal utterances evidenced that she was blending dance and geometry perceptual registers. For instance, in perceiving the vertex of the angle constructed by her feet configuration (see Transcript 2), Anna was: (1) gesturing—depicting the shape of the vertex as a point; (2) quantifying—describing the size of the vertex as “this big”; (3) naming — metaphorically referring to the vertex as the “big center point”; and (4) sounding —depicting the vertex as the location where her heel s  “click.” Finally, as the coordination problem became complex, Anna developed a new geometric attentional anchor (the triangular system, see Figure

4) that integrated the various auxiliary elements. We do not know of previous research demonstrating b ilateral

reciprocity between mathematical and movement practices.

## Conclusion

Marshall McLuhan famously asserted, “The medium is the message.” He argued that the medium we select to convey our message impacts how the meaning of the m essage is conceived (McLuhan, 1964). In our study, we built a medium, GRiD, that affects how students perceive, communicate, and interact with and about mathematical objects and concepts. Dancers’ tacit perceptual constructions—imaginary points and lines guiding the enactment of their movements—became available for others’ shared scrutiny as ind exable semiotic objects. GRiD enables students to render their covert auxiliary construct ion as overt diagrammatic constructions. The medium  we designed offers material anchors for blending dance  and mathematics discourses with low-cost technology affordable for students in remote areas.

The designed learning environment served as a field of promoted action (Abrahamson & Trninic, 2015; Reed & Bril, 1996)  occasioning productive opportunities to talk about mathematics when dancing and to talk about dance when mathematizing. Thus, the merged da nce/geometry activities elicited, encouraged, and legitimized multimodal and multimedia forms of mathematical reasoning and discourse that included whole-body movement, gesture, speech, and drawing. Dancing geo metry or, more technically, blending dance and geom etry discourses, enables students to experience complex mathematics as practical situated forms of reasonin g. More broadly, situating students’ mathematical experienc es in personally meaningful cultural heritage activ ities could render schooling more playful and creative (Thompson, 2022).
