# Cognitive Equilibrium and Multimodal Synchrony: A Case Study from Pair Programming

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Gaze, emotional, and physiological synchronies are predictive of collaborative performance and quality. However the relationship among these synchronies are not clear. We present a pair programming study to understand the relationship between facial expression synchrony, joint visual attention, joint mental effort, joint stress and joint engagement. We collected eye-tracking, heart rate variability, electrodermal activity, facial expressions from 40 pairs while they were collaboratively debugging a given program. We computed gaze, facial expressions, stress and engagement based synchronies. We also computed the joint facial expression cycles and compared the different cycles with the debugging performance. We then examined the relationships between different synchronies and joint facial expression cycles. Our results show a clear difference between the high and low performing pairs based on their joint facial expression cycles. Moreover, our results also show that there are significant relationships between joint facial expression cycles and the multimodal synchronies.

## Introduction and background

Pair programming is a complex phenomenon that invol ves multimodal interaction and coordination between individual students, student groups, the learning environment, and the knowledge artefact (Stahl & Hakkarainen, 2021). Specifcally, the multimodality can be reflec ted through student pair’s communication (Ouyang & Xu, 2022), behavior (Stahl, 2017), emotion (Kwon et al., 2014), interaction (Zemel & Koschmann, 2013), etc. Furthermore, multimodality emerges during the colla boration with complex, multilevel, multilayered characteristics, which may influence the quality and success of collaborative learning (Hilpert & Marc hand, 2018). Multimodal learning analytics (MMLA) has been used within the collaborative settings for the past few years to understand and predict collaborative succe ss (Spikol et al, 2017a, Olsen et al, 2020), regula tion (Noorozi,et al, 2019) and reflection (Yan et al, 20 24a,b), collaborative processes (Xu et al, 2023), d iscussions during collaboration (Riquelme et al, 2019), assessment (Moon et al, 2024) and classroom orche stration (Prieto et al, 2016).

Using the recent work within collaborative learning  and MMLA, we can observe three types of synchrony. The first is the physiological synchrony, characterized by the interdependent physiological processes of multiple individuals, which has been linked to s tudents’ learning success and social regulation (Ha ataja et al., 2018; Liu et al., 2021). The second is emotional sy nchrony, which has been shown to predict subjective  and objective performances within collaborative setting s. Finally, gaze synchrony, which has been studied in CSCL settings using dual eye-tracking almost for the past two decades (starting from Richardson et al. 2005), in various collaborative learning (Sharma et al, 2021)  and problem solving tasks (Nuessli et al., 2009). The recent MMLA focus on understanding the synchrony of physio logical, emotional, and gaze synchrony motivated th is work to combine all three synchrony types in one co ntext, which is pair programming. Our main aim is t o understand the interplay of emotional synchrony wit h the two other types, that is, physiological and g aze synchrony. More specifically, we address the follow ing research questions: Research Question 1 (RQ1):  Which cognitive equilibrium expressions sequences characterize pair debugging performance?  Research Question 2 (RQ2): How are these sequences related to the joint multimodal measurements? The model of affect dynamics (D'Mello & Graesser, 2 012) serves as a framework for understanding how emotions interact with cognitive processes in c ollaborative environments. By recognizing the impor tance of emotional awareness, emotional exchange, cogniti ve appraisal, coping mechanisms, feedback loops, and cognitive equilibrium, teams can enhance their coll aboration and performance, particularly in complex tasks. These models include cognitive equilibrium, which i s defined as a balanced state where both dya d members share similar cognitive states, understandings, and  expectations related to their tasks. Achieving thi s balance is crucial for effective communication and problem-solving. The model also considers task complexity, as higher complexity may increase the need for emotional and cognitive alignment between partners. In addition, these models also assume that emotional exchange (van Kle ef, 2009) is a critical component of this model. It  predicts positive emotions can quickly spread among the dyad s, enhancing motivation and cooperation. In additio n, these models also include cognitive appraisal (Moor s & Scherer, 2013; Scherer, 2021) as it plays an im portant role. Team members must interpret their own and oth ers' emotional states, which shapes their responses  and actions. For instance, viewing a partner's frustrat ion as a call for support rather than a personal fa ult can foster better teamwork. Emotional states significantly inf luence cognitive processes, such as problem-solving and decision-making—a positive emotional state can enhance creative thi nking. At the same time, negative emotions may lead to a narrow focus or avoidance be haviors. In this study, we leverage the theoretical assumptions mentioned in the introduction and apply  them to a very particular context —i.e., pair programming, a powerful technique in software development that p romotes collaboration (Graßl & Fraser, 2024), improves code quality (Hulkko & Abrahamsson, 2005), and enhances learning (McDowell et al, 2003). By leveraging the strengths and perspectives of two programmers, this  approach can lead to more efficient problem-solving and a better understanding of coding practices (Hanks et al, 2011). It’s a valuable practice in various prog ramming and development contexts, contributing positively to team dynamics and project outcome.

## Methodology

There were 80 university students who participated in the debugging study (32 females). The average ag e was 23.6 years (SD = 2.4 years), and all participants h ad at least two years of Python experience. Upon th eir arrival in the laboratory, the participants signed a consen t form, and the eye-tracker was calibrated for each individual participant using a 9-point calibration. Then the participants were expla ined their task. The simple instruction to the participants was to find and fix six bugs in th e given Python code. The Python code was developed using pygame library to create Space Invaders. The participants were shown how the clean (withou t bugs) version of the program was supposed to work. They were also to ld that there were no syntactical errors in the exp erimental code (with bugs) and the bugs were related to the p hysics of the game (e.g., the collisions were not w orking properly, the arrow keys would not do their intende d tasks, the score was not incrementing properly and at the correct event). The setup is shown in Figure 1 (Right panel) The total duration of the session was 45 minutes (30 minutes for debugging and 15 minutes for signing, c alibration, and explanations), after which the part icipants were told to stop debugging (to remove the time-on-task effect) and were given a gift card equivalent to USD

35. We saved all the programs once the sessions wer e over and counted how many bugs were resolved. We then

applied a median split on the number of bugs resolv ed. This was our dependent variable, i.e., debugging performance. For the present study, we developed a code editor t hat supports concurrent collaboration, integrated with extensive logging, gathers data fro m multiple data streams (i.e., eye tracking, webcam, audio and wristband) and synchronized all gathered data f rom editor and user sensors. For the collaborative code editor (Figure 1, left panel), we extended the fire pad code editor to enable the logging of the user e vents. After developing a functioning collaborative editor, the last step was to find a way for users to compile and run their code. Initially, this was done using the library Py odide (The Pyodide development team, 2021) which is  a Python distribution for the browser. In this experi ment, we collected the following data from the pair programmers: Eye-tracking, Heart rate Variability (HRV), Electrodermal Activity (EDA), and Facial Videos. Measurements Joint facial expression cycles:  To compute the joint facial expression cycles, we f irst computed the following facial expressions from the Action Units computed o n the individual faces: Boredom, Confusion, Delight, Disengagement, and Frustration. Then we compute the  Joint Facial Expressions (JFE). To compute JFE, we simply counted the number of frames per second wher e both faces had the same expressions (with a time lag of 5 seconds). The JFE of a given expression was assig ned to the expression that had most frames per seco nd together. For example, if for one minute most of th e frames in that minute had boredom for both participants (including the 5 seconds window) that minute’s JFE was JFE-boredom. Once we had the JFE calculated for the whole interaction, we then used a fuzzy string clus tering (Kalyanathaya et al, 2019)  algorithm to find the JFE cycles.

Joint Visual Attention (JVA):  is the amount of time the peers spent looking at th e same set of objects in a given time window. Using each token in the program, we fi rst created a proportionality vector. This vector c ontains the total time spent on each token in a given time window of 10 seconds (Sharma et al, 2021). Once we have this proportionality vector for both the participan ts in a dyad, we simply compute the cosine similari ty of the two vector. A value of 1 would indicate perfect synchrony, and a value of 0 will indicate no synchrony at all. Average Mental Effort (AME) and Joint Mental Effort  (JME):  is the amount of time the peers spent in exerting the similar amount of cognitive load on the task.  To compute JME, we first calculate the cognitive lo ad as the Index of Pupilary activity (Duchowski et al, 2018).

Average Stress (AStr) and Joint Stress (JStr):  is the amount of time peers spent being in similar physiological stress. Once, we calculate the stress from heart ra te variability (Lim et al, 2018) for individual peers. We use the exact cross recurrence method to compute the JStr a s in JME. Apart from the JStr, we also compute the average stress (AStr) for the dyad.

Average Engagement (AEng) and Joint Engagement (JEn g): is the amount of time peers spent being in similar engaged level. Once, we calculate the engagement fr om electrodermal activity (Hernandez et al, 2014)  for individual peers. We use the exact cross recurrence  method to compute the JEng as in JME. Apart from t he JEng, we also compute the average engagement (AEng) for the dyad. Analysis To answer RQ1, we computed the proportion of the different joint  facial expression cycles present in the data for each pair. Then, we conducted a t-test with the debugging performance as an independe nt variable. To answer RQ2,  we grouped the different cycles into two groups: 1)  the cycles that are associated with high performance and 2) the cycles that are associated w ith low performance. Then we conducted t-tests with the joint MMD measures as dependent and the cycle group s as the independent variables. Prior to t-test, we checked for normality distribution using the Shapiro-Wilk test and homoskedasticity with the Breucsh-Pegan test. If the normality was violated in any case, we normalized t he data by computing the z-score, and if the homoskedasticity was violated, then we used Welch correction for the degrees of freedom with the t-test.

## Results

From the temporal clustering of joint expressions, we found the following cycles from the dyads: Cycle 1: neutral --> frustrated --> bored --> disengaged --> neutral; Cycle 2: neutral --> frustrated --> confused --> delight --> neutral; Cycle 3: delight --> confused --> bored --> disengaged --> confused --> delight; Cycle 4: frustrated -- > bored --> disengaged --> frustrated; Cycle 5: confused --> bored --> frustrated --> delight --> confused. Comparing their proportions across the two debuggin g performance levels, we observe that the high-performing dyads have significantly higher proportions of cycles 2 (t(38)  = 10.35, p <.0001), 3 (t(38)  = 3.25, p <.01), and 5 (t(38)  = 7.79, p <.0001). On the ot her hand, the low-performing dyads have significantly higher proportions of cycles 1 (t(38)  = 12.10, p <.0001)  and 4 (t(38)  = 10.37, p <.0001).  Next, we compare the multimodal measurements while the dyads were within  one of the joint expression cycles (Figure 3). For  this, we grouped the cycles into two categories based on which performance levels they had a higher affinity  for. That is, cycles 1 and 4 were grouped together becau se the low performing dyads had higher proportions of such cycles; and cycles 2, 3, and 5 were grouped togethe r because the high-performing dyads had higher proportions of such cycles. Upon comparing the multimodal measu rements across these two joint expression cycle gro ups, we observe the following. Cycles 1 and 4 have lower  joint visual attention than cycles 2, 3, and 5 (t(31.89) = - 8.12, p <.0001). Cycles 1 and 4 have lower joint m ental effort than cycles 2, 3, and 5 (t(37.42) = -4.96, p <.0001). Cycles 1 and 4 have lower average mental ef fort than cycles 2, 3, and 5 (t(33.41) = -4.23, p <.001). Cycles 1 and 4 have higher joint stress than cycles  2, 3, and 5 (t(30.90) = 5.37, p <.0001). Cycles 1  and 4 have higher average stress than cycles 2, 3, and 5 (t(35.58) = 3.56, p <.01). Cycles 1 and 4 have lower jo int engagement than cycles 2, 3, and 5 (t(35.35) = -3.01, p <.01). Cycles 1 and 4 have lower average e ngagement than cycles 2, 3, and 5 (t(36.23) = -9.19, p <.0001).

## Discussion and conclusions

Using multimodal data (gaze, facial expressions, HRV, EDA) from 40 pairs of programmers, we show that there are clear differences between the high and low perf orming pairs based on the cognitive equilibrium exp ression sequences (CEES, or, joint emotional states). Moreo ver, when we analyse the groups of CEES that are associated with high performance, we observe high j oint visual attention (JVA), high joint mental effo rt (JME) with high average mental effort (AME), low joint st ress (JStr) with low average stress (AStr) and high  joint engagement (JEng) with high average engagement (AEn g). On the other hand, when we analyse the groups o f CEES that are associated with low performance, we o bserve low joint visual attention, low joint mental  effort with low average mental effort, high joint stress w ith high average stress and low joint engagement wi th low average engagement.

The key difference, based on the CEES between high and low-performing pairs, is the presence of the sequence “frustrated   bored  disengaged” for the low performing pairs. The high-performing pairs also show the individual expressions (i.e., frustration,  bored and disengaged); however, the high performin g pairs always show confusion and delight between these sta tes in their CEES. From further analysis of multimo dal synchrony, we see that the CEES associated with hig h performance also have high JVA, JME, JEng, and Lo w JStr. We argue that because of high visual, cogniti ve and engagement synchrony between the peers the h ighperforming pairs succeed in resolving the issues ca using frustration and boredom (even disengagement), perhaps using emotion regulation strategies (e.g., cognitive reappraisal; McRae & Gross, 2020) and mov e to expressions that have a higher association with lea rning performance (i.e., confusion and delight, Sharma et al., 2022). Furthermore, the CEES associated with high perfo rmance also have low JStr, showing that while the dyads might be frustrated, bored, or disengaged, th ey are not stressed together. This might also aid t hem in overcoming these cognitive equilibrium states that are detrimental to learning (D’Mello & Graesser, 2012). We also observe that the high synchronies (JME and JEn g) coincide with high average values (AME and AEng). Meanwhile, low joint stress is accompanied by low a verage stress. These results indicate towards the p ositive cognitive and behavioural processes from the high performing pairs. Our study is novel in that it includes multimodal d ata during pair programming. Using multimodal data to study cognitive equilibrium synchrony offers sev eral significant advantages, particularly in unders tanding how different modalities influence collaboration and teamwork. First, multimodal data provides a more comprehensive view of team dynamics. By integrating  various sources of information, such as audio reco rdings, video footage, and physiological signals, researche rs can capture the complexity of human interactions  and better understand how cognitive and emotional state s align.  The advantage of applying affect dynamics with multimodal data is that it allows for exploring ind ividual differences in task responses. For example,  different team members may demonstrate unique patterns of cog nitive and emotional responses, which can be analyz ed through varied data sources, leading to tailored te am strategies for better performance. In addition, examining multimodal data can help identify disruptions in co gnitive equilibrium. For example, a sudden change i n a team member's non-verbal communication or physiological state can be detected, providing insights into potential disruptions in teamwork dynamics. Also, multimodal data enables longitudinal analysis that tracks cogn itive equilibrium over time. This is particularly relevan t for understanding how teams develop synchrony reg arding affective states and cognitive processing as they e ngage in collaborative tasks. Finally, insights gai ned from multimodal approaches can inform better interventio n strategies to improve cognitive equilibrium. For instance, specific training programs can be developed to addr ess emotional or cognitive misalignments identified  through data analysis. In conclusion, the affect dynamics m odel provides a framework for understanding the int erplay of emotions and cognition in collaborative environment s, especially when employing multimodal data. Recognizing the significance of emotional awareness, emotional exchange, cognitive appraisal, coping mechanisms, feedback loops, and cognitive equilibri um can help teams enhance their collaboration and performance, particularly in complex tasks like pair programming.
