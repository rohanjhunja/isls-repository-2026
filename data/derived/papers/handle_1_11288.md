# Taking a Scientist Perspective: Constructing Science Identities through Embodied Modeling

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In embodied science modeling, perspective taking is often used to help students take on the different points of view within a scientific phenomenon to understand how it arises. Perspective taking is also a central practice in identity construction (e.g. imagining and taking on new roles), yet the implications of perspective taking as scientific entities for students’ science identities is under explored. Therefore, we ask how fifth grade students construct science identities when they are taking on the perspective of a scientist in an embodied model versus acting as themselves. We draw on discursive psychology to conceptualize science identity, viewing it as the ways that students position themselves and are positioned by others in relation to science through discursive resources. We found that when students were acting as scientists, they positioned themselves and each other as scientific, but when they talked about their own scientific practices, they positioned themselves as non-scientific.

## Introduction

In embodied modeling contexts, perspective taking c an be a powerful vehicle for sense making and model construction. The roles that students adopt in mixed-reality embodied models, or scientific models in which students control virtual avatars with the movement of their bodies,  substantially shape the learning experience, often driving the narrative of the model and giving students a purpose or motivation (Lindgren & DeLiema, 2022; Danish et al., 2022). Additionally, moving between first and third person perspectives can support students to play with agent behaviors within a system  from the agent’s point of view, and then author models of these behaviors and evaluate them from a system-wide perspective (Vickery et al., 2021). This idea of imagining and taking on new roles and perspectives is a key component of id entity and identity development (Nasir, 2002); yet,  the ways in which perspective taking may offer new identitie s to students is under  explored in the embodied modeling literature (Steinberg & Danish, 2024). As such, we designed an embodied model in which students could take on the perspective of a scientist character in order t o help them explore their ideas about what real sci entists do. Taking science identity to be the ways that students position themselves and are positioned by others with respect to science through talk, we ask: How do students discursively construct science identities differently as they are and are not taking on a scientist  perspective while planning an embodied model ? That is, how did students construct science identities while explicitly pretending to be a scientist, versus while speaking as themselves.

## Theoretical framework and literature review

We draw on d iscursive psychology (DP) to understand how students discursively constructed science identities (Wiggins, 2017). DP takes a social constructivist approach to studying how psychological concepts are constructed and displayed through interaction. It respecifies concepts like identity, that in traditional psychological views, are treated as primarily internal mental con structs by treating them as external devices that do work i n interactions (Edwards & Potter, 1992). Therefore, DP argues that “our identities are multiple and produced in interaction” (Wiggins, 2017, p. 8). That is, identities are constructed as individuals draw on sh ifting discursive resources in interaction. Discursive psychology ass umes that identities exist in the discourses themselv es; there are no hidden meanings that the discourses reveal about an internal identity. Taking this approach, we understand science identity as the ways that students position themselves and a re positioned by others in relation to science through discursive resources.

Researchers taking a discursive psychology framing to science identity tend to focus on two main questions: how environments afford science identiti es (Kumpulainen et al., 2017; Wade-Jaimes et al., 2022) and how science identities are discursively construc ted (Brown, 2004; Due, 2012; Hsu et al., 2009). For  example, taking the former approach, Wade-Jaimes and colleagues (2022) focused on understanding how different settings / STEM ecosystems afforded different kinds of ident ity. In the latter approach, researchers focus less  on what supports different kinds of science identities, but rather on how the identity itself is discursively constructed. For instance, Brown (2004) identified four types of science identities: an opposition identity, for example, meant that students did not use science discourses. We build on both approaches, exploring the discursiv e construction of science identity in the context of embodied modeling.

## Methodology

Data collection for this study was part of a larger research project that was conducted at a public elementary school in a suburban area in the Midwestern United States. A class of 22 fifth grade students (18 consented) participated in the curriculum. Students engaged in a series of ten science lessons focused on energy transfer in lake and garden ecosystems, supported by the GEM-STEP software, a platform that supports students to enga ge in mixed-reality modeling (Danish et al., 2022). Students could control virtual agents (e.g. worms, bunnies, scientists) via either moving through the classroom wearing pozyx commerci al tracking tags, or through an iPad, which control led their avatars within the GEM-STEP platform (Figure 1). The lessons were taught by the research team  and each lasted approximately forty-five minutes to one hour.

Figure 1 Students Embody Scientists on Day 10 (left) and the Projected GEM-STEP Screen (right) In this paper, we focus on the last day of instruct ion in which students could explore their ideas aro und what it means to do science. In this lesson, studen ts were given the opportunity to embody scientists in a model of scientists building knowledge together. The goal  was for students to explore their collective under standing of how scientists study the topics that the class had just learned about (pond and garden ecosystems). Af ter an introduction to the model (Figure 1), students worked in four small groups (three consented) to make a plan for where they wanted to go and what they wanted to do there as scientists to learn about and help the ecosystems. Students were encouraged to talk about or act out what they were doing at each place. After planning, each group engaged in a round of modeling and debrief. We hoped that this activity would help students to bring in their own understandings about science, to imagine what it mi ght be like to be a scientist, as well as to realiz e that science happens in many different places. Prompts were designed to encourage students to think about places where they have done science and what science looked like in each place, thus helping them to articulate their understandings of science in relation to what scientists might do. The collected data includes video recordings of each small group engaging in the lesson and screen recordings of the GEM-STEP software. Analysis We began by becoming familiar with all the data from t he focal lesson; watching and rewatching it and cre ating content logs that included information about what w as happening. We became most interested in the planning conversations, as students seemed to articulate  and enact interesting ideas around science  as they moved in and out of a scientist perspective (talking as their scientist characters and talking as themselves). We created verbatim transcripts of these conversations, focusing initially on captur ing what was said, beginning with Otter.AI, an automatic transcription service, and cleaning by ha nd. We imported the transcripts and the videos into MAXQDA24 analytic software and rewatched the conver sations for each of the three consented groups with  the transcripts open, adding modified Jeffersonian symbols to excerpts where we noted significant positioning with regards to science happening (Jefferson, 2004; Table 1). In subsequent viewings of the videos/ readings of the transcripts, we focused on how the discourse served to position students. This included paying attentio n to pronoun use (did students refer to the scienti sts as themselves or something separate; when did they use  “I” versus “we” versus “they” in presenting ideas) and to self-positioning (how much each student was talking; their confidence level in the talk, as seen through quietness or slowness of speech, forming ideas as questions o r statements based o n tone, etc.). We also considered how these ideas may have been related or not to how students had been learning science in the classroom, through embodied science modeling. We looked for evidence that movement or perspective ta king were relevant (or not) to students. Finally, we identified specific discursive resources  (Wiggins, 2017) that were relevant for students’ science identity construction, including pronoun us e/footing (moving across roles in ways that manage accountability for what is being said), hedging  (when a turn in talk is marked as provisional or te ntative), and category entitlements  (categorizing something in a way that entitles it to  specific kinds of knowledge or experiences), as doing work to construct science identities. Th erefore, we coded the videos/attached transcripts for these devices (marked where we saw them happening) in order to identify how they were being  used and patterns across the three groups. In what follows, we share a conversation analysis inspired line-by-line analysis of how these devices served to construct science identities, using excerpts from one group as illustrative examples of patterns in the data. This group included five students (JB, MW, Drew, Summer, and Elenor; all pseudonyms) and was facilitated by the first author.

## Findings

Asking students to pretend to be scientists in an e mbodied model through embodying virtual scientist characters facilitated perspective taking, which served to ena ble students to imagine what it might be like to be  a scientist. Students seemed to move smoothly between seeing the mselves as the scientists in the model and seeing t he scientists as separate from themselves. We argue that when students took on the role of scientists more explicitly (through making plans as their scientist characters  in the model), they were trying on a “scientist identity” and able to position themselves and others as affiliate d with science. However, when students spoke as or about themselves as learners and their own scientific practices  and their own science practices, they positioned themselves as non-scientific.

## Discussion and conclusion

These analyses suggest that  when students discussed their own experiences with science, they did not construct themselves as people who were competent at science.  Yet, when they took on the perspective of a scient ist character, their talk constructed themselves as people who w ere competent at science. This work  points to the promise of embodied activities for fostering positi ve science identities. However, it also highlights the potential divide between personally authentic and professiona lly authentic scie nce activities (National Academy of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine, 2021) and sugg ests that explicit bridges are needed to help stude nts to interrogate their understandings of science and to relate their own personal experiences to the disciplinary practices of scientists.
