# A Humanities Approach to Data Literacy: Contextualizing and Relating Data with Network Visualization

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In this paper, we propose situating data literacy within a humanities framework as a complementary method in data literacy education, using network visualization as a tool. As data becomes increasingly essential in understanding our world, there is a growing need for a multidisciplinary approach to data literacy. We argue that network visualization techniques can foster deeper engagement with data's complexities in certain contexts. We explore various definitions and frameworks of data literacy, emphasizing context, critical thinking, and holistic understanding, and highlight network visualization as a tool to explore data within a humanistic framework. By using networks, students candevelop a critical and contextual understanding of data, preparing them to navigate the complexities of our data-driven society more effectively.

## Introduction

Historically, data literacy has been a component of mathematics, computing, statistics, and, now, data science and computational thinking (Donoho, 2017; Weintrop et a l., 2016). However, as data continues to become ess ential in understanding our world, educators recognize that data literacy must move beyond the confines of its traditional disciplines and interpretations and encompass a multidisciplinary approach that allows for constructive and critical approaches (Atenas et al., 2020; Klenke et al., 2020; Ryan et al., 2019). We propose an integration of data literacy within a humanities framework, using network visual ization as a tool to develop holistic data literacy  skills. By employing network visualization as a critical pedag ogical tool, educators can fo ster engagement with data's complexities, encouraging students to see connectio ns, patterns, and stories without needing to rely u pon quantitative analyses that may be beyond their current skill set or interest. This is particularly important given our goal of having all learners, even those who do not take basic statistics, have a baseline understanding of data and data literacy to protect themselves when engaging with data-driven online tools. Data literacy definitions vary depending on context  and discipline, but tend to focus on reading or creating data, making sense of data, and making inf erences from data (Vahey et al., 2012). As comprehe nding, interpreting, and inferring from data continue to be crucial skills, educators and researchers are calling for stronger data literacy education within K-12, beginning as early as kindergarten (LaMar & Boa ler, 2021; Martinez & LaLonde, 2020). While data literacy was often releg ated to math lessons (Rubin, 2005), there have been  several novel approaches to integrate data literacy through out multiple disciplines, such as social science, m athematics, science, and English Language Arts (Vahey et al., 2 012). In the next sections, we discuss some relevan t data literacy frameworks and how a humanities approach t o data literacy, particularly using network visuali zations, can enhance data literacy education outside of common statistical methods. Data literacy frameworks Summarizing the data literacy literature, Rubin (20 20) developed a framework for categorizing the cruc ial components of working with data: context, variability, aggregation, visualization, and inference, and suggests that students must become “data journalists,” investigating the who, what, where, why, and how of data. While Rubin designates context as a separate category, we argue  that context is a prerequisite for the other categ ories as it is infused throughout the framework.

Wise (2020) also emphasizes the importance of conte xt in data, suggesting that educators consider a critical and contextual approach to data literacy, one where students develop a relationship to data t hrough the generation, storage, transformation, interp retation, and representation of data. While student s will, more often, work with others’ data, she argues that involving s tudents in the data process allows them to understa nd data complexity, context, and sources of bias. Wise and Rubin’s conceptualizations of data literacy are complementary.

When students investigate data and its context —learning about data by actively involving themselve s in its production and interpretation, data literacy become s less about calculating means and more about criti cally examining how data influences and affects their lives. There are many tools to aid this understanding, but network visualizations are intuitive and easy to learn, involving students in the data production process and allowing them to investigate and make sense of relationships and connections within data (Craig et al., 2021; Zhou et al., 2024). Network visualization also aligns with Rubin’s and Wise’s data literacy frameworks, allowing students to actively engage with data and construct their own understand ing. As Roberts et al. (2014) argue, visualizations  enhance comprehension by providing contextually rich inform ation that supports explanation and argumentation. From our work on the Visualizing Funds of Identity (2024) project, working with middle school ELA and Histo ry students, we have developed a framework that connec ts high-level data literacy goals with network visualization activities and learning outcomes. This framework hi ghlights how Rubin and Wise’s conceptualization map s tonetwork visualization and conversely how student learning from visualizations contributes to data literacy. Networks, data literacy, and the humanities approach When we say a “humanities approach” to data literacy, we are emphasizing the interpretive, critical, and contextual skills that are central to the humanities to make s ense of data (Craig et al., 2021). Rather than view ing data as purely quantitative or objective, this approach enc ourages questioning the underlying assumptions, met hods of collection, and socio-cultural implications of data (Vance et al., 2022).  This approach recognizes data as a form of narrative that both reflects and shapes the human experience. Network visualizations are uniquely positioned tools to integrate humanistic approaches into data literacy (Craig et al., 2021). By representing datasets as n etworks of interconnected nodes and edges, visualiz ations emphasize relationships and interdepende ncies rather than isolated variables. For students,  this approach can foster a deeper understanding of the complexities inherent in real-world data by providing a visual representation that demonstrates interconnectedness. For example, visualizing social media interactions as a network can reveal power dynamics, information flows, and the potential for bias or exclusion within digital communities. Zhou et al. (2024) illustrate how network visualizations engage students in exploring humanities content and, using personally relevant data, help them see data as connected and dynamic rather than static and isolated. This aligns with the broader goals of  fostering data literacy as a critical and contextua l skill rather than merely a technical one. However, this isn’t to claim that th e two aren’t related. The authors argued that using  networks helped students develop early data literacy skills such as crea ting data, finding relationships, and categorizing data.

In summary, a humanities approach to data literacy emphasizes contextual, critical, and interpretive investigations into data throughout the data proces s cycle, including its creation, transformation, re presentation, and use in making inferences. Network v isualization provides an easy and powerful entry po int for students to learn these skills outside of a typical math or sci ence curriculum, widening the scope of data literac y and conceptualizing it as multidisciplinary. As student s build their data analysis skills, they bring with  them a repertoire of investigative tools that allow them to ask crucial questions necessary to understand the world around them.

## Conclusion

While understanding quantitative and statistical te chniques is critical for data science, not all stud ents will be interested in data analysis. Regardless, students l ive in a world dominated by data. By integrating da ta literacy within a humanities framework and using network visualizations to explore data concepts and context, educators can prepare students for living  in a data society. This approach fosters critical t hinking, ethical awareness, and contextual understanding—key competencies for navigating complex societal challenges. Network visualizations, in particular, provide an accessible and engaging way for students to explore the interconnectedness and dynamic nature of data, bridging the gap between quantitative analysis and qualitative insight. Ultimately, this perspective empowers students to see data not merely as numbers or graphs but as a powerful tool for understanding the world in all of its complexity.
