# From Conceptualization to Practice: Faculty Views of Learning and Emergent Practices

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This analysis investigates how faculty beliefs about teaching and learning influence their methods. Through interviews with 14 professors across disciplines, we identified four key views: Career-Content, Social-Dynamic, Joyful-Coaching, and Authority-Foundations, with Career-Content associated with traditional lecturing and Social-Dynamic with active learning. Findings indicate that professors' beliefs significantly affect their teaching, often at odds with evidence-based practices. Encouraging a shift in faculty conceptualizations appears crucial for adopting effective teaching strategies.

## Introduction and Background

The primary rationale for the project is that practices in teaching and learning are emergent from conceptualizations of learning, a proposition groun ded in complex conceptual systems theory (Donaldson  & Allen-Handy, 2023). From a complex conceptual systems the ory perspective, conceptualizations emerge from one's experiences, beliefs, and understandings abou t the world which then act as deeply rooted mental models through which new information and experiences are i nterpreted. As a result, these conceptualiz ations become a sort of guiding principles which shape and constrai n our behaviors as well as influence the practices we advance or engage in. Once established, conceptualizations and the practices that emerge out of them often become highly resistant to change. With regard to teaching and learning, the conceptualizations that professors use may influence the practices that emerge in (and out) of the classroom. There is a robust body of literature and vigorous research studies that have been developed and conducted by l earning scientists that discuss evidence-based high impact practices recommended  to professors (Bransford et al., 2000; National Aca demies, 2018). The practices that learning scientists advocate differ from the practi ces that many faculty members engage with because o f their differing conceptualizations. The only way that faculty can adopt these empiricall y supported high-impact practices in their own teaching is if we help them reconceptualize teaching and learning. We first need to try to understand the common conceptualizations that facul ty tend to lean on and the practices to emerge from  these perspectives. As such we seek to answer the following research question:  What are conceptualizations of learning used by faculty, and what practices emerge out of those conceptualizations?

## Methods

To answer our research question, we analyzed prelim inary data from a convenience sample of 14 professors (the full study will include at least 40 professors) of various academic disciplines from a public universi ty in the Southern region of the United Stat es. Following an interview protocol meant elicit in-depth detail about faculty members’ worldviews, paradigms, and conceptualizati ons of teaching and learning. These professors were interviewed for an average of 30 minutes using vide o conference software, Zoom. These interviews were audio and video recorded and transcribed. The transcripts  were loaded on mixed method data analysis software, MAXQDA for coding according to the categories of metaphors, non-metaphorical characterizations, worldviews, paradigms, and practices. Utilizing Girvan-Newman cluster analysis (Girvan & Newman, 2002) and  coding cooccurrence correlation symmetrical matrix tables, a longside the UCINET network analysis software (Borg atti, Everett, & Freeman, 2002) and the NetDraw network m apping software (Borgatti, 2002), resulted in the visualizations of network maps.

## Findings

Through analysis of correlations between conceptual  metaphors, conceptual stories, and worldviews/para digms we found four conceptualizations of teaching and le arning: Career-Content, Social-Dynamic, Joyful-Coaching, and Authority-Foundations.

In eight of fourteen interviews, the Career-Content conceptualization was prevalent, seeing education as essential for career preparation, emphasizing knowledge as objective truth for transmission. This view prioritizes lectures for content delivery, expecting students to internalize and utilize information in their careers, thus valuing continuous learning. Teaching, viewed more as an obligation, focuses on content comprehension via lectures and tools like Learning Management Systems, aiming to p roduce career-ready individuals through knowledge acquisition and application. In five interviews, th e Social-Dynamic conceptualization emerged, emphasizing learning through broadened perspectives via exposure to diverse views and valuing human relationships and social experiences. This approach prioritizes accessible learning focused on global and personal improvement,  viewing learning as an active, meaningful process beyond gr ades, rooted in open-mindedness and social connectivity. It highlights the importance of emotional and social c onnections in education, encouraging practices that  cater to students' interests and contexts, foster engagement  through interest-based activities, offer experiential learnin g, share learning agency, and support learner self-evaluation and social-emotional development. Techniques like discussions, peer feedback, group projects, and critical thinking exercises are employed to deepen understanding and engagement. In one interview, the Joyful-Coaching conceptualization was highlighted, presenting learning as enjoyable and aligned with student interests, empha sizing assimilation and memory. Teachers act as gui des, introducing content and assessing understanding thr ough directed pro blem-solving. This approach fosters practices that connect topics to student interests,  boosting engagement and attention, making attendan ce crucial for engagement. In one interview, the Authority-Foundations conceptualization stood out, prioritizi ng teaching with the teacher as the key authority. This approac h focuses on teacher-determined content and knowledge transmission. It views knowledge as assessable and measures learning by evaluating students' applicati on of information to real-life scenarios. Thi s perspective encourages student interaction with c ontent, highlighting its future career relevance through real-world roleplay and scenarios, suggesting a more tea cher-centric model of knowledge delivery.

## Implications and conclusions

This study identifies various faculty conceptualiza tions of teaching and learning, highlighting their practices, benefits, and limitations. The Social-Dynamic approach, emphasizing collaborative learnin g and relationships, may neglect individual skill and content mastery, challenging student assessment. Ca reer-Content links learning with career relevance, boosting motivation but poss ibly overlooking critical thinking and broader skil ls, limiting education's role beyond workforce preparation. Joyful-Coaching focuses on motivation and self-directed learning with the teacher as a guide, but its lack of struct ure might lead to gaps in essential skills or conte nt. Finally, the Authority-Foundations model offers clear curriculum and expectations through a teacher-centric lens, prioritizing foundational knowledge at the expense of student creativity and deeper understanding, potentially leading to rote memorization without genuine comprehension.

This preliminary analysis found that professors' co nceptualizations of teaching and learning strongly influence the instructional practices they use in t he classroom even when those practices contradict e mpirical research on effective teaching. Therefore, shifting faculty's underlying conceptualizations of teaching and learning may be a prerequisite or leverage point for increas ing their adoption of evidence-based practices advocated by learning sciences.
