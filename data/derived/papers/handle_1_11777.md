# Amplifying Teacher Voice in Collaborative Research

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Research practice partnerships (RPPs) are a promising approach to transforming educational practice by amplifying the voices of teachers. This approach is especially powerful for disrupting educational inequities by positioning local actors as experts to generate solutions and take action. This cross-case study examines how engaging mathematics and science teachers in collaborative inquiry gave voice to teachers’ experiences as they sought to transform their instruction for equity and justice. Informed by process and practice theory perspectives, we describe how the social arrangement of cowriting supported teacher researchers in generating new concepts and practices for equitable and just instruction. We identify four specific practices-gaining clarity and consensus on equity for STEM instruction, generating representations, thinking through example cases, and cycles of feedback and accountability. Together, these practices enabled teachers to contribute to knowledge generation by advancing theory on instructional practice for justice-oriented practice through experimentation and analysis of teaching practice.

## Introduction

Teachers are transformational intellectuals who bri ng scholarly reflection and practice to their work to support students to become critical and thoughtful citizens  (Giroux, 2018). Research practice partnerships (RP Ps) are a promising approach to enable teachers to transform instruction (Coburn & Penuel, 2016). Broadly, RPPs embody principles that have been identified as important f or changing teaching and learning-partners collaboratively define problems of practice, and they mutually engage in addressing those problems, through long-term, sustained relationships. Some models of RPPs offer practition ers support to engage with and use research to impr ove their organizational routines and practices (Penuel et al., 2015). One critique of this partnership model is that they can be construed as research moving to practitioners, with practitioners translating research to make it accessible and usable in local contexts (Coburn & Penuel, 2016). O ther models take a more collaborative approach, cen tering the rich knowledge and expertise that exists inside  communities and organizations with researchers sup porting processes of local inquiry (Gutierrez & Jurow, 2016; Philip et al., 2022). These approaches are partic ularly powerful for unveiling and disrupting inequities in  educational systems because they are necessarily l ocated in the communities that are most vulnerable and position local actors as experts to generate solutions and take action. Though this latter model shows promise, less is known about how the process of partnership activities unfolds to empower practitioners to define research goals and contribute to knowledge generation. The two projects in this study are cases of teacher  research partnerships that aim to disrupt inequiti es in mathematics and science instruction through RPPs with practitioners. One study followed a community-engaged approach to desettle dominant perspectiv es that shape teachers’ classroom noticing in mathe matics education by bringing teachers and community educators together to engage in collaborative research to advance theo ry on teacher noticing (Payne & Zeichner, 2017). The seco nd study followed a design-based research approach (DBR Collective, 2003) that includes secondary science t eachers focused on co-designing, enacting, and studying justice-oriented practices and curricular units (Morrales-Doyle, 2017). Teacher-research partners reported experiencing transformations in their commitments, identities, and classroom instruction toward justic e-oriented instruction (Cooke  et al., 2024; Munzer & van Es, 2024) suggesting tha t participating in collaborative inquiry invited them to engage with problems of practice in ways that were generative for their learning. One common activity structure to these partnerships  included collaboratively generating research through cowriting. Recognizing that teachers’ voice s are often peripheral to efforts to transform educ ational practice, yet they are responsible for realizing visions of instruction set forth in policy document s and reform initiatives, both projects aimed to position teache rs with agency to define research questions and sha pe analysis and findings. We conjectured that engaging teachers in research would empower them to develop deeper insights into the purpose of and vision for equitable and ju st instruction in mathematics and science as they i nvestigated these questions, with others, in the context of the ir practice using data from their instruction. More over, we envisioned that moving from collaborative analysis to co-developing artifacts that represented their learnin g and practice would result in usable resources for pract itioners who sought to embark on learning to teach for equity and justice. Thus, we ask in this study, what features of cowriting as collaborative researc h were generative for teacher learning, and in what ways? Our goal is to contribute to calls for research to examine how the process of collaborative design and development influences participants and, through comparative analysis, begin to generate design principles to inform collaborative inquiry and cowriting (Coburn & Penuel, 2016; Feliciano et al., 2021).

## Literature review and theoretical perspectives

Honoring teacher agency in collaborative research Honoring teacher agency in collaborative research i s fundamental for fostering equitable, impactful, a nd sustainable educational innovations (Priestley et al., 2015). With widespread adoption of participatory approaches to educational transformation (e.g., Bang & Vossoug hi, 2016, Penuel, 2019), attention is needed to how  power structures organize collaborators’ activities, as w ell as to how the sociopolitical context of schooli ng and the inherent power dynamics between administrators, tea chers, families, and youth organize classroom instr uction (Philip et al., 2022). Traditional research models often adopt a top-down approach, reinforcing hierarchical relationships that marginalize teachers’ voices and limit their agency (Coburn & Penuel, 2016; Kyza & Agesilaou, 2022).  Such approaches diminish the potential for research to resonate with classroom realities, thereby reducing its overall impact. However, t eachers contribute unique and significant insights to the academic research community, bringing context-specific knowledge of pedagogy, classroom practices, and school communities (Billings & Linn, 2024; Cochran-Smith & Lytle, 1990). Moreover, as described  by Philip et al. (2022), they are historically excluded from decision-making about the content and focus of educational p ractice and innovation, while simultaneously responsible for carrying out e ducational practices that reproduce inequities. We join others who see teachers as intellectual practitioners who can actively create knowledge to improve educationa l practice for social justice through collaborative inquiry (Cochran-Smith & Lytle, 2009). Reframing teachers as co-researchers and knowledge creators is essential to disrupt these hierarchies and ensure their lived expertise informs educational re forms. Collaborative frameworks like RPPs and participatory action research intentionally position teachers as equal partners i n the research process (Billings & Linn, 2024; Mensah, 2022; Philip et al., 2022). These frameworks prioritize r eciprocal relationships, shifting the center of knowledge production from academic institutions to the communities and schools where teaching and lear ning occur (Ghiso et al., 2019; Priestley et al., 2015).  For example, the teacher solidarity methodology hi ghlights the importance of leveraging teachers' critical knowledge of pedagogy, school contexts, and student needs, partnering with them to co-design solutions and lead innovations in equity-focused practices (Philip et al., 2022). Similarly, Billings and Linn (2024) illustrate how RPP activit y structures empowered a teacher to design a curric ulum integrating Henrietta Lacks' story, bridging scienc e education with social justice. Similarly, Luehmann et al., 2024b) and Zhang (2022)  describe how culturally sustaining pedagogy allowed  teachers to create place-based inquiry projects, connecting their instructional pr actices with their students’ lived experiences and addressing systemic inequities. Research partnerships can amplify teachers’ voices by integrating reflective practices, collaborative tools, and equity-focused frameworks, enabling them to shape meaningf ul educational innovations and address systemic barriers. These principles set  the foundation for exploring specific collaborative struc tures, like cowriting, that further expand teachers' roles as co-creators of knowledge and foster transformative learning. Collaborative tools and activity structures are vital for amplifying teacher agency. Severance et al. (2016) demonstrate how tools like standards-aligned storylines enable teachers to adapt researc h findings to their classrooms while fostering critical fee dback and adaptation. Similarly, Matuk et al. (2016) show how artifacts such as functional prototypes and student work inspire teachers' ideas, encouraging reflective practice and creative problem-solving. Professional Learning Communities (PLCs) p rovide s tructured environments for teachers to share insights, refine strategies, and take leaders hip roles in systemic improvement efforts. These to ols enhance teacher agency and democratize expertise by enablin g mutual learning and collaboration among educators  and researchers (Feliciano et al., 2021; Shim & Thompson, 2022). Cowriting as a process for expanding teacher insight The potential of RPPs to shift power relations and support teacher learning is well documented in the literature (Coburn & Penuel, 2016; Datnow et al., 2023; Ishima ru et al., 2022). Teachers’ participation in partne rship activities can empower them to make  novel contributions and take on leadership roles to  develop mutually beneficial, reciprocal relationships (e.g., Billing s & Linn, 2024). Moreover, teachers report benefitt ing from collaborating with researchers, by being prompted t o think differently and more expansively about conc epts central to partnership activities and to their inst ruction (Feliciano et al., 2021). Feliciano et al. (2021) credit the integration of a particular participant structure-the one-on-one meeting-serving to break down barriers between RPP partners, enabling teachers and researchers to gain deeper insight into each other’s ideas, resour ces, norms, and systems that structure each other’s activities.  Participation in boundary practices, such as the one-on-one meeting, promotes mutual learning and joint work that can lead to RPP effectiveness. Cowriting is one potential participation structure to foster mutual engagement and expansive learning for instructional improvement. Though there is a long history of teachers engaging in research to desettle who defines knowledge of and for teaching (e.g.  Cochran-Smith & Lytle, 1990), it remains challenging for te achers to contribute to knowledge mobilization. Johnson and S teele (2024) explain that structural challenges can  inhibit teachers’ participation in cowriting, as districts and schools do not have the resources to incentivize and reward teachers’ labor for this work. The genre itself may  also feel unwelcoming and inaccessible to teachers. Learning to write for publication-for practitioners or researchers-requires enculturation into a new set of practices, such as situating the work in existing research, analyzi ng data, and organizing findings to tell the story that emerged (Arce-Trigatti et al., 2023; Florence & Yore, 2004). At t he same time, the process of cowriting invites clos e and deep analysis of data for reflection and meaning-making, suggesting that the process of cowriting can itself serve as an opportunity for teacher learning. This raises  questions about whether and how to structure and s upport the process of cowriting in ways that serve practitioner and research partner audiences.

## Methods and data context

A cross-case analysis (Yin, 2012) of two nationally-funded projects was conducted to address the question, what features of cowriting as collaborative research wer e generative for teacher learning, and in what ways ? Data sources included artifacts generated in the cowriting process such as slide presentations, reflective res ponses, transcripts and recordings of working meetings, and  posters and presentation handouts. Qualitative methods guides our analysis, through cycles of coding and theme generation (Locke & Feldman, 2022) to identify routines common across projects that supported teachers and scholars to produce writing that captured the experiences and findings from shared inquiries in each teacher PAR project. This analysis was informed by the theory o n routine dynamics (Feldman & Pentland, 2022) that is concern ed broadly with how activity is accomplished throug h emergence, reproduction, replication, and changes i n patterns of action. This perspective focuses on i dentifying and theorizing how routine practices support member s in organizations to accomplish their activity. We  were specifically interested in understanding how participants engaged in cowriting activities and what types of routine activities in the cowriting process supported teach ers’ expansive learning.  Thus, data analysis included a first cycle of analysis to identify emergent practices of  cowriting and subsequent coding phases that examin ed how these practices supported teachers developing, expanding and honing their conceptions and commitments to equity in mathematics and science education, and in their teaching in particular. Both projects engaged teachers and scholars in a co llaborative study of equitable teaching, one in mathematics and the other in science. One project, Equitable Noticing (project names are pseudonyms to preserve anonymity), consisted of middle and high school mathematics teachers, community youth leaders, and researchers who sought to develop a professional learning model to advance theory on teacher noticing for equitable and just mathematics instruction by positioning community me mbers to offer alternative narratives of students’ experiences in the mathematics classroom (Bang & Vo ssoughi, 2016). The project included two cohorts of research collaborators who each met first in a summ er institute to develop shared understandi ngs of teacher noticing as a feature of teaching practice and its relevance for equitable instruction, and mathematics education as a white institutional space that perpetuates narratives that serve to minoritize culturally, racially, and linguistically diverse communities (Louie, 2018; Skovsmose, 2020). D uring the academic year, the research collaborators  met once monthly to investigate whether and how teacher s’ classroom noticing upheld or disrupted inequitie s in mathematics instruction. In the second summer, the research collaborators identified and elaborated no ticing for equity themes and considered differences in their c lassroom noticing and how their noticing practices supported disrupting inequitable practices. Each group genera ted proposals that for med the basis of collaborative writing projects, including conference proposals and manuscripts for practitioner audiences. Data for this analysis comes from the cowriting process with four teachers in the second cohort, two middle and two high school teachers. The second project, the Equity Team, supported a ne twork of 15 professional learning communities (PLCs) comprising 55 secondary science teachers and  seven scholars. The project aimed to collaboratively develop a framework of core practices to center justice in ambitious science teaching and ultimately succeeded in creating it  (Luehmann et al., 2024a). Each PLC conducted design-based research cycles focused on a shared, persistent problem of practice related to equitable  science teaching and learning. Using Philip and Az evedo’s

(2017) four discourses of equity in research on science e ducation, the Equity Team worked to push beyond

definitions of equity as access (discourse 1) and i dentity development through authentic science practices (discourse 2) to more transformational equity aims of expanding what counts as science (discourse 3) a nd using science for social transformation (discourse 4). In year one, PLCs met bi-weekly with frequent written input from scholars and verbal feedback from scholars and netw ork teachers three times a year. The same format co ntinued in year two, with one difference: a scholar was a participating member of each PLC. PLCs documented their cycle designs and findings in various formats provided by  project leadership across each year of two. Findin gs in the form of artifacts were shared across the network of  PLCs and at national meetings for researchers, tea cher educators, and practitioners. This analysis will foreground the work of one PLC led by Tia.

## Findings

Our analysis identified three shared practices and an additional one for the Equity Team that supporte d teacher learning through cowriting. We define the practices  here and provide evidence from each project to ill uminate how they served to support practitioners in generat ing and integrating new and deeper insights about e quitable and just mathematics and science instruction.

Gaining clarity on what counts as equity in STEM The process of cowriting provoked teachers to more deeply engage with the broader vision for equity in mathematics and science instruction. Throughout the  process of cowriting, the teachers continued to re fine their understanding and gain clarity on what c onstitutes equity in their practice, identifying ba rriers that need to be broken down in systems of mathematics and science education that perpetuate inequities. For example, each cycle of the Equitable Noticing project began with the re search collaborative defining and refining a vision for equity in mathematics, with particular attention to how pe rsonal and social histories of mathematics educatio n and schooling more broadly subvert students’ access to high-quality, rigorous learning opportunities (Gutierrez, 2013; Martin, 2019). The group explored different topics,  such as teachers’ positionality and experiences as  math learners, narratives of success in mathematics educ ation, and historical roots of tracking and assessm ent. They then discussed how these issues relate to their cla ssroom noticing and how their noticing practices up hold or disrupt inequitable learning opportunities for youth. During the academic year, teachers analyzed data  from their classrooms through an interview structure to explor e how their classroom noticing was tied to these br oader themes. These analyses resulted in identifying particular questions to pursue in cowriting activities. Cowriting activities furthered the teachers’ unders tanding of these constructs, particularly as they c ame to life in their teaching. One teacher, John, obser ved that students entered his classroom each year c arrying a narrative of “I can’t do math” and he wanted to use  cowriting to share how he sought to disrupt that n arrative through the kinds of tasks he posed, how he organiz ed students to learn, and through interactions with  students during 1:1, small group, and whole class interactio ns. Reviewing literature invited John to engage wit h different narratives that frame mathematics instruction (Skov smose, 2020), and to consider if and how these narr atives obstruct racial and linguistic minority students’ a ccess to rigorous mathematics in his classroom. Sub sequent discussions included reflections on his experiences  as a mathematics learner and how they shape how he  notices students’ experiences. These readings prompted disc ussions about his positionality as a white man and his academic success in mathematics, in contrast to the hurdles two of his Latinx colleagues overcame in mathematics to pursue careers as mathematics teachers. Though d iscussions related to race, positionality, academic opportunity, and achievement had been explored thro ughout the project, it  was not until John participated in cowriting that he reflected directly on what it mea ns for him to be part of a system that is organized to benefit some communities over others. The process of cowriting positioned John to grapple with these issues, broadening the interpretive lenses that he took to the analysis of his classroom noticing to promote equitable instruction. This ongoing grappling through writing around what “equity” truly means for minoritized students in science classrooms was similar in the other project  team. Though the Equity Team aimed to focus on transformative equity aims that disrupt the status quo, the initial PLCs’ DBR efforts tended to focus on equal participation (access) or building community. In year one, Tia’s PLC’s DBR focused on students’ construction of higher-ordered questions. A scholar offered feedback on a draft of Tia’s PLC Practice Brief, pressing them to “consider the difference between equity and equalit y. Does this strategy create a more just space for learning?” After similar conversations with different PLCs, sc holars added a step in the DBR design process they called, “Interrogate for Justice,” providing examples and f acilitating full group conversations about what it might look like to disrupt the stat us quo. At the end of year one, scholars introduced  a draft framework of core justice-centering practices to the network that had been sh aped by their work (Luehmann et al., 2024a). This framework offered a welcome organizational tool as well as sh ared language and vision to situate PLCs various ef forts concerning varied equity aims. From that point on, teachers positioned their DBR work related to core sections of the framework: e ither transformational aims such as expanding what counts as science, using science for social transformation, or building a welcoming, critical, and joy-filled community, or simply focusing on access or identity through authentic practices. It was common  to hear teachers situate different practice ideas s omewhere within the framework as they considered them. Thoug h not all teachers prioritized more transformationa l aims, some teachers within most PLCs were ready and able,  thus leading to the writing of DBR practice briefs  that contained cases that had more and less transformative potential. Generating representations through scaffolding structures Both projects also created structures to elicit and  represent teachers’ thinking through the process o f cowriting that supported further refinement and deeper understanding of equity in mathematics and science classrooms. For the Equitable Noticing project, this took the form of several iterations of the t eachers reflecting on their learning as part of the professional development program, th e researchers diving into the data to identify inst ances that represent teachers’ claims, and then further discus sions and refinement of teachers’ data. This process of generating data representations for the teachers to  interrogate as it relates to their learning locate d discussions in evidence and provoked deeper sensemaking and reason ing about teaching and learning (Lefstein et al., 2020). In practice, the process began with an initial reflect ion, followed by several rounds of data analysis and discussion for refinement. At the start of the cowriting proce ss, teachers responded to several prompts to reflec t on their learning through participation in the professional de velopment: a) identify important learnings, greates t hits, and aspects of the project they would be excited for ot hers to learn about; b) explain how they perceive p articipation in the project impacted the ir perceptions of themselves as teachers, their ins truction, and their students; and c) identify key learning moments related to teacher noticing and their experiences in the PD program. The template provided an accessible structure to identify questions of interest and potential data points for initial analysis. Using their responses, university researchers returned to  the teachers’ data (classroom observations, interv iews) to identify moments that seemed to reflect the ideas they shared. Selected segments of data were organized on slide decks with prompts to structure teachers’ narration  of what was noteworthy about these moments and if they reflected the themes they identified in the first m eeting. These narrations raised additional insights  that required additional data or further refinement of segments to illustrate themes emerging for teachers. This process mirrors iterative data analysis cycles that researchers use  to code, identify patterns, and refine interpretat ions. However, by responding to prompts through templates and slid e deck representations, teachers’ responses pointed researchers to moments of interest that could be further interrogated to gain greater clarity on how they understood their noticing as situated in broader educational systems and their roles in perpetuating or disrupting inequities. An example comes from the case of Alyssa who starte d with an interest in the relationship between her personal and professional identities and the collaboration she experienced as part of the Equitable Noticing project. Through the iterative process of exploring and refl ecting on data, Alyssa decided to shift the focus t o examine data that spoke to her professional identity. Unive rsity researchers generated data representations, c onnected to Alyssa’s professional identity, that established three emerging themes on how her identity shapes her noticing and practice: (1) positions students to feel good about  themselves and be co-creators of knowledge in the classroom;

(2) connects with students on personal, cultural, a nd linguistic levels; and (3) leverages her  personal educational

experiences to ensure students know she believes in  them. While these initial data representations wer e designed to explore Alyssa’s professional identity, after co llaborative reflection and analysis, Alyssa decided  she wanted to shift the analysis to explore the theme of “love and jo y” in her mathematics pedagogy specifically. In tur n, university researchers aggregated literature on thi s theme in educational contexts to share with Alyss a, and subsequent cowriting sessions included using the re search literature to articulate our own definition of love in Alyssa’s context and recast the data representations so they supported this shift in perspective. Unique to Alyssa’s case, university researchers highlighted her at a conference during this phase of cowriting, which prompted further refinement of the representations to illustrate her teaching and understanding of equity in mathematics. This phase resulted in the following question: How did Alyssa use love to pursue equitable mathematics?  Our findings included: (1) shared identities with her students, (2) differentiated relationships with students, (3)  and used students’ assets as a bridge to the mathematical co ntent. Importantly, Alyssa’s interests, questions, and sensemaking evolved over time through the cowriting process. In preparation for the conference, Alyssa worked with university researchers to identify still images from video-recorded observations and important quotations to represent how she “noticed” love arising in her classroom interactions to share in the presentation slide deck, that also supported the continued drafting of the writte n piece submitted to the conference. These cycles o f data analysis that emerged from teachers’ interests invited teachers to look at and make sense of data to develop deeper and more nuanced understandings of their noticing as it relates to their personal and social histories of schooling. The Equity Team PLCs generated various representations of their cycles of DBR, some with scaffolding provided by project leadership and some without. Th e various representations foregrounded different as pects of the study’s purpose, context, approach, findings, and meaning, the construction of which nurtured and represented deeper dives in particular areas. During the first cycle of writing, teachers decided which aspects of  the template were important to them. In the first iteration of T ia’s PLC’s slide deck, there was a large amount of placeholder text. Across the network, PLCs immediately began to reorganize the template to best suit their needs, shifting text and rearranging the order to create a flow that better matched the path they wanted to take to share their findings. PLCs used a combination of provided and original re presentations. In year two, Tia’s PLC visualized each participating teacher’s unique approach to sup porting students’ productive struggle with a self-authored flowchart. The flowchart began with “Justice-Centered Persistent Problem of Practice” at the top and ended with unique branches for enactment in each of the PLC te acher’s contexts. In a similar vein, one teacher in  that PLC developed a different flow chart in which colors communicated where in the process the strategies were modified, in what ways, and in service of what outcomes. One table provided to PLCs in the skeleton slide deck elicited and organized each step of the cycles of DBR for each of the three cycles: the persistent problem, work done, lessons learned, and needed revisions. Final artifacts refe rred to as “practice briefs” took different forms s uch as a two-page PDF with provided column headings, a slide dec k with each slide representing different elements o f DBR, or a “one-pager,” a handout designed to be shared with out-of-project teachers at National Science Teachers Association annual meeting  or other workshops. Designated sections on each, su ch as “Related Resources” and “Why this matters,” served as scaffolding for a PLC’s collaboration, and when needed, requests for support from scholar partners or peers. Shared representations such as these supported cross-network feedback and learning, as all PLCs worked to address the same criteria.

Thinking through examples and cases The process of generating representations also prom pted teachers to think through examples and cases a nd determine which ones represented claims they made a bout their classroom noticing and instruction embod ying equitable aims. Thinking through cases provoked tea chers to justify and defend how the examples they s elected represent their understanding and efforts to enact equitable and just pedagogy. The process of justification of cases positions the teacher researcher to determine “what  is this a case of” (van Es & Sherin, 2002; Yin 2012) and get inside the details of practice to contemplate if, i n fact, they are moving toward the vision of equita ble instruction developed in their professional learning experiences. This kind of up-close analysis of teaching is not inherent to teachers’ practice but is an important component of  professional development that provokes teachers’ l earning (Lefstein et al., 2020). In the Equitable Noticing project, after data collection concluded, teachers regularly met with university partners over six months to think t hrough examples and cases generated from their obse rvations and noticing interviews. In the first session, teac hers and researchers reviewed data (videos, transcr ipts) and analytic memos, identifying interesting moments and  interactions, and then compared noticings. By comp aring examples, collaborators were able to discuss which emerging themes and cases might be most generative to explore further. In the case of Lawrence (teacher) and Alison (scholar), they decided to examine how Lawrence’s personal identities shaped his professional identit y. Alison went back to Lawrence’s data and pulled e xamples that spoke to Lawrence’s personal and social histor y, the identities of his students, and relevant ped agogical choices. By thinking through these examples and cases, Lawrence was able to articulate his interest in the role of language and translanguaging in his mathematics cla ssroom. From there, Alison and Lawrence explored th e research literature on translanguaging, considered how it related to their examples and cases, and discussed how they might define translanguaging in the context of  Lawrence’s mathematics classroom. Thinking through examples and cases offered Lawrence and Alison an iterative and collaborative context where they could establish which data was of most interest to them and develop  a shared language and vision of translanguaging in mathematics education and why it was important for Lawrence’s instruction. Iterating through data in this way afforded Lawrenc e and Alison the opportunity to bring theory and practice together to deepen their understanding of how Lawrence’s classroom noticing supported his translanguaging practice. Again, Lawrence and Aliso n nego tiated their different roles to document and characterize Lawrence’s noticing for equity. Specif ically, Lawrence responded to prompts to further hi s analysis of how he noticed students whose learning would be augmented by translanguaging, and how his personal history shaped how he understood the experiences of multilingual learners in his classrooms. These prompts opened space for informal reflections on the cases that then ser ved as discussion points between the scholar and te acher. This iterative process enabled the research partners to adopt their mutually beneficial roles of the teacher narrating their practice through the examples and cases, and the sc holar representing these narrations in the form of academic artifacts, such as conference proposals, presentations, and manuscripts. Narrating experiences of classrooms engaged in part icular justice-centering practices came naturally to teachers in PLCs on the Equity Team. Each PLC consisted of 4-6 teachers, giving each teacher many opportunities to narrate their own classroom experiences as they related to the PAR both verbally and in writing. In  so doing, their experiences served as examples and provided feedback to one ano ther, deepening and enriching the written representations of the just practices and their impacts. This access to diverse cases and representations grew larger when the network of PLCs met to offer updates and p rovide each other feedback. When teachers were prep aring a handout for outside-of-network teachers attending a project workshop, they  reached out to the full network to find complementary examples of a particular justice-centered practice being used in other disciplines o r grade levels. For example, in year thre e, Tia created a one-pager on “Modeling with Equity” outlining how to su pport students in modeling a scientific phenomenon as it takes place within unjust social contexts, providin g scaffolds for events happening at different time scales, the scientific phenomenon (e.g. spreading COVID) happen ing at a much shorter timescale that led to the predominance  of people of Color working at the front lines. In preparation for a conference presentation, Tia networked with a  teacher from another PLC who provided additional c ontext in the form of a model representing the Radium Girls. In providing and comparing the contexts, Tia and the teacher identified common model components and questions that were designed to push student thinking toward a deeper understanding of s ocial justice. Collecting diverse cases from projec t-wide teachers allowed Tia to think collaboratively about modeling with equity across diverse contexts.

## Discussion

This study offers valuable insights into how collaborative cowriting with scholars can result in amplifying teacher voice in the generation of knowledge that is richly  informed by teacher practice while also supporting  teacher learning through their development of equity-focused instructional practices. By using cowriting as a participatory research method, the projects examined in this study effectively showed how teachers and researchers could work together to create and theorize equitable STEM educ ation practices in real time. By examining two NSF-funded projects, we identified four key practices: gaining  consensus on equity, generating representations, t hinking through examples and cases, and engaging in cycles of feedback and accountability. Teacher agency to advance equity in action One of the most significant contributions of this s tudy is its demonstration of how RPPs enhance teach er agency and prioritize equity.   This study strongly reinforces the argument for cen tering teacher agency in collaborative research, as emphasized by Priestley et al. (2015). The collaborative process of defining and refining what counts as equity in and through mathematics and science cl assrooms enabled teachers to identify and confront systemic inequities in STEM education through the developmen t of justice-oriented pedagogies. Through reflective dialogue and iterative collaboration, teachers deve loped their visions and voices through the construc tion of narratives. For instanc e, John’s reflective journey in challenging deficit  narratives in mathematics echoes Gutiérrez and Jurow’s (2016) call for participatory  frameworks that treat equity as a dynamic, co-constructed process. These efforts also align with Coburn and P enuel’s (2016) emphasis on reciprocity in RPPs, whi ch shifts the balance of power toward teachers and fosters shared knowledge production. Moreover, the study exemplifies Philip et al.’s (20 22) argument for disrupting hierarchical knowledge production by positioning teachers as co-creators of educational innovation. Teachers were not merely consumers of research but active contributors, shaping and in terrogating justice-oriented frameworks. This participatory model reflects a broader trend in equity-focused research to democratize expertise and integ rate community knowledge into systemic reform (Billings & Linn, 2024; Ghiso et al., 2019). Cowriting as a tool for learning The cowriting process provided a structured and iterative approach for teachers to engage deeply with theoretical and practical aspects of equity-focused instruction. Scaffolding tools, such as ref lective prompts, templates, and visual representations, enabled teachers to articul ate and refine their understanding of equity. These  tools mirror the co-design practices that Severance et al. (2016) empha sized, which empower teachers to adapt research findings to their specific classroom contexts. For instance, Alyssa’s focus on love in mathematics ped agogy evolved through iterative reflection and data repre sentation, illustrating how cowriting fosters both professional growth and actionable insights. This process connects to Matuk et al.’s (2016) findings on how collaborative tools inspire reflective practices and creativity. Additi onally, the study addresses barriers identified by Johnson and Steele (2024), who noted that structural challenges  often limit teachers’ participation in academic wr iting. By integrating accessible scaffolding, the study mitigated thes e challenges, creating pathways for teachers to contribute to equity-focused scholarship.

Cowriting also facilitated knowledge mobilization b y translating complex theoretical constructs into practical tools and approaches. The use of visual s caffolds such as annotated slide decks and flowchar ts allowed teachers to represent their learning in actionable formats, fostering cross-network collaboration and dissemination. This aligns with Shim and Thompson’s (2022) emphasis on professional learning communities (PLCs) as vehicles for collective knowledge building and systemic improvement. Equity as a highly contextualized process The study’s iterative process of clarifying equity in STEM education underscores the importance of mov ing beyond surface-level interpretations. Teachers’ engagement with transformational equity aims, such as expanding what counts as science and addressing social justic e through STEM, reflects Philip and Azevedo’s (2017) framework for equity in science education enacted and studied the very real contexts of everyday classrooms. By interrogating their positionalities and practices, teachers like John and Lawrence exemplified how equity-focused pedagogy evolves through critical reflection and collaboration. This process also connects to culturally sustaining pedagogies highlighted in the literature. For instance, Alyssa’s exploration of love in mathematics paralle ls Billings and Linn’s (2024) case study on integra ting Henrietta Lacks’ story into science curri cula, where equity was centered through culturally responsive frameworks. Similarly, Lawrence’s focus on translan guaging in mathematics bridges Gutiérrez and Jurow’ s

(2016) emphasis on embedding equity within specific  cultural and linguistic contexts. Thes e examples

demonstrate how teachers can and do contextualize e quity within their unique instructional environment s, advancing justice-oriented practices that resonate with their students’ lived experiences and in so doing, generate important insights for the field.

## Limitations and future directions

We recognize that this study is not without its lim itations. We identified four routine activities of cowriting that were generative for teacher learning, with a limite d number of cases. Future analysis will examine how  this framework can become useful to study teacher learning through other types and contexts of collaborative research. Additional inquiry into how these routine activities support teacher inquiry and learning can provide further insight into how to design and organize collaborative resea rch to mutually transform teaching practice and scholar ly activity. A second limitation is the continued press by scholars for the production of academic artifacts. Teachers’ creation of alternative representations for knowled ge dissemination invites the field  to imagine new types of resources designed by and for teachers. Teachers invite us to begin to imagine how to represent and communicate not only what was learned but to also maintain the cyclical, iterative, and nonlinear nature of learning.
