# FLIP: Tabletop Role-Play Game for AI Ethics Education through Cognitive Dissonance

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study developed a tabletop role-playing game (TRPG), “FLIP,” for AI ethics education by engaging players in the ethical reasoning surrounding “Killer Robots.” Designed as a dialogic space, the game encourages players to explore diverse moral perspectives through cognitive dissonance. Specific game mechanics, such as ethical dilemmas and role conflicts, were incorporated to provoke cognitive dissonance and deepen ethical reasoning. Fifteen undergraduate students participated in the gameplay, and their in-game discourse was analyzed to unpack ethical reasoning. The analysis of their dialogues showed that the game effectively engaged players in understanding the complexity of AI ethics issues by sharing and challenging ethical judgments and arguments through collective reasoning. Overall, this study highlights the potential of TRPGs as a promising approach to AI ethics education.

## Introduction

The rapid adoption of artificial intelligence (AI) across various fields has brought numerous ethical challenges to our society and daily life.  For example, while deepfake technology has reduced content production costs, it can be misused to spread false information and compromi se personal privacy (Li & Wan, 2023). Likewise, deb ates surrounding Killer Robots (Taylor, 2021) show the c omplexity of AI ethics.  The international community has been actively discussing and formulating principles  for the ethical use of AI (OECD, 2024). Concurrently, it has become increasingly important for the public to understand the ethical impact of AI, emphasizing the urgent need for AI ethics education.

Currently, AI ethics education resources such as books and videos often explain the terminologies in AI ethics principles and emphasize the normative importance of following the guidelines (Kim & Jeon et al., 2023). Such principle-based approaches may not adequately address the com plexity of AI ethical issues. R apidly evolving nature of AI technologies lead s to continuously shifting definitions and understand ings of the same issues at different stages.  Furthermore, since AI ethical issues involve diverse stakeholders (Akinrinola et al., 2024), individuals with differing moral foundations may interpret the same ethical issue differently (Haidt, 2013). Consequentially, ethical dilemmas often lack consensus, as seen in d etermining accountability in accidents involving autonomous vehicles.

When people face a complex ethical issue, individual moral judgment tends to be biased  due to the reliance on intuition rather than reasoning. To facilitate improved moral judgment and reasoning, it is essential to provide situations where diverse values collide  (Haidt, 2013; Mercier, 2011). In group reasoning, people experience cognitive dissonance to justify their choices or positions (Kim, 2011). For this reason, we  argue that AI ethics education should move beyond simply deliv ering ethical principles and provide learning environments that encourage the sharing of diverse moral judgmen ts and reasoning. This study, therefore,  aims to design a tabletop role-playing game (TRPG) about Killer Robots as a dialogic space where participants with diverse roles and moral values can reason about their judgments through dialogue with other players, thereby deepening their understanding of the complexity of AI ethical issues  and enhancing moral judgments. The study employed a qualitative approach to analyze the dialogues in the gameplays to address the following questions: RQ1. How did the players’ interactions show a deeper understanding of AI ethics issues rela ted to Killer Robots?

RQ2. To what extent did players experience cognitive dissonance during the game play?

## Theoretical framework

Understanding the complexity of AI ethical issues AI ethical issues are usually dilemmas where many stakeholders’ moral values and interests collide. In this study, we used the Critical Systems Heuristics (CSH) and Moral Foundations Theory (MFT) as theoretical frameworks to understand the complexity of ethical issues. Firstly, CSH is a framework developed to enhance critical thinking in social planning by giving heuristic support through structured boundary questions. To understand the “systems” in our society as groups of interrelated or interacting elements forming collective entities (Arnold & Wade, 2015), critical judgment is required to determine what should be considered relevant within the system, a process known as “boundary judgments ” (Ulrich, 1996). CSH categorizes boundary issues into four types: motiva tion, power, knowledge, and legitimacy. Each type is further divided into three boundary category questions. For example, the motivation boundary explores (1) the beneficiary, a sking whose interests are served, (2) the purpose, identifying the desired outcomes, and (3) the measure of improvement, determining how success is defined. These questions serve as a tool for boundary judgments, which identify and analyze stakeholders, their concerns, and the potential conflicts or challenges arising from these concerns (Ulrich, 2005). CSH has been applied to better understand the ethical issues in socio-technical problems and political decision-making (Raza, 202 1), which align with the complex considerations required for addressing AI-related issues. Haidt’s (2013) Moral Foundations Theory (MFT) is a framewor k that explores the diversity of moral principles underlying people’s moral judgments.  MFT identifies six moral foundations people have: care (avoiding physical, and emotional damage to others), equality (considering equal treatment and outcome s for others), proportionality (emphasizing rewards in pr oportion to one’s contribution), loyalty (cooperati ng with ingroups and competing with outgroups), authority (having deference toward authorities), and purity (avoiding degradation) (Atari et al., 2023). For example, individuals who value “proportionality ” may view the system of punishment as natural, while those who prioritize “care” might feel discomfort with the retributive nature of such penalties (Haidt, 2013). Since various moral foundations often lead to different perspectives, exposure to different perspectives is  c rucial to analyze ethical dilemmas. Prior s tudies have indicated that MFT can be a  useful framework for learners to understand and reflect on the different viewpoints on ethical issues (Bezalel, 2020). Facilitating cognitive dissonance for moral reasoning We adapted Reeve’s (2008) model of the Cognitive Dissonance Process  to the context of ethical issues to demonstrate that eliciting cognitive dissonance could effectively facilitate moral reasoning processes in AI ethics education (see Figure 1). According to Haidt (2001), moral reasoning is a post hoc process that happens after the moral judgment and does not arise automatically without e ffort. Moral reasoning emerges as individuals engage in social interactions where they are called upon to justify their judgments to others (Haidt, 2013). In this transition from moral judgment to moral reasoning, cognitive dissonance can be intentionally created to prompt individuals to justify and reason their judgments. Cognitive dissonance refers to the psychologically uncomfortable tension arising from between conflicting cognitions. Individuals are strongly motivated to resolve the d issonance and implement strategies to escape from the cognitive dissonance status  (Reeve, 20 08). In the context of moral education, cognitive dissonance is viewed as the motivator to foster learners’ inquiry and curio sity about ethical issues and facilitate their moral reasoning to resolve the dissonance (Kim, 2011). Reeve (20 08) suggested four situational events that can arouse cognitive d issonance. First, cognitive dissonance occurs when an individual must choose between two or more equally valued options. After the choice, the unselected option may still seem appealing, cre ating dissonance. Ethical dilemmas, therefore, are frequently used in moral education to promote cognitive dissonance and facilitate moral reasoning (Kim & Lee et al., 2023). Second, cognitive dissonance arises when one’s actions lack sufficient justification and are unable to explain their behavior. Third, cognitive dissonance can happen when externa l pressure forces an individual to engage in extreme or effortful actions. Finally, cognitive di ssonance occurs when one is exposed tonew informat ion that conflicts with their existing beliefs. These situational events were used as a framework to induce participants’ cognitive dissonance in our game design.

To provide a safe environment for such social interactions to naturally occur, we chose to design a TRPG  as a form of moral practice game  (Wright et al., 2020)  where multiple players are engaged with imaginary characters in an imaginary environment (Fine, 1983). An initial step of the TRPG design was to select a theme that could provide players with diverse intuitions and judgments, as well as opportunities to experience cognitive dissonance that prompts  ethical reasoning. We selected  Autonomous Weapon Systems (AWS), commonly referred to as “Killer Robots” as the main AI ethical issue of our game design. AWS is defined as “a category of weapons that can (1) ‘select’ a human target and ‘decide’ to engage it, and (2) execute this attack without human intervention” (Solovyeva & Hynek, 2023, p. 2550). AWS brings both potential opportunities and significant ethical dilemmas. The ethical concerns surrounding Killer Robots prim arily focus on the morality of allowing machines to make life-and-death decisions without human oversight (Umbrello e t al., 2020) and the challenges of assigning accountability (Taylor, 2021).

The original CSH questions were adapted to fit the Killer Robots context. To foster deeper interaction among participants, we added ‘action prompts’ to the Dialogue Cards, inspired. These action prompts, based on the dialogic interaction moves of opening, broadening, and deepening (Palmgren-Neuvonen et al., 2021), were designed to scaffold discussions by encouraging the sharing, challenging, and reflec tion of diverse perspectives. We designed a game-specific action prompt: “Talk with someone who disagrees with your opinion.” Phase 3: Diving-in In this phase,  players are expected to dive deeper into ethical dilemmas u sing MFT Cards. These cards include six specific questions and ethical issues related to Killer Robo ts, each based on one of the six moral foundations in MFT. Players with different moral values and assigned roles may interpret and approach the issues from diverse perspectives. This phase exposes players to varied intuitions and judgments, encouraging interactions and cognitive dissonance. By challenging each other’s a rguments, players are guided toward reasoning and forming more reasonable judgments  (Haidt, 2001). To further enrich discussions, some Dialogue Cards include action prompts that encourage players to engage more actively. Additionally, Scenario Cards are introduced during this phase to activate new intuitions and provide fresh perspectives. These cards present specific scenario s, some of which support the development of Killer Robots, whi le others argue against it. This design exposes participants to diverse viewpoints, fostering thought-provoking discussions. Phase 4: Decision-making After discussing all the Dialogue Cards, participants move to the final phase of decision-making. The two Ending Cards correspond to the participants’ different decisions: allowing or banning. Once an agreement is reached, participa nts choose the Ending Card aligned with their decision to reflect on the consequences of their decision ten y ears into the future. This reflective process provi des closure to the game discussion, allowing participants to consider the broader implications of their collective decision. Triggers for cognitive dissonance The game introduces complex ethical dilemmas to create cognitive dissonance. As players share their perspectives during interactions, the exchange of potentially co ntradictory information provides additional trigger s for cognitive dissonance (Reeve, 2008). This iterative process of cognitive dissonance and reasoning enhances the quality of interactions, fostering a higher level of moral reasoning. Additionally, Identity Cards are expected to induce cognitive dissonance. Players are randomly assigned an identity (role), reflecting stakeholders and cou ntries involved in the Killer Robots argument in th e real world. These identity assignments are designed to represen t players with diverse judgments and perspectives and foster the likelihood of conflicting information based on different roles. Players are randomly assigned role s and nationalities separately, which may lead to cogniti ve dissonance when they must decide whether to app roach an issue from their assigned country’s standpoint or t heir positions. Besides, players’ real-life values may conflict with the perspectives expected of their game roles. For instance, a real-life pacifist with a core belief in avoiding violence and promoting global peace may choose to b an the development of Killer Robots. However, when assigned the role of Minister of National Defense i n the game, which demands prioritizing national int erests and military strategies, they may experience cognitive dissonance due to the conflict between their person al values and the responsibilities of their in-game role.

## Methods

The study involved 15 undergraduate students in Kor ea who were divided into three groups of five members: Group A (five male computing majors),  Group B (five female social sciences majors), and Group C (5 male, National Military Acad emy). The participants  were informed of the recording procedures  during gamep lay to ensure clarity and transparency in data collection.  Their in-game dialogues were analyzed unpack ethical reasoning.

Two researchers first analyzed the in-game discourse from one of the three groups using MAXQDA software to develop the coding scheme. For RQ1, a deductive approach was employed, guided by the game design objectives and the content of the cards. The discussions that occurred concerning specific cards were coded under the corresponding card names. Additionally, we identified discourse where players engaged in discussions beyond the prompts provided by the cards. For RQ2, we began with deductive coding based on the theoretical framework of cognitive dissonance (Reeve, 2008) to identify discourse where players showed cognitive dissonance and its triggers. Subsequently, an inductive coding method was applied to further categorize the identified discourse into two main themes: (1) expressions of cognitive disso nance and (2) triggers of cognitive dissonance.  We coded these expressions that extend beyond the game’s set ting, introducing knowledge, analogies, examples, o r cases from real-world contexts into two categories: Knowledge and E veryday analogies and cases.  T he two coders discussed and finalized the coding scheme  (see Table 3). This coding scheme was then applied to analyze data from the remaining two groups. The two coders independen tly coded the data and subsequently reached a consensus on any discrepancies.

## Findings and discussions

RQ1. Deepening understanding of AI ethics issues The analysis of in-play dialogue data  shows both alignment with the intention of our game design and players’ engagement in understanding ethical issues of Killer Robots. We used the “Code Coverage” function in MAXQDA to calculate the proportion of coded discour se out of the total dialogues among the three group s. As shown in Figure 4, player dialogues were  largely aligned with the topics in CSH Cards  and MFT Cards. Specifically, CSH and MFT together accounted for 56.1% in Group A, 70.9% in Group B, and 81.1% in Grou p C, confirming that most dialogues followed intended game design. Data categorized as “Others” (48.2% in Group A, 12.9% in Group B, and 48.3% in Group C) primarily included dialogues (1) for understanding the game setting, game rules, and card content, and (2) on ethical issues not explicitly designed within the game. The Code Coverage analysis of the proportion of discourse contributed  by each role indicates a relatively balanced distribution (see Table 4). Within each group, contributions from dif ferent roles remained fairly similar. In Group A, t he contributions ranged from 15% to 31%; in Group B, f rom 10% to 33%; and in Group C, from 14% to 26%. T his suggests that discourse was evenly distributed among players in each group, with no single role disproportionately dominating the conversation.

We found that certain moral foundations such as Care and Loyalty were often discussed even before playing the corresponding cards  in all groups. Participants initially raised concerns about the Killer Robots’ potential harm to humans and whether such development would raise the problem of loyalty. For example, Group B discussed the CSH Card with the topic “Legitimacy” from the perspective of the potential ethical harm caused by robot technology to humans. This implies that the card design created an open and expansive dialogical space that encouraged players to spontaneously inquire ab out additional ethical considerations beyond the de sign provided. Most discussions aligned closely with the  game design while participants also raised additio nal questions and engaged in broader discussions. These  findings suggest that the card design effectively guided participants while fostering an open and expansive environment for deeper exploration of AI ethics iss ues. Notably, despite initial differences in opinion, al l three groups ultimately reached the consensus to prohibit the development of Killer Robots globally.

Diving into cognitive dissonance To answer RQ2, we focus ed on when and why players experienced cognitive disso nance during the gameplay. Among the three groups, cognitive dissonance occurr ed over twenty times, and the analysis of code relations showed that cognitive dissonance appeared across di fferent game phases. In the game-play dialogues, the main triggers we coded for cognitive dissonance included  (a) new information that challenges others’ belief s, (b) reminders of other players’ roles, and (c) self-triggered role conflict in the game. Some role conflicts were self-trigged and caused cognitive dissonance. In Group A, for example, the FAI Chair from Country X experienc ed a clear self-triggered role conflict when discussing who would b e the beneficiaries of the development of Killer Robots and whether to support its development. He struggle d with whether to approach the issue from the perspective of his country’s interest as a technolo gically advanced nation or from his position as the  chair of an International Peace Organization. This inte rnal conflict triggered cognitive dissonance, highl ighting the tension between the role and nationality in the game context.

Throughout the game, the interactions among players with different in-game roles and perspectives about various ethical issues continuously challenged each other’s  beliefs, triggering cognitive dissonance and reaso ning processes, and deepening their understanding of AI ethical issues. For instance, in the negotiation phase of Group A, the FAI Chair began to reconsider his decision a fter other participants mentioned non-combat uses for Killer Robots, such as combating terrorism, and eventually shifted to support its development. Connecting to real-world contexts Players frequently used expressions that are connected to real-world contexts, spanning all sections of gameplay. These expressions were categorized into two types: general knowledge and everyday analogies and cases. First general knowledge, such as nuclear development, nati onal power dynamics, and gun legalization in the Un ited States, appeared over 50 times across the three groups. Next, everyday analogies, like running competitions, were used to draw parallels to the competition in Killer Robots development. One player explained that just as a stronger runner will eventually win despite everyone startin g from the same line, countries with superior techn ical capabilities are likely to widen the gap in Killer Robots development. In particular, everyday analogies and cases served as tools for participants to comprehend unfa miliar information and clarify complex problems. Mo reover, real-world connections were closely tied to  the expressions of cognitive dissonance. Participants t ended to use real-world connections to reason their judgments when ex periencing cognitive dissonance. S uch connections provided participants with novel perspectives  for prompting reasoning and facilitating deeper engagem ent with AI ethical dilemmas.

## Discussion and conclusion

This study developed a  TRPG, FLIP, for AI ethics education. This study selected the ethical dilemma of Killer Robots to provide a dialogic space where players co uld engage with complex ethical issues, encounter d iverse intuitions and perspectives, and continuously reason about their judgments through dialogue. Overall, the analysis of the in-game dialogue shows that the game created a dialogi c space that encouraged moral reasoning and in-depth discussions among players. Participants actively engaged with the game play and extended their discussions to additional ethical dilemmas, showing their deep involvement and understanding of AI-related ethical issues. The game also successfully elicited cognitive dissonance among players, which was triggered by new information challenging existing beliefs, role reminders, and self-triggered role conflicts. These triggers prompted  players to reason their judgments and address conflicting pers pectives, facilitating moral reasoning processes. M oreover, players frequently used real-world connections in their dialogues as tools to clarify complex problems and reason through unfamiliar concepts. These references also served as effective triggers for eliciting cognitive dissonance, further enhancing the reasoning process. This align s with “analogical reasoning”, a common approach in ethics (Spielthenner, 2014), highlighting the game’s role as a facilitator of moral reasoning. Some limitations of this study should be acknowledg ed. First, the small sample size consisting of university students may restrict the generalizability of the findings. Future research with a larger and more diverse participant pool would offer deeper insights into t he effectiveness of the proposed approach. Additionally, since this study was conducted in South Korea, the discou rse data may have been influenced by sociocultural factors such as ongoing military tensions with North Korea and the mandatory military service system for all m ale citizens. These contextual factors may have shaped participants' moral reasoning and ethical perspectives in ways that differ from those in other cultural settings. Future studies could extend these findings to different sociocultural contexts to examine potential similarities and diff erences in moral discourse. Furthermore, the game design focuses on the specific topic of " Killer Robots" within AI ethics. Its scope could be broadened to include other moral issues in AI development to improve its applicability and educational potential. By addressing these limitations, future research could further explore the potential of TRPGs as an innovative and interactive approach to understand complex ethical dilemmas in AI ethics education.
