# Modeling-Based Learning in Pre-School Science: Affordances of Different Types of Children-Constructed Models

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Despite its proven added value, Modeling-based Learning(MbL) in science is not commonly incorporated into early grades science education. Following a growing body of research on using MbL in early grades, this multi-case study seeks to provide detailed descriptions of the implementation of MbL with 3 groups of pre-K children engaged in the study of three different phenomena, taught by 3 different teachers participating in a professional development program of pre-school science education. Findings across the different cases suggest that participating pre-school children were successfully engaged in authentic MbL activities and developed several different types of models using knowledge and experiences, as well as a variety of features of both analogical and mechanistic reasoning. I use this evidence to argue that (a)different MbL tools may afford different learning opportunities; (b)pre-school children have modeling resources that can use to utilize different tools using MbL in natural ways of young children learning (e.g.,playing).

## Introduction

Within the larger context of inquiry-based learning, models and the process of modeling are considered integral parts of science learning, providing students oppor tunities to understand and explain natural phenomen a (e.g., Gilbert & Justi, 2016). The authors of the Next Generation Science Standards (NGSS Lead States, 2013) define modeling as a practice that involves the developmen t, revision, use, and evaluation of models aiming a t predicting and explaining physical phenomena (Appen dix F, p. 6).  Externalized through various means a nd representation tools, a student-generated model alo ng with the process of its development becomes a le arning tool enabling students to understand the mechanism underlying natural phenomena (Schwarz et al., 2009). This helps students develop concrete representations of their understanding of a structure, a process, or a  natural system, as well as to use models as tools for engag ing in scientific reasoning. Recognizing the proces s of modeling as a key component of learning in science,  NGSS Lead States (2013) suggests that learning sci ence should include developing an understanding of natur al phenomena through the construction and refinemen t of models (Louca & Zacharia, 2015), as well as learnin g the processes of developing and refining those mo dels (White & Frederiksen, 1998). In this respect, model s and modeling are considered integral parts of sci ence learning, primarily because they provide the means for students to represent, describe, examine, under stand, predict and explain natural phenomena (e.g., Justi & Gilbert, 2016).

## Theoretical framework

Modeling-based Learning (MbL) has been widely advocated as an approach that meaningfully engages students in authentic practices of learning of and about sci ence (e.g., Louca et al, 2011; Louca & Zacharia, 20 15). Despite this emphasis, only a limited number of studies have investigated MbL among K-6 students (e.g., Louca, 2021). As part of their efforts to enhance our unde rstanding of MbL with young learners, Louca et al (2011) developed a framework for analyzing student-constru cted models of natural phenomena. Aligned with othe r research on MbL in science (e.g., Clement, 1989; Gi lbert, 1995; Glynn & Duit, 1995; Ingham & Gilbert, 1991; Penner et al, 1997; Penner, 2001), the Louca et al framework includes five elements of student-constru cted models: representation of (i) the phenomenon’s phys ical objects, (ii) the phenomenon’s physical entiti es, (iii) the physical processes, (iv) the interactions among physical objects, entities, and processes, and (v) the accuracy of the phenomenon depiction. This framework provides a coherent system for analyzing and evaluating studentconstructed models based on their structural compon ents, it can capture the model’s sophistication acr oss the 5 structural components, and it may provide a picture of model progress over time during instruction. Louca et al’s (2011) application of their framework  suggested, for example, that the students in the study tended to move from simplistic representations of physical entities to more sophisticated ones. Further, in the case of the representation of object behavior(s) elements, the findings suggested that students’ initial models used more non-causal or semi-causal representations of the objects’ behavior, as opposed to their fina l models, in which they used more causal representation of the objects’ behavior. Such a framework is particularly useful for providing a picture of how students’ models, as  well as their learning, progress (e.g., Lehrer & S chauble, 2006; Lesh et al., 2000; Metcalf et al., 2000; Wind schitl et al. 2008). Moreover, the framework reveal s ICLS2022 Proceedings 75 © ISLS differences among different modeling tools, which could enable teachers and curriculum developers to select the more appropriate environment for the construction of models for a specific physical phenomenon. Models as student representations of their understa nding of a structure, process, or system in science, are directly related to the representation of causa l, coherent explanations of the target phenomenon, that is, causal mechanisms. Ke and Schwarz (2019) explain th at “developing and revising models that address the mechanism of phenomena lies at the core of the scie ntific endeavor” (p. 183). In this respect, success ful modeling should result in models that include the underlying mechanism of the phenomenon or system. Russ et al (2008) developed a scheme of 7 components of mec hanistic reasoning. Those components include (i) descriptions of the target phenomenon (what we see happening), (ii) identification of the set-up condi tions that are necessary for the phenomenon to happen, (iii) identification of entities (conceptual or real objects that play a particular role in the phenomenon, (iv) identificat ion of the entities activities, (v) identification of the entities properties, (vi) identification of the entities org anization, and (vii) chaining (talking about what h ad happened prior to a phenomenon and what will happen). Limite d research on MbL with young learners, however, has highlighted that they are able to engage in MbL, de velop a number of different types of models, using various features of analogical and mechanistic reasoning (Louca, 2020). MbL also involves analogical reasoning, namely a way of thinking that relies upon an analogy (May et al., 2006). Individuals use analogical reasoning to present commonalities between two phenomena or systems to support predictions or explanations for one of the systems while drawing information from the second s ystem, which usually is more familiar to them. In this sen se, analogical reasoning is an important ingredient for causal and scientific thinking because it supports the tra nsfer of knowledge from a well-known or better-know n phenomenon or system to a less well-known one. For example, in an attempt to explain how the heart wor ks, individuals may use as an analogy a pump. In this v iew, expert mechanistic reasoning (Ke & Schwarz, 20 19; Russ et al., 2008) involves the generation, use, and evaluation of analogies (May et al, 2006) because analogies can be valuable tools for constructing one’s own un derstanding in a variety of contexts. Unlike most o f the research about analogies (focused almost exclusivel y on their pedagogical value in curriculum material s and teacher explanations for promoting conceptual chang e in students (May et al, 2006)), we are interested  in students’ abilities for analogical reasoning that i ncludes (i) the generation of analogies (that inclu des a target case (unknown), a base case (known) and a relation that maps elements from one case to the other), (ii) the evaluation of the validity of an analogy and subseq uent refinement (that includes identification of th e key features of an analogy and its limitations), (iii) the use of analogies to create new knowledge by mak ing new inferences about the target case and creating abstr act generalizations and (iv) the use of analogies t o communicate ideas in science to others (Clement, 1998; May et al, 2006). Purpose This is a qualitative multi-case research study seeking to provide detailed descriptions of the different ways preK children may engage in MbL using a variety of mod eling tools and representation media as well as the  types and characteristics of the models they constructed. With an emphasis on the representation of the mechanism of natural phenomena, in this study, I analyze evidence from 3 case studies to comparatively describe the different ways that pre-school children may use different mod eling and representation tools while learning in sc ience through MbL.  I use this evidence to argue that (a)  different modeling/representation tools may afford different learning opportunities; (b) pre-school children have modeling resources (Louca & Zacharia, 2019; Louca, 2020) that can use in order to utilize different modeling /representation tools using MbL in natural ways of young children learning (e.g., playing); and (c) educator s may find it productive to choose among these tool s depending on children’s prior modeling experiences and the mechanism(s) to be represented.

## Methods, data sources, and analyses

Following a multi-case study approach (Yin, 2017), this study involved the analysis of data from 3 dif ferent groups of pre-school children (age range: 4,5-6) in  Cyprus taught by 3 different teachers. All 3 teach ers participated in the same professional development (PD) program organized by the author over the durati on of a school year. The cases selected for this study were  the teachers’ first attempt to implement an MbL-in-science unit. Teachers developed their modeling unit from s cratch after they participated in PD sessions about  inquirybased learning and teaching science in preschool, and MbL in pre-school science. For the purpose of this study, all lessons (about 40-minute duration) in each case  study were videotaped and transcribed for analysis. Children's work (models) was also collected for analysis. Since this study involved children aged 5-6, I adopted the approach of drawing-based modeling as used by various researchers in the field (Ainsworth, Pra in, & Tytler, 2011; Brooks, 2009) in which children  use annotated drawings to represent their models. Thus, when in this study children created drawings, 3-D structures ICLS2022 Proceedings 76 © ISLS or used gestural/bodily representations as their re presentations of the phenomenon under study (Gilber t, 2004), they also accompanied their models with oral descriptions or annotations of their model. ## Findings

Below I briefly present an overview of each of the three cases and then turn into a discussion about c ommon emerging themes from all the 3 case studies.

## Case study 1: Plant parts and their functions

For the first lesson, children working in 4 groups identified similarities and differences among sever al wild plants they collected. In a whole classroom discuss ion that followed, they agreed that all their colle cted plants shared the same 4 parts: roots, stem, leaves, and f lowers. They also identified that despite those sim ilarities, their plants were different in the type of roots th ey carried, the shape and the size of their leaves,  and the color, the size, and the petal shape of the flowers. In th e second lesson, the teacher asked children to draw  their findings in order to show them to the children of t he next-door classroom. Each group chose a differen t part of the plants, which they drew independently and put t ogether at the end, making a large poster (model 1)  as depicted in Figure 1a. At the end of the session, c hildren compared their poster with one of the real wild plants they collected, identifying a series of disadvantag es, mostly related to the way they depicted the inf ormation they knew about all the plants into a single drawin g. This, children suggested was helpful for letting  others know about that information provided, but on the ot her hand, it was misleading in terms of what a sing le plant looked like. Thus, children spent lesson 3 revising  their first model. Their teacher, however, propose d to work on a 3-dimensional structure as their second representation, using materials that they were familiar with (Figure 1b). This time, each child developed their own mode l. The comparative evaluation of their models ident ified a different number of limitations, this time focused on the ways they represented each part of the wild plant and the selection of the materials used for the representation of the plant’s parts. In lesson 4, each child worked on a revision of their model, still using 3-dimensional materials. The lesson took place after an investiga tion of the process of transpiration, using experiences and col lecting evidence from an experiment carried out in their schoolyard. This time, their models were substantia lly revised (see Figure 1c) utilizing materials for  their representations that had implications for the plant  part functions such as roots showing uptake of wat er, leaves represented in a way that implied the process of wa ter retention and transpiration, and the stem as a vehicle for water transportation from roots to the leaves. During the evaluation of their models, children agreed that despite their models' revised representation of the plants’  parts, their representations contained little info rmation about ICLS2022 Proceedings 77 © ISLS the process that takes place inside the plants’ parts. Thus, they collectively decided to develop a new model, that would contain information about those processes (e. g., water uptake, water transportation, transpirati on), and they discussed how 3-dimensional models had limitat ions in representing this information. Thus, during  lesson 5 they decided to revert to a single, collective paper-and-pencil model, having their teacher noting in words their explanations about their drawings of the processes (Figure 1d). ## Case study 2: Solution of substances in water

The first lesson of case study 2 focused on describing how different substances behave in water in an attempt to operationally define the phenomenon: some substances dissolved in the water (e.g., instant coffee, flo ur), while others did not (e.g., small rocks, beans). In the s econd lesson, children collected data from the foll owing experiment: In a clear glass of water, they placed a drop of brown food color at the bottom of the glass. Every 2 minutes, children recorded on a piece of paper what they observed in the glass. After collecting the observations, they gathered into the whole class setting, and the  teacher displayed children’s drawings in the seque nce they ICLS2022 Proceedings 78 © ISLS were recorded on the whiteboard to talk about what they had recorded, specifically focusing the discus sion on the changes in the water. Through this discussion, children reached a consensus that by the last drawi ng, the water became brownish, although it was not as brown as the initial drop of the food color that they had recorded in their first drawing. The teacher decided that th is drawing could play the role of children’s first model and used it to ask for interpretations of the data collected to be incorporated in a revised model by asking: “Suppose you are in the water. What would you see happening?” Children went back to their group tables, were provided a clean sheet of paper, and asked to draw that stor y. This drawing was the children’s model of the phe nomenon under study. In the third lesson, children presente d their 18 models to the rest of the class, talked about them, and discussed their differences and similarities. Figure 2 presents 3 examples of children’s models that differ in the way they represented the phenomenon. The first one is a sequential approach, in which children foc used on presenting in their paper-and-pencil model a sequence of drawings in an effort to represent sequential scenes of the phenomenon. A second type of model depiction in cluded all these scenes of the phenomenon happening over time in a single drawing. A third type of depi ction simply provided the final scene of the phenom enon, with the brown “pieces” randomly distributed in the water. This third type of model does not include o r refer to any mechanism of the phenomenon under study. More d etails about this case can be found in Louca (2020). Due to time limitations, children in this case stud y did not work on further revisions of their models. They simply talked about the similarities and difference s of their models, in an effort to identify the adv antages and disadvantages of their representations.

## Case study 3: Shadow formation

Case study’s 3 lesson 1 started with children inves tigating the conditions under which a shadow is cre ated. To resolve this, children designed an experiment durin g which they found that in order to create a shadow  they needed a light source, an object, and a screen to g et the shadow on, in that order. In the following l esson, children worked on figuring out how to increase the  size of a shadow. They experimented with materials  and identified 2 different ways to change the size of a  shadow. In the 3rd lesson, the teacher asked the c hildren to develop a representation of how they thought the sh adow is created, asking them to reflect on their ex periences from experimenting with shadow in the last two less ons. This resulted in their first model (Figure 3a). During the comparative evaluation of their models, one com mon issue identified was the ways children used the  light rays as a conceptual idea playing a critical role in the process of creating a shadow, as well as the characteristics of the shadow produced. These were extensively discussed and revised in most of the drawings/models 2 during lesson 4 (Figure 3b). In lesson 5, the children along with their teacher got outside their classroom, into an openair patio, which was always windy. The teacher aske d children to think about ways to represent the sha dow formation, as they represented it through their pre vious models. Children had to think/invent ways to represent the various physical objects taking place in the ph enomenon, the physical entities (such as light rays) that are involved, and the interactions between them that re sulted in the physical processes of the phenomenon.  This discussion resulted in their collective model 3 (figure 3c and 3d) in which some children function as the “lightproduction cells”, holding long paper stripes as the light rays. One child was the object for creating  the shadow (wearing a red piece of cloth) and a child wearing a black piece of cloth represented the shadow created. ## Discussion

My purpose in this multi-case study was to provide detailed descriptions of the different ways pre-K c hildren may engage in MbL focusing on the variety of the mo deling tools and representation media used by child ren. Agreeing with prior studies suggesting that pre-sch ool children are able to represent mechanisms under lying natural phenomena (e.g., Louca, 2020), data from th is study suggest that, overall, pre-school children  are also able to use a variety of modeling representation me dia such as paper-and-pencil, 3-dimensional materia ls or physical/body representations to represent that mec hanism. In at least 2 of the cases presented, child ren in the study were able to talk about the possible different ways of representing their models, engaged in a conversation about different ways’ affordances in terms of their  representational power, and collaboratively decide  which way better suited their representational needs. Thu s, I contend that some evidence suggests that child ren as young as pre-school may be able to move from one ty pe of representation to another, with the possibili ty to invoke what Louca & Zacharia (2019) have suggested as modeling resources in order to utilize different modeling/representation tools. While I do not sugge st that these children were experts in MbL, these f indings may further suggest that educators may find it productive to choose among these tools, depending on children’s prior modeling experiences and the types of mechani sm(s) to be represented. Future research should fur ther investigate this direction.

Towards this direction, it is also important to hig hlight children’s abilities not only to use differe nt modeling media, but also to choose among different modeling media, in terms of enabling them to show aspects of the mechanism underlying the phenomenon. Although this needs further investigation, I suggest, it m ight be related to epistemological implications of the MbL as described by Schwartz (2019) in terms of epistem ic considerations within the disciplinary norms of the  scientific community, which includes the nature of  the knowledge provided by the model, the justification of that knowledge, its generalization and the audience of the model. Lastly, I contend that the findings I descri be in this study may prove useful for educators see king to engage pre-school children to MbL in science by helping them choose among different modeling/representation media depending on children’s prior modeling experiences and the mechanism(s) to be represented.
