# AI-Feedback System: Integrating Human-in-the-Loop for Customized Support

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study presents an iterative human-AI collaboration design process for developing an AI-Feedback system aligned with learning theories, providing timely, customized feedback to support teachers and students in developing usable knowledge. The process involved collaboration among teachers, content experts, and learning scientists to refine rubrics and validate AI models for analyzing elementary students’ written responses on complex science tasks—often vague or inconsistent —to identify weaknesses and uncertainty in students’ understanding. Using a multi-agent human-in-the-loop system, we improved analytical accuracy, quantified uncertainty, and generated analytical rationales to guide customized feedback. Analyses of 90 student responses demonstrates an overall accuracy 88%, showing that integrating Generative AI with continuous expert guidance can provide reliable and meaningful feedback, even for lower elementary student writing.

Usable knowledge enables learners to transfer understanding beyond the classroom–applying core ideas to explain phenomena, solve real-world problems, and make informed decisions. These skills are vital for success in a global, future-oriented society and workforce. Since such complex learning does not occur naturally without intentional design, well-designed learning environments  should engage learners with challenging tasks and provide appropriate supports to help them reach optimal learning outcomes (Shin et al, 2025). Feedback refers to information provided to learners  by an agent (e.g., teacher, peer, or AI tool) about their understanding or performance relative to learning goals, with the purpose of helping them close the gap between current and desired performance (Hattie & Timperley, 2007). To be effective, feedback must  align with targeted goals, be informed by accurate analysis of student performance, and be delivered in a timely manner to guide learners toward achieving their goals (Harris, et al, 2023). Recent advances in Generative AI  (GenAI) using large language models  (LLMs) enable real-time, interactive support with the potential to enhance feedback practices and strengthen teaching and learning across K-16 education. We envision AI as a collaborative partner  with researchers, teachers, and learners, sharing tasks that require frequent, immediate attention. To realize this vision, we incorporate Human-in-the-Loop (HITL) approaches–integrating human judgment, expertise, and decision-making–into the development and operation of AI systems for performance analysis and feedback  generation (Kamar, 2016). HITL ensures that AI outputs remain accurate, ethical, and context-sensitive, enhancing the reliability of analyses of learner performance and enabling the generation of customized feedback that aligns with learning goals and responds to indivi dual needs. This study presents an iterative HITL design process for collaboratively developing an AI-Feedback system with AI and human expertise including teachers, content experts, and learning scientists in refining scoring rubrics and validating AI models to improve accuracy in analyzing elementary students’ written responses on complex science tasks.

## Conceptual framework

AI as a partner We envision AI as a partner that collaborates  with another individual to potentially enhance thinking, creativity, problem-solving, and learning in K-12 education. This partnership challenges each other’s thinking through the exchange of ideas, knowledge,  and perspectives; offer critical feedback; help refine each other’s ideas; and provide support (Shin et al., 2025). In this sense, human and AI can function as more knowledgeable others  for each other, enabling them to accomplish more than they could without such collaboration (Vygotsky, 1978). AI, when used as a collaborative partner, has the potential to share some roles traditionally held by teachers and other adults that particularly tasks requiring frequent and timely attention such as analyzing written responses to assessment s. In this partnership, teachers and researchers contribute deep knowledge of students, classroom contexts, instructional experience, and learning goals, while AI efficiently analyzes written responses 1387 based on specified contexts and datasets and generates analytic rationales. Within this conceptual framework, we adopt a HITL approach in which researchers, teachers, and AI collaborate as partners to enhance the accuracy and efficiency of written-response analysis of usable knowledge. Human-in-the-loop Human-in-the-Loop is an approach to artificial intelligence in which human expertise is actively integrated into the system’s training, testing, and refinement processes. Rather than relying solely on automation, HITL ensures that humans provide oversight,  validate outputs, and guide improvements, making AI systems more accurate, ethical, and contextually meaningful (Kamar, 2016). Our design process incorporates a HITL approach in two key ways. First, during the development phase, HITL emphasizes active res earchers and teachers’ involvement in refining AI algorithms. Researchers  and teachers  iteratively validate and adjust automated outputs through examining discrepancies between human and AI scores, ensuring alignment with learning goals, and refining scoring rubric to guide clear direction for AI analysis. Second, during the classroom implementation phase, applying a HITL approach, teachers can revise AIgenerated analysis, adopt and adapt AI-generated feedback, or provide their own feedback to best support their student learning. These teacher adaptations are used to improve AI scoring accuracy and the quality AI-generated feedback. Our AI models focus on identifying uncertainty in students’ written responses. Because our target populations are elementary school students, their writing often includes vague phrasing, spelling and grammatical errors or inconsistencies within paragraphs. Such issues reduce AI analysis  accuracy and pose risks when deploying AI models in real classrooms. In our system, teachers play an essential role in reviewing AI-generated allowing them to prioritize which scores need review rather than checking every response. Teachers can then accept or revise the AI scores and feedback before sharing them with students. Feedback The goal of leveraging AI in this study is to provide timely feedback by accurately analyzing students’ understanding of usable knowledge to meet both teacher and student needs. Development of usable knowledge is a complex, ongoing process that requires  tailored support to help  learners actively monitor and regulat e their performances toward building usable knowledge (Clark, 2012). To achieve this, learners need timely, constructive, and tailored support that enables learners to reflect, rethink and reapply their knowledge, and revise their work in ways that sustain engagement and deepen understanding (Harris et al, 2024). We propose that feedback is most effective when it supports five areas: (1) cognitive–focusing learners on goals and criteria, (2) metacognitive–encouraging reflection and identification of gaps in individual learning,

(3) motivational–ensuring alignment with learners’ prior knowledge and goals, (4) collaborative–fostering shared

resources and interaction with others, and (5) timely and customized–acting as a responsive partner in learning. In our AI-Feedback system, cognitive, metacognitive, and motivational supports primarily guide the development of feedback statements, while collaborative and timely, customized supports inform the design of interfaces and AI scoring models that are the focus of this study.

Collaborative support Distributed intelligence emphasizes the interaction between internal cognitive processes and external resources (e.g., artifacts as tools, diagrams, and computer interfaces) and collaborative practices in shaping thinking processes (Pea, 1993). These external resources carry embedded intelligence that can aid in problem-solving, learning and performing complex tasks through iterative collaborative interactions. Building on this perspective, design principles for collaborative support include:  1) providing distributed resources that  foster collaboration and to support the use of resources that expand cognitive abilities  by offering a web-based dialogue  tool that enable collaboration among teacher, student, and AI to improve analytical accuracy of learner performance and quality feedback, and 2) fostering interactive engagement rather than one-way information delivery by encouraging seamless dialogue among teacher, student, and AI that supports learning as a shared process, rather than simply transmitting information.

Timely, customized support Research emphasizes that timely performance information enhances students’ ability to meet learning goals (Harris, Pellegrino & Krajcik,  2024). Our d esign principles for timely, customized  support foucs on 1) automatically analyzing learner performance to identify areas where students can improve and uncertainty, and 2) delivering highly accurate feedback to inform next steps. To enact these principles, we employ four strategies: a) using GenAI to analyze learner performance, b) researchers and teachers refining rubrics to ensure analysis aligns 1388 with learning goals in the development phase, c) allowing teachers opportunities to revise and adapt AI-generated over when and how feedback is released, rather than relying on AI to deliver it directly. Iterative design process: Human-in-the-loop This study explore s the research question: “How can an AI-Feedback system incorporate HITL approaches to improve analysis accuracy and generate  timely, customized feedback that promotes usable knowledge in the teaching and learning of elementary science?” The assessment task s and holistic rubrics were developed to align with the Next Generation Science Standards (NGSS) (Lead States, NGSS, 2013)  three-dimensional (3D) performance expectations and unit learning goals (see Table 1). These assessments aim  to gather information about  students' ability to apply and synthesize knowledge to explain novel but related problems (Bartz & Chen, 2021). Ninety fourth-grade students responded to the tasks, which often included vague or inconsistent writing. Using these rubrics and human analys es, we developed AI models to detect weaknesses and uncertainty in students’ usable knowledge and performance, along with analytical rationales, enabling timely, customizable feedback to support teaching and learning  in usable knowledge.

Multi-agent systems In this study, we utilize a multi-agent system to improve analytical accuracy of learner performance and to calculate the uncertainty of the performance of  learners’ written responses. Multi-agent systems consist of multiple LLMs or LLM-based agents working together towards shared goals. This decentralized approach is wellsuited for handling complex tasks that exceed the capabilities of a single LLM (Guo et al., 2024; Chu et al., 2024). Our strategy involves developing the multi-agent system including five models by systematically varying prompts to improve accuracy and identify weakness and uncertainty in student performance. We aggregate the outputs from different models to quantify uncertainty  when results differ. Since the prompt in our assessment includes many components, we can generate different prompt versions by considering two key factors: (1) the amount of scoring rationales provided in the demonstration examples, and (2) manually refining the prompt to explicitly guide the AI models handling students’ language to focus on the keywords, recognize  synonyms, and ignoring irrelevant information.

The final analytical result is determined by the majority vote. To measure accuracy, we calculated the ratio of matching outputs vs non-matching outputs between the majority vote of human raters and AI. To measure AI uncertainty, we counted the number of cases where the five models produced different scores. A response was flagged as uncertain if fewer than four agents agreed. For example, if the five model outputs were B, A, A, A, and B, we flagged the response as uncertain, used the majority vote of “ A” as the final prediction, and reported the uncertainty level as 40%, reflecting the proportion of models that disagreed with the final result.

## Data analysis and results: Three-phase experiments

We conducted three-phase experiments to improve AI analysis accuracy and refine rubrics for analyzing weakness and uncertainty of student responses.

Phase One: We randomly selected 30 student responses, which were individually analyzed  with a analytical rationale by three experts: two who authored the assessment and curriculum, and one with expertise in assessment and curriculum design principles. The overall inter-rater reliability among the three experts was 0.87. Discrepancies were discussed to reach consensus, and the rubric was revised accordingly. These agreed-upon human result served as the ground truth for developing AI models and evaluating the AI analysis performance. The accuracy of the multi-agent system was 0.90 (3 incorrect out of 30). The number of cases of AI uncertainty is 7 out of 30 cases. Experts reviewed AI results and analytical rationales, revised rubrics and added better response examples, and reanalyzed the 30 student responses with rationales (see Table 1). Phase Two: The revised rubrics, additional examples, human analyses and rationales from Phase One were used to improve AI performance. Because the proportion of responses  with a human analysis of 0 or 1 is significantly lower than the other categories  (2 or 3), we performed stratified sampling to include  additional 30 student responses with a higher proportion of low-performing responses. These were analyzed  individually by three experts and AI models to measure inter-rater disagreement and better understand both human and AI uncertainty. The inter-rater reliability among human analyses was 0.87. The AI accuracy between human and AI analyses is 0.87 (4 incorrect out of 30). Additionally, we examined the reliability of uncertain responses between human and AI. The results show that 10% of cases (3 out of 30 responses) are flagged as uncertain by the AI. 1389 Interestingly, human experts also showed disagreement on 13%  (4 out of 30 responses) of the stratified samples, indicating that the vagueness of some of the responses limit ed both AI and human accuracy.  We revised the rubrics by clarifying descriptions of each category based on the Phase Two AI results  and reanalyzed the 30 responses.

Phase Three: Using the Phase Two revised rubrics and human analyses to check reliability between human and AI, we selected 30 samples with varying degrees of AI analysis  uncertainty (including 20 flagged as uncertain) and assigned them to three experts for analyzing. The inter-rater reliability among the human raters was 0.90. The AI accuracy is 0.87 (4 incorrect out of 30). The experts analyzed all 90 responses using the Phase Three rubric and the agreed-upon results were used to evaluate AI performance for analyzing elementary students’ written responses. The overall AI analysis accuracy is 88% (11 incorrect out of 90). AI-Feedback system, incorporating HITL approaches, achieved an average analysis accuracy of 88%. These enabling the generation of timely, customized feedback that supports the development of usable knowledge in elementary science teaching and learning.
