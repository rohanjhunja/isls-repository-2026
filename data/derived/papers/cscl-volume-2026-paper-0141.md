# Do Anti-democratic Bots Dream of Digital Riots? Modeling Anti-democratic Decision-making and Collective Knowledge Construction Using Natural Language Processing

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
We introduce a tool for visualizing reasoning trajectories in online interactions. We leverage natural language processing to corpora from Brazilian communities involved in anti-democratic speech to compare bots’ and people’s trajectories around “discourse crossroads” where epistemic stances towards democratic norms shift. We hope to inform tools and intervention design that guide epistemic trajectories toward more productive engagement, highlighting the role of civic education and digital literacy in fostering critical dialogue.

Anti-democratic groups have promoted their agendas through practices that go beyond simple  repetition of nonfacts: they employ practices resembling agential knowledge construction  and community-based epistemologies, but distorted (de Nadal, 2024; Lewis et al., 2021; Russo & Blikstein, 2022, 2025). The rise of QAnon, for example, reflects collective sense-making which scholars link with “communities of practice” (Ylä-Anttila, 2018; Lave & Wenger, 1991) and informal miseducation (Greenhalgh et al., 2021). In other words, a supposed rationality is used to promote harmful agendas online through sense-making that feels engaging and agential. Our methodological contribution addresses a challenging issue: misinformation thrives when reasoning drifts away from reciprocity and contestability. That raises an epistemic  problem, beyond a merely factual one. We introduce a tool for modeling and visualizing reasoning trajectories in online interactions. The tool focuses on discursive tipping points —junctures where epistemic stances shift from compliant or borderline to noncompliant with democratic norms. We frame misinformation as a process of epistemic change and ask: how might we visualize the tipping points where epistemic stances depart from democratic principles?  Our replicable design (Figure 1) compares  how bots and humans fare in a decision tree intentionally designed to present “ideological crossroads ” that lead either to anti-democratic choices or to choices compliant with established frameworks for democratic policymaking in Brazil (our home country). We contribute to the study of the pedagogy of misinformation and the fragile reasoning processes that sustain democratic life online. We build on previous research (Russo et al., 2024) to extend research analyzing  large-scale online discourse to trace knowledge construction and epistemic change (Sherin & Chavey, 2025) and draw connections with two other strands of literature: public pedagogy scholarship demonstrating how anti-democratic discourse spreads online as forms of collective knowledge construction; (studies of social media as learning spaces. Design rationale We created a decision tree to be run through by seven bots (six anti-democratic ones and one “neutral”). The decision tree had several turning points where discourse "drifted” from compliant with democratic principles to non-compliant. Additionally, with the intent to simulate an analysis that juxtaposes robots’ performance in the task with humans’, we ran a preliminary study with a limited sample of human respondents. Brazilian participants from Prolific (N =54) performed a task similar to that performed by  bots: given a root node, choose a response; from that response, choose 1 out of 3 options. Finally, participants indicated their political stance using a slider where 1 meant Left and 10 meant Right. Of special interest to the analysis of human respondents were the leaf nodes they chose and the frequency of each label (compliant, dubious, non-compliant). ## Preliminary findings

We identified that all “anti-democratic bots” made the same three choices throughout the tree: After the parent node, all went to node #2 (“You can’t trust anyone… media, politicians, everybody is feathering their own nest”), then node #7 (“Go check 2, 3 sources… There is always a different version spreading. Good information comes from comparison #SuspectAndMultiply), and then node #24 (“Comparing everything can be helpful, but it brings about confusion ��� � � � � �”). The order of labels was, thus, dubious > compliant > dubious. In contrast, the neutral bot followed only compliant nodes: #3 (“Does traditional journalism only serve the boss? Let’s make independent media stronger, then ����”); #11 (“Media regulation could be the solution… but it depends: if there is no transparency, it may end up in censorship”); #34 (“Saw a news story? Most humans finished at a compliant node, and only 2 participants finished their trajectories at a dubious node (#24), one participant at each e xtreme of the political spectrum. Also, the majority  chose compliant nodes only. No comparison is possible across political ideologies.

Intriguingly, about 1/4 of humans (mostly self-identified centrists) made the same first choice as the antidemocratic bots (node #2, “You can’t trust anyone… media, politicians, everybody is feathering their own nest”). What made that node  attractive to those bots and to all those humans? If—as we hypothesize —the bots truly represent stances collectively taken by the users whose voices make up the bots’ decision-making, then, possibly,

(i) node #2 might be acting as a gateway premise  that leads viewers to statements that diverge from compliance

with our frameworks; and/or (ii) the “collective intelligence” of YouTube comments might have already incorporated that belief into its “sense-making” supported by a blend of black-box algorithms and visible human user input. To extend our metaphor, some bots in the wild might already be dreaming of digital riots —like the ones their human counterparts put in place in Brasilia in January 2023. It is  also intriguing that crucial node’s content evokes the erosion of trust in institutions, an emerging phenomenon associated with groups involved in the Capitol riots (e.g., Marwick & Partin, 2022).

## Brief discussion and conclusion

We plan to extend the decision-tree framework by introducing finer-grained distinctions between nodes. We also aim to test the same decision-tree framework with larger human samples, representing a range of political alignments. By comparing human participants’ and bots’ behavior, we can examine whether our hypothesized tipping points hold across contexts and perspectives. This will provide critical validation for the model and highlight potential variations between automated and human reasoning. Together, these extensions strengthen the methodological contribution of our work by demonstrating the tool’s capacity to trace reasoning pathways with greater precision. By operationalizing discursive tipping points and testing them with both bots and human participants, we aim to provide researchers and educators with a replicable resource to make visible the fragile mechanisms by which democratic reasoning can be reinforced—or disrupted—in online environments. Our design allows us to trace trajectories through successive discourse junctures, foregrounding pathways along which reasoning develops. This hybrid framework creates new opportunities to analyze agency and critical engagement as learners navigate contested epistemic terrains. Results also raise design implications. Nudges, prompts, or collaborative tasks could be used to help learners sustain productive engagement even when exposed to manipulative discourses. Importantly,  these findings remind us that simply providing corrective facts or additional evidence is insufficient.  What matters is how we scaffold reasoning, how standards of justification are upheld, and how learners position themselves for accountability within pluralistic dialogue. de Nadal, L. (2024).
