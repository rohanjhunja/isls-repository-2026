# Pre-Service Teachers and Co-construction of Pedagogical Terminology

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
We show three examples of terms that create situations of discursive contestation for pre-service teachers using data from a larger project. The ways that the pre-service teachers construct their meanings independently or communally is described. This work has implications for teacher education and the importance of training pre-service teachers to be flexible in their understanding of and approach to the role of teacher.

## Introduction and conceptual framework

Teacher preparation programs include reform oriente d pedagogical knowledge, disciplinary terminology a nd curricular standards, and discipline specific degre e work. Pre-service teachers (PSTs) are asked to navigate a complex conceptual and linguistic terrain. PSTs enc ounter many educational terms such as equity, commu nity, hands-on, and student engagement that are used in vastly different ways across coursework and instructional contexts (Philip & Azevedo, 2017; Russ, 2018).  P STs as well as teacher educator s should be aware of the loose and context-dependent ways in which these concepts appear throughout their careers. We expand upon Louie (2018), Philip and Azevedo (2017), and Russ (2018) who demo nstrate that meanings for certain terms are co-constructed in real time, based on the shared beliefs, experiences, and ideologies of a given community of teachers. For example, Russ (2018) notes that a “...real instructional tension arises from the vagueness of ‘attention’” —a vagueness that comes from differences in how teac hers and researchers operationalize the term. We ask how PSTs reason about the  same term in different contexts. We argue that encountering differing definitions for the same ped agogical term in different contexts  creates situations of discursive contestation leading to conceptual uncertainty (consciously or otherwise) for the PSTs. We take a socio-political approach to learning to make sense of the  act of dialogically and communally constructing connotative meanings to these otherwis e academically defined terms. We draw on Hall’s 198 2 conception of interconnectedness —that words or phrases gain their meaning from the i nteraction between the discourse and context they are used in to form a “distinctive set or chain of meanings” (p. 81)—to unpack the way that pedagogical terminology is used in the context of PST reflective practices.

## Methods

This study is part of a larger 2-year study on PST reflective practices (n=9, n=12) in a teacher preparation capstone course (Spring ‘23). This is the final course in a 4-year teacher certification program at a University in the Southern United States. It included a 3-day mini Project-Based Learning unit that PSTs created and executed in local 6-12 grade STEM classes.  Video of PST’s in-class reflective presentations about their mini units  was recorded and transcribed using Rev.com. Iterative transcript cod ing and analytic memoing was performed by members o f the research team to identify overarching themes  such as affect, co-construction of best-practices/terminology, and identity and ideology within teaching.

## Analysis and findings

Our analysis shows that PSTs draw on all forms of r esources to communicate what they know/observed in their time in mentor teacher classrooms, leading to const antly shifting meanings for pedagogical terminology  derived from socially defined contexts that play a strong r ole in defining PSTs conceptions of knowledge. We i dentify three ways in which pedagogical terms were operationalized and their meanings negotiated in practice. Floating definitions: new definitions are not easily taken up During the course, the “Project” (purposeful capitalization) in project-based instruction/learning was discussed as being distinct from “project” in common STEM lesson formats such as 5E. Projects are meant to be authentic and dynamic investigations that mimic the work of disci plinary experts as the process of discovery and con structing an answer to a posed driving question. Despite the formalization o f Project in Project-Based Instruction, the cultural context that the PSTs were working within emphasized the definition of project that they had previously experienced as students themselves, and in other teacher preparation courses: a project as a t ype of summative assessment-not a conceptual approach. The PSTs enacted floatin g definitions which impacts the uptake of new meanings for a previously understood term by simply  adding to possible definitions for deployment rath er than having a new one supplant the old. For two teaching  teams, they made little effort to follow any eleme nts of project-based instruction (PBI), -having students complete projects in line with trad itional pedagogical methods: creating pretend creatures out of food stuffs as a way to demonstrate phenotype and genotype, and having students make a traditional Unit Circle for t rigonometry. Both of these included driving questio ns, but followed the 5E format otherwise, in part due to time restrictions in the course. Even for students who attempted to embrace the new meaning of Project, their narratives in the reflection assignment reveals that they still conceptualized i t as a project-separate from the learning portion of the lesson. One student whose lesson plan followed bes t practices for project based instruction stated “t here was a lack of accountability when students were actually doing th e project, um, and then do a better job creating th e need for computer science in the lesson”. We can see the way  they combined p/Project as they activate the proje ct and lesson separately. This aligns with theories of ide ological change in which the students shift their d efinition deployment based on the context and community they are interacting with at the time. Indefinite definitions: terms are defined through examples of ideal or subpar behavior The PSTs utilized multiple pedagogical terms that h elped them measure the success of their lesson exec ution. These terms represented teaching practices or outco mes that the PSTs felt acted as metrics for good te aching. These included: “assess/ments/ing”, “flexibility” and, the one our analysis here will focus on: “engage/d”. During their reflections, one PST stated: “I don't think there was really engagement 'cause if you're engaged in something you won't want to do something else...” Another reflected: “I would say that students were really engaged… I had a news clip they watched …and  by the end they made these really great, um, sort of storyboards of like a homeless person talking about  their life”. These instances demonstrate the way that PSTs use the term “engagement/ed” as a metric and define  it based on expected student b ehavior. They state  that engaged is not wanting to “do something else” and t hat the assignment execution was “really great”.  T hus, “engage/d” remains a loosely defined element of ong oing discourse, without any definite definition. Ye t, no one in the community questions or feels the need for a more cohesive definition. Curricular obsolescence: terms originally from the curriculum are used in new ways. The last few iterations of the Next Generation Scie nce Standards (NGSS) have focused heavily on authen tic science practices-having students at all levels engage in science (and other STEM disciplines) in a way that mimics the work done by experts in the field. The current NGSS highlights phenomenon based learning and lab work as ways to engage in “authentic” (according to NGSS) scientific practices, but a past version introduced the term “hands on” as a method of teaching t hat encourages students to actively participate in science through experiments, projects, and real-world applications (Furtak & Penuel, 2019). However, we see “hands-on” used in a manner tangential to the way it was defined in state and national curriculum, instead it is used in a reductionist ma nner that works to strip away the complexities of t eaching. It excludes the concept of “real-world” or authentic applications. Instead, the PSTs operati onalized it as activities for learning that involved the students manipulating an object, rather than simply taking notes or doing worksheets. One group stated the students… “really liked the da y-two hands-on. …Whenever they did migration and they were like using doing punnett squares but with pape rs instead of like writing instead of writing on pa per they're using like cutouts and like I think that they just really enjoy the more like hands on”. From this exa mple we can see that the term “hands-on” is constructed more inline with the pedagogical strategy of manipulatives rather than the authentic science practices it was originally defined as in national standards.

## Discussion

Based on the ways that pedagogical term s were constructed in a similar manner by several members of the PST cohort for, we posit that the PSTs are making sense of the  constantly evolving profession through discursivel y and communally constructing key pedagogical terms in ways unique to their specific cohort and program. Future work may expand upon the three categories examined, to identify more cases of each type of discursive definition switch and work to make sense of the historical context in which these new meanings emerged. This work has broader implications for teacher prep aration and the way that the learning science and STEM education communities think about educational terminology and the broader experiences and context s of pre-service STEM teachers. Literature suggests that standardizing conversations, the way we as teacher educators use terms and having definitions formally published  in research helps to bridge the theory-practice-discipline divide (Parker et al, 2019). Over time, these terms  become devoid of real meaning, hampering some teac her learning and influencing their broader ideologies a nd epistemologies of the nature of knowing, learnin g and teaching (Louie, 2018).  As teacher educators, we h ave the responsibility to make PSTs aware of the co nceptual imprecisions of the field of education and support them in navigating the linguistic terrain of the field.
