# Four Causal Problems that Engage Laypeople in Interacting with Science

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study explores how laypeople in a clinical context engage with causal scientific information. Using a framework of four causal scientific problems—causal inference, explanation, prediction, and control— We examined the questions posed to medical staff in online cancer-related forums. The findings showed that most of the questions raised were causal in nature, with the most common goal being a desire for control—that is, seeking ways to influence various situations. Framing lay engagement with science through causal problems provides a valuable lens for understanding laypeople’s diverse aims and may be used for enhancing their scientific literacy through tailored educational approaches.

## Introduction

Laypeople often engage with science only when science helps them so lve particular problems (Feinstein, 2011). Consequently, many recent studies in science education have focused on the knowledge laypeople should possess to effectively use scientific information. Some stu dies examine their ability to evaluate credibility (e.g., Allchin, 2023; Chinn et al., 2021; Sharon & Baram-Tsabari, 2020), while others explore how well they understand information for practical application (e.g., Boerwi nkel et al., 2017; Feinstei n, 2014; Ryder, 2001; Tabak & Dubovi, 2023). Less attention, however, has been pa id to the kinds of problems laypeople are trying to  solve. A general framing of these problems is needed to better understand their strategies and support them in overcoming challenges. One such framing is the "causal problems" approach, which proposes four types of problems relevant to both scientists and laypeople: causal inference,  explanation, prediction, and control (Illari & Rus so, 2014; Authors, submitted). These problem s are general and adaptable across contexts, offeri ng a way to categorize laypeople's engagement with science.

While based on how scientists frame problems, laype ople may approach information differently (Davis & Russ, 2015). This study empirically examines how laypeople engage with the four causal problems, focusing on cancer, a situation where information can be a matter of life and death. Patients and their families face pressing concerns, from symptoms to treatment decisions. Since most tr eatment happens at home, patients must independentl y manage and report their symptoms without professional support. In such high-stakes contexts, people may define unique problems that guide their search for information. Though not representative of everyday engagement, this case offers valuable insights into how laypeople interact with different types of causal problems.

## Theoretical framework

This study draws on recent philosophy of science ap proaches to causal reasoning, particularly the fram ework by Illari & Russo (2014), which identifies five causal problems in sc ientific practice. We focus on four of these, causal inference, explanation, prediction, and control, as relevant to laypeople (Haskel-Ittah et al., submitted):

• Causal inference involves finding out what causes what.

• Causal explanation seeks to understand how and why a cause produces its effect.

• Prediction focuses on anticipating what will happen next based on current knowledge.

• Control aims to influence outcomes by applying causal knowledge

While distinct, these four problems are closely interconn ected, often overlapping in practice (Illari & Russo, 2014). In this study, we sought to explore h ow these problems are manifested in laypeople’s eng agement with scientific information. The rationale for pursuing this exploration is that these problems can frame laypeople's aims in engaging with science. To that end, we analyzed questions posted in online oncology forums, where users consult medical professionals. Our study aimed to i nvestigate whether and how lay people engage with t he four types of causal problems in a medical context, and whether certain problems are more prevalent in the questions raised on online forums.

## Methods

This study analyzed 120 posts from online oncology forums, where individuals seek advice from medical professionals. The forums were identified through a Google search using the keywords "ask an expert nurse doctor oncology." Four publicly accessible forums were selected, two in English and two in Hebrew, all of which included active ‘Ask the Doctor’ sections focused on oncology. We applied a top-down approach to code the posts from the forums. Questions were considered causal if they addressed relationships between variables o r causes of outcomes. The codes were mutually exclusive, meaning each question was classified as one of the causal problems or labeled as "other" if it was non-causal, too general, or unclear. However, some forum posts contained multiple questi ons, in which case each question was coded separately.

Intercoder reliability was assessed on 20% of the d ata, with two independent coders achieving 8 6.6% agreement. After discussing incompatibilities and making some changes to both participants’ observations, 100% intercoder agreement was obtained.

## Results

Out of a total of 145 questions (in 120 forum posts), 117 (80.7%) were causal and 28 (19.3%) were noncausal. Out of the causal questions, 23.1% were coded as “causal inference”, 28.2% as “causal explanation”, 17.1% as “prediction” and 31.6% as “control”.

• Causal inference: The problem of causal inference was reflected in forum questions primarily as a desire

to understand what caused a particular phenomenon. For example, people sought to determine whether a specific symptom was caused by the disease or its  treatment. For instance: "Is vision impairment a known side effect of these medications?".

• Causal explanation: The desire for a causal explanation was expressed in forum questions that presented

and requested interpretations of imaging or blood markers test results. Except for a few cases, the questions coded as a goal of causal explanation did not indicate an inte rest in how or why something causes something else. Instead, they demonstrated a desire for a phenomenal description, serving as a model that describes the dynamic behavior of the ph enomenon (like the levels of blood markers in different types of cancer), or for the characteriza tion of entities without activities (such as when presenting imaging results).

• Prediction:  Many people posted in the forums seeking  insight into  what was likely to happen. For

example, users asked for prognosis assessments or probability estimations regarding certain outcomes in specific situations. Questions such as "What are the chances of treatment success?"  or "Can I expect a full recovery?" were commonly raised.

• Control: People often ask questions with the intention of controlling various  situations —that is,

attempting to influence the occurrence of a phenome non, either by preventing it or encouraging it.  For example, many posts requested information or clinical guidance about possible treatment options  (e.g. “What cough medication is best for her case? ”). Another common type of question concerned drug interactions—such as whether taking a certain drug might affect the effectiveness of another medication (e.g. “I wanted to use ginseng to get added energy and was told it would interact with osimertinib... What other natural vitamin or supplement can I take for energy?”).

## Discussion

This study explores how laypeople engage with science to address cancer-related concerns. Prior research shows that people seek scientific information when it becomes personally relevant (Dalyot et al., 2019; Feinstein, 2014). By framing questions through four types of causal p roblems, we found that laypeople pr imarily use information to manage or control situations —supporting the view that non-scientists engage with science to solve real-life problems (Feinstein et al., 2013). This includes using information to act, decide, or predict outcomes. However, we also found that laypeople pursue less d irectly practical goals, such as causal inference (23.1% of questions) and explanation (28%), often seeking to understand what caused a symptom or how something works, even without asking explicitly. Many sought non-mechanistic explanations, like descriptions or characterizations. Framing laypeople’s engagement through causal problems reveals varied goals that shape their inquiry (Chinn et al., 2014). Recognizing these goals is key to understanding how people approach science and the challenges they face. This perspective is vital for science education research and aligns with broader calls to understand how nonexperts interpret and apply scientific information (Allchin, 2023; Tabak, 2020; Tabak & Dubovi, 2023).  Future studies should explore how these causal problems and the use of information are interrelated.
