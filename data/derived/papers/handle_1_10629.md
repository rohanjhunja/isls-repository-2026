# Generative AI and the Human Touch: Investigating the Changing Landscape of Feedback in Higher Education

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study investigates how students and educators perceive feedback generated by Large Language Models (LLMs), such as ChatGPT, in comparison to feedback provided by human educators. The study, conducted through a series of focus groups with students and educators, specifically focuses on the interactional, relational, and emotional aspects of feedback. Both students and teachers acknowledge the value of LLM in providing quick, automated feedback, particularly for grammar and spelling, but emphasise the limitations of AI in understanding their specific needs, engaging in nuanced discussions, and responding to their emotional states. The delivery of feedback from one human to another is built on personal relationships and has the potential to be emotionally charged. Using an anthropomorphic language to describe feedback from LLM – for instance, by conceptualising the technology as a “collaboration coach” or talking about the resulting interaction in terms of “participation” – can therefore be misleading.

## Introduction

Since autumn 2022, the potential for interactive and dialogue-based feedback has greatly improved, thanks to the rapid progress of large language models  (LLMs) like ChatGPT (Generative Pre-trained Transformer). In April 2023, UNESCO released a “Quick Start Guide” titled “ChatGPT and Artificial Intelligence in Higher Education” (Sabzalieva & Valentini, 2023), outlining the potential use of generative AI in teaching and learning. Building on the work of Sharples (2023), the report distinguish es several roles for LLM s and generative AI within higher education. These roles include acting as a “Socrati c opponent,” where the AI serves as an adversary to  facilitate argument development, a “collaboration coach” aiding groups in collaborative problem-solving, a “personal tutor” providing immediate feedback to individual students, a “study buddy” assisting students in reflecting on their learning materials, and a “motivator” offering chal lenges that extend the learning experience (Sabzali eva & Valentini, 2023, p. 9). It is noteworthy that the t echnologies are assigned roles traditionally fulfil led by human teachers or student peers. This exemplifies how the  anthropomorphic language commonly used to describe  AI tends to blur the lines between human and artificial entities. Distinguishing AI roles as humans easily lends itself to conceptualising LLMs and generative AI in terms of various forms of participation. As Sfard (1998) notes, metaphors “tend to travel from one domain to  another practically unnoticed” (p. 5). While this might be unavoidable and perhaps unproblematic in many circumstances, it can also have negative consequences for educational design and theorising: “it may bar fresh insights, undermine the usefulness of the resulting conceptual system, and—above all—perpetuate beliefs and values that have never been submitted to critical inspection” (Sfard, 1998, p.5). Reeves and Porcheron (2022), for instance,  question whether it is accurate to say that AI systems are t ruly participating in an encounter with humans and whether we really should design those systems with that perspe ctive in mind. They argue against such an approach,  stating that “the version of participation adopted by researchers and designers of interaction with AI systems tends to be naïve” (p. 573). While it is possible to model feed back from ChatGPT based on a “collaboration coach” or a “personal tutor”, this does not mean that the feedback is produced or understood in the same way or that it would be sensible to talk about participation in terms of  human relations. In interpersonal situations, feed back delivery tends to be sensitive and can trigger various emotional reactions: feedback intended as constructive advice might be perceived as complaints or accusations (see, for instance, Lindwall, Lymer & Ivarsson, 2022). When feedback is delivered by an AI, however, the interpersonal a nd emotionally loaded aspects of feedback are large ly removed.

With these considerations as a starting point, this  study investigates how students and supervisors understand feedback generated by LLMs such as ChatGPT in relation to feedback given by human educators. The study, based on a series of focus groups involving both students and educators, focuses on the interac tional, relational, and emotional aspects of feedback.

## Methods

The focus groups were organised in Sweden between M arch and April in 2023, around two weeks to a month after GPT4 had been released. Participants were rec ruited using a combination of opportunity-and snowball sampling, and 17 international university students (five at a  bachelor level and 12 at the master level) and nin e educators (n = 26) took part in the study. The eigh t focus groups consisted of either students or educ ators and there were no mixed groups. The students had various academic backgrounds and experiences of higher education, and all had some experience of using generative AI for their study or work. The students were mixed in  terms of gender, had a background in Sweden, Portugal, Italy, Latvia, Iran, Pakistan, China and Japan, and repr esented diverse faculties, including Education and IT; Comp uter Science and Engineering; Infrastructure and Environmental Engineering; Chemistry and Chemical E ngineering; Social Sciences; Business, Economics and Law; and Humanities. The educators were primarily p rofessors who had experience in academic supervisio n of undergraduate and master's students. The educators were all from the Swedish University and belonged t o either Education, IT, or Social Sciences faculty. Each session, facilitated in English, unfolded over an approximate onehour time span.  The data analysis respected reflexive thematic a nalysis that involves identifying, analysing, and reporting patterns or themes within the data (Braun  & Clarke, 2022). The authors familiarised themselv es with the data as pre-coding work, created the initial codes, clustered c odes into some themes, reviewed, revised, and finalised them as findings.

## Findings

Student perceptions According to the students, AI and human feedback ea ch have their strengths and weaknesses. Several stu dents recognized the potential benefits of AI in providin g quick, automated feedback for certain aspects lik e grammar and spelling. These students see AI as a valuable t ool for efficiency, but view AI as a supplementary resource rather than a replacement for human teachers. They argue that AI can assist with certain aspects of fe edback but could or should not repl ace the human touch.  “AI feedback is really good as a first step. But aft er that, I really want to receive that active, vibrant feedback. And then when I get feedback face-to-face, I can ask some questions at the same time.” Students perceive d AI feedback  as lacking adaptability, providing fixed responses and struggling to engage in nuanced discussions. They a lso point ed to the limits of LLMs in understanding their recipients. Being ignorant of the needs of the reci pient, ChatGPT would provide the same answer regard less of who poses the question, whereas, as one student formulated it “my needs might be different from a ten-year-old's needs.” One student formulated the sensitivity toward recip ients and conversational in terms of humans being able to put more “emotions” into the feedback and that they thereby would be able to adjust their feedback in ways that are sensitive to how the other person is feeli ng: “when someone is really stressed, you tend to give more feedback and encourage the person and so on. The AI-generated would be only a couple of sentences and t hat's it.” T he emotional aspect of human feedback was not only,  or purely, seen as a positive feature. Several students reported discomfort in receiving feedback from supervisors, making them reluctant to talk: “I don't speak much in front of the supervisor, so ChatGPT is real ly good for me. You can ask a stupid question and […] you don't need to think about the other person.” Anothe r student noted the relaxation in  asking many questions to ChatGPT, with a peer adding that asking a person the same question repeatedly would likely be met with frustration. With a machine, “You don't need to think about the f uture. If I ask a stupid question, the hu man supervisor will think like, ‘Who are you? From where do you come from? You are studying a masters”.  Using an LLM can thus be a way of getting feedback without putting yourself in a position where you risk exposing yourself to some discomfort. Receiving feedback from AI is s een as “easier”, as it is less “emotionally charged ” and therefore could be listen ed to or treated in a more “neutral” way. One student reported that they were not necessarily “emotionally prepared” to learn that th eir papers were ba d. If the feedback was produced by an AI, however, it would “be much easier to accept”. This distinction is used in highlighting the positive si des of AI-generated feedback, but it is also used to raise th e importance of having feedback from a human superv isor: “Because the person there, as much as I am maybe afr aid to bother, I am also eager to get maybe probati on sometimes, or the opposite, so that I can grow and become better. I don't feel this urge with the machine”. Issues of trust and credibility differ between humans and machines. Several students portrayed feedback from supervisors as more trustworthy than feedback from machines. Humans are said to have experiences and expertise that the machines lack, especially in specialised or complex areas. Accordingly, feedback from humans is seen as having more “credibility” than AI-generated feedback. As one of the students pointed out: “I can trust this person's judgement because they have been in this situation. [...] Then I feel more trust towards the feedback being given to me than when I have a machine doing it.” Students raised concerns about AI’s ability to understand the context of their work, especially in complex subjects or when personal experiences are involved. They worry that AI may miss the nuances of their assignments. However, the students displayed that they understood  that there were different tradeoffs. Discussions about r espect and trust are sometimes connected to issues of power and authority. One student argued that you can choo se whether to accept or reject an AI comment, but t hat you would have to accept what the supervisor says. Part ly disagreeing with this statement, however, another student in the same focus group proposed that it might have to do with the “supervisor’s personality”, and that the student’s supervisor would accept that the student might choose not to follow the advice for different reasons. Again, these different attitudes could be contrasted with the se emingly ‘neutral’ feedback from LLMs which easily c ould be listened to or ignored at the will of the students.

Teacher perceptions In the context of feedback production and reception by humans and machines, the supervisors explored topics that diverged from the concerns raised by the students. Despite recognizing the efficiency advantages of AI, multiple supervisors stressed the significance of a human filter in the educational process. They argued that understanding students' intentions and providing personalized feedback are beyond the capabilities of AI. Some su pervisors mentioned that teachers can read between the lines of students' work to understand their intentions, a human skill that AI lacks. One of the supervisors contrasted the “instrumental feedback on texts” that AI can prov ide and the more “nuanced feedback” that a supervisor can give. Another supervisor noted that AI is able to provide feedback on “lower order concerns”, like grammar and spellin g, but falls short on “higher-order concerns” such as argumentation and structure. Feedback on these high er-order concerns requires a human touch, as they invo lve knowledge about content and context.

As pointed out by several supervisors, moreover, th e difference between humans and machines cannot be reduced to different orders of writing skills: “ the big thing here, what it all comes down to, is w hat relations you have.” Several supervisors emphasized the significant resp onsibility of teachers in guiding students and the importance of empathy and understanding in the educ ational process. They recognized that students have  a life outside academia that might affect their per formance: “It's not just a matter of commitment to the thesis. Sometimes you are committed, but there are other things you are committed to and can have an influence on what you do.” Providing support and understanding in suc h cases is seen as crucial, and feedback might requ ire care and empathy. The supervisors highlighted that well-structured feedback that encourages improvement empowers students to make improvements in their work. They also mentioned the challenge of balancing being too harsh or too gentle in feedback.

When the supervisors talked about human feedback, it was often placed in a face-to-face situation. Such situations have affordances for continually monitor ing the students understanding that is not only mis sing in an LLMs but in any chat-based conversations: “I can see it in their face, i n their body language, all the rest of it.” The supervisors discussed the differences between f ace-to-face interaction and AI-generated feedback. Face-toface interactions allow for a more nuanced exchange, with the ability to ask questions and clarify feedback in real time.  As one supervisor describes it, sometimes there might  be a sense of “urgency” – that the student really needs to change things – and therefore it becomes central to monitor careful ly whether the student really has got it: “I wonder how a system like GPT could assess th at. Because I assess it in the moment and I still d on't get it right half the time.” Several of the supervisors de scribed how they used the emotional responses to ge t insights about how the  students understand the feedback, which, in turn, b ecomes central for how the feedback is then developed. From this point of view, the worst emotional response by students is when they “starts to clam up, and claim to understanding everything that you are saying in your feedback, and you have no idea if you have reached the student with that feedback”. According to this supervisor, “that is the most difficult emotional response to deal with, actually, I don’t mind if they get upset, or want to discuss something.” There was also a caution against anthropomorphizing AI-generated feedback. Supervisors suggested that AI should maintain a dispassionate, non-human tone, as overly human-like responses might not be appropriate or effective in all situations. One supervisor  mentioned that care is a profoundly human trait, em phasizing the importance of human understanding and support in ed ucation. Understanding the context of a student's s ituation remains a vital aspect of effective education. Regardless of the design features or how well the f eedback fits the students' concrete needs, moreover, the supervisors  highlighted that there are intrinsic differences b etween humans and machines which have consequences for how  the feedback is received – at least if you are aware that the feedback comes from the machine  ”As a student, if you know you're  getting immediate feedback from a machine, I think it's probably less emotionally loa ded than if it comes from a human.“ As one of the s upervisors pointed out, the question boils down to the following question “what relation do the students have with the teacher and with the AI?”

## Discussion

The results of the study show the multifaceted views of both students and educators regarding the capabilities and limitations of LLMs like ChatGPT. While the literat ure on AI in education sometimes blurs the lines be tween humans and technology, the students and educators i n this study highlight what they perceive to be cri tical differences. Students acknowledged the value of LLMs in providing quick, automated fee dback, particularly for grammar and spelling. However, they emphasized the limitations of AI in understanding their specif ic needs, engaging in nuanced discussions, and responding to their emotional states. Educators, on the other hand, stress the significance of human skills in inferring stude nt intent, providing nuanced and personalized feedback, and offering support and understanding beyond academic performance. They believed that generative AI can excel in providing feedback on lower-order concerns, such as language issues, but falls short when it comes to higher-order concerns, such as argumentation and context-specific feedback. It is important to note that the  difference between humans and machines is not reduced to issues of quality alone. The difference runs deeper than that and concerns how students and teachers can participate in face-to-face interaction and establish interpersonal relationships that have temporal horizons and emotional underpinnings; and consequently, how both parties could be understood as participants in shared communities of practice. The issue is not only whether LLMs can provide feedback on grammar, spelling, or structure of the same quality as their human counterparts. Consistent with the observations of Reeves and Pocheron (2022), the results of this study show that AI feedback is not understo od in the same manner as human feedback, making it highly problematic to frame it through the lens of human relations. In anticipation of feedback from supervisors, students may experience emotions such as excitement, nervousness, or even regret. In contrast, AI-driven systems typically sidestep these emotional subtleties, cultivating a relatively neutral feedback environment. Consequently, students rarely articulate such sentiments regarding feedbac k received from ChatGPT. However, despite the p otential benefits provided by the neutrality of LLMs, especi ally in certain circumstances amid asymmetrical pow er dynamics and negative encounters with critical feed back, it is important to consider that if the centr al aspects of identity and identificatio n are integral to academic literacy, the use of generative AI to replace rather than supplement human tutors may risk the loss of something central. Technology is evolving to incorporate more emotiona l and contextual aspects into AI-generated feedback. While advancements hint at a future where AI might echo more human-like considerations in feedback, it is not necessarily the case that the most productive goal would be to attempt to mimic or replicate the feedback of supervisors. As long as the students are aware that the feedback is produced by technology and not humans, it will still be understood as intrinsically different, and the alternative  to hide this fact would be unethical and problematic in several respects (Ivarsson and Lindwall, 2023).
