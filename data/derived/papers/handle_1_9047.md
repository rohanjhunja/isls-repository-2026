# Advancing the Goals of Problem-Posing Education in a Knowledge Building Community: An Exploratory Case Study

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Scholars of critical pedagogy have long argued for the transformation of schools from oppressive to emancipatory. Although Knowledge Building Communities (KBCs) were initially designed to achieve epistemic goals there exists a significant overlap between the theory underlying the KBC approach and Freire’s notion of problem-posing education. Therefore, we asked how KBCs could mediate some key features of problem-posing. In this paper, we present qualitative findings from an exploratory case study of a KBC that was designed for the purpose of problem-posing. Our findings indicate that repurposing KBCs to achieve ethical aims holds promise, and we conclude with some specific question that we believe would be worthwhile to investigate in future studies.

## Introduction

Paulo Freire is best known for his book Pedagogy of the Oppressed  (Freire, 2005), in which he articulated a compelling critical social theory of education. Fre ire argued that education systems can be either opp ressive or emancipatory, depending on how learners interact with teachers and content. Freire’s impact has grown in recent years, especially in light of the “political turn” in the learning sciences (Philip et al., 2018). In addition, contemporary scholarship has identified similarities between Freire’s work and some of the sociocultural learning theories that have become increasingly influential in the learning sciences (Vossoughi & Gutiérrez, 2017). Knowledge Building Communities (KBCs) have long bee n recognized as a promising alternative to traditional learning environments (Scardamalia & Be reiter, 2014). The KBC approach fuses theory, pedag ogy, and technology, and has been successfully applied in many educational contexts (Chen & Hong, 2016). Although individual KBCs differ from one another they share the goal of knowledge creation rather than internal ization. Consequently, while KBCs typically aim to achieve e pistemic aims, repositioning students as agentive s ubjects also realigns classroom power dynamics.

Notwithstanding clear differences between the KBC literature and Freire’s vision, there is a substantial degree of overlap between them. Both approaches see k to reposition students as more agentive and to in spire open-ended knowledge-building trajectories that con nect between students’ real-world experiences and t he content they engage with. This article begins by reviewing the theoretical overlap between Freire’s vision and the KBC approach, and then presents findings from an ex ploratory case study intended to flesh out the connections between them. Ultimately, our aim is to open the door to a new avenue of KBC research that defines its aims in ethical terms in addition to epistemic ones.

Problem-posing education Some goals of critical pedagogy Widely recognized as the father of critical pedagogy, Freire (2005) maintained that many traditional le arning environments are fundamentally oppressive, because they quell curiosity and creativity, thus denying learners the ability to think critically about the world around them and their place within it. In addition, they treat knowledge about the world as value-neutral, abstract, and static. Coupled together, these two features “create people who see no other serious possibility to the economic and cultural assemblage now extant” (Apple, 2004, p. 6). Indeed, scholars of critical pedagogy have argued for the transformation of schools from oppressive to emancipatory, into institutions “where students can be educated to take their places in society from a position of empowerment rather than from a position of ideological and economic subordination” (Giroux, 1997, p. 120). In Freire’s (2005) seminal work, emancipation is achieved through praxis, as learners combine reflection and action to assume the role of agentive subjects. Hence, one  primary goal of critical pedagogy is to employ ped agogic means to empower students to rethink what they know about the world and their place within it, in a process that can promote equity and social justice.

From students-of-the-teacher to students-teachers ICLS2022 Proceedings 35 © ISLS At the heart of Freire’s (2005) pedagogic vision is  a contrast between banking education and problem-posing education. The former conceptualizes learning in terms of teac hers depositing knowledge into students’ minds, while the latter opens the door to more dynamic forms of learning (2005, p. 79). Shifting from banking to problemposing repositions teachers, students, and knowledge vis-à-vis one another. When practicing banking education, teachers are active, learners are passive, and knowledge is value-neutral, objective, and static. Yet, in a problemposing context, teacher, student, and knowledge dynamically co-constitute one another: “Through dialogue, the teacher-of-the-students and the students-of-the-teacher cease to exist and a new term emerges: teacher-student with students-teachers… People teach each other, mediated by the world”. (Freire, 2005, p. 80). How is problem-posing enacted? Freire outlines three unique features of the students-teachers who can be found in emancipatory learning environment. Firs t, problem-posing aims to elevate students-teachers ’ awareness of reality and of themselves. This is achieved by transcending investigations that are mechanical and limited in scope, and by seeking out “the links between themes, a concern to pose these themes as problems, and a concern for their historical-cultural context” (p. 107). Second, students-teachers de-mythicize and problematize elements of their own world. Knowledge of real-worl d experiences (for instance, learners’ own poverty)  is no longer perceived as inevitable; rather, it is subject to change. Finally, teachers do not rely on claims of authority to motivate students. Instead, responsibility is distributed between the teacher-student and students-teachers. Knowledge Building Communities Theoretical underpinnings The Knowledge Building Community (KBC) approach first emerged in the early 1990s (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 1991), and has been a central thrust of design-base d research in the learning sciences ever since. KBC s are distinguishable by (1) their expectations from learners, (2) their ontological view of knowledge, and (3) the role played by discourse, dialog, and community (Chen & Hong, 2016). Theoretically, these three points are all grounded in a distinction between learning and knowledge building, where learning is defined as “a change in [private and internal] mental state”, and knowledge building as the “production of [out-in-the-world] designs, t heories […] and the like” (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2014, p. 397). In a KBC, learners’ primary aim is not to internalize something that is already known to the teacher or to other students, but to produce and advance novel, o ut-in-the-world, ideas (cf. Popper, 1978) by engagi ng in productive knowledge building discourse (Bereiter & Scardamalia, 2016). The Copernican shift that positions ideas – rather than learners – as the objects of change (cf. Sfard,

advance many different ideas of their choice in myr iad ways (e.g., Zhang et al., 2007). In addition, humanistic KBCs have also been shown to afford the co-construction of self and of ideas in humanistic learning environments that were inspired by the legacy of Carl Rogers (Hod & Ben-Zvi, 2018; Hod & Katz, 2020; Rogers, 1970). Thus, notwithstanding the different theoretical und erpinnings underlying problem-posing and KBCs, there exists a significant degree of overlap betwee n them, most notably in how they view learners, kno wledge, and the connection between the two. Both approaches value efforts to “rise above” (Scardamalia, 2002) specific real-world knowledge to inductively articulate broad and inclusive categories; both view knowledge and identity as dynamically and reflexively co-constituted; and both seek to transfer responsibility from teachers to learners. KBC design principles: Socio-cognitive and technological dynamics Scripted, procedure-based, pedagogical approaches p rovide detailed instructions for teachers and stude nts to follow. In contrast, principle-based pedagogies define “core values and principles” (Zhang et al., 201 1, p. 263), which teachers adapt to local contexts. The KBC approach is principle-based, offering teachers and researchers a set of twelve design principles that “serve an important regulative function for both teachers and students, helping to keep higher-level goals in mind” (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2014, p. 403) (cf. author, 2021). Each design principle comprises socio-cognitive and technological dynamics. Socio-cognitive dynamics are actualized through different types of participant structures; i.e., the “different norms and right s for speaking that different group arrangements[…] afford” (Micha els et al., 2004, p. 2355). Technological dynamics are embodied in the design of The Knowledge Forum (KF),  an proprietary online tool designed to support KBC s (Scardamalia, 2017). Because it is principle-based, enacting the KBC approach in a given context is challenging. However, the existing set of principles and technology means that the KBC approach is more readily applicable than Freire’s notion of problem-posing education, which is theoretical and abstract. The KBC design pr inciples and much of the research surrounding them can be viewed as Principled Practical Knowledge, “which […] unlike formal theoretical knowledge, its main purpose is not explanation or prediction but practical guidance” (Bereiter, 2014, p. 6). Freire’s work, in contrast, is theoretical and focused on explanation rather than on implementation. Research focus The purpose of KBCs has seldom been defined in term s of critical pedagogy. According to Scardamalia and Bereiter, the value of KBCs is primarily epistemic, in that they promote the type of design thinking required for “an ‘innovation-driven’ society” (Scardamalia & Ber eiter, 2016, p. 3), or in their ability to mediate authentic scientific discourse (Bereiter et al., 1997). However, we conjectured that the KBC design could also be harnessed to achieve some of the goals of problem-posing education. Specifically, this article asks how the socio-cognitive and technological dynamics detailed in the KB design principles rise above, real ideas and authentic problems, and collective responsibility for community knowledge, can mediate the features of students-learners?

## Method

General approach The empirical data presented herein comes from an exploratory case study (Yin, 2009) intended to flesh out some of the ways in which the goals of critical pedagogy  might be advanced by means of the KBC design. Give n the lack of any existing theoretical or empirical work, the primary aim of our investigation was to pave the path for future research on how KBCs might be harnessed in support of the goals of critical pedagogy.

## Participants

We collaborated with a tenth-grade class in Central  Israel comprising 25 students and their teacher, A mos (all names are pseudonyms). All the students identified as Jewish, and none were outwardly religious. Students came from diverse cultural, racial, and socioeconomic backgrounds. The school was publicly funded and belonged to the Jewish state-secular stream. Most of the students were unacquainted with each other or with the teacher. Curricular content Emancipation and oppression through learning are both tied to “everyday, informal, and popular culture and how the historical patterns of power that inform such cultures are imbricated in the formation of individual subjectivity and identity” (McLaren, 1994, p. 21). In Israel, th e discourse surrounding race, nationality, religion, socioeconomic status, and other social categories i s often dominated by the larger question of what it  means to belong to the Jewish majority, or to a non-dominant minority, most notably Palestinian or Arab-Israeli. Israel is a Jewish state, but being Jewish means different things to different people, and can serve as a catch-all phrase that is unconsciously associated with the elements of one’s identity that are most valued. Given the fluid definition of Jewish identity, its position at the crux of Israel ’s identity discourse, and its immediate ties to everyday life, we conjectured that exploring what it means in a KBC environment could offer students an emancipatory experience. The curriculum highlighted several points. First, that definitions of identity can overlap, complement, or contradict one another. Second, Jewish identity is not abstract; it shapes everyday life in Israel. Third, questions of identity are inescapable, but answers are always provisional. Finally, that identity discourse inevitably leads  to marginalization. Students read op-eds and news articles, philosophical and legal texts, and additional resources.

## Participant structures

Roughly a third of the time was dedicated to group meetings and the remaining two-thirds were spent in  small groups. For Group meetings the students and teacher sat in a circle. Sometimes, participants discussed a new assignment or recent progress; and other times, group meetings were dedicated to personal conversations in which students reflected on their experiences and established trust and openness in the learning community. Small groups, usually numbering 2-4 students, typically gathered around one laptop as they read assignments, discussed various themes, and co-authored new notes that were added to KF. Knowledge Forum Our analysis requires explicating some basic functions of KF: Notes are the most basic type of contribution to KF. Notes include a title, co-authors and timestamp, scaffolds, and the note’s content. Scaffolds are tags students select from a sidebar to bracket a segment of text in thei r note, which help ensure discourse is productive. Build on allows students to add a new note in response to an  existing one. Rise above notes have all the functionality of standard notes, while also allowing users to drag-a nd-drop multiple existing notes into one space. Views are individual webpages, which begin as an empty canvas to which students can add notes, images, links, or files. Data Collection Data included all notes from KF; students’ end-of-year essays; video recordings of 25 lessons; and unstructured interviews with the teacher and students. The author attended all the lessons and took field notes throughout. Selection and analysis We drew on Sandoval (2014), who distinguished betwe en the embodiment and mediation phases in DBR. To answer our research question, we sifted through the data, seeking out rich points (Agar, 1994, p. 232). For each rich point we selected, we conducted a detailed micro-analysis of what transpired in the mediation phase, asking what students did, wrote, or said; what motivated t hem; and what other alternatives were available. We  then reflected on the mediating role of the curricular content, participant structures, and KF, which together constituted the essence of the embodiment phase, and asked how the design impacted the learning experience.

## Findings

Elevated awareness For the first assignment, the class was provided links to ten online news articles that illustrated how Jewish identity shapes everyday life in Israel. Articles touched on race, ethnicity, religion, and more. After splitting into groups and contributing ten notes to KF that summarized the articles, students were given the following instructions: ICLS2022 Proceedings 38 © ISLS [note title] Assignment #2: rising above [author] Amos [the teacher] [date] 11/12/2018 [note body] One of the most crucial steps in knowledge building is called “rising above”. Now that we have made some progress it’s time to stop a nd think, so we can take the knowledge we’ve built to the next level… If you haven’t done so already, read all the notes summarizing news articles. Each note discusses a different case, but some of them are connected. Join with another group or two whose notes’ content is connec ted to yours. From 10 groups, you now must create no more than 4 groups in total. After creating a new group add a “rise above” note that takes our knowledge to the next level… One group of students summarized an article about a controversy surrounding Ethiopian immigration to Israel. The Jewish community in Ethiopia is divided between Beta Israel, whose customs and rituals are outwardly Jewish, and the Falasha Mura, whose ancestry conver ted to Christianity. Because Israeli law grants aut omatic citizenship to all Jews except those who converted to a different religion, Beta Israel Ethiopians are  typically allowed to immigrate to Israel and Falasha Mura are not. Another group read an article about Yoram Kaniuk, an Israeli author who won a heated court battle after demanding that he be identified as a “Jew of no religion”. The four members of the two groups joined together and co-authored a new rise-above note (scaffolds italicized): [note title] Rise-above [authors] Ron, Eli, Zack, Shani [note body] merging ideas We decided to merge the articles “Falasha Mura” and “A religiousless man” because both talk about “who is a Jew” – under what circumstances someone is considered Jewish. In the first article, the Falasha Mura… are seeking recognition as Jews from the State. In the second article Yoram Kaniuk… is a sking the court to be recognized as nonJewish. Therefore, the articles are about the same principle of [recognizing] who is a Jew, yet each of them has an opposite view about the Jewish religion. In this case, the move towards elevated awareness  was significant. By seeking out the common denominator between the articles, the students were  able to articulate a central theme in identity dis course – namely, mutual recognition (Ricoeur, 2005). This theme was new to them, but i t became central to the class discourse especially as some students went on to explicitly demand recognition for themselves or for marginalized groups in Israeli society. This development was mediated by the tools and participant structures embodied in the KBC. KF allowed students to drag-and-drop their sep arate ideas into a shared space, while scaffolds in dicated that this was a desirable knowledge building move [authors, 2020]. The participant structures (small groups that merged into larger ones) likewise mediated the process. Merging the groups, merging the notes, and merging the content, were three processes that were all embodied into the design of the KBC, and together they mediated an important facet of students-teachers.

Students were asked to form groups, select and read one text, and add a note to KF summarizing key takeaways. In addition, participant structures i ntentionally created an atmosphere of trust where learners were willing to share intimate details about their own lives. Indeed, Lia, Amber, Ella, Neta and others all felt comfortable voicing their opinions about the design of the KBC, regardless of whether they were happy with  the norms or not. However, this episode also illum inates two challenges facing KBC designers who are oriented towards problem-posing. First, sharing responsibility with students is unlikely to mitigate disruptions. In th is case, Amos felt compelled to assert himself as a n authority figure despite the communal atmosphere and his opening comment “we do everything together”. Second, shared responsibility also means various ideas that cannot necessarily be reconciled. Although their status was officially equal, Ella was a more central figure than the other three students, and as a result, her views were most influential. Revoking authority from the teacher created a power vacuum but did not necessarily mean that the environment was more equitable. The KBC ended up being emancipatory for Ella, but perhaps less so for Lia and Amber.

## Discussion: Directions for future research

Our findings show how the design of a KBC mediated the features of Freire’s conception of students-teachers. In each of the three episodes, the content, participan t structures, and technological affordances of KF w orked in concert to mediate some concrete goals of critical pedagogy among students. Yet, although our study was informative, demonstrat ing the efficacy of the KBC approach as a means for accomplishing ethical aims, the rich points tha t we analyzed left us with a series of lingering qu estions that must be further explored. While Freire’s model aspires for a form of praxis that combines reflection and action, our analysis of how rising above mediated elevated awareness was limited to reflection, begging the question of whether KBCs might mediate action as well, and if so, how? Mediation of the co-construction of knowledge and i dentity through the principal real ideas and authentic problems was achieved by means of content and participant s tructures but was not supported by the technological affordances of KF. What new features might be introduced to future versions of KF to support this kind of identity work, which is crucial for problem-posing? And finally, relinquishing authority and distributing responsibility evenly between teachers and students was partial and problematic. First, because although the teacher intended to “do everything together”, he felt the need to assume an authoritative role as soon as two  students disrupted the lesson by arriving late. And second, ICLS2022 Proceedings 41 © ISLS because the power vacuum that he left behind was not filled by the students in an equitable way, but rather enabled one student to claim a leadership role based on her  charisma and determination. Thus, the question of how to distribute power equitably in KBC remains open and requires further investigations and contemplation.

## Conclusion

The “political turn” in the learning sciences has led increasingly more scholars to recognize that their work should take ethical considerations explicitly into account. Indeed, some have concluded that in addition to a dvancing learners’ knowledge, skills, or expertise, their scholarship should also focus on achieving ethical aims. But doing so does not necessarily require them to go back to the drawing board to design new learning environments from scratch. On the contrary, this paper shows how exis ting scholarship can be modified or repurposed to d esign educational innovations that seek to promote social justice and other values.
