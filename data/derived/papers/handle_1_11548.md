# Equitable Classrooms through Learning Spaces

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This literature review examines how the spatial organization of learning environments reproduces inequities, particularly in STEM education, by maintaining exclusionary practices that disproportionately affect students from historically marginalized communities. By integrating historical and contemporary research and a DisCrit Classroom Ecologies framework to analyze the physical layout of STEM classrooms, the paper explores how these spatial arrangements are linked to student learning, feelings of belonging, and persistence in STEM fields.

## Introduction

I posit that the spatial organization of a school-be it micro-level  in a single classroom, macro-level of the layout of the full campus, or community level of a  distri ct-is one of these grammars of schooling that has beco me so ingrained in the US  educational experience that it  goes unnoticed and unquestioned, maintaining undes irable issues  of equity in the educational spaces meant t o empower and uplift all students to their full  ac ademic potential. STEM standards tend to focus on space and materials, which means that  when it comes to actual implementation of STEM programs, there is a strong financial barrier to  equitable engagement in STEM across communities to who’s public schools may lack the  a llocation flexibility and/or raw funding to support  strong STEM programs (Le & Orlando, n.d.;  Philip & Gupta,  2020; Tribal Colleges as STEM Leaders, 2014) This manifestation of educational inequities in STEM  classrooms in particular has been shown to have lasting impacts on feelings of belonging and  thus persistence and engagement in STEM fields for students from histori cally marginalized  communities (e. g. Bell et al., 2017; Calabrese Barton et al., 2013; Cheryan et al., 2011; Gosling et al., 2002; Vossoughi et al., 2016).

Using historical and recent academic and practition er/public facing literature on the effects of physi cal layout and appearance of STEM classrooms the following questions are addressed: How are student learning and learning environments connected? How are inequities in STEM reproduced by learning environments? Framework Using a sociocultural approach, I take Gutierrez (2002) definition of equity in mathematics education and expand it to all of STEM contexts. This definition of equi ty takes into account the interconnected nature  of educators, students, communities, and policy to define equity as a construct measurable on  many different temporal and structural scales. To this, I add the element of in tentionality in facilitat ing more justice-oriented approaches to STEM teaching-particularly justice oriented learning experiences for students-acting as agents of change within their own educational spaces, working towards equitable and rigorous outcomes (Madkins et al., 2020). I narrow this sociocultural view through Annamma and Morrison’s (2018) theory of DisCrit Classroom Ecologies. They propose that DisCrit Classroom Ecology as ba sed on the following tenants. DisCrit resistance calls for re-imagining and defining what schools call for and wa ys to attribute value to the knowledge and experiences students, especially multiply marginali zed ones, bring to the classroom.  DisCrit curriculum centers the ways that multiply-marginalized people have pushed  back against unjust systems both in historical and present-day contexts. DisCrit pedagogy is committed to reframing teacher notions of dis/ab ility in their classrooms through explicit enactment of pedagogical approaches and perspectives that  counter deficit narratives of student skills, knowledge and abilities. These p edagogies push  back against tracking and segregation for multiply-marginalized Students of Color. Finally, DisCrit solidarity which focuses on the idea of mutual trust and power in the classroom, shared between teachers and students. It argues that classroom interactions and climates built upon the idea that students must be “managed” by their teachers create dysfunctional learning ecosystems.

## Methods

Literature was selected through snowball sampling, beginning with several key pieces: Annamma and Morrison (2018), Gosling et al. (2002), Renzulli and Evans, (2005), and Bell et al., (2017). Practitioner/publi c facing pieces were identified through EBSCO search using a university library database search. Sampled papers were coded for “identity”, “belonging”, “physical space”, “decoration”, “digital space”, “segregation”, “te acher positioning” and “historical roots”. These codes we re both emergent from the l iterature and based on DisCrit and Classroom Ecology theory. Codes were cross chec ked through multiple rounds of collaborator feedbac k. Major findings of papers were then sorted into the four DisCrit principles based on applicable codes. Analysis of literature Literature has shown that, despite its antiquated origins, eugenics has and continues to play a role in the formation of standards, assessments, and behavioral expectations (Winfield, 2012; Lowe, 1998). These form the underlying structure of much of the US education system and cr eate the perception that teachers must act as the h olders of knowledge and authority figures within a learning e nvironment. This foundation of eugenics ideas is bu ilt upon by legal and social  constructs such as  segregatio n and  exclusion to enforce unequal power dynamics (e.g. Donato & Hanson, 2012; San Miguel, 1983). Through the DisCrit Solidarity frame, these underlyi ng structures prevent meaningful engagement in STEM for students because without mutual trust and power between students and teachers. Educators can aim to create a classroom environment of  mutual  trust and power in  the classroom through  acknowledging the cultural-historical foundations of issues of equity in  educational spaces. DisCrit Resistance provides structure to the relati onship between learner  identity, physical  layout, spatial  positioning and classroom design. DisCrit Pedagogies can be co-enacted to address issues of access to learning. These principles call for re-imagining what a learning space where everyone’s ident ity is respected could look like. A number of researchers have examined how the appearance of a classroom-such as the pictures on the walls and other environmental cues impact perceptions of learning spaces. The work of Weinstein (1977;

1979) serves as the baseline for our understanding of how the physical classroom setting impacts learn ing and

behavior within the classroom. Classroom appearance  such as lighting, space and decor are especially a pparent in schools serving lower income students. Additionally, the arrangement of  learning spaces plays  a role  in determining  engagement (Kuller & Lindstern, 1992; Tanic et al., 2020). For example, physical spaces, such as offices and bedrooms have been observed  to influence personality judgments, suggesting that  environmental cues play a significant role in shaping social perceptions (Gosling et al., 2002). In another study, which examined how the design of virtual classrooms can influence gender disparities in computer science education, Cheryan et al. (2011) suggests that classroom environments play a key role in students' disciplinary engagement, which has been shown to be linked to student sense of belonging in STEM spaces (Bell et al., 2017). Madkins et al. (2020) argue that traditional teaching methods in computer  science often overlook the cultural, social, and c ommunity factors that influence students' learning experiences, leading to unequal opportunities for success. This relates to the placement of the teacher, the groupings of students, the type of seating available and more (e.g. Garrett, 2008; Tanic et al., 2020; Philip & Gupta, 2020). Pedagogi es related to the execution of a given, disciplinar y specific curriculum are also important to recognize as certain STEM fields  have greater equity  issues based on cost  and necessity of  materials (Bang et al., 2012; Madkins  et al., 2020).Overall, these studies and others like them (e.g. Tanic et al., 2020; Weinstein, 1977; Bang & Marin, 2015; Vossoughi et al., 2016) suggest Space contrib utes to the student sense of  belonging in STEM  and their prolonged  engagement through reflecting the community the educational space services.

The broader school environment can be viewed through DisCrit Curriculum which manifests in the ways communities have pushed back against de facto and d e jure  segregation in education through  time whic h  has influenced the geographical spatial relationshi p  between learning and  equity. Studies across the  US have noted that segregation continues to persist via cultural attitudes, family choice, and  by government  action/inaction. For example, Renzulli and Evans (2005) found that schoo l choice and charter schools play a key role in enc ouraging white flight and the continuation of racial and cla ss segregation in education through creating and ma intaining culturally and racially distinct communities. Addit ionally, educators themselves have been shown to misappropriate their student’s cultures and further marginalize them through misconstruing and misunderstanding the communities in which they teach (Philip et al.,  2013). DisCrit Curriculum helps explain the ways t hat communities can combat educational inequities throu gh explaining how school quality and  community distribution are connected (McKinney de Royston & Vossoughi, 2021).

## Conclusions

Student learning and learning environments are deep ly interconnected through the physical, social, and  cultural aspects of a learning space. Inequities in STEM education are often reproduced and reinforced through learning environments that fail to acknowledge or address th e factors that affect both student engagement and a cademic success.  Learning spaces should be designed to giv e students equal access to resources, opportunities  for collaboration, and the ability to engage in active learning. Educators must go beyond traditional classroom layouts that may unintentionally reinforce power dynamics to create spaces that facilitate student-centered learning, foster a sense of belonging, and acknowledge the diverse cultural and social identities students bring with them.
