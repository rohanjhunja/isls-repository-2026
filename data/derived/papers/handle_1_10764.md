# A Qualitative Comparison of Mathematics Teachers’ and Students’ Experiences in a Virtual Reality Algebra Application

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Prisms VR, a secondary math learning application, allows for users to see, manipulate, and engage with mathematical concepts in an embodied way in Virtual Reality (VR) environment. We examine cases in which mathematics teachers and middle school students worked through Prisms and reflected upon their experiences. Findings indicate that VR environments can help teachers find alternative ways of presenting algebraic concepts in a hands-on, interactive, and embodied manner; and these findings were confirmed based on the student experience. VR allows teachers to make connections through scaffolding and personalized experiences, while also aiding students in the exploration and meaning-making.

## Introduction

In mathematics education, virtual reality (VR) has the potential to create an interactive, embodied, a nd engaging learning experience capable of helping students und erstand and visualize concepts (Lindgren & Johnson-Glenberg, 2013). VR goggles immerse users in a dynamic 3D virtual environment, enabling direct interaction and manipulation of digitized elements through intuitive hand controls, such as pinching fingers to adjust a graph with real-time feedback. VR's link to embodied cognition theo ries may enha nce its role in advancing mathematics education (Walkington et al., 2023), enabling users to "embody" the learning material. Through simulated actions, concepts become tangible (Abrahamson et al., 2020),  connecting to users' prior knowledge and real-world experiences (Bujak et al., 2013; Johnson-Glenberg et al., 2014). Literature indicates VR's p otential to positively impact user learning experience and mathematical un derstanding (Huang et al., 2023; Walkington et al.,  2023; Washington et al., accepted). Further research is n ecessary to understand teachers' and students' expe rience and offer guidance for VR’s potential classroom use (Wa shington et al., accepted; Walkington et al., 2021). Addressing these gaps, we conducted two studies analyzing teachers' and students' mathematical reasoning using the VR application, Prisms VR. Our analysis encompassed verbal communication, ac tions, and manipulations of virtual objects. Our research questions are:

1) What opportunities does immersive VR offer for embodied math learning, for teachers and students?

2) What are teachers’ and students’ perceptions of immersive VR activities for math learning?

## Theoretical framework: Embodiment and actions

Previous research suggests VR's affordances, such a s immersion and gestural congruency (Bujak et al., 2013; Johnson-Glenberg et al., 2014). Our framework highlights th e utilization of actions and embodiment as integral components of VR technologies. Actions are crucial for facilitating comprehension, visually representi ng symbolic or dynamic movements (McNeill, 1992). Symbolic actions involve using body or hand motions to create visual representations (Walkington et al., 2023), like illustrating a parabola by curving fingers together. Dynamic actions demonstrate changes, such as enlarging the parabola (Walkington et al., 2023). In VR, gestures effectively render concepts more concrete through dynamic visua lizations (Lindgren & Johnson-Glenberg, 2013). Embodiment, tied to bodily movements, is crucial for know ledge enactment (Abrahamson & Lindgren, 2014; Alibali & Nathan, 2012). In VR, embodiment involves  physical movements or connections with real-world contexts to enhance understanding (Abrahamson & Sánchez-García, 2016). Research underscores the importance of embodied experiences in mathematics education, with gestures playing a significant role (Nathan et al., 2014). However, the potential of VR in facilitating embodiment and gesture in mathematics remains underexplored.

## Methods of inquiry

This study involved 11 in-service mathematics teachers (study 1) and nine middle-school students (study 2), using the Prisms VR app with Meta Oculus 2 goggles. Teachers were enrolled in graduate-level Geometry and Algebraic Reasoning courses at a private university, with teaching experience ranging from 1 to 12 years. The teachers were racially diverse, with six White, four Black, and one Asian, and seven females, three males, and one n on-binary individual. Teachers engaged in a VR simulation of a viral pandemic outbreak, followed by analyzing the spread in a lab using exponential equations. They explored  how infection rates varied with different behavior s. Data collection occurred during a three-hour class session, including pre-surveys, VR activities, and post-surveys. For study 2, three of the middle school students had co mpleted eighth grade, and six had completed seventh  grade. The participants were comprised of seven Hispanic s tudents and two Black students, with six females and three males. These students engaged in simulations in Pri sms VR, manipulating a satellite orbiting the ear th and controlling a train's deceleration to understand pr e-algebraic concepts. Using VR goggles, they complete d two lessons over four sessions. They answered questions about their experiences afterward. All participants wore VR headsets, with video record ings capturing their interactions in the environmen t, transcribed later. Thematic analysis, guided by Saldana (2020), was employed. U sing MAXQDA, responses were categorized into positi ve, neutral, and negative (Tables 2-3). Video was examined for actions, gestures, and m ovements using multimodal analysis techniques (Walkington et al., 2023; McNeill, 1992).

## Results

In study 1, virtual avatars (VRS in Figure 1) guide d users through tasks. In Figure 1, teachers calculated viral spread, considering conditions like mask-wearing and social distancing (Line 1-2, P1), while noticing uninfected individuals (Line 2). The environment used spatiall y instantiated representations, enabling teachers t o identify patterns (Line 3-6, P2) and explore alternative models (Line 7-8, P3). Lastly, they plotted functions to calculate new infections for any given week (Line 9, P3), transitioning from concrete spatial concepts to equations. Teacher data (Table 1) revealed an overall positive experience (83%), citing enjoyment and transformative potential ("Easy to adjust to and fun to play with" or "It can shift  their entire reality to be in the middle of a real-world math problem. This makes the classroom feel less like a classroom and more like real life."). However, challenges were noted, with a small percentage feeling neutral (0.9 0%) and a larger proportion (16%) encountering diff iculties, primarily related to logistical issues and integration in classrooms. ## Discussion and conclusion

This study suggests that VR environments offer teachers alternative ways to present algebraic concepts, fostering interactive and embodied learning experiences (Abra hamson et al., 2020; Lindgren & Johnson-Glenberg, 2013). Students benefit from VR's ability  to aid in exploring and understanding algebraic con cepts and  clarifying misconceptions through interactive manipulation and  visual aids. Both students and teachers reported p ositive experiences with VR, emphasizing its potential for creativity and enjoyment. However, some users may encounter challenges, including difficulty with instructions or understanding the math without guidance (Washington et al.; accepted). Additional barriers to VR adoption inclu de cost, accessibility, and time constraints. In ou r studies, participants expressed a need for more time for exp loration, reflection, and collaboration. Future res earch directions inc lude leveraging VR's collaborative features and inv estigating users' sense-making processes, particularly through embodied, gestural, and collaborative experiences.
