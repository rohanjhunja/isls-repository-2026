# Exploring the Similarities in Identity of Adolescents Based on Psychological, Social, and Environmental Factors

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
The study explores adolescent identity development across psychological, social, and environmental factors in the USA, Ghana, and Kenya. It analyzes pre-assessment data from a virtual STEM program, focusing on how adolescents navigate self-understanding, resilience, and emotional growth (psychological factors), peer relationships and leadership (social factors), and the role of school and STEM exposure (environmental factors). The findings reveal both universal and culturally specific influences, showing that while the developmental processes are similar, each region's unique context shapes how identity is formed.

## Introduction

The adolescent years are a critical period for iden tity development, marked by hormone-driven changes in physical appearance and the social and academic shifts from primary to secondary school (Afful et al., 2021; Dahl and Forbes, 2010). During this time, adolescents question their self-concept, sense of belonging, and future goals, while navigating personal reflections, social interactions, and cultural influences, which are seen as psychological, social, and environmental factors, respectively (Azmitia et al., 2023; Benson & Bundick, 2015). Altogether, these factors help shape identity development in adolescents. From a psychological standpoint, personality traits form internal identity (Crocetti et al., 2023; Tajfel & Turner, 1979). This allows oneself to determine how  they see themselves. For instance, exhibiting high  levels of empathy and a strong sense of self has been associa ted with positive social relationships and the ability to ad apt to varying social environments, both of which contr ibute to identity development  (Doumen et al., 2012; Adams et al., 1985). From a social standpoint, behavioral attributes allow oneself to see how others view them (Tajfel & Turner, 1979). Adolescents may modify their behavio r, to impact how others view them as an individual and determine their thoughts regarding their abilities and ca pabilities. For instance, developing positive peer relationships is an important aspect in identity de velopment, as it serves as a role for adolescents t o experience emotional support and gives rise to identity explor ation (Pugh & Hart, 1999; Nawaz, 2011). From an environmental perspective, an adolescent's surroundings play a significant role in shaping their sense of belonging (Prioste et al., 2020). School, where adolescents spend a substantial amount of time interacting with teachers and peers, is particu larly influential in identity development (Verhoeve n et al., 2019). Being part of a school community fosters a sense of significance and enhan ces self-worth, making it a key factor in the environmental influences on identity formation.  In this research study, we explore the similarities  in the identity of adolescents in the USA, Ghana, and Kenya based on psychological, social, and environmental factors.

## Methods

This study focuses on participants in a virtual, co llaborative STEM informal learning program for adol escents from schools across various countries. The program enables students worldwide to collaborate on STEM projects while creating media that not only s howcases their final outcomes but also documents th e process behind their work. The data analyzed in this study consists of individual pre-assessment interviews taken of 27 adolescent students from the US, Ghana, and Kenya from March  to December 2023 prior to their engagement with the program. While this paper focuses solely on the pre-assessment data and does not explore the program's role in identity development, the context of the program is  crucial. Students self-select to participate, which means they are likely already inclined toward STEM, influencing the dynamics of their engagement. We utilized Braun and Clarke’s (2006) approach to c onduct a thematic analysis of the interviews.  Each interview was transcribed and reviewed separately by two researchers and then a smaller portion of the interviews were coded independently by four raters who followed a process of social moderation to reach final consensus on the coded data (Herrenkohl & Cornelius, 2013). The remaining interviews were independently coded by two raters using the same social moderation process, and then the four re-convened to develop themes to characterize the similarities in identity of the students based on psychological, social, and environmental factors.

## Results

Focusing on key psychological, social, and environmental factors that shape identity, seven themes were identified to provide insights into how adolescents are navigating identity formation. Psychological factors Three themes described the psychological factors of identity development: Struggle for Self-understanding, Development of Resilience, and Emotional Growth. Across all regions, students were actively struggling with questions about who they are and what they value for self-understanding. Many students displayed confidence in certain aspects of their identity  (e.g., leadership or STEM abilities) while acknowle dging uncertainties in others (e.g., public speaking or peer relationships).  For example, one Ghanaian student reflected, “I'm Strong in chemistry, biology umm. But the calculations I'm go od, but not with speed like when it comes to calcul ating fast. I can get the answer, but it may take a while yes. But with the sciences and physics as well I'm ok.” Across all regions, students demonstrated a strong sense of resilience, often describing their ability to overcome academic, social, or personal challenges through persistence, reflection, adaptab ility, and seeking support from support systems. Emotional self-awareness, such as recognizing shyness, nervousness, or frustration, was consistently expressed. Students also emphasize d their efforts to manage emotions and adopt coping strategies.

Social factors Two themes explained  the social factors of identity development:  Peer Relationships and Role of Leadership. Across all groups, peer relationships were central to identity development. Students sought respect, understanding, and camaraderie in group dynamics, and collaboration was viewed as essential, even though many acknowledged struggles with personality differences or conflicts. For example, one student reflected on being chosen to be a member of the national science team in their school, “when they selected me to be part of the team. Yeah. It really had a very huge impact on me. It made me feel like I have more capabilities and I can really improve myself in a way more better. I can produce the best possible fo rm of myself because I can really do great things t o my capabilities yeah. So ever since I joined the team that really impacted me positively, giving me the m orale, the morale to continue to learn. Yeah.”  Leadership emerged as a shared value, with students  aspiring to lead by example, support peers, or influence their communities. Environmental factors Two themes defined the environmental factors connected to identity development: School as a Developmental Arena and Impact of STEM Exposure. Schools provided a critical environment for explorin g identity, offering academic challenges, social interactions, and leade rship opportunities.  In Ghana, the secondary school is a boarding school, which offers its own unique development. For example, one student lamented about an inability to go out to study and other students in the dormit ories being too loud, “Okay. What I think is limiting when it comes to school is the fact that girls are not allo wed to trip out late. And I find this funny, but and the girls dormitories have a wall around it that girls have to be in the dorms at certain times and if. You can't study in the dorm and you need to be outside, but here's the cas e, we can't be outside. So I think I find that limi ting because there's so much noise in the dorm.”  STEM-related experiences (e.g., science fairs, projects,  or extracurricular programs) were transformative for students in all regions, fostering curiosity and shaping academic aspirations.

## Discussion

The students across all regions are navigating adolescent years  through similar pathways of exploration and validation; however, their psychological, social, and environmental contexts  shape distinct approaches. Within psychological factors, Ghanaian and Kenyan students  reflect humility and spirituality, while U.S. stud ents focus on self-expression and emotional resilience. Within social factors, Ghanaian and Kenyan students lean on family and cultural norms, while U.S. students emphasize peer dynamics and individuality. Within environmental factors, educational experi ences are pivotal everywhere, but resource availabi lity and cultural influences vary widely. These findings highlight the interplay between univ ersal developmental processes and culturally specif ic influences, underscoring the richness of identity formation in diverse contexts.
