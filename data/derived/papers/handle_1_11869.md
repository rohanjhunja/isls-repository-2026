# Generative AI-Supported Collaboration in Interdisciplinary Research Practice

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
We report an empirical study of a three-day workshop on interdisciplinary research practice by 14 doctoral students supported by generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) to investigate how they use GenAI for their knowledge-building. They formed five teams and conducted presentations on promising interdisciplinary research proposals. Two high-performing teams’ discourses were analyzed in detail using a mixed-methods approach. We found that 1) students assigned four roles to GenAI (e.g., theme generation), 2) the frequency of students’ GenAI use was considerably limited, and 3) while GenAI use was limited to the initial theme establishment phase, students engaged in idea improvement by effectively integrating GenAI’s ideas. These findings suggest that the most difficult part of interdisciplinary collaboration for doctoral students is setting boundary objects in the initial phase, where GenAI can well-function. This study demonstrated that knowledge creators, such as doctoral students, can effectively collaborate using GenAI to create quality interdisciplinary collaborative research proposals.

## Introduction

One of the essential research questions in interdis ciplinary education programs is “How can we design for productive learning across and beyond disciplines?”  (Markauskaite et al., 2024, p.2). Studies have designed technology-mediated interdisciplinary learning environment to address this question (e.g., Kidron & Kali, 2015; Muukkonen & Kajamaa, 2024). For example, Muukkonen and Kajamaa (2024) reported that digital technologies (e.g., spreadsheets and mobile phones) mediate interdisciplinary learni ng Further, Naganuma et al. (2024) used generative artificial intelligence  (GenAI)—an emerging technology —to create interdisciplinary collaborative research themes using students’ research informatio n, on which  students collaborated to evaluate AI-generated themes and improve promising ones. This line of study suggests that productive interdisciplinary learning can be facilitated by preparing technology-mediated learning settings that can embrace learning as knowledge creation. However, with some exceptions (e.g., Muukkonen & Kajamaa  2024), the interdisciplinary learning context has not been sufficiently discussed in the CSCL field. Specifically, collaboration in AI-supported learning settings wherein students can use GenAI (Borge et al., 2024) in interdisciplinary education in higher education institutions has not been explored. Therefore, we developed a GenAI-supported collaborative learning setting for interdisciplinary research practice and investigated how students collaborated with GenAI. Theoretical orientations We considered GenAI a broker, an actor that crosses boundaries to bridge the gap between differ ent activity systems (Akkerman & Bakker, 2011). The design principle, “breaking boundaries between disciplines” (Kali, 2024), indicates that boundary object—a jointly constructed object among multiple activity systems that allows those systems to effectively collaborate beyond boundaries (Engeström, 2022) — is one of the central concepts in interdisciplinary education. This theoretical orientation originated from our pilot study (Naganuma et al., 2024), wherein GenAI was employed to assist students by providing candidate research themes as boundary objects. Two modes of learning  were also focused. Learners use the belief mode  to investigate their current community knowledge level to highlight problems, and the focal question is, “Is it true?” Meanwhile, learning in the design mode enables learners to create knowledge to solve probl ems, and the focal question is, “How can we make it better?” (Oshima et al., 202 0; Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2017). In a well-functioning knowledge-creating community, both modes coexist (Bereiter & Scardamalia, 2016). Students’ modes of using GenAI are noteworthy in the context of human–AI collaboration in interdisciplinary research practice. Purpose of the present study We aimed to investigate how knowledge creators, such as doctoral students from various disciplines, collaborated in GenAI-supported interdisciplinary knowledge-building (KB) discourse. We set the two research questions: RQ1. What roles do students assign GenAI in their interdisciplinary research practice? RQ2. How do GenAI’s outputs contribute to students’ KB discourse in interdisciplinary research practice?

## Study context

Fourteen doctoral students from various academic departments in a funding program at the first author’s university voluntarily p articipated in a three-day workshop. It was conducted between September and October 2024 at a national university in Japan and comprised eight sessions, with each lasting 90 min. Four main activities composed our workshop. After orientation (Sessions 1-2), firstly, they were assigned to groups and judged the promisingness of AI-generated themes twice as exploratory collaboration  experiences (Sessions 3-4). Second, they talked with others through four rounds until the final team for mation (Session 5). Third, teams created research proposals (Sessions 6-7). Finally, each team gave a 10-minute presentation and was assessed by five expert s (Session 8). From Session 5 to 8, the use of GenAI was not mandated but entirely left to learners’ agency. AI technology We prepared two AI technologies  for students’ use in either belief mode or design mode. First, we developed Interdisciplinary Collaborative Research Theme Gene rator (ICRTG), which can generate interdisciplinary collaborative research themes (Figure 1a). When users upload the necessary information (e.g., name, doctoral research title, abstract) and assign students’ group, it generates a spreadsheet with seven collaborative research theme candidates as a default.

Secondary, Research Assistant-GPT (RA-GPT) is created by Dify (dify.ai). This GPT contains several apps (e.g., PubMed, dalle) and has a ChatGPT-like user interface  (Figure 1b). It enables students to search  for information, find academic literature, draw illustrations, and perform web scraping.

## Data collection

During the research proposal activity  from session 6 to 8, we collected students’ discussion data (audio recordings), online chat data on Slack, voluntary online meeting records (if any), and presentations. Five experts with experience in interdisciplinary education eval uated students ’ final presentations  using the following f ive criteria on a scale of 0 to 10: a) The research proposal demonstrates importanc e, novelty, and originality. b) The research proposal exhibits interdisciplinary integration. c) The team comprises students from different departments (e.g., graduate schools, majors, laboratories) or specialt ies. d) Each member’s  mission is clearly defined in the research proposal’s execution. e) The research proposal’s content  is explained simply and clearly for researchers (reviewers) from different specialized fields.

## Data analysis

To answer RQ1, we transcribed the discourses of two high-performance teams (Teams A and B) from the final five teams. Team A comprised students specializing in media design, ergonomics, informatics, and law, whereas Team B  comprised students specializing in forestry, c oastal engineering, meteorology, and psychology. We arranged the transcribed audio and chat data chronologically to create an integrated discourse dataset. When the Figure 1 Images of Two AI Technologies AI’s output was mentioned orally by students or uploaded on chat, we considered that GenAI had participated in the discourse. Thereafter, we  qualitatively interpret ed how students intentionally used GenAI in their interdisciplinary collaboration, that is, the roles that students assigned to GenAI.

To address RQ2, a mixed-methods approach was employed. First, the Knowledge Building Discourse Explorer (KBDeX; Oshima et al., 2012) was utilized to quantitatively analyze how students engaged in idea improvement through interdisciplinary knowledge-building discourse using GenAI. We calculated the degree centrality coefficient—a measure of a network structure’s cohesiveness—and traced the changes in its coefficient over time. When they relate their idea(s) to another in the discourse, the total degree-centrality generally increases. In the analysis, we focused on the transition of the degree centrality of key ideas in the teams’ final presentations (i.e., target ideas). The authors selected the target ideas, focusing primarily on the nouns. Teams A and B had 36 and 32 target ideas, respectively. Results contain three lines: the transition of degree centrality on 1) all target ideas, 2) target ideas that students initiated in the ir discourse, and 3) target ideas initiated by  GenAI in the discourse. Second, we focused on the discourse turns when total degree  centrality fluctuated with GenAI’s outputs. Thereafter, we qualitatively examined how GenAI’s outputs contributed the interdisciplinary discourse.

## Results

To protect the doctoral students’ intellectual property, we refrained from demonstrating specific ideas and masked them, without losing nuances. For example, the target ideas were labeled as Idea 1, Idea 2, and so on. W e first counted the number of times students in the two teams used GenAI in the ir discourse (RQ1). Team A used GenAI 10 times out of 1,709 discourse turns, while Team B used GenAI three times out of 1,693 turns. In the limited use of GenAI, it newly introduced nine and two target ideas for Teams A and B, respectively. Second, GenAI’s roles assigned by the students were identified through qualitative analysis. Both teams used ICRTG to generate t hemes (i.e., theme generation) and asked RA-GPT to elaborate on the output (i.e., theme elaboration). Only Team A prompted RA-GPT to provide the division of labor for each member (i.e., provision of the division of labor) and confirm their ideas’ correctness (i.e., idea confirmation). ## Discussion

This empirical study aimed to investigate how human–AI collaboration can be conducted during interdisciplinary research practice among doctoral students. Concerning RQ1, the four roles that students assigned to GenAI in their interdisciplinary discourse were identified, while students’ number of uses of GenAI was considerably limited. Regarding RQ2, GenAI unquestionably contributed to students’ interdisciplinary collabor ation, particularly in the early phase, by setting themes as boundary objects. They only used GenAI in design mode learning (Bereiter & Scardamalia, 2003) to create research themes as boundary objects (i.e., new knowledge). As well-functioning knowledge-creating teams (Bereiter & Scardamalia, 2016), both Teams A and B might have opted to use GenAI only in the design mode, while guarant eeing belief-mode learning using only members’ expertise or other authoritative resources (e.g., research paper), which requires further study. The findings suggest that  the most difficult part of interdisciplinary collaboration for doctoral students is setting boundary objects. Therefore, they sought support from GenAI, especially in the early phase. After setting their boundary objects, they forwarded their activity to elaborate on their plan with their epistemic agency without GenAI’s support. For this design-mode use, even some uses of GenAI can provide sufficient information fo r doctoral students’ interdisciplinary collaboration because GenAI can output a large amount of informat ion; for example, ICRTG outputs seven themes at once. These results suggest that GenAI acted as a subagent or supporter, not as a leading agent or full participant (Sharples, 2023), occasionally inspiring students’ discourse by developing problem spaces and providing ideas that students could explore and use with their epistemic agency.

## Conclusion

Consistent with studies designing technology-mediated interdisciplinary learning environments  in higher education institutions, our study investigated the collaborative dynamics of doctoral  students using GenAI in an interdisciplinary knowledge-building discourse. We observed that high-performing teams effectively leveraged GenAI. Taken together, this study demonstrated that experts, such as doctoral students, can effectively collaborate with GenAI to create quality interdisciplinary collaborative research  proposals. Therefore, fostering competent knowledge creators who can proficiently utilize Gen AI as a collaborative tool will become increasingly  crucial across the K-16 and advanced educational landscapes.
