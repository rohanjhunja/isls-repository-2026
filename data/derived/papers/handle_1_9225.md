# Cognitive Analysis of Composite Instructional Designs: New Directions for Research on Problem-Solving Prior to Instruction

**Conference:** ISLS 2023

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In problem-solving prior to instruction (PS-I), an initial problem-solving phase is followed by an explicit instruction phase. Research on PS-I designs has produced conflicting results that cannot be explained by the learning mechanisms discussed so far in this line of research. The present paper applies a recently developed framework, aimed at – more generally – analyzing composite instructional designs (CID; i.e., designs that include multiple instructional phases) to PS-I research. The CID framework supports an understanding of PS-I effects by attending to the effects on intermediate knowledge (i.e., the knowledge state after the initial problem-solving phase) and discussing how this intermediate knowledge affects the learning processes that occur during subsequent explicit instruction. The analysis with CID also highlights that experimental variations of the instructional design (e.g., comparing PS-I with I-PS) often affect multiple learning processes and, thus, demonstrates the need for alternative research strategies when investigating the learning mechanisms of PS-I.

Problem-solving prior to instruction (PS-I) Problem-solving prior to instruction (PS-I) is an instructional design in w hich students are first asked to solve a problem targeting a yet unknown concept (often implemented in a collaborative setting; Brand et al., 2023; Mazziotti et al., 2019) before receiving instructional explanations about the underlying concept and the canonical problem solution (Kapur & Bielaczyc, 2012). Several studies have found positive ef fects of PS-I compared to I-PS (i.e., explicit instruction prior to problem solving) on the acquisition of conceptual knowledge (for overviews see Loibl et al., 2017; Sinha & Kapur, 2021).

However, the evidence from studies that compare the effects of variants  of the instructional design on learning outcomes is not yet conclusive: Sometimes PS-I is more effective  than I-PS, sometimes it is not (Loibl et al., 2017; Sinha & Kapur, 2021). Sometimes watching someone else solve a problem  (so-called vicarious problem solving) prior to explicit instruction is as effective as own problem s olving, sometimes it is not (e.g., Hartmann et al., 2022 vs. Kapur, 2014). Sometimes studying worked solutions prior to explicit instruction is more, sometimes less effective than problem solving (e.g., Glogger-Frey et al., 2015 vs. 2017). In a theoretical review, Loibl et al. (2017) identified three mechanisms that may explain the PS-I effect:

1) In the problem-solving phase, students activate their prior knowledge when generating solution ideas. 2) As

they usually do not come up with a canonical solution, they may become aware of their knowledge gaps. 3) This awareness, in turn, may help students to focus on the relevant features  when they receive instructional explanations. While these assumed mechanisms are grounded in the general literature on learning and instruction, they have not been studied in isolation in the context of PS-I. Thus, it is unc lear whether and at to what extend each of these mechanisms actually contributes to the PS-I effect. A key feature of PS-I is that it comprises two phases with different learning goals and different pedagogical approaches. Such composite designs are frequent in instruction – in research as well as in practice (Kunter, 2005; Prediger et al., 2021; Stigler et al., 1999). In order to resolve the c onflicting findings in PS-I research presented above, it seems relevant to better understand the under lying mechanisms resulting from the relationship between the two phases. An appropriate means to do so is a recently developed framework for the cognitive analysis of composite instructional designs (CID) (Loibl et al., subm.) which allows us to look beyond the instructional design level.

Framework for cognitive analysis of composite instructional designs (CID) Research on PS-I usually focuses on the level of instructional design by comparing PS-I with I-PS or different variants of PS-I and investigating the effect of these experimental variations  on learning outcomes (Loibl et al., 2017; Sinha & Kapur, 2021). Only few studies investigate the learning processes in PS-I, mostly by relying on data on the quality, quantity, and diversity of students’ solutions (e.g., Kapur & Bielazyc, 2012; Loibl & Rummel, CSCL 2023 Proceedings  © ISLS 322 2014a). However, a better understanding of the learning processes at play would be e ssential to provide explanations for the effects on learning outcomes. Koedinger et al. (2012) proposed th e so-called KnowledgeLearning-Instruction (KLI) framework. One of the intentions of the authors was to relate elements at the three levels of knowledge components, learning processes, and instructional design to provide a  better understanding of how instructional design elements stimulate learning processes and result in better learning outcomes. Building on this rationale, our recently developed framework for conducting cognitive ana lyses of composite instructional designs (CID; Loibl et al., subm.) links the three levels – (K) knowledge components, (L) learning processes, and (I) instructional design – across multiple phases (see Figure 1; Loibl et al., subm.). Analyses which apply the CID framework highlight that changing the instructiona l design of the first phase (I 1) changes the learning processes in this phase (L 1), resulting in different intermediate knowledge (K 1) (i.e., the knowledge state after the first phase, in PS-I after the initial problem-solving phase). This intermediate knowledge, in turn, is the starting point for the subsequent phase. As the learning proc esses in the subsequent phase (L 2) are not only affected by the instructional design of the second phase (I 2), but also build on the knowledge resulting from the first phase (K 1), the learning processes (L 2) necessarily unfold differently after different instructional designs of the first phase.

We sugges t that research on the learning mechanisms underlying PS-I could benefit from incorporating the following two steps: (1) specifying the elements of the CID framework for the proposed PS-I design, and (2) analyzing (theoretically and empirically) the changes that result at all three levels when implementing variations of the PS-I des ign. By analyzing the effects of the design of the problem-solving phase (I 1) and the corresponding problem-solving processes (L 1) on intermediate knowledge (K 1) and how this intermediate knowledge alters the learning processes (L 2) initiated during the subsequent explicit instruction (I2), we can gain a better understanding of the learning mechanisms that make PS-I conducive for learning. We suggest that a systematic reason for the conflicting results found in previous research (e.g., Glogger-Frey et al., 2015 vs. 2017; Hartmann et al., 2022 vs. Kapur, 2014) lies in the interdependence of the learning mechanisms across the different phases, which could be highlighted by applying the CID framework. Applying the CID framework to PS-I research A first example for the application of CID to PS-I designs refers to research that compares two types of first phases: solving the problem in a PS-I condition (cf. specification in the white boxes of Figure 1) with working through a worked solution to the same problem in a worked-solution condition (WS-I) (Glogger-Frey et al., 2015, 2017). This instructional variation can alter the depth of knowledge acquired during the first phase, which in turn also affects the learning processes in the second phase. Glogger-Frey and colleagues conducted two studies using this design. The only difference between the two studies consisted in the duration of the initial phase. Students in the first study (Glogger-Frey et al., 2015) went through one problem (either solving the problem or working through a worked solution depending on the condition), whereas students in the second s tudy (Glogger-Frey et al., 2017) went through two problems during the initial learning phase (also both conditions). In the first study, CSCL 2023 Proceedings  © ISLS 323 students in the problem-solving condition (I 1) likely generated solutions that included relevant and irrelevant surface features (L 1), resulting in unstructured intermediate knowledge, including relevant and surface features (K1). In contrast, students in the worked-solution condition encoded only relevant features. During the subsequent explicit instruction (I 2), all students encoded the given explanations (L 2). Students in the problem-solving condition also had to sort irrelevant surface features from relevant features (L2), whereas students in the workedsolution condition did not. Not surprisingly, students in the worked-solution condition outper formed their counterparts on the post-test (K 2). In the second study, students in the problem-solving condition could acquire negative knowledge (Oser et al., 1999) about the irrelevance of surface features  (K 1) by working through two problems (L1). In this case, the problem-solving condition outperformed the worked solution condition at posttest (K2). Thus, the key to explaining the seemingly inconsistent results reported by Glogger-Frey and colleagues (2015 vs. 2017) lies in the differences in the quality of the intermediate knowledge influenced by the type and the duration of the first phase: Only sufficiently comprehensive and structured inte rmediate knowledge provides fertile ground for learning from subsequent instruction. As a second example for the application of the CID framework on PS-I designs, we discuss research that switches the sequence of problem solving and explicit instruction (PS-I vs. I-PS), which has been a paradigm in research on PS-I (Sinha & Kapur, 2021; Loibl et al., 2017). Studies comparing PS-I and I-PS have been conducted in cognitive domains (e.g., on the concept of variance, Kapur, 2012; Loibl & Rummel, 2014b) as well as in motor learning (javelin throwing, Loibl & Leukel, 2023) with similar beneficial effects  of PS-I. At the instructional design level, comparing PS-I and I-PS seems to be a fair comparison to test the effect of the sequence, at least when using the same materials in both conditions (Loibl & Rummel, 2014b; Loibl et a l., 2020). However, when explicitly considering the level of learning processes, it becomes clear that c hanging the instructional sequence (PS-I vs. I-PS) has multiple effects: Generating own solution attempts in an initial problem-solving phase allows for forming negative knowledge (i.e., knowledge about what does not work) and for encoding own examples (intermediate knowledge, K1). This intermediate knowledge (K1) then can be linked to the processed explanations in the second phase (L 2). This learning process is not present when the explanations are given in the  first phase. Additionally, the switching of instructional types also completely changes the proces ses in the problem-solving phase: In PS-I, the learning processes in the problem-solving phase are activating prior knowledge and identifying knowledge gaps, while generating own solution attempts (L 1). Together, these processes promote negative knowledge and encoded examples as early forms of conceptual knowledge (K 1). In I-PS, the learning processes in the problem-solving phase, which comes second here, are more about practicing the application of the procedure that was introduced in the initial explicit instruction phase (L 2). This learning process does not affect conceptual knowledge but rather only strengthens procedural knowledge (K2). Thus, by changing the order of the instructional phases, more than one learning mechanism is altered, as the intermediate knowledge affects the learning processes in the second learning phase in both implemented orders. Therefore, while unc onfounded at the instructional design level, any assumptions on the specific mechanisms at work are empiric ally highly confounded at the learning process level.

## Conclusions

Based on our analyses of PS-I research using the CID framework, we could demonstrate that intermediate knowledge plays a significant role in understanding the underlying mechanisms of PS-I, as it influences the learning processes in the second phase. Moreover, by specifying all three levels (knowledge components, learning processes, and instructional design) across the two phases for the example s tudies (for more detailed analyses, also beyond PS-I research, see Loibl et al. subm.), we have demonstrated that modifying the instructional design can have an effect on various mechanisms at the other two levels. Conse quently, specifying all three levels can potentially explain previous conflicting results. In addition, the CID analyses show that understanding individual mechanisms requires research strategies that not only carefully control the instructional design but also consider the resulting alterations in learning processes and knowledge across all implemented conditions.
