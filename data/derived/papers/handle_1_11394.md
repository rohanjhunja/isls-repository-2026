# Making Visible the Brilliance of Black-Latinx Communities in Engineering: A Collaborative Autoethnographic Inquiry about Researchers Designing Engineering Learning Environments

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Previous engineering education research highlights the role that community resources—language practices and cultural understandings—play in the learning experiences of students. Theoretical and pedagogical approaches in engineering vary widely and rarely incorporate these community resources to understand and support the engineering identity development of historically underrepresented students. In this paper, we document our journey attempting to make visible the experiences of Black-Latinx communities, Latino/a/x/e of Black ancestry, in engineering practices. In a collaborative inquiry following ethnographic methods, we, multilingual women in engineering, reflect about our own identities as we attempt to make sense of community resources in the discipline. We present how our identities influence the ways in which we think about the research design, the opportunities and challenges of integrating cultural and linguistic systems of practices and understandings in engineering courses at a predominantly White institution (PWI) and Hispanic-serving institutions (HSI). By engaging with our own experiences conducting this work, we hope to motivate other researchers and faculty to also reflect on how our intersecting identities shape who we are, how we approach our work, and how others may view our approaches.

## Introduction

Engineering learning environments present students with particular disciplinary practices. Sometimes, it is at the expense of portraying the discipline as purely obje ctive and reductionist, in juxtaposition with what it means to engage in technical work (G odfrey & Parker, 2010). In other words, who address es the problem is often not assumed to influence the developed solution to the problem, even when students are tasked with designi ng solutions to indigenous community problems, notably the Māori community for a study conducted at a New Zealand School of Engineering by Godfrey & Parker (2010). As the authors further noted, “culturally appropriate solutions were viewed as just one of the constraint s engineering solutions must work within rather tha n recognizing that alternative epistemologies might exist” (p.  12). But engaging with the epistemic practices of engineering, from problem solving to making informe d decisions using scientific and technical knowledg e (ABET, 2020), tends to be a highly sociocultural and community-centered endeavor where people who are of diverse identities (e.g., in terms of intersecting racial, ethnic, gender, asylum-migration status, abilities, LGBTQ+, and other identities) play an important role in des ign processes and engineering p roblem-solving (Patrick et. al., 2016).

## Conceptual framework

In response to the numerous sustainability challeng es that our society faces (Rieckmann, 2017), engine ering learning environments are tasked with preparing students to grapple with large scale societal problems. As a result, students will need to develop an understanding of t he self—including their intersecting identities, how they w ill expand their engineering competencies, and discipli nary ways of thinking that support possible futures  with solutions to societal challenges (e.g., systems thi nking, anticip atory thinking) (Hadgraft & Kolmos, 2020). Learning environments should highlight the intercon nectedness of the “resources that shape and enable activity” from people and their communities, to materials, en vironments and situations (Pea, 1993, p. 50). This  is particularly important in engineering, where people  navigate disciplinary epistemologies and how onto-epistemologies come to be in technical spaces. Indi viduals in engineering learning environments naviga te complex interactions between the resources of  the discipline and those of the communities where t hey claim membership in an attempt to develop and make sense of technologies and solutions, the people who desig ned them and those who use and are most directly affected by technological artifacts (Pérez et al., 2025). Who people are and what resources they bring into a ctivities in engineering learning environments is crucial, particularly for groups historically marginalized, such as Black-Latinx. Wilson-Lopez et al. (2016) found that community funds of knowledge obtained through volunteerism, community organizations, and growing up in working-class families enabled pre-college Latino/a students to acquire engineering-related funds of knowledge. For example, students with parents in wo rking-class jobs (e.g., packaging meats, transporting chickens) were exposed to engineering practices  like problem-solving, management, data collection, and data analysis. However, for students such as Black-Latinx whose experiences are made invisible through  monoracial and essentialist conceptions of identity, the possi bilities for how to engage the funds of knowledge s triving from their experiences in their communities are rare at best (Pérez & Sheppard, 2024). Despite the growing body of literature on the experiences of Black and Brown students in engineering education in recent years (Ho lly Jr & Quigley, 2022; London et al., 2021), Black-Latinx communities, who represent more than 15 mill ion people in the U.S. (Pew Research Center, 2016), have remained invisible in the broader society and in engineering education research, evidenced by the absence of any publication addressing the intersectional identities of these communities in the flagship Journal of Engineering Education (J EE) and its omissions from the U.S. Census Bureau (UCLA Latino Policy & Politics Institute, 2024).

As learning scientists and engineering educators, we come to this work understanding the importance of sociopolitical consciousness (Ladson-Billings, 1995) when imagining affirming ways of le arning where Black-Latinx communities can see themselves in engin eering. Inspired by Ladson-Billing (2014), we define sociopolitical consciousness as the awareness of ou r intersecting identities and the ability to create  solutions for problems in the broader context of society, not solely the classroom. We draw on this sociopolitical consciousness to imagine engineering learning environments as spa ces where students are "transformative intellectual s" (Morales-Doyle, 2017) because their ways of knowing and speaking are important resources in their communities and in engineering, positioning them as competent i n both contexts. As done by Holly Jr (2020) in his work on Black man teaching engineering to Black boys, we se ek to leverage our collective experiences growing u p and interacting with the community to connect the reali ties of students outside of the classroom while als o fostering engagement inside the classroom. In his work as in ours, culturally relevant approaches provided an op portunity for his Black male students to see engineers who lo ok like themselves and deconstruct the stereotypes of who or what an engineer should be. Thus, as three multilin gual women in engineering, we reflect on our positi onalities and educational experiences and how it may inform h ow we engage in making visible the experiences of B lackLatinx communities in engineering and reimagining learni ng environments as spaces of possibilities where students can see themselves and their communities reflected.

## Data and methods methodology

In this study, we employ a collaborative autoethnog raphic inquiry design based on Secules et al.’s (20 21) work on positionality as a tool in approaching research in engineering education. The data sources are our personal experiences collected through refl ections. In these reflections, we grapple with the question: How do our backgrounds as Black and Brown students and scholars in engineering and learning sciences influence the ways in which we envision engineering learning environments for communities? These reflections are produced during our weekly lab meetings where we discuss approaches  to incorporate language practices and cultural understandings of communities into engineering lear ning environments at the undergraduate level. In do ing this work, we dig deeper into how our intersecting identities shape who we are, how our positionalities inform ho w we approach this work and how others perceive it. S pecifically, we consider the impacts of epistemolog ical and communication positionality on our research design.  Epistemological positionality asks how the researc her’s positionality impacts how they know what they know,  while communication positionality asks how the researcher’s positionality impacts how they represe nt themselves and others in writing. When reflectin g on the intersections of his positionality and epistemologi cal practices in research, Mejia stated, “I believe  we are all a product of colonial educational systems… We have be en taught what is ‘true’ and there are no alternati ve ways of knowing… Recently, I have relied more on my own gut feeling and have tried to detach from tradition al (or canonical) ways of knowing in engineering education, and opted for a personal epistemology that is rooted in my own cultural and social background. Working with Latinx and indigenous communities made me realize that they are the expert witnesses of their own knowledge” (Secules et al., 2021, p. 28). Following the practice outlined by Mejia, we engage our knowledge from our lived exper iences to understand  how  we think about designing engineering learning environments centered in Black-Latinx people. We first reflect on our individual j ourneys to becoming graduate students and a professor in en gineering. Next, we document how our identities inf orm our research design.

This study is part of a larger project that investi gates the connections between the practices of Blac kLatinx communities and engineering practices. Altho ugh in this paper, we primarily focus on our person al experiences connecting communities with engineering, in the larger project, we seek to understand the implications of this connection for theory and peda gogy by designing a unit within undergraduate engin eering design courses to address sociocultural issues and encourage alternate ways of knowing in engineering design beyond the predominant epistemologies. The larger study will take place at three institutions of higher education: a predominantly White institution (PWI) and two His panic-serving institutions (HSIs) with different demographics in terms of how Latino/a/e/x students identify racially. We are members of the PWI. How do we know what we know? Engaging our positionalities in the work We, the authors, comprise different engineering bac kgrounds and multicultural and multilingual experiences. Pérez is a civil engineer and learning scientist in  her role as an Assistant Professor of Engineering Education at Tufts University. As a scholar in engineering educa tion and learning sciences at a private, research i ntense and predominantly White institution in the Northeastern, she has witnessed how engineering learning environments continue to be a male-dominated discipline where monolingual and monocult ural norms prevailed. In these spaces, the systems, resources, and practices of marginalized communities are rarely acknowledged or integrated in what it means to be an engineer and do engineering. Her experiences as an engineer and in academia have been marked by her ways of speaking and systems of knowledge resulting from her background as a Latina of African ancestry and who is fluent in Caribbean Spanglish. These identities along with her own background as a  1.5generation immigrant make her realize the complex, intersectional experiences of Afro and Black indivi duals within the Latinx comm unities and beyond who are often overlooked and who se ways of knowing, being and speaking are rarely at the center of engineering ed ucation efforts, even though these communities have  thrived and engineer spaces for learning and innovation thr oughout generations. Throughout her career, from studying water resources and environmental engineering in Pu erto Rico to being a bilingual STEM educator in the  South, she encountered learning environments where people and their communities were both freed and constrai ned. Even in highly restrictive contexts where norms lim ited which resources were legitimized for STEM lear ning, communities and students opened spaces of possibili ties where they could fully embrace their cultural and linguistic identities, and where multiple ways of communicating were valued and supported. These transformative experiences ignited her passion to join others in i magining learning environments through a culturally  and linguistically inclusive lens (Pérez & Sheppard, 2024; Secules et al., 2023). Eshun is a Civil and Environmental Engineering Ph.D. student, Black Ghanaian-American woman, and speaker of Akan Twi, or Twinglish (mixed Twi and English). Her Ghanaian community was her first engineering teacher. At 9 years old, she witnessed and learned of the revolutionary innovations that dramatically changed operations within the developing country, such as t he Akosombo Dam, the first major hydroelectric dam to provide electricity across Ghana. Back then, she st ood on a narrow bridge that overlooked th e beautifully-constructed infrastructure. From community innovati ons, she conceptualized engineering as a tool to su pport people, in which the engineer was the mechanically-or technologically-inclined community member who helped troubleshoot issues. Initially, she did not think o f engineers as theorists who derived equations in i solation, yet this was what many of her chemical engineering cour ses portrayed. The community that attracted her to the idea of engineering was nowhere present or considered in the curriculum, and she felt that she was rather immersed in an engineering college culture rooted in individual ism and competition, values that seemed inherently incompatible with her cultural upbringing. Therefore, she approaches this work with a deep interest in integrating community engineering practices into the curriculum and is committed to reshaping engineering education for all students to develop a true sense of belonging and f orm a positive engineering identity. Although she d oes not identify as Afro-Latinx, her unique lens as an underrepresented and multicultural engineering student allows her to empathize with Afro-Latinx engineering student experiences and perceive  covert cognitive biases that view their language and culture as deficient.

Shrestha is a master’s student in STEM education from Nepal, a country in South Asia with a background in Electronics and Communication Engineering. To he r, engineering is about creating solutions that mak e tasks easier and are tailored to the unique needs  of different communities. However, during her time in engineering school, the focus was different. Using more electro nic components and advanced concepts was what ultim ately gained recognition. This practice led her to believ e that an engineer must be kno wledgeable about the latest technologies, and think that first-world countries were far more advanced thandeveloping countries like Nepal in the field of electronics and communications. This p erspective made her think that there was little sco pe for engineers like her in her own country. However, in think ing of engineering as being fundamentally about simplifying things, a definition that recognizes the use of advanced or cutting-edge technology as well as everyday tech, she now sees engineering problem-solving as being rooted in understanding what a commu nity truly needs and appreciating its culture and people to serve it  better. This led her to design a culturally driven  engineering activity where students celebrate a festival called  Tihar, the “festival of lights,” by creating Tihar lights by connecting LEDs of different colors. She found that  this human-centered approach—using simple, introductory engineering tools that students can play with and t ying them to culturally relevant activities, helps students learn without feeling overwhelmed. Moreover, she is interested in broadening the definition of engineering to recognize and appreciate community roots. She feels that her former mindset in solely recognizing technological advancements in developed countries often overlooks  community resources. Many ancient and traditional engineered systems in communities have been ignored  as engineering achievements, as we chase after adva nced futuristic technologies. So she comes to this work,  aiming to show people engineering systems that do not seem beyond their reach or not meant for them.

The challenges and opportunities of imagining community-led engineering learning environments At the predominantly white institutions (PWIs) wher e we engage in scholarship as in many other institu tions of higher education, learning about engineering while being Black-Latinx often means acculturating to disciplinary practices at the expense of one's  lived experiences and living with the creeping feel ing that one may not belong. Grant et. al (2022) explains the racial marginaliza tion we described stating “the [engineering] curric ulum is not set up to talk about race… talking about race remin ded [racial-ethnic minoritized students] of their differences from peers”. Therefore, in communicating Afro-Latinx engineering-related practices in undergraduate learning environments, we acknowledge the importance of hono ring and supporting the richness within our differences and highlighting our similarities, understanding th at communicating is not enough. The ultimate goal i s for underrepresented students to not feel isolated and misrepresented, and that they candevelop trust in a system that has been historically exclusionary. In highlighting Black-Latinx engineering practices, Black-Latinx students will inevitably be hypervisible unless we carefully frame the narrative. One way we intend to frame the narrative is by providing counterstories as the ones that Shrestha experienced. Students will have opportunities to reflect in class and out of class on how cultural practices in their  communities related to engineering. This narrative  enables students to broaden their definition of who an engi neer is, what an engineer does, and what engineerin g design encompasses. As in the case of Pérez in Puerto Rico and Eshun in Ghanaian communities, counterstories expand students’ conceptions of language and culture as dy namic practices and connect these practices across disciplinary, family, neighborhoods and regional contexts. It offers students the opportunity to consider how their language and cultural practices could inform their engineering design processes and products while simultaneously disrupting notions about White culturelessness (Perry 2001). We also see Hispanic Serving Institutions (HSIs), j ust as the Black-Latinx communities, not as monoliths. The HSIs in the study have different com positions of Black-Latinx backgrounds within their student body and faculty, resulting in different power dynamics at play. One of the institutions is characterized by a large population of students with Mexican and Central Ame ricandescent while the other university is predomi nantly of Spanish-speaking Caribbean backgrounds. We want to be cognizant of such demographic dynamics and power structures. Given that the three research sites are  in different regions of the U.S., and our own posi tionalities as scholars, we want to ensure that the ways we design  the learning environments remain rooted in the cul tural and linguistic realities of the students at these institutions. We acknowledge that we can’t possibly embody all of these realities. Instead, we will seek to connect with ex perts, professors and students from these instituti ons and the communities who share similar language, cultural, and engineering practices.

## Conclusion

This work seeks to inform future scholarship which aims to understand processes of Black Latinx engine ers' development of sociopolitical consciousness and mor e affirming forms of learning. Our racial-ethnic identities and personal experiences as graduate students and a n engineering faculty member provide us unique insi der perspectives to view and analyze this work. Of cour se, we cannot embody the experiences of everyone and it is not our intention to portray our lived experiences in such a way. Instead, we put forth who we are, how we know what we know and how we approach this work as an invitation for scholars to reflect about these questions when conducting research in engineering education resear ch, particularly in scholarship seeking to imagine possible futures of equity and justice in learning environme nts. We have considered the opportunities and poten tial challenges with developing a culturally and linguis tically sustaining learning environment that center s BlackLatinx engineering practices and engineering contributions associated with their communities. Lastly, we call for other educational researchers, who are partial “insiders” to the population they are studying, to abandon absolute engineering education research objectivity for positionality transparency (Secules et al. 2021), for there should be “nothing about us without us” (Costanza-Chock, 2020).
