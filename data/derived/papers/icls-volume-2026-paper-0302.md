Situating violence, pain, resistance, and learning I wake up. I  am exhausted. I am at 0 capacity. The world, life, and its day-to-day requirements to exist in this capitalist and colonial space require me to pull it together, wear my smile, and face the world. Hustle to meet the next deadline, meaningfully engage in meetings that are tightly booked in my calendar, then spend the rest of my time writing. Writing to continue to meet the demands of work, school, and research and publications pressure to be a competitive being to secure the career I want and the life I envision.  Just another day. I turn in bed and pick up my phone to engage in the habitual mindless scrolling. I open Instagram to the first post on my feed: “Supreme Court Formally Asked to Overturn Landmark Same-sex Marriage Ruling” from Obergefell v. Hodges 2015 case that legalized same-sex marriage in the US.

My spirits drop lower. I read the comments, one most striking, in my paraphrased and analytical words: Gays for Trump, why did you think you could abandon your transgender counterparts who you chose to dissociate with because you claim you are the standard for queerness? The “normal queerness”? The LGB claims they want a divorce from the TQ+ of the LGBTQ+ because the TQ+ are weird —the sheerest demonstration of toxic queer masculinity. Did you not know he would come for the entire queer and LGBTQ+ community? This experience is not in isolation as we see similar rhetorical discourses when considering Latinos for Trump and People of Color for Trump. My heart races with anxiety. I consider if I need to take my Xanax. My antidepressants treat both my depression and anxiety, but this was triggering me out of control. My psychiatrist prescribes me Xanax for extreme instances only. My phone has become a facilitator of pain to highlight the  violence of the world that I so want to turn off from sometimes—most times. This incident is but part of a long and exhausting year of fascist rule with a determination to render violence, pain, and ultimate erasure of trans* bodies. Trans* denotes the broad spectrum of non-cisgender identities, across transgender, nonbinary, and gender nonconforming (TNBGNC), emphasi zing the intersections of Black, Indigenous, and People of Color (BIPOC) (Salas-SantaCruz, 2024). This excerpt of my story, as a broader  representation of current US political fascism, beckons me to indulge the question, what is our role as learning scientists and scholars of educational discourse in centralizing the political as inherent to learning?  I attempt to answer this question through five trans* student-athletes’ experiences of violence, pain, and resistance juxtaposed with my experiences of my Black trans* body as a sociopolitic. I draw on our major learning scientists, theorists, and scholars who have pushed the boundaries of the field to create the space for discourses on the politics of learning (McWilliams, 2016; Uttamchandani, 2020) in situating the space for my work. I engage these onto-epistemological perspectives from these young foundations within our field to explore this politics of learning, specifically for trans* bodies and in the context of sports as learning. The sociopolitical in Learning Sciences: What counts and equity detours In drawing on the antecedent scholars and literature that engage this line of political discourse within learning processes, I specifically engage three core pieces: the first Berland  and Halverson’s (2025) editorial note on expanding what counts within the L earning Sciences; the second, the Learning Toward s Justice Team (2025) work on avoiding detours to equity within Learning Sciences; and thirdly, Jurow and researchers’ (2025) discourse on designing for healing in the L earning Sciences. I firstly frame t his work as a response to Halverson and Berland’s commitment to reimagining countering  the inequitable systemic structure and gatekeeping practices 2001 that have historically governed our field of Learning Sciences that continue othering bodies and their perspectives as invalid. I further engage each of  the four principles that the Learning Towards Justice Team offers to prevent detouring from the work  of equity as a method of operationalizing these principles. I engage with five trans* student athletes through a methodology of portraiture to elucidate trans* bodies and how their learning is challenged and further reinforce the principles that the Learning Towards Justice Team offers. Finally, I indulge Jurow and researchers’ (2025) discourse on designing for healing within the Learning Sciences. I move across these three scholarships as I embrace and articulate how each situates my goals in this work. Body politics: Queer and trans* bodies in learning sciences Trans* bodies have been historically and contemporarily politicized. This politicization means that the trans* body has consistently maintained its position as a site of contention, being constantly surveilled, around invalid and valid transness, pathologization, and surgical and hormonal properties of trans* bodies  (Uttamchandani, 2020). These arguments centralizing who is allowed to be or not, who is allowed to produce or not, who is allowed to use what bathrooms and where, who is able to access healthcare and not are these representations of trans* body politicization (Tofel-Grehl et al., 2024). As we continue to recognize and contend with these politicizations within the contexts of education and learning, more of our scholars have focused on queer and trans * bodies within their discourse and scholarship (McWilliams, 2016; Uttamchandani, 2020). Yet even as we recognize this shift to capture and centralize queer and trans* politics more recently, the stark absence, erasure, and underrepresentation of these bodies within our Learning Sciences field is another systemic representation of the invalidity of trans* bodies: this is not the place for this discourse; this is out of the field’s scope; this is not an issue big enough for education and Learning Sciences’ attention (Learning Towards Justice Team, 2025). Current politics and its significant impact on our work demonstrate otherwise. Body politics: Trans* policy and experiences in sport The National Collegiate Athletic Association (NCAA) governs student-athlete functions in  the US and consequently surveils trans* bodies. Their most recent policy bans transwomen from competing, further amplified by the International Olympic Committee (IOC’s) most recent policy  also banning transwomen from women’s Olympics, limiting strictly to “biological females ” (ESPN, 2026; NCAA, 2025). Much of the contention around the trans* body politic within sport stems from the preoccupation with biological and physiological considerations between male and female bodies (Harper, 2022; Snorton, 2017). Yet, the literature consistently demonstrates that performance levels are much more complex than predicted by  standardized testosterone levels (Canadian Centre for Ethics in Sports, 2022; Jones et al., 2017). As literature highlights  hormonal differences to be  the main distinguishing feature between males and females, we face the contentions now that trans* people who ensue hormone replacement therapy (HRT) may actually be at a greater disadvantage compared to cisgendered females. What happens when we then consider “ natural” hormonal levels among female bodies: there is variation among these female bodies, which —following the hormonal, biological logic —position some females at a greater advantage “naturally”? One thing remains starkly clear: the sex-gender contention remains incredibly complex.

## Theoretical framework: Lugones’s coloniality of gender

I situate this discourse as grounded within Lugones’s (2007) theory on the coloniality of gender. Lugones developed her theory following Quijano’s (2000) coloniality of power. Lugones agreed with Quijano  that the function of social structure is all about power, but she criticized his theorization as lacking adequate depth into the salience of gender and sexual ideology, asserting that this lack of engagement hegemonically perpetuates the rhetoric that it should deface.  Lugones’s coloniality of gender provides a n intimate and deep theoretical underpinning to explore the current W esternized practices of sex and gender, as evidenced within systems, including education and learning, that are rooted in capitalism and colonialism. Coloniality remains entrenched within our systems of governance, at the federal, state, and institutional levels, as evidenced by the attacks on trans* people, democracy, higher education, policy, and our learning processes. I draw on Lugones’s theory to frame the experiences of the trans* body (in)validities within sport and learning. Lawrence-Lightfoot and Davis (1997) define their portraiture approach as “a method of qualitative research that blurs the boundaries of aesthetics and empiricism in an effort to capture the complexity, dynamics, and subtlety of human experience and organizational life” (p. 15). Portraiture is operationalized through five core elements of the research process: context, voice, relationships, emergent themes, and the aesthetic whole. Context refers to the physical situatedness of the research process, which must be accounted for within the research and how it 2002 shapes the narrative and discourse. Voice captures the intentional balancing of power across the portraitist’s voice and that of the subjects of their portrait. Relationships play an essential role in the co-creation of portraits through which an alignment of lived experiences creates the space for authentic discourse. Co-construction of context, voice, and relationships within the drawing of the portrait creates the space for the emergence of authentic themes, while being guided by the theoretical framewor k and portraitist’s subjectivity. Finally, the aesthetic whole is the final portrait that denotes this authentic blurring of art and science. Portraiture in education and learning research: A method of power and politics As with any provocative perspective that seeks to destabilize normalized theories and methods, portraiture has been criticized as lacking rigor and methodological depth given its arts-based incorporation with scientific-based inquiry (Brooks, 2017)—a challenge those of us who embrace politically-charged orientations in our theoretical and methodological approaches are all too familiar with within the Learning Sciences (Learning Towards Justice Team, 2025)—a systemically hegemonic assertion by those who bask in gatekeeping practices. Yet scholars have demonstrated the utility of portraiture in education and learning research as a method that engages issues of the systemic through power and politics (Ahrens, 2019; Bruhn & Jimenez, 2020).

## Context and participants: Trans* student-athletes

This study is part of a larger multi-case portraiture analysis across three sub-cases, canvases, of portraiture: trans* student-athletes, athletic trainers, and higher education athletic administrators. This work engages the canvas—as I term it to represent the onto-epistemological site through which we engage in the co-construction of our portraits—of trans* student-athlete experiences navigating this current fascist, hostile sociopolitical US climate. This study is approved and protected through Penn State University’s  Human Research Protection Program, STUDY00027213. I recruited five trans* student-athletes across five US higher education institutions with the inclusion criteria: at least 18 years and over, current or former student-athlete status, identifies as trans* (within the TNBGNC umbrella), and currently enrolled within a US university. Analytical process: Abductive thematic analysis As I centralize my trans* student-athlete experiences as a juxtaposition of lived experiences with my international Black transnational trans* body, I apply an abductive thematic analytical  process to facilitate this joint construction and drawing of representative portraits. Abductive analysis requires a balanced movement between deductive and inductive logic in processing data.  This method of analysis is inherent  to my process as I rely on this joint construction of portraitist and subject of the portraitist— the method beckons abductive analysis, and it would be a disservice to myself, my participants, and the data to choose any other route. Earl Rine hart (2021) notes that the portraitist is inseparable from the intimacy of data, especially when it is a deep representation of the researcher’s lived experiences (representation of arts-based inquiry). This closeness and intimacy to the data must be brought into  conversation with the data from participants to inform the authentic generation of thematic portraits (representation of science-based inquiry). As Brink mann (2014) asserts, the successful application of abductive analysis entails reasoning that is grounded in the relationship between situation and inquiry. I present findings that demonstrate four portraits as aligned with the Learning Towards Justice Team’s (2025) detours to equity within L earning Sciences. Ada is an international, queer, nonbinary student-athlete; Thai is a bisexual, nonbinary transfeminine student-athlete; Ona is a bisexual, nonbinary transfeminine student-athlete; Sia is a queer, transwoman student-athlete; and Love is a homosexual, (trans)woman student-athlete. Together, we draw portraits of how  our lived experiences connect with each other, as guided by the coloniality of gende r and the four detours of equity within L earning Sciences, as a method of imagining our immersion with the self and the other, stories grounded in arts-based and science-based inquiry that indulge our body politics. Portrait 1: Superficial diversification through my violence—Thai & Ona I reflect on how my Blackness, queerness, transness, citizenship status, and systemic representations of my intersectional identity have been used in ways that present a narrative of diversity. It is used frequently in higher education—or perhaps have been given the current mess of an administration—and other organizational spaces. Thai and Ona both engage the observation of our 2025 Pride celebration, as one example of this corporate body politics as economy that govern transness, which is further represented through institutional politics and higher education athletic spaces. Thai acknowledges, “ I know I can ride a bike fast. And to me, that makes me feel like powerful, and I feel that power being acknowledged and almost like people taking pride in my competitiveness depending on my teams and where I am. But there’s also if someone clocks that I am trans, and 2003 how that changes the experience, again depending on where I am. It’s like constantly these shifting dynamics  of when my transness is most beneficial or detrimental to a crowd, you know? Ona continues, “we’re [trans* studentathletes] generally not perceived very positively, at least right now with the politics. I think when you include the adjective trans*, a lot of people like cling on to that and focus on that and all of their misconceptions and all of the propaganda that's been sprouted about us. It’s like people are pushing misinformation about us. But then you also think about years ago, the boasting of inclusion of diverse people and embracing these people in our universities and spaces. We’ve always been contentious in sports. But it’s so much worse now. I just want to play. I don’t want to think about all this.” Portrait 2: My side, my pain—Ada Speaking with Ada stirs up unique connections within my spirit. They talk about their experiences as an international queer, nonbinary student-athlete coming to the US and how this narrative of the great Global North, the first world, the Western modernity, what they tell us is the dream comes at such a cost of pain. Ada laments, “it's not just gender nonconforming and gender diverse people; it's allies, people who are part of any marginalized community being affected. We feel like we’re no longer safe and protected, especially by sport, which in my view is meant to protect us. You don’t know those hateful things that are said in the locker rooms and by coaches, that they're not even going to be punished for. Because if I speak up or report, who will they believe? Little nonbinary international student-athlete or the cisgendered and coaches in power?” Portrait 3: Expanding Learning Sciences to represent my trans* body—Sia My Black trans* body has consistently been a site of learning and exploration, a sociocultural and sociopolitical site of learning about myself, which has always been most strongly situated within the educational context. How could you tell me that my discourse on how I learn about myself through institutionalized and politicized notions as immersed within this context and setting with the other is outside the scope of education and the L earning Sciences? Education and Learning Sciences are inherently political because of their  model of capitalism and institutionalization.

Sia deliberates on the role of higher education and learning: “[It should be] we do support you. We will push back on this. We will always be that place, because higher education is supposed to be a place where all these different people come together. That is the critical part of education that you're supposed to gain from going to a higher education institute; you're supposed to meet people that don't look, think, act like you, don't have the same background as you, come from a different environment. You're supposed to be exposed to all these different people with the end goal of gaining more tolerance, better empathy, and a better understanding of people’s lived experiences.” Portrait 4: Embracing the political as trans* body empowerment and advocacy—Love As we resent the narrative that the political is outside the scope of the L earning Sciences, I embrace my Black trans* body and experiences of power, privilege, and oppressions that inform how I approach this space. I am not too much. My lived experiences are valid and worth sharing. I will not be ashamed of the less-than-proud stories that implicate the learning and navigation of my politicized trans* body subjected to colonial violence. I embrace these positionings because they empower me to advocate for the communities I love, indulge, and represent. Love engages, “It gets tiring. It definitely does. We won legal battles before, that all trans* people have historically had to face. We have to fight for our rights. We're due for a social revolution. We need more science. Perhaps more importantly, we need people to trust science more given the sudden increase in people not trusting science. People don’t believe the IOC study with over 40 trans* athletes. We need more research. We need more money. We need more power. We need our voices.” While space does not permit me to engage the levels and depths of discussion I would ideally like to indulge, the centralization of the coloniality of gender (Lugones, 2007) demonstrates its salience within and across these four co-constructed portraits and in the context of the four detours toward equity (Learning Towards Justice Team, 2025). The Learning Towards Justice Team highlights some important methods that we may attempt to avoid these hegemonic and gatekeeping practices. These include examining the politics o f work, embedding a constructive approach to discourse, understanding sociohistorical relations, interrogating feelings of resistance, and fostering accountability relations  with affected populations. That is how we move forward together, specifically to centralize the trans* colonial body politic.
