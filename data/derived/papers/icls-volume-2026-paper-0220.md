# From Doing to Doing Together: A Design Framework for Hands-On and Open Science Engagement In K-5

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This conceptual paper explores how hands-on and open science frameworks can be synthesized as design principles for fostering authentic student engagement in science learning. Building on sociocultural and constructivist theories, the paper examines engagement not as compliance or activity, but as participatory meaning-making situated in shared inquiry and collaboration. By integrating literature on embodied learning, democratic participation, and scientific literacy, the paper proposes that combining hands-on and open science approaches supports engagement as both experiential and communal. This paper aligns with the ISLS mission to advance design-based theories of learning through participatory and equitable practices. Four propositions and design principl es are presented to guide pedagogical and research innovations that promote more inclusive, participatory, and equitable science education.

Keywords: engagement, participation, open science, hands-on learning, design principles, sociocultural theory, constructivism, K-5 Science education has long grappled with the challenge of fostering authentic student engagement. While inquirybased and experimental activities are common, they often yield surface-level involvement characterized by task completion rather thandeep scientific reasoning (Hadzigeorgiou & Stivaktakis, 2008; García -Carmona, 2020). Many classrooms emphasize procedural compliance over curiosity-driven inquiry, leaving learners disconnected from the epistemic and social practices of science.

Recent research reframes engagement  as multidimensional, encompassing behavioral, emotional, and cognitive dimensions (Inkinen et al., 2019; Vilhunen et al., 2022). Behavioral engagement refers to participation in tasks, emotional engagement involves curiosity and belonging, and cognitive e ngagement represents deep reasoning and reflection. Authentic engagement occurs when learners not only “do science” but also think and feel like scientists, connecting empirical inquiry to personal meaning (Fan et al., 2018). Two frameworks, hands-on science and open science, offer complementary pathways for advancing this vision. Hands-on learning emphasizes direct, embodied interaction with materials, fostering curiosity and conceptual understanding (Hofstein & Lunetta, 2004). Open science promotes transparency, collaboration, and collective reasoning (Fecher & Friesike, 2014). This paper argues that integrating both provides a model for democratizing participation in science learning, shifting classrooms from sites of activity to communities of practice (Lave & Wenger, 1991).

Conceptual preposition and claim Sociocultural and constructivist foundations Drawing from sociocultural theory (Vygotsky, 1978) and constructivism, learning is viewed as a participatory process of meaning-making mediated by tools, dialogue, and community. Learners construct understanding through social interaction, reflection, and engagement in authentic practices. Wenger’s (1998) concept of communities of practice  situates learning within participation and identity; individuals understand who they are by contributing to shared activity.

From this perspective, engagement is not compliance but participation in a community’s epistemic practices. Knowledge is co-constructed through collaboration, and understanding emerges from the tension between participation (social involvement) and reification (producing shared artifacts). Engagement as participatory practice 1582 Authentic engagement integrates three dimensions:

1. Behavioral engagement—active involvement in scientific activities (Inkinen et al., 2019).

2. Emotional engagement—a sense of curiosity, belonging, and ownership (Vilhunen et al., 2022).

3. Cognitive engagement—intellectual investment in reasoning and reflection (García -Carmona, 2020).

When these dimensions intersect, students move beyond procedural science to reflective participation—bridging the experiential (hands-on) and social (open science) dimensions of inquiry. Building on sociocultural and constructivist perspectives, this paper advances four interrelated propositions that explain how the integration of hands-on and open science can transform student engagement into a participatory and democratic process of learning. These propositions form the conceptual foundation for the design principles that follow.

The first proposition asserts that engagement in science learning is best understood as situated participation rather than as behavioral compliance or isolated activity. Following Wenger’s (1998) view of learning as participation within communities of prac tice and Vygotsky’s (1978) conception of knowledge as socially mediated, engagement emerges when learners actively contribute to the shared practices of inquiry, learn not by merely observing or completing tasks but by inhabiting the social and intellectual spaces of scientific discourse. Engagement, therefore, involves belonging to a community of learners who make sense of phenomena together and recognize their roles in the collective construction of knowledge. The second proposition highlights that hands-on learning embodies cognition and deepens conceptual understanding. When students manipulate materials, test hypotheses, and observe outcomes, they externalize their thinking and transform abstract ideas into tangible experiences (Hofstein & Lunetta, 2004). Through such embodied engagement, learners construct meaning from physical interaction, developing a richer, more intuitive grasp of scientific principles (Fan et al., 2018). From a constructivist perspective, this process of “thinking with materials” (Hadzigeorgiou & Stivaktakis, 2008) allows students to confront misconceptions, make revisions, and internalize understanding through iterative cycles of experimentation and reflection. Thus, hands-on learning is not a supplementary activity but a cognitive process through which learners integrate theory and practice. The third proposition proposes that open science democratizes knowledge and broadens participation. Open science operates on the premise that the production of knowledge should be transparent, collaborative, and accessible (Fecher & Friesike, 2014; Arza & Fressoli, 2017). Translating this ethos into classroom practice means cultivating spaces where students are invited to share findings, critique peers’ reasoning, and engage in collective interpretation of data. When students engage in such practices, they move beyond being consumers of information to becoming co-authors of scientific understanding. The open science framework, therefore, challenges traditional hierarchies in classrooms —empowering learners to experience science as a living, social process that values multiple perspectives and emphasizes epistemic humility. Finally, the fourth proposition contends that the integration of hands-on and open science cultivates participatory scientific literacy. Scientific literacy, in this sense, extends beyond the mastery of concepts or procedures; it involves the capacity to i nquire, critique, and act with scientific awareness in society (Duschl & Bybee, 2014; Andersen, 2022). When learners engage in hands-on experimentation and subsequently participate in open, dialogic meaning-making, they develop both the technical and civic  dimensions of scientific literacy. They come to see science as not only an empirical pursuit but also a human and ethical one —rooted in collaboration, communication, and social responsibility. Through this integration, engagement transforms into a sustained practice of inquiry that connects the personal with the collective and the experiential with the reflective. Together, these four propositions form a coherent conceptual argument: that authentic engagement emerges when learning is simultaneously embodied, collaborative, and open to reinterpretation. By positioning students as active participants in both the physical and social dimensions of scientific work, this integrated framework supports the development of curiosity, agency, and belonging, qualities essential for nurturing lifelong engagement with science.

This section presents the design principles and conjectures derived from the conceptual synthesis of hands-on and open science frameworks. Each principle is articulated with associated conjectures and observable outcomes that link embodied inquiry and collaborative participation to authentic engagement and participatory scientific literacy. Boundary conditions and equity framing The integration of hands-on and open science assumes access to materials, digital infrastructure, and collaborative norms—conditions not always met in under-resourced contexts. Democratization thus requires intentional scaffolds for inclusion: Access: Ensure material and technological resources are equitably distributed. Agency: Recognize diverse cultural ways of knowing as valid contributions to scientific reasoning. Authenticity: Align tasks with real-world issues relevant to students’ communities. As Fontila et al. (2022) note, post-pandemic educational disruptions revealed inequities in access to inquiry-based learning. Addressing these disparities is critical if open science is to serve as a democratizing, rather than stratifying, force.

This framework positions engagement as participatory learning that unfolds through iterative cycles of doing, sharing, and reflecting. Teachers function as brokers (Wenger, 1998), mediating between students’ localized experiences and the broader epistemic cultures of science. In this capacity, they help learners navigate boundaries between novice and expert, classroom and community, and personal experience and public knowledge, by facilitating access to authentic tools, discourse, and reasoning practices. E mbedding open science norms such as transparency, collaboration, and critique within hands-on inquiry not only mirrors the authentic practices of professional scientists but also empowers students to adopt scientific habits of mind grounded in curiosity, reflection, and shared responsibility for knowledge.

1584 Practically, this framework suggests several design moves for educators seeking to cultivate epistemic agency and participatory scientific literacy.

● Embedding open data portals or “mini-repositories” in classroom projects allows students to archive, visualize, and revisit shared data sets —creating an evolving body of collective evidence that fosters accountability, peer validation, and iterative sense-making (Lager & Lavonen, 2023). ● Using narrative reflection journals supports metacognitive growth by helping students trace their learning trajectories, articulate shifts in understanding, and integrate personal experience with scientific explanation (Hadzigeorgiou & Schulz, 2019). These  reflective practices make reasoning visible and situate science as a lived process of inquiry rather than a fixed body of facts. ● Reimagining assessment to emphasize inquiry processes, collaboration, and public reasoning rather than final products encourages persistence and creativity in problem-solving (Duschl & Bybee, 2014). Assessments that value dialogue, revision, and uncertainty align with the open-science ethos and legitimize diverse ways of participating in knowledge construction. Together, these practices cultivate what might be termed participatory scientific literacy, a synthesis of conceptual understanding, epistemic agency, and ethical awareness. Students who experience science as both embodied and shared come to see themselves not only as learners but as contributors to a broader community of inquiry. Such learners engage with science as democratic citizens —capable of questioning evidence, evaluating claims, and responding to real-world challenges through informed, collaborative action. In this way, hands-on and open science practices converge to transform classrooms into microcosms of participatory democracy, where engagement becomes both an educational and civic act.

This conceptual synthesis contributes to the ISLS community by proposing a dual design framework—hands-on (embodied) and open (collaborative) as complementary foundations for engagement in science education. Together, these frameworks offer a coherent lens  for designing learning environments that integrate physical exploration with the social negotiation of meaning. The model advances a design-oriented claim that open practices—transparency, public reasoning, and shared data —amplify and stabilize the engagement generated by hands-on inquiry by making students’ embodied work publicly accountable, thereby cultivating epistemic agency and participatory scientific literacy.

## Discussion & Implications

Future research should empirically test these design conjectures through iterative design-based studies and classroom interventions. Such investigations might examine how embodiment and openness interact to shape students’ cognitive, emotional, and social engagement over time. Longitudinal studies could explore how openscience practices, such as collaborative data curation, peer critique, and reflective narration, transform the quality and sustainability of students’ participation. Similarly, mixed-method approaches may reveal how the designed coupling of material and social inquiry influences equity, identity development, and distributed expertise in science classrooms.

In an era of rapid scientific change, pervasive misinformation, and widening inequities in access to knowledge, democratizing science learning is both a pedagogical and civic imperative. By intentionally designing environments where students do science together, building, sharing, and revising ideas in community, educators can foster curiosity, competence, and collective responsibility, the hallmarks of lifelong scientific engagement. Ultimately, this work invites researchers and practitioners to view engag ement not merely as an instructional outcome but as a democratic practice that prepares learners to participate ethically and critically in the scientific discourses that shape our shared future.
