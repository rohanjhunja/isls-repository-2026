# Knowledge Links in the Wild

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study explores learning processes in an outdoor experiential learning opportunity to understand how learners may use their prior knowledge in these settings. Grounded in constructivism and generative learning, we operationalized students’ meaning making through knowledge construction links, capturing the links between what students already know and what they are learning via a system of cues and trajectory dimensions. Using qualitative methods, we observed and recorded interactions of students from three sixth-grade classrooms while they attended School of the Wild. Students also participated in follow-up interviews and a writing activity. Findings indicated that while knowledge links in this outdoor environment were similar to those in classrooms document ed in prior research, differences included a more robust definition for the function cue, the enactment of elements from students’ media trajectories, and the juxtaposition of prior learning with historical artifacts that problematized their learning task.

Students do not enter the classroom as blank slates. Rather, they carry with them a diverse range of prior knowledge, cultural experiences, and personal narratives that shape how they make sense of new content. Foundational work (Bransford & Schwartz, 1999; Jonassen, 1999; von Glasersfeld, 1998) underscored the central idea that learning is constructed upon existing knowledge. These personal experiences, although not always explicitly stated, can emerge in meaningful ways when students engage with instructional content. In our work, we operationalize the construction process using knowledge construction links (KCLs) whereby students draw on their lived experiences to form links between what they already know and what they are learning (Schuh, 2017). Teachers often create learning environments that foster such connections, whether through guided grade students connected classroom prompts to personal experiences, such as a student linking “meadow,” a word in their reading story, to a retirement community where a family member lived, highlighting how elements in classroom discourse can activate prior knowledge. These personal knowledge links help trace students’ learning and life trajectories over time and place, revealing how understanding is shaped across various settings. Experiential learning (EL) provides an especially rich context for the formation of these links. Defined as an active, participatory, and often multi-sensory process (Beard & Wilson, 2018), EL occurs when learners are immersed in complex, ill-structured environments that prompt reflection and meaning making. Outdoor and wilderness-based educational experiences are particularly effective in this regard, as they challenge students to engage deeply with their surroundings and often provoke memorable, meaningful learning moments. Previous studies have found that students report growth in areas such as leadership (McGowan, 2016), emotional and social skills (Blaine & Akhurst, 2020), and academic motivation (Kuo et al., 2018) following outdoor EL. Much of this research has relied heavily on perceptual, self-reported data about their experiences. What remains underexplored is how students build their understandings as they learn in these environments. Specifically, we sought to operationalize learning in an outdoor learning environment by considering the prompts through which these students linked to other experiences they have had, either in or out of their traditional classroom learning environments. Further, we wondered how these experiential links might differ from those that students may express in their classrooms. The purpose of this study w as to address this gap by investigating the types of KCLs that learners make during a week-long outdoor program, School of the Wild (SOTW). We discuss the types of links identified, understanding them in light of prior KCL research in traditional classroom experiences. By examining these student experiences as elements  of KCLs, this study contributes to a growing body of research that seeks to better understand the knowledge-building mechanisms that come into play in EL beyond the benefits of student satisfaction and other non-cognitive outcomes.

## Literature review

101 Scholars have defined EL as a cyclic, participative, multi-sensory, interactive and applied sense-making process (Beard & Wilson, 2018; Bradberry & De Maio, 2019; Gentry, 1990; Kolb, 1984; Obenchain & Ives, 2006) and as a way of learning that could exist in the cognitive, affective, or behavioral domain (Gentry, 1990; Hoover, 1974; Illeris, 2007). Through EL, knowledge is constructed via the transformation of individual or group experiences that immerse and engage the learner (Beard & Wilson, 2018; Hoover, 1974; Kolb, 1984). For EL to take place, the learner must directly be exposed to a learning environment where the experiences are complex, ill-structured, highly variable, and uncertain (Gentry, 1990; Illeris, 2007). The EL tasks must also have some structure with clearly delineated learning opportunities designed by and conducted under the guidance of skilled facilitators who have been trained to stimulate student reflection and to monitor and evaluate the students’ interactions with the environment (Gentry, 1990). Studies investigating EL  within outdoor learning contexts have reported several positive benefits including student satisfaction and positive attitude towards the activities (Asfeldt et al., 2018; Berg et al., 2021; Blaine & Akhurst, 2020; Cooley et al., 2016; Fägerstan & Blom, 2013; James & Williams, 2017), suggesting an increase in students' sense of freedom (Berg et al., 2021; Davidson, 2001). These results provide evidence that students have enjoyable moments during their outdoor learning. Further, the literature on EL  provides evidence that students are developing important skills related to their social, personal, and academic/professional life. Research on EL in outdoor settings shows gains in social skills (Blaine & Akhurst, 2020; Richmond et al., 2018), team building (Pyun et al., 2020), leadership (Orson et al., 2020; McGowan, 2016), and communication (Orson et al., 2020). Personal benefits include self-efficacy (Richmond et al., 2018), emotional skills (Berg et al., 2021; Chang et al., 2019), problem solving (Collins et al., 2016; Obenchain & Ives, 2006), personal growth (Greffrath et al., 2011), self-esteem (Goldenberg & Sole, 2015), self-confidence (Goldenberg & Sole, 2015; Richmond et al., 2018), self-respect (Goldenberg & Sole, 2015), self-competence (Richmond et al., 2018), mental strength (Davidson, 2001), self-concept (Gibbons et al., 2018), and self-authorship (McGowan, 2016). Overall, EL supports positive outcomes across learners’ personal, school, and work domains. Although these studies reported favorable outcomes of outdoor EL, findings were often based on self-reported, perceptual data (e.g., James & Williams, 2017; Kuo et al., 2018; Meerts-Brandsma et al., 2020; Orson et al., 2020; Richmond et al., 2018) and not on actual academic performance, observed in-class behavior, or documents generated during outdoor activities. For example, the studies conducted by Asfeldt et al.  (2018) focused more on the perception of their participants as they wrote in journals rather than on actual content extracted from the journals. While these studies provided support and insight into learning in outdoor spaces, they did not investigate how what students linked in the  experience may provide a foundation for generative learning in other contexts. To address that gap, we explored the links that the students made in their outdoor experience. Theoretical and conceptual grounding In the current study, we ground our work broadly in constructivism and the notion of generative learning, drawing on the Mind as Rhizome metaphor (MRZ, Schuh & Cunningham, 2004). MRZ pushes our understanding in noting that what we know and, essentially who we have become, is the consequence of being a dynamic, self-organizing system. While we collectively develop the rhizome, what one person candirectly know is their own path or trajectory as the rhizome is created and trav ersed. This metaphor captures the  ideals of what honoring prior knowledge can be in the learning process and how individual’s varied experiences become part of their meaningmaking process. It is a long-held view that learners build their understandings of new information on what they already know (Bransford, Brown, & Cocking, 1999; Jonassen, 1999; Sawyer, 2006; von Glaserfeld, 1998), thereby constructing their knowledge. At the core of perspectives such as constructivism, whether cognitive-or socio-constructivism, is the individual learners’ lens that is developed from prior experiences and used in the interpretation of new events and information they encounter (e.g., Cunningham, 1992; von Uexkull, 1957). Similarly, generative learning, as described initially in the theories coined by Wittrock (1974; 1992), assumed the learner to be an active participant in the learning process: one who is constantly working on the construction of meaningful (Grabowski, 1996), semantic, and idiosyncratic understanding of stimuli available in the environment based on prior experiences and learning (Osborne & Wittrock, 1985). MRZ  supports that these understandings are developed and colored through a lifetime of experiences in school and beyond. In this, considering what one knows becomes functional in that the trajector y, as it amasses the past, forges the future based on prior experiences and opportunities encountered.  MRZ reinforces this view, suggesting that knowing is an interconnected process. Rather than seeing it as a static structure, knowing is continually reshaped as new experiences enter and reconfigure prior pathways of meaning (Schuh & Cunningham, 2004). In that vein, Wittrock stated that generative learning provides a functional look at learning, rather than a structural one, considering the generation of relations among information that the learner knows and what is being learned. The functional model proposes that people use generative processes to “generate meaning of events by constructing relations between 102 new or incoming information and previously acquired information, conceptions, and background knowledge” (1992, p. 532). Thus, the idea of generative learning fits well with the constructivist tradition (Osborne & Wittrock,

1985) and how learners build on understanding as they engage in learning opportunities and use those

opportunities to learn in other places. In the current study, we operationalize this functional process through KCLs. Students (or anyone!) create KCLs between what they learn and what they already know (Schuh, 2003, 2017). KCLs capture a system of cue types, which are prompts that create the link between current and prior learning, and trajectory dimensions, which capture elements of students' prior experiences. Cues may be as simple as similar characteristics such as the word “manuscript” reminding as student of the word “planetarium” (the teacher suggested Latin roots as  an explanation) or as complex as detailed analogies, such as the relationship between chess and the class hierarchy of the Middle Ages (Schuh, 2017). The trajectory dimensions follow students' life experiences indicating with whom and where they have gained this information (e.g., family, friends, schools, media, society). In EL, “school” is often broadened to move beyond the classroom  and may prompt different types of links and, thus, different experiences from the learner’s trajectory. These experiences may add varying rich cues that can prompt new or deeper connections with prior learning, potentially from a wider variety of venues, that may foster generative learning both in the EL environment and beyond. The implications of better understanding how student-created links may differ in various sorts of learning environments prompts us to consider how those student offerings can be of value as they learn. For example, in classrooms that value open dialogue, students may spontaneously share their outside experiences. Teachers may create learning tasks and experiences to provide opportunities on which students candraw to create and share links related to their unique trajectories. U nderstanding student trajectories prompts understanding of how students learn across multiple dimensions of time and place. Given that, it makes sense to explore the experiences in which students participate outside the classroom, such as outdoor learning opportunities, to see how the y may differ in prompt ing knowledge construction processes. Exploring these links allows us to better understand the value of school-related EL experiences, how students build understandings from them, and the multi-dimensional nature of a student’s learning trajectory. The current study will address this point by exploring a nature-oriented, week-long outdoor learning activity. The question that guided this study to better understand the mechanism of a knowledge construction process in an EL environment was: What are the types of KCLs that students draw on in an outdoor EL opportunity and how do they differ from prior descriptions of classroom-based KCLs?

## Participants

In this qualitative study, we recruited participants from three sixth-grade classrooms attending SOTW  in collaboration with the local school district. Originating from the University of Iowa’s Wildlife Camps established in 1991, SOTW  is an accredited special-purpose and environmental school in Iowa, offering week-long day environmental education experiences to students from Iowa  schools (School of the Wild, n.d.). Consenting teachers (n=3) and their students (with parental consent, n=42) participated under the purview of the University’s IRB. Of the 42 student participants, 17 students identified as white, 7 as black or African American, 5 as Asian or Asian American, 3 as Hispanic, Latino/a, or Spanish, and 3 identified as Native American or Alaskan Native. Data sources We gathered video observation, interviews, and student-generated writing to capture learners’ meaning-making across multiple forms of expression. Video recordings are widely used in learning sciences research to document interactional and embodied aspects of learning  that may not be captured through field notes alone (Derry et al., 2010; Jordan & Henderson, 1995). Interviews and a writing actvity  provided additional insight into students’ interpretations of their experiences and supported triangulation across data sources (Merriam & Tisdell, 2015). During SOTW, observations were scheduled with program staff, allowing flexibility based on activities and student consent. Researchers and consenting students wore GoPro cameras to record the observations. During the week in which the students participated in SOTW, we observed the students on four different days in which they engaged in archeology, woodlands, ornithology, and wetlands exploration, amassing 46 video files, of which we analyzed 24 taking place on archeology day. We focused primarily on archaeology day because it more explicitly required students to interpret unfamiliar artifacts and generate hypotheses about historical contexts, the task structure providing a dense context for observing KCLs as students attempted to identify and explain objects inside ill-structured settings.  We also observed the students in various subjects as decided by the teacher and researcher in their school-based classrooms four times before and three times following their week at SOTW. We do not include data from those classroom observations in our data analysis for this paper. 103 After SOTW week, we interview ed 27 students, representing all three classrooms  and demographic groups; each student's interview lasted 10-15 minutes, was recorded, and transcribed. Questions for the se semistructured interviews included asking them about their week in SOTW, what it was like being back in the classroom, differences between learning in SOTW and their classroom, if anything about SOTW had popped into their head once they were back in their classroom, as well as their favorite parts of the week. Additionally, students completed  a survey via Qualtrics on their Chromebooks, which included demographic questions and a writing activity. In the writing activity,  students were asked a variety of questions about their experiences with school, learning, and SOTW. Prior to their writing they were prompted to include “things that your experiences and what you learned at SOTW  reminds you of. These might be things you’ve learned in school, experiences you’ve had outside of school, or any thoughts that you have had about anything.” They were provided several  examples of how to accomplish this task as well as being  given permission to stray from the SOTW  narrative to other things that they were reminded of. Of the 28 writing activities collected, 13 included KCLs that were included in the analysis.

Our analysis began with a review of video files by two of our research team to identify KCLs – those instances in which students made apparent that they were linking what they were encountering in the environment with their prior learning or experiences. A third team member reviewed these KCLs for viability. In identifying KCLs, utterances and writing had to be beyond general declarative knowledge (i.e., knowing that a tree was a tree) demonstrating that the learner was  1) trying to identify something unknown or 2) pointed to an experience or context on which they drew either explicitly or implicitly. For this subset of data, we identified 90 links. Following identification of KCLs, we coded them using a cue and trajectory dimension system (Table 1, Schuh, 2017) and open coded using the constant-comparative methods for additional insights about the data (Merriam & Tisdell, 2015; Strauss & Corbin, 1998). Once KCL s were categorized, they were sorted and summarized based on cue type and trajectory dimension. Given our  KCL analysis was ground ed in MRZ, we sought individual student trajectories, even if seemingly ignored or dismissed by others, each representing a unique vantage point in the collective rhizome.  Links were not restricted to being coded as only one cue or trajectory type. Multiple codings allow ed for considering how different categories and/or trajectories may work together to provide for a  unique student link, allowing for multiple dimensions of time and space  and the complexities of what a personal link could be, how different cues and trajectories appeared in combination in the outdoor environment. as well as differences from existing classroom links. To support qualitative trustworthiness, two researchers independently identified and coded KCLs, with a third researcher reviewing the analysis and discrepancies resolved through discussion. While member checking did not occur due to the age of the participants  and time of school year, interpretations were further supported through triangulation across observational data, interviews, and student writing. The purpose of the current analysis was to note the flavor of the KCLs that learners created in an outdoor nature environment and in their expressed reflections on their experiences via a writing prompt and interviews. During our observations at SOTW and in our analysis of students' interviews and writing we found that the types of KCLs that had been identified in prior research persisted (c.f., Schuh, 2017). Further, there were other links that were not present and some for which the nature of the links were enhanced to provide additional depth in what they were and how they appeared. Most of the links that emerged were prompted by sensory or conceptual cues. Although experiential learning environments often engage learners across cognitive, affective, and behavioral dimensions, the analysis presented here focuses specifically on the cognitive and behavioral processes reflected in students’ KCLs. During data analysis, instances of affective expression were evident in the dataset. 104 Because affective KCLs can be identified as either cue or trajectory, we bracketed it from the present analysis to maintain analytic clarity around the cognitive linking mechanisms, in particular. Sensory links: Embodied and functional connections Sensory links include sights, sounds, and the tactile nature of an environmental element that  prompts a learner to draw upon their prior learning. There were surprisingly few aural cues in the nature environment (an aural cue is not just that they heard a sound or word, but the “sound” of it reminded  the learner of something else). In our 90 identified links in the outdoors, we identified no aural links. No one commented on the sounds of nature as reminding them of something else.

Function cues capture where something functions like something else and are often tactile. We noticed that this type of cue in nature seemed to be embodied differently than was noted in prior research. Similar to a student holding a large wooden broken geometry compass and intuitively using it as a drumstick to tap a beat during classroom group work (Schuh, 2017), a student at SOTW “curled” a ladder [used in the program to build a wickiup] as if it were part of an exercise routine, each action shaped by the object’s physical characteristics. In both cases, the object functioned as something else because of its physical characteristics and the student’s tactile experience (perceived affordance). The physical experience of curling a ladder versus holding a broken compass points to how the availability of objects, space, permissions, and norms  may vary between indoor and outdoor learning environments. We hesitate to use the word “embodied” because we see all learning and activity as being embodied, yet noting it as a “physical” experience seems to not fully capture the link. In other examples, a student walked on a boat ramp and commented about walking on a ramp at the back of their house  and another shouted “I’m Spiderman” as he slid down a hill. These types of cues had not been noted in prior KCL research likely because the embodied experiences that may happen in typical classrooms do not link to something like varied terrains (although we acknowledge that a PE class or recess might prompt those) and the varied objects that may be available. Classrooms, as more contained spaces, may  be limited to what is  available and what is normed activity, whereas an outdoor environment may include more and a greater variety of opportunities. While students engaged in SOTW  activities, they were asked to explore and look for things in the environment, e.g., searching for bugs in a lake  water sample, or nestled in with the prairie grass. On archeology day students explored an area of an old homestead (unaware of the nature of the site prior to their exploration) that included a variety of  physical remnants of  earlier life there including artifacts and some crumbled building foundations. It was clear that what something looked like (a visual sensory cue) prompted their thinking and supported their identification of artifacts. SOTW, in general, prompted experiences of vacations  or various camps, often based on family; not surprising given the age of the children in the study. The media trajectory dimension was prevalent in a different way in our outdoor data than had been in previous research. While it makes sense that media will change over the span of decades (in earlier research there were no identified “Karens”) in that there are different movies, games, media types – what we noticed in the nature setting was that the media links were often enacted. One persistent component, new in this study, was the media trajectory dimension being prompted by a function cue seemed to come  from the research project itself – using GoPro cameras. The links were apparent from the students ’ role playing that they were broadcaster s on a news TV show or being a vlogger. In prior research  (Schuh, 2004) media links were often illustrated through watching or game playing. They were conceptual links. In this study,  the links that came from media were not always about the media content (although there a number of mentions about basketball and soccer players), but students interacted with media in a different way than in the past. It seemed that media had become much more active in that students linked it with the environment enactively. As with the lack of aural cues noted in the data, we were surprised at the paucity of a friends dimension (n=1), which had been prevalent in prior research (Schuh, 2017). In addition, school was a prominent trajectory dimension in our prior research as students made links across content areas in their classroom and across school years. In the archeology data we analyzed from our SOTW  week, there were very few school links (e.g., Christopher Columbus in the gas candiscussion). Several  students mentioned in interviews and in writing when prompted that they found connections mainly to science (they had studied fossils) and social studies (they connected with artifacts). Generally, these link s were within their current school year.  The structure of the task and environmental factors may be considered while explaining these patterns. For example,  historical framing of the task may have prompted students to draw on everyday knowledge rather than formalized academic knowledge. 107 In short, we believe that the absence of certain factors like aural cues and the friends and school dimensions is not incidental but are reflective of how the context and task features can shape which trajectories become salient. In summary, the types of links that we noted in our observations of the students, in their interviews, and their writing were similar in many ways to those in prior research. Where we noticed differences were in the nonexistence of the aural sensory cue type which we had thought would be present given the environment, the limited links to school content, and the lack of friends that were linked from their prior experiences. We noticed an expansion in the function/tactile cue when experienced  in that the embodiment of the experience was different. The media dimension, as would be expected, had changed given the evolution of media. What was of interest was the enactment that accompanied much of it – although media might be considered non-physical, apparently there are elements that are more likely to be enacted now than in the past. In addition, this learning  activity in which students were to explore an archaeological site with artifacts and crumbled building foundations  prompted challenges as they juxtaposed what they saw with their current understandings of places they had experienced on their life trajectory.

## Findings & Analysis

Our analysis revealed that outdoor EL triggered familiar KCLs, though with notable shifts. Function cues became embodied, where students curled ladders as if weights or pretended to be superheroes  while sliding down hills. The outdoor setting seemed to  expand how prior knowledge was activated through a more physical process. Learners often made tacit “now and then” juxtapositions, projecting contemporary understandings onto historical artifacts. Together, these patterns suggest that outdoor settings expand how prior knowledge is activated, making linking a more physical and performative process.  Meaning was made in these cases by weaving together new encounters with prior experiences (Wittrock, 1992). As guided by the MRZ metaphor, learning was a network of experiences that grew and intersected across contexts such as family, media, and everyday life. Importantly, these links emerged through interaction as students shared interpretations of artifacts, responded to peers’ ideas, and revised their thinking in response to facilitator prompts. The archeology exploration may have positioned students as distributed interpreters of artifacts across space, yet  they often engaged  in parallel rather than tightly coordinated dialogue which may have limited peer uptake. Despite that, knowledge construction occurred through a dynamic process in which individual trajectories intersected within the shared learning environment. Media trajectories were also enacted, with students taking on “vlogger” or “broadcaster ” roles while using GoPro cameras; illustrating that linking is a cognitive process tied to the body. In earlier research, media connections surfaced primarily as conceptual references where students mentioned shows, games, or characters (Schuh, 2017). However, during this study,  students performed these roles. This shift reflects  a change in how young people engage with media, moving beyond media content to  identities. Concurrently, the use of wearable GoPro cameras may have shaped these interactions as their presence created a context in which students  adopted familiar media roles. While this method offered rich first-person observational data, it may also have amplified the visibility of media trajectories. This should be considered when interpreting these findings. Equally telling in our analysis was the absence of aural cues and minimal references to school or friends. This pattern underscores how the learning setting itself shapes which parts of a student’s experience rise to the surface (Bransford et al., 1999). Outdoor programs, by their nature, seem to privilege lived, embodied, and homebased knowledge while placing less emphasis on formal schooling or friendships as resources for linking. Finally, although affective responses were visible in our data, we did not include them in this analysis; our future work examines how emotion intertwines with KCLs to shape meaning making in outdoor learning. Our study was filled with several  challenges – students tiring of wearing cameras, adjusting for or ignoring non-consenting students, and the challenges of logistics. These are not unusual for studies in nature. Further work should continue to refine ways to better capture individual knowledge building efforts as well as interactions to better understand the meaning the students take away. These findings refine the KCL framework by highlighting embodied functional cues, distinguishing media-as-content from media-as-enactment and drawing attention to tacit temporal comparisons. For educators, the implication is that outdoor EL offers unique opportunities to broaden the range of connections students make. However, it may require intentional scaffolds to draw in aural noticing, scho ol-based knowledge, and peer resources. Designing with these affordances and absences in mind may  deepen generative learning across both outdoor and classroom contexts.
