# Understanding Data Practices in How Hobbyists Conduct Their Everyday Lives

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
With the shared goal of empowering people in their data use, researchers have taken different paths to understanding how people learn about and use data. The present work aligns itself with the view that data use is a situated practice and attempts to expand this notion to person’s multiple pursuits. We conducted a case study of two hobbyists who begin to use data and find themselves shifting their practices in multiple domains as a consequence of their data use. We conducted a multi-phase qualitative analysis of semi-structured interviews with these hobbyists to understand this relationship. Our analytic understanding is premised on the conduct of everyday life (Dreier, 2008), a concept that brings together a person’s perspective, situated participation, and changing social contexts in understanding how they organize their day-to-day activity. We discuss the implications of this work in relation to education and design.

## Introduction

The use of data is integral to human activity, and,  over the last few decades, data production has rea ched unprecedented degrees of scale and prevalence. With  the shared goal of empowering people in their data  use, researchers have taken different paths to  understanding how people learn about and use data. One strand of research focuses on improving and promoting data sc ience education to support educational and vocation al pathways for students (National Academies of Scienc es, Engineering, and Medicine, 2023). A major goal in this area is to define effective and appropriate standards of data use that reflect the data practices of professionals and experts. This work considers how variations across disciplines, ages, and settings are important factors in learning to use data. Other researchers have widened their interest in data practices beyond institutional standards, shifting attention toward the emergence of data practices in  everyday, contemporary life. This view emphasizes how the use of data is situated in the fabric of social practice (Wilkerson & Polman, 2020). This perspective includes how historical and ideological conditions influence the  use of, attitudes toward, and normalization of dat a. Research that aligns with this perspective has examined how  data practices are situated in people’s routines (P ink et al., 2017), distributed across technology and activity (Lee & Dubovi, 2020), and a part of how people make sense of themselves and the world around them (Radinsky & Ta bak, 2022). In this paper, we use the phrase data practice to situate the technical use of data in the social practice of hobbies. Doing so allows us to align with the widened view of data use and to consider the ramifications of data in practice. Furthermore, we use the conduct of everyday life as a conceptual framework for understanding how data practices are implicated in shaping someone’s ongoing experience. The conduct of everyday life is a concept that brings together a person’s perspective, situated pa rticipation, and changing social contexts to unders tand their active effort in organizing their day-to-day activity (Dreier, 2008, 2020; Kristensen & Schr aube, 2014). This perspective allows us to build upon the situated vi ew of learning by emphasizing how learning observed  in local social practices is distributed across different places and time periods in one’s life (Bell et al., 2012; Penuel et al., 2017). It also attunes us to the ongoing and confli ctual nature of social life that is evident as we w ork to control our pursuits along pathways that constrain us (Drei er, 2020; Højholt & Kousholt, 2020). This lends ins ight to understanding how participation is historically and culturally reinforced as well as individually experienced. Such problems are implicated in the ways people experience immedi ate situations and organize future pursuits. We build on these conceptual premises by exploring how  hobbyists understand and organize their participat ion in relation to data practices. This line of researc h is useful in unearthing how data practices are wo ven into how people experience the conduct of their everyday lives.

## Design and participants

The present paper represents a theory-driven analysis of a subset of data from an explora tory study examining data practices among hobbyists. Participants were recruited through criterion sampli ng and snowball sampling schemes (Onwuegbuzie & Collins, 2015). The main criterion was that participants self-identified as users of data within their hobby. Snowball sampling was used to r each participants in hobbyist communities that were  not otherwise accessible to the researcher. Participant s were informed of their rights as participants, co nsented to participation, and compensated for their participation. The cases presented in this study  were selected for their diversity in hobby, type of data, use of tools, as well as their subjective reporting on emotions, attitudes, and social contexts.

## Data collection

Interviews were the primary form of data collection because of their capacity to elicit personal experiences across time from the point of view of the participant (Cre swell & Creswell, 2018). This was essential to our line of inquiry since we were interest ed in how people experience data use in the conduct  of their lives. The interviews were conducted through teleconferencing because of the affordances of screen sharing and location and the constraints of the COVID-19 pandemic. The semi-structured interview had two phases. The first phase involved asking the participant questions about their hobby and their use of data in that hobby. Because people ’s conceptions of data are known to be wide-ranging (Zins, 2007), the researcher initiated the interview with a conversation to gain an understanding of the participant’s own conception. In the second phase of the interview, the participant was asked to demonstrate some aspect of their data use on camera or through screen sharing while the researcher observed. Throu ghout the interview, impromptu follow-up questions were asked to better understand the participants’ statements and their hobby practices.

## Analytic method

We conducted a multi-phase qualitative analysis involving independent co ding, analytic memo s, collaborative negotiation of meaning, and data visualization. After reading through each of the transcripts, the first author used structural coding (Saldaña, 2021) to code responses  that aligned with the aims of this research. Then, this organization was used by both authors to conduct a constant comparative analysis as described by Leech  and Onwuegbuzie (2007). Furthermore, this process infor med the creation of concept tables (Miles et al., 2 020) to facilitate sensemaking across cases. Memos were prod uced from analytic sessions to both deepen our understanding of the data and to foster communication among team members (Birks et al., 2008).

## Findings

In the following sections, we share how these hobbyists  moved toward and away from the use of data. For ea ch participant, we begin with a biological sketch of their data use and end with implications about their data practices in the conduct of everyday life.

Tia Tia was a 21-year-old woman pursuing her interest in computer program ming. Her pastime on LeetCode, an online program for technical interview preparation, blended with these professional aspirations. Conceptually, she viewed data as the phase preceding inf ormation: “When we’re trying to extract meaning fro m data, we get information.” In her practice, data were displayed to her: the runtime of her code, the percentile of her runtime compared to other LeetCode users, a categorical dif ficulty (easy, medium, ha rd), and a problem’s frequency on technical interviews. She was looking to secure an internship, so she followed the recommendation of o ther jobseekers to try LeetCode’s practice problems. Tia  used the data from LeetCode to assess her performa nce and identify problems that might benefit her most. For examp le, after she submitted her code, LeetCode showed whether the solution was acceptable and her code’s runtime percentile relative to other users. When the percentile was high, she knew “that’s how you know you’re doing great.” In addition, she was concerned about wasting time on unnecessary problems: “My goal was to find a commonly asked question, such that I wouldn’t waste too much of my time working on a super hard question but in reality no one actually brings it [the super hard q uestion] up in actual interviews.” To help her be efficient, she would browse data about each problem, including its frequency in actual interviews, the submission acceptance rate, and the difficulty (designated by the problem’s author). Interpreting these sources of data was a challenge. There was no transparency surrounding the calculation of the runtime percentile. The platform did not share whose submissions, the number of submissions, or the range of submission dates that her solution  was compared to. Tia felt frustrated whenever she g ot a low percentile or had difficulty solving a problem labeled “easy.” It  caused her to doubt her ability to get a job, whet her she was good enough for the internship, or if she was even ready to pursue a computer programming career. Because of these doubts, she stopped using LeetCode altogether  for a while. However, Tia spoke with some friends and learned this was a common reaction: “People told me that they all feel the same way when they just started.” From there on, she resumed using LeetCode until she successfully landed an internship. In Tia’s pursuit of an internship, Tia encountered the mediating role of community in her understanding of data practices in LeetCode. Understandably, feeling like her career prospects were threatened pushed her away from the practice until she discovered that these feelings were normal and not predictive of her actual career outlook. The solution to continuing on LeetCode was  not simply persisting in its environment but engag ing with its community of users and her trusted friends. Thu s, her use of data was  central to how she conducted herself across places and activities. This demonstrates how  understanding data, an important part of data lite racy (D’Ignazio & Bhargava, 2016), is one way by which practices encourage or constrain people’s pursuits. Jay Jay was a 28-year-old man who was pursuing personal fitness. In 2018,  he wanted to “reinvent” himself before starting college, so discipline, sustainability, and efficiency were important to him. Conceptually, he equated data with information itself: “Data is just any information that I use to make a decision.” He used several categories of data daily for over four years, including calories and macronutrients consumed, sets and repetitions (reps) of exercises, and body weight. Jay’s goals were layered: he wanted to gain weight and build strength because he also wanted to feel better about his appearance, have his clothes fit better, and perform better in any physical activity. He said he was embarrassed to go to the gym in publ ic, so he used online sources t o plan his diet and create exercise routines he could do at home. He manually documented most of his data: he used a notebook and  Excel sheet to track his exercise and MyFitnessPal for his diet. He counted calories and researched macronutrient values for long enough that he could visually estimate, for exa mple, how much chicken would meet his protein intak e goal for a meal. Eventually, he could apply this strategy to plan meals for a week. This ability to estimate his food portions marked a  period of transformation in his data practice and hobby. In comparison, calorie counting was becoming a cumbersome practice. “It’s just very challenging to have to like constantly input everything,” he re called. Because he could now recognize the food tha t met his needs without checking the app, he realized, “There wasn’ t really a need for me to continue counting.” From then on, he began to rely on his ability to intuitively esti mate an appropriate food portion, though he would c ontinue to research nutrition information for new recipes.

Although the numerical tracking stopped, Jay’s persp ective demonstrated that the emergent practice of measuring intuitively was an evolution of the origi nal, more visibly data-driven practice. He used data for long enough that he developed a data handling skill that was suited to his needs. He could answer the same real-world questions about his diet while no longer depending on MyFitnessPal. Counting calories, though popular, requires a sizable amount of time. Jay determined that this was not an indefinitely sustainable practice, so he transformed it. This exemplified the very conduct of everyday life—a flow wherein people must participate in the organization of their usual routines or improvisationally try new practices.

## Discussion and conclusion

In this paper, we attempted to show how people’s ch anging data practices were a part of how they exper ienced multiple pursuits. This work builds on existing research by demonstrating that data practices are not just situated in a single time and place but in how one acts across their everyday life activities. For example, it shows how the emotions that influence discrete  data practices (Radinsky & Tabak, 2022) are consequ ential to entire pursuits (e.g., pursuing a career in computer science) and h ow one conducts themself elsewhere (e.g., turning t o friends for advice). In addition, our research foregrounds the creativity and agency that hobbyists exercise b y experimenting with using and not using data in their pursuits. This research supports transparency in design that accounts for the user’s experiences outside of the local place and time. As evidenced by both participants’ changing practices, the design of a tool can have a n immense impact on one’s pursuits. In particular, Tia’s case speaks to the ongoing conversations about ideological concerns with data in contemporary society, such as the rank ing of users. We encourage designers to consider ho w their user’s future may be influenced by the representations of data in their environments. This may involve being more explicit in encouraging how users should use and interpret data. Alternatively, designers could be more transparent about what is being represented in the data and what its potential limitations are. Regardless of the specific design decisions, we advocate for a culture of design that  does not position users as insufficient; rather, o ur designs should account for the ways that users are already creatively interacting with data. Our implications for the schooling of data practice s build on the fluidity of data practices we observed. First, as Jay exemplified, we caution educators to conclude that a student has stopped or is failing to develop data literacy simply because their activities have chang ed. Jay’s initial use of MyFitnessPal fit the typic al practice of counting calories, but this was not the perpetual f orm of his data practice. His use of data had trans formed—not ended. Second, the use of data marks a particular practice people have amidst their many other pursuits (e.g., selfimprovement, seeking a job, entering college). For many people, the use of data may never be a part of  one of their pursuits, not because they did not participat e in school or do not see its contextual possibilit y, but because its possible realization may stand in direct conflict with the way they choose to conduct their lives.
