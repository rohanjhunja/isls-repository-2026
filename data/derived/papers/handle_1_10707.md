# Data Visualization Renarrated: Probing the Role of Renarrating in Data Visualization Reimagining

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Data visualization reimagining can be an effective way for students to develop a deep understanding of a given data visualization. It encompasses three different dimensions: reformatting, reframing, and renarrating. In this study, we explored the role of renarrating in a data reimagining activity. With semi-structured interviews, we found that renarrating can support reformatting and reframing by providing a concrete context and renarrating established personally meaningful connections between students and the data visualization. Our study suggested a data visualization reimagining practice should initiate with renarrating and utilize renarrating to support personally meaningful learning.

## Introduction and theoretical framework

In this data era, students encounter numerous data visualizations every day. Engaging with these data visualizations can be informal yet valuable learning opportunities in the realm of data science. Traditional school mathematics curriculum emphasizes form alisms (Nathan, 2012), so data visualization learni ng relies on symbolism. However, there are other ways to comprehend data visualization, such as reading and reimagining it, which are worth exploring.

In this study, we use Rubel et al.'s (2021) connected frameworks for critical reading and reimagining data visualizations. In contrast to traditional mathematical symbol-based methods of teaching students how to interpret data visualization, this framework considers unders tanding data visualizations from a “think with data” mindset, as advocated by Hardin et al. (2015). According to Rubel et al. (2021), critical reading of data visualization is defined as an approach to reading data visualizations beyond t heir instrumental use and mathematical properties to their role and production as social texts.

Rubel et al. (2021) also propose a framework for re imagining data visualizations, which is defined as “reflection about what these data [in an existing visualization] might tell, to whom, and why” (p. 255). Data visualization reimagining includes three dimensions: reformatting, reframing, and renarrating. Reforma tting considers the possibilities of involving other meas ures that are not in the current dataset or data vi sualization; reframing is to use an alternative way to connect different variables and/or novel relationships between variables; renarrating is defined as thinking of new stories from the reimagined data visualization and potential audience of those stories. Within the process of data visualization reimaginin g, students can engage in “hypothetical reasoning,” a mathematical practice that allows students to seize the potential consequences of a not-yet-realized mathematical model (Skovsmose, 2020). This practice  candeepen their comprehension of the existing dat a visualization by proposing potential changes to it without the need for extensive implementation effor ts. This process also enables students to author the data visualization from their own perspectives (Rubel et al., 2021): by actively thinking and making decisions about the po ssible meas ures, relationships, and narrative structures, students can envision the possibilities and realize the limitations of the given data visualization. Among the three dimensions, we agree with Rubel et al. (2021) that renarrating is particularly significant. Empowering students to construct their own narrativ es around data visualizations can anchor their data  science learning in personally meaningful contexts (Wilkerson & Polman, 2020). Renarrating encourages thinking of new stories from the reimagined data visualization and potential audience of those stories (Rubel et al., 2021). Renarrating embodies a narrative mode of thought. A ccording to Bruner (2009), people have two modes of thought exploring the world: a logico-scientific mode of thought, which focuses on a formal mathematical system to describe and explain the world through logical categorization, and a narrative mode of thought that emphasizes deriving meaning from stories. Conventional school e ducation in mathematics and data science predominat ely prioritizes the logico-scientific mode, centralizing the role of formal no tations and symbols (Nathan, 2012), but the narrative mode of thought should  be addressed more. The narrative mode of thought he lps students anchor their understanding of observed phenomena in a meaningful structure–their constructed narrative. Narratives can contribute to the uniqueness of each individual’s s tory. When renarrating a data visualization, studen ts connect their story to their real-life experiences, bridging their understanding of d ata visualization and their personal context. Hence, when reimagining a data visualization, renarrating welcomes their own narrative mode of thought and embraces their personally meaningful constructs.

The purpose of this study is to dive deeper into the process of data reimagining, with a specific emphasis on understanding the role of renarrating.  Previous studies have shown that engaging in data-based narration can improve students’ data literacy (e.g., Kahn, 2020). Although constructing narratives can help students understand data visualizations, there is a limited amount of literature focusing on researching the role of narrative in specific data science learning practices. To better understand how youth participate in renar rating data visualizations, we investigate the following research question: What is the role of renarrating in data visualization reimagining?

## Participants and data collection

This study was set in the context of a 14-week informal education program focused on critical  perspectives towards community-centered data visualization for middle grades youth in Eastern Tennessee. The program aimed to cultivate youth data understanding and develop a rt-based data visualization skills.  In those learning sessions, they were asked to think and draw their own stories  based on their understanding of three different da ta visualizations. The participants of this program were from a local afterschool club. Given that this study intended to explore the participants’ reimagining of data visualization, we designed a semi-structured interview  based on the Rubel et al. (2021) framework. The interview was designed around reading and reimagining a new data visualization ab out food and calorie intake (see Figure 1). The int erview contained four separate sections of interview prompts in the following order: (1) Data visualization reading (e.g., “What do those colors, percentages, and numbers mean ?”), (2) Renarrating (e.g., “If you were a reporter using this graph in an article, what kind of story do you want to tell in your article?”), (3) Reformatting, (e.g., “What is the data in the visualization? ”) and (4) Reframing (e.g., “ What patterns did you notice in this data visualiza tion? Why are those patterns interesting to you?).

We recruited three participants (pseudonyms: Ronald, Roy, and Ben) for this study, who voluntarily took this interview and were in grades 6, 8, and 9 respectively, from a middle school and a high school in a Southeastern medium-sized city.  The first author  conducted three individual interviews in Fall 2023  that were videotaped in person at the after-school club where their weekly data science learning program took place. The interviews were conducted after two weeks when participants reimagi ned data visualizations in weekly sessions. Each in terview took about 20 to 30 minutes. All interviews were transcribed. ## Data analysis

The first author conducted a qualitative analysis of the interview transcripts using a combination of deductive and inductive coding approaches. Deductive coding was based on the Rubel et al. (2021) framework that informed the design of the interview protocol. Inductive codes w ere developed within each  deductive coding categor y to understand how interviewees approached each aspect  because inductive coding is suitable for emergent, data-driven qualitative analysis (Saldaña, 2009). In thi s study, the unit of analysis was the participant’s  turn of talk, which was equivalent to their complete response to an interview question or prompt. ## Findings

Overall, each participant created their own data narrative when reimagining the data visualization in the interview. Ronald noticed Americans’ high-calorie intake, drawing from their experiences of e ncountering calorie-dense snacks displayed in easily accessible places at grocery stores and finding fast-food restaurants all over the place. By comparing the pie charts of different countries, Roy discerned the variations in diet structures around the world and suggested healthcare professionals should use t hese charts to encourage healthier eating habits. I n addition, Ben noted the significantly low proportion of produ ce in most countries, sparking an interest in the t rends in produce and meat consumption over the years. We use two excerpts to show the role of renarrating. In Excerpt 1, the researcher asked Ronald “So what do you think this data will be used for?” to consider the potential application of the data provided in the data visualization: Ronald: I guess. Some people, you know, they just eat what they want and stuff, and if like they don't. It's hard to explain. Some people don't know how many calories they're eating in one day because they don't really, you know, not really watch themselves per se. […] Ronald: Since I've seen this [data visualization] for the f irst time. It's just usually like that the place where I live isn't like very healthy. Well, I already kind of knew that, because, you know, every other, not every other street, but like  you can go down the road, and you can see, like KFC, Mcdonald's… All that stuff, and it just, you know, it's not very healthy.

Ronald’s first answer was coded as People of Interest or Benefit  and second as Personal Connections. Proposing the potential application of the data vis ualization could be considered a form of “hypotheti cal reasoning” (Skovsmose, 2020) because this question encouraged learners to think about the meaningful u se of the data visualization beyond perceiving it merely as a collection of mathematical symbols and notations. Ronald’s answer, integrated into his renarrating of the data visualization, reflected their awareness of people’s ignorance of daily calorie intake and concerns about those fast-food chain restaurants selling unhealthy foods ever ywhere. Their renarrating closely related to their life experiences, frequently seeing those restaurants, and their background knowledge, unhealthy foods being sold in the fast-food chain restaurants. This connection eventually led to their concern about calorie overconsumption and their suggestion to utilize this data visualization to enhance people’s awareness of heal thier diet. In Excerpt 2, Ronald responded to a question from t he interviewer asking, “ If you bring this pie chart, and they see like different s izes of the pie charts here. So who might be those people?” This question aimed to elicit a response about the people who might be interested in the pattern that Ronald identified previously (the correlation between the size of the pie charts and the total amount of calorie intake): Because I don't know about everyone as a whole, bec ause, you know everyone thinks differently. But I know that my family specifically  would be well, specifically my dad and his side of the family. And also a bunch of cousins because they're really into like data and graphs and stuff. My dad, I think, graduated from college.  First, it was like not musical, camera and filming. So during his classes he would spend like forever trying to do graphs and stuff to show what people like, and what kind of like it's this whole thing. He's pretty cool… (Ronald) This response was coded as Personal Connections. Instead of thinking about a general audience, the y specifically mentioned their family members, especi ally those on their father’s side. Using their fath er as an example, Ronald emphasized their father’s interest in data and graphs, stemming from his college cours es in camera and filming where he sought to depict people ’s preferences through graphs. Their response demonstrated personal meaningfulness by incorporating their family members into his renarrating.

## Discussion and conclusion

The findings above underscore the essential role of  renarrating in the three data visualization reimag ining dimensions. Although the design of a data visualization inherently includes a space for storytelling (Segel & Heer, 2010), renarrating serves as a key to unlocking tha t space and inviting learners into it. By evoking a  narrative mode of thought (Bruner, 2009), renarrating provide s learners with a concrete context to construct the ir reimagining. Within this context, renarrating promo ted the integration of learners’ own knowledge, experiences, and perspectives into the reimagining process. Based on our findings, we suggest that future research can further investigate the use of  low-stakes renarrating activities to build the main str ucture for their data visualization reimagining, and incorporate their personal knowledge, experience s, and perspectives into their renarration to build up this structure.

In conclusion, our study reveals that renarrating can provide a context to support students’ incorporation of their personal knowledge and experiences into th eir renarration. However, this study is preliminary  and only explores one dimension of data reimagining. Future studies can explore the relationship between these three dimensions and whether/how renarrating can support reformatting and reframting. By illuminating the process of data reimagining, data visualization reimagining may be an effective practice in the data science curriculum.
