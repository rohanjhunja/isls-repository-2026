# From Mechanics to Meaning: Tracing Systems Thinking in Learners’ Game Design Talk

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Game design has emerged as a promising context for fostering systems thinking, yet little is known about how such thinking manifests during the design process itself. This study investigates how learners develop systems thinking while collaboratively designing tabletop games. Drawing on frameworks that treat games as systems, we analysed the discourse of two 9th-grade students participating in a two-day game design workshop. Our analysis revealed that learners exhibited five key systems thinking features through their design talk: interconnectedness through rule dependencies, causality through action-consequence mapping, synthesis through coherence checking, emergence through playability patterns, and system function through design evaluation. These findings suggest that systems thinking emerges as an evolving process embedded in the collaborative act of game-making. The study contributes process-level insights into how game-making activities support systems thinking development.

Over the past three decades, game-making has gained increasing attention as a productive learning context for students. This approach is rooted in constructionist philosophy (Kafai, 1995; Papert & Harel, 1991). This paradigm positions learners as designers  who learn by constructing artefacts that express and test their understanding. When learners design a game, they are required to create a rule-based interactive system that embodies their reasoning about how elements interact. This act of constructing a p layable system invites reflection, iteration, and collaboration.

Empirical research on what students learn by making  games has shown diverse learning benefits, including computational thinking, creativity, and problem-solving (Denner et al., 2019; Earp, 2015; Kafai & Burke, 2015; Wu et al., 2025). More recently, researchers have begun exploring how game design may foste r systems thinking (Akcaoglu & Green, 2019; Parekh et al., 2021; Puttick & Tucker-Raymond, 2018), which refers to understanding phenomena through the relationships and feedback among their parts rather than through isolated components (Arnold & Wade, 2015; Meadows, 2008). It involves recognising key features such as non-linearity, interconnectedness, feedback, emergence, and causality, which together describe how actions and interactions within a system generate collective behaviours. It is considered to be an important 21st-century skill that enables learners to understand dynamic and interdependent phenomena. Because games are interactive systems by nature, game design inherently involves reasoning about interrelated components, feedback, and balance (Fullerton, 2004b; Tekinbaş & Zimmerman, 2003; Tekinbaş, 2007). We take this view of games as systems of rules, interactions, and outcomes. Designing a game involves constructing and testing a system in which each design decision alters gameplay and player experience. It also requires learners to identify how parts (like rules, mechanics, goals, and player actions) fit together and how changes in one component influence others. In this sense, to design a game is  to build an interactive system. Thus, when learners discuss how rules fit together or predict the effects of a change, they are engaging in forms of systems thinking. While recent studies provide evidence that learners candevelop systems thinking while making games, these studies predominantly measure systems thinking through pre –post assessments, interviews, or analysis of game artefacts (Akcaoglu & Green, 2019; Parekh et al., 2021; Puttick et al., 2023). What remains unclear is how systems thinking emerges during the game design process itself. This includes moment-to-moment reasoning about rule interactions, predictions about gameplay consequences, and iterative refinements that constitute gamemaking activity. We address this gap in this pilot study by analysing learners’ design discourse. Our research question is: How does engaging in the game design process enable learners to think in terms of systems?

## Method

We conducted the study as a two-day after-school workshop with two ninth-grade students in Mumbai. On the first day, participants collaboratively redesigned a partially developed tabletop game. They were then introduced to the theme of the socio-ecological lifecycle of a cotton T-shirt and explored relevant information. On the second day, they designed and playtested an original game based on this theme. We collected audio-video recordings, screen recordings and the final game prototype. We transcribed the audio using a turn-taking approach. We used 2892 deductive coding to analyse four episodes in which participants reasoned about a particular game mechanic or dynamic. We defined episodes as stretches of interaction where participants discussed how elements influenced one another or evaluated the behaviour of the game. We drew on Arnold and Wade’s (2015) framework to identify systems thinking features in participants’ design discourse. We present our findings through two episodes showing how engaging in the game design process enabled learners to recognise and reason various aspects of systems.

## Finding 1: Learners recognised interconnectedness when they noticed

dependencies between their devised game rules.

This episode took place during a game redesign activity. Bharati and Aryan were modifying a partially developed game. They introduced a new rule involving an ‘exploding block.’ While doing so, Bharati began examining how this rule would interact with an existing movement rule for the character, Ute. Bharati began by checking the rule: “Ute moves in a straight line?” Aryan replied, “Yeah, this is like the rule.” Bharati then raised a concern: “But if the player knows that this will explode, would they change  directions?” Through this question, she noticed a dependency between the exploding block and the movement rule. She pointed out that player behaviour under one rule could affect how the other rule works. Aryan then explored possible changes. He suggested modifying movement or removing the dice: “Should we change this or that?... Should we keep the dice or not?”  Bharati responded, “I think we should.” We interpret this interaction as evidence that learners recognised interconnectedness in the game system. This happened when they noticed dependencies between their devised game rules. Bharati identified how one rule could influence another. Aryan considered how changing one part would require changes elsewhere. They shifted from thinking about individual rules to thinking about how the rules work together. Finding 2: Learners reasoned about causality through mapping player actions to gameplay consequences.

This episode took place during the design of a trivia-based card game, one of the ideas Aryan and Bharati explored while creating their original game based on the t-shirt lifecycle theme. Aryan and Bharati were working out how player actions would shape gameplay. They began by deciding the structure of the game: “How many questions should we make?” Bharati suggested, “Twenty. If one player answers five questions.”  Aryan then explored how turns would work: “You will have 5 cards; I will have 5 cards… What will you do if I don't know the question? Do I have to take another question from the deck?” Bharati responded, “ You can just swap. I’ll read one card, and others will answer.” The focus then shifted to the consequences of player actions. Aryan asked, “ OK, so if I answer, what will happen?” Bharati replied, “Then you will gonext.” Aryan extended this further: “Okay, so the first one to complete all the questions wins… So, it's more than a three-player game.” He also noted, “So, we need many different questions, right?” Later, Bharati suggested, “I think let’s do three questions for now and see if it works.” Aryan then raised a longer-term concern: “But wouldn’t these questions get older? As you play, the questions will get older… they get old really quick.”  Bharati concluded, “Then… let’s not do it?” We interpret this interaction as evidence that learners reasoned about causality in the game system. This became visible when they mapped player actions: answering questions, taking turns & completing sets to their consequences in gameplay progression and outcomes. Aryan and Bharati moved from proposing rules to explaining what effects these actions would produce on the gameplay. This showed an understanding of how gameplay unfolds through cause-and-effect relations between different game elements. This study set out to understand how game-making enabled different dimensions of systems thinking. By analysing learners’ reasoning as they created and iteratively refined games, we found that the design process itself functioned as a medium through which systems thinking became visible to the learners and was actively constructed. While earlier studies have demonstrated that game design can support systems thinking, they have largely focused on end products or performance measures (Akcaoglu & Green, 2019; Parekh et al., 2021; Puttick & Tucker-Raymond, 2018). This work contributes by showing mechanisms through which systems thinking unfolds during the design process through learners’ discourse. These findings affirm that game design can serve as a productive  context for fostering systems thinking. This happens particularly when learners reason about interdependencies, predict outcomes and iteratively refine their games. This study is limited by its focus on a single dyad and two episodes, constraining generalisability. The analysis relies on observable discourse, and some reasoning may not have been articulated. Additionally, facilitation and task framing may have influenced learners’ attention to relationships and feedback within the game system. 2893 Akcaoglu, M., & Green, L. S. (2019).
