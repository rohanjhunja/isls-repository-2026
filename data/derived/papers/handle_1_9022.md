# Creating Space for Formative Peer Feedback in a Community-Oriented MOOC

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
To advance community-oriented instructional approaches within MOOCs, we examine the impact of creating space for formative peer feedback using a digital workbook in a Resilient Teaching MOOC. We use a coding scheme for classifying peer feedback to analyze responses to shared workbook entries. Findings revealed the presence of five attributes in the feedback: content, explanations, advice, questions, and community connections. Our study elaborates approaches for creating productive forms of knowledge exchange among peers.

## Introduction

Massive open online courses (MOOCs) generally embody individualistic approaches to instruction (i.e., valuing independence), which could hinder opportunities to engage in significant learning with peers. We investigate how MOOCs can become more collectivist (i.e., valuing communities), exploring ways that in structors can pragmatically advance forms of social interaction that connect learners, their coursework, and their peers’ ideas. This study investigates peer feedback exchanged in a custom digital workbook and gallery space situated in a MOOC on Resilient Teaching. Our overarching aim is to advance community-oriented approaches in open, online learning environments. The following research questions guide our inquiry: ● What can we observe about the ways in which learners “take up” opportunities to give formative peer feedback in a MOOC on Resilient Teaching?

● What are the characteristics of the feedback offered? ● In what ways might opportunities for formative peer feedback allow instructors to advance collectivist approaches within open, online learning environments?

Relevant literature Shute (2008) defines formative feedback as “information communicated to the learner to modify their thinking or behavior to improve learning” and asserts that the aim of formative feedback is to “increase student knowledge, skills, and understanding of content areas and general skills” (p. 154). To understand the quality of peer feedback (format ive or summative), scholars have developed various frameworks to assess it. Prins et al. (2006) advanced a framework that included the following categories: contentrelated remarks, explanations of remarks, suggestio ns for improvement, questions to foster reflection,  and formulation and structure of feedback. The Prins et  al. (2006) framework is the basis of the coding sc heme we used to understand the characteristics of formative feedback given and received by learners in our study.

## Research context

The Resilient Teaching MOOC launched on the Coursera platform in June 2020. A stated goal of this communityoriented MOOC was to foster a sense of connection and community amongst learners in the course. The course culminated in a resilient teaching plan, a final pr oject that learners could apply in their respective  instructional contexts. Throughout the course, a digital workbook developed by the authors’ home unit incrementally supported learners’ development of their resilient teaching p lans through a series of reflection prompts indexed  to course topics. The digital workbook included 12 total prompts distributed throughout the course. The digital workbook included a gallery space that allowed learners to s hare portions of their teaching plans and receive f ormative feedback from peers that could inform their final assignments. Learners could browse shared entries, ask questions, and offer feedback on their peers’ submissions. For  this unstructured task, learners were encouraged t o review gallery submissions and leave open-ended comments on peers’ entries.

## Method

Data sources We collected data from the Resilient Teaching MOOC from the time of course launch in June 2020 to Octo ber

2021. During this time, 2,247 learners were “active” participants in the course, meaning that they eng aged with

We restricted our analysis to initial conversation thread responses (n=72). Coding scheme and coding approach We simplified Prins et al.’s (2006) coding scheme t o develop our codebook for classifying feedback: co ntent, explanations, advice, and questions. Two coders col laborated to code peer feedback responses using a d irected content analysis approach (Hsieh & Shannon, 2005). They applied all relevant codes in the codebook to peer feedback responses (i.e., simultaneous coding). Cod ers did not apply any code to responses that could not be considered “feedback” (i.e., one word or off-topic responses) (n=22). Coders evaluated the remaining v iable responses that did not fit the coding scheme and developed a new code to describe these responses—community connections—and updated their codebook to include the new code and re-coded all viable responses (n=50).

## Findings and discussion

Analysis revealed the following distribution of codes: content (n=17), community connections (n=12), questions (n=9), advice (n=5), and explanation (n=3). We structure our findings according to our three research questions: The ways in which learners “take up” opportunities to give formative peer feedback. The majority of shared entries did not engender peer feedback, with  only 5% of entries receiving a response. Of these,  roughly two-thirds were “viable” feedback, available for co ding. We discovered two unanticipated behaviors: selfresponders (20.8% of total analyzed responses) and super-posters (55% of total analyzed responses) (Huang et al. 2014). Self-responders provided context for their original post, shared additional connections to course content, and requested others to review their work (i.e., community connections). The characteristics of the peer feedback provided. The majority of responses drew connections between course content and their peers’ ideas. Our analysis also revealed  instances where a peer offered specific pedagogical suggestions, indicating that the peer l ikely shared a similar instructional background and  thus felt comfortable offering advice. These types of comment s were less frequent, however, as evidenced by the lower occurrences of the explanation and advice codes. When guiding questions were posed, they aimed to direct peers to useful alternative instructional approaches or encouraged peers to refine their initial teaching plan. Responses coded as community connections included elements such as the sharing of personal experiences and instructional approaches, expressions of appreciation for peers’ efforts, and requests for feedback. Opportunities to advance collectivist approaches wi thin open, online learning environments. The responses we analyzed demonstrated that many learne rs embraced the ethos of a community-oriented learn ing environment. Learners elaborated on their peers’ ideas and critiqued peers’ applications of course themes as they pertained to their final resilient teaching plans. These behaviors embody the collectivist mindset adv ocated by Ladson-Billings (1995), necessary to achieve deeper understanding and higher levels of academic success.

## Limitations and future work

Despite a sizable pool of shared digital workbook e ntries (n=1503), only 50 of these received substant ive responses worthy of analysis. This low rate of inte raction between peers severely limited our investig ation of formative peer feedback in a MOOC. Future work will explore how providing more structure and guidance around formative feedback tasks in community-oriented MOOC s could influence the way that learners take up the practice and shape the characteristics of feedback exchanged.
