# Exploring Virtual Space, Reasoning about Place

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In this paper, we examine how people reason about space during virtual neighborhood explorations (NEs): iterative, disciplined, and virtual walking tours of local neighborhoods designed to teach people about issues of spatial justice. We video recorded STEM preservice teachers who participated in three virtual NEs-using maps, readings, and Google Street View-to learn about urban heat islands and their racist legacies. Using interaction and stance analysis, we document how ideologies about place constrained teachers’ efforts to more critically reason about space. To make virtual NEs more fruitful, we believe instructors must recognize and disrupt dominant ideologies about place that hamper critical spatial reasoning.

## Introduction

Critical pedagogy of place (Gruenewald, 2003) is an  instructional framework which blends commitments a nd practices from social justice pedagogies (e.g., Freire, 1970; Giroux, 2004; Phili p & Azevedo, 2017) and place-based pedagogies (e.g., Smith & Sobel, 2014; Tuck & McKenzie, 2014). This hybrid framework promotes critical spatial reasoning: the ability to make connections between the present-day structure of spaces/places and historic systems of power and domination  (Soja, 2009), often through collaboratively exploring (e.g., Mari n, 2020) and mapping space (e.g., Taylor & Hall, 2013).  To bring critical pedagogy of place into STEM prese rvice teacher education, we crafted virtual neighborhood explorations (NEs): iterative, disciplined, and virtual walking tours designed to foster critical reasoning about locally relevant spatial justice issues. In this st udy, we examine PSTs who virtually explored neighborhoods in Austin, Texas-using maps, readings, and Google Street View (GSV) - to learn about the history of redlining and its relationship with present-day urban heat islands (Wilson, 2020). Although there were many successes, we  witnessed how challenging it is to support PSTs in reasoning critically about space. We see two reasons for this  difficulty, and each reason led us to wonder how P STs think about space while engaging in virtual NEs: First, r easoning about space is an epis temically complex endeavor, involving 2D representations of space (e.g., maps, texts, data visualizations) and 3D embodied experie nces in space (e.g., walking, driving, bicycling) (Dixon & Jones, 1998; Taylor, 2020). How do PSTs pivot between maps and the real (or virtual) world, when attempting to understand space? Second, reasoning about space is (also) an ideologically complex endeavor. Spaces become places as they inherit social meanings  regarding their aesthetic (e.g., beautiful and ugly places) or moral value (e.g., good and bad places), as well as norms for who /what is natural or unnatural within a place (Cresswell, 1996; 2004). What do PSTs understand about various places in their community? How might these understandings show up, as they read maps, explore space on foot, or explore space virtually with GSV? Finally, how might variou s epistemologies of space and ideologies about plac e interact? These are the questions we take up in our study. Describing virtual neighborhood explorations Virtual NEs unfold in four movements: First, the te acher selects a spatial justice issue to serve as t he anchoring phenomenon. Second, teachers and learners select sp ecific neighborhoods to explore to ‘witness’ the sp atial justice issue firsthand (or in the case of virtual NEs, secondhand). Third, the learners ‘drop in’ to the neighborhood using GSV and move throughout the space by clicking arrows superimposed onto the roads. GSV allows learners to virtually see the space as if they were walking or driving through it. Fourth, while exploring the space in GSV, learners are encouraged to: (a) point out interesti ng spatial features; (b) ask questions about the co mposition of space; (c) posit potential variables of interest; and (d) hypothesize possible historic or present causes of the spatial justice issue. Virtual NEs can be conducted once, or multiple times – each with their own disciplinary focus. For example, the first virtual NE could be open-ended (i.e., no disciplinary focus), whereas the second virtual NE can be targeted to a specific STEM discipline (e.g., fo cusing only on biological elements of space). Furth ermore, virtual NEs can be conducted with or without other resources, such as maps, readings, or data visualiz ations. Taken together, virtual NEs are meant to serve as an introductory activity for a broader project designed to teach about spatial justice issues.

Theorizing critical spatial reasoning We aim to trace how people engage in collaborative and critical reasoning about space and spatial just ice issues during virtual NEs. To do this, we draw on the lite rature which describes how spatial epistemologies (Dixon & Jones, 1998; Taylor, 2020) and ideologies of place are co-constructed in interaction (Cresswell, 1996; 2004). Taylor describes two distinct epistemologies for reasoning about space: grid and mobile epistemologies. Grid epistemologies are ways of reasoning about space informed by representations of space, such as maps, texts, or data visualizations (Dixon & Jones, 1998; Taylor, 2020). During virtual NEs, we considered any statements PSTs made about space which derived from interactions wi th maps, texts, or data visualizations to be grid-epistemic stances. Mobile epistemologies are ways of reasoning about space informed by embodied exper iences moving through space, such as walking, bicycling, o r driving (Cresswell, 2008). During virtual NEs, PS Ts used GSV to collaboratively and virtually explore space from the first-person perspective. Therefore, we considered any statements made about space which derived from movements in GSV to be mobile-epistemic stances. To understand how ideologies constrain or support these twin spatial epistemologies, we draw on Cresswell’s writings about place (1996; 2004). Cresswell describes three elements that constitute an ideolog y about place: classification, differentiation, and n aturalization. Classification involves naming or ev aluating a place, differentiating involves describing how one place is different than another, and na turalizing involves naming beings, actions, or materials which belong or do not belong within a place. During a virtual NE, teachers often made remarks on the affective quality of plac es (e.g., good and bad neighborhoods), remarked on the differences between places (e.g., tree-covered and barren neighborhoods), and discussed what people/entities are natural to a place (e.g., “this is where wealthy people live”). We considered these remarks to be ideological, a nd represent classifying stances, differentiating stances, and naturalizing stances.

## Methods

This qualitative case study (Ragin & Becker, 1992) took place in Austin, Texas during a project-based instruction (PBI) course for STEM PSTs. Because of the COVID-19 pandemic, this course occurred synchronously ove r Zoom. Because of space constraints, we only focus on one group of STEM PSTs (Aaron, Drew, and Jordan; all pseudonyms), their course instructor (the second author), and a teaching assistant (the first author). The first and second authors designed a six-lesson series to engage STEM PSTs in learning about urban heat island (UHI) effect in Austin, Texas. UHI effe ct is an atmospheric phenomenon where urban areas h ave significantly warmer surface and air temperatures t han rural areas (Oke, 1973). However, recent research has revealed that the temperatures within urban areas can vary up to than 10 degrees Fahrenheit (Wilson, 2020). These present-day differences can be traced, in part, to a banking practice called redlining. Between 1930 and 1968, the cities (in collaboration with the federal government) ranked neighborhoods according to risk factors, to determine whether residents could receive loans for home mort gages. Predominantly white neighborhoods were ranked “Best” and marked on a map with a green line, whereas predominantly Black and Hispanic neighborhoods were ranked “Hazardous” and marked with a red line. Neighborhoods in between were rank ed ‘Still Desirable’ (bluelined) or ‘Definitely Declining” (yellow-lined). The goal of the UHI lesson series was for PSTs to l earn about connection between historic redlining practices and present day UHIs in Austin, Texas. To do this, the PSTs participated in three virtual NEs. The first NE was open-ended: PSTs selected a neighborhood and used Google Maps and GSV to explore the neighborhood virtually and discuss aspects they believed might c ontribute to UHI. The second NE focused on the geog raphic causes of UHI effect: PSTs read an article about physical causes of UHI (e.g., surface coverage, tree canopy) and revisited their neighborhood to refine their unders tanding of how an UHIs might form. The final NE focused on the historic causes of UHI effect. PSTs read an article about the influence of redlini ng on present-day UHIs and used a historic redlining map to determine if their chosen neighborhood was redlined and how redlining may have shaped the present-day composition of the neighborhood. We used Zoom’s recording feature to capture Aaron, Drew, and Jordan’s NEs. The open-ended NE lasted 32 minutes, the geographic NE lasted 24 minutes, and the historic NE lasted 36 minutes. We drew on techniques from interaction analysis (Jordan & Henderson, 1995) and stance analysis (Goodwin, 2007) to analyze th e data. First, we created one content log per NE. Second, we reviewed the content logs and flagged any discussions where PSTs were reasoning about UHI using maps (i.e., gri d epistemologies), GSV (i.e., mobile epistemologies), or based on personal knowledge about the city (i.e., i deologies of place). Third, we transcribed each fla gged discussion and engaged in turn-by-turn analysis to describe when and how grid/mobile epistemologies and/or ideologies of place related to one another, during sensemaking.

## Example findings

To illustrate the how ideologies of place constrain ed reasoning about space, we deconstruct two episod es from Aaron, Drew, and Jordan’s virtual NEs. Although the re were many more such episodes, we focus on these two because (1) the PSTs are focused on the same area of the city (neighborhoods to the west of  downtown Austin); and (2) the PSTs beliefs about the historic and pre sent racial/class make-up of different neighborhoods (i.e., ideologies of place) caused them to miss an opportunity to reason critically about space. Episode 1: Demographic data and race-related ideologies of place During the first virtual NE (open-ended NE), Aaron, Drew, and Jordandecided to explore Lake View Gardens, a neighborhood just west of downtown. Other groups in the class chose neighborhoods which, they believed, would be extremely hot. In contrast, Aaron, Drew, and Jordan chose Lake View Gardens because they believed it would have cooler temperatures than the rest of the city.  Therefore, the purpose of their NEs was to identif y aspects of Lake View Gardens which prevented this neighborhood from experiencing urban heat island effect. The first episode took place during the end of the first NE (the open-ended NE). Prior to this episode, the group had explored Lake View Gardens virtually using GSV, pointed out a variety of spatial feature s that might make it cooler (e.g., ample tree cano py, larger lot sizes, and fewer roads) and construc ted an ideology that framed Lake View Gardens as “upscale” or wealthy. T o better understand the neighborhood, Drew opened a Wikipedia page (i.e., a grid representation) and the following conversation unfolded: Line Speaker Talk ((notes, gestures, vocalizations)) 1.01 Instructor Population 3063 in 2010.

I’m shocked] I’m shocked. Shocked. Who let the other 3.8% in? ((stated in a sarcastic tone)) ((laughs)) 1.07 Group ((3.0 seconds of silence after Aaron’s remark)) This conversation demonstrates the entanglement of grid epistemologies of space and ideologies about place. The course instructor explained that the population of West Lake Hills was just over 3,000 (1.01) and Drew explained that this population was 95.6% white (1.02). We interpreted these remarks as grid-epistemic stances, because Drew and the course instructor were reasoning about Lake View Gardens using data rather than (virtually) embodied experiences. Following these grid-epistemic stances, the group made a variety of ideo logical stances about place. Jordan laughed at the demographic stat istic (1.03), Drew explained that it was “100% expe cted” in an obvious tone (1.04), and Aaron repeated that he was “shocked” in a sarcastic tone (1.06). We interpreted these remarks as ideological, because they revealed that the group expected this neighborhood to be white. This expectation works to naturalize white people as belonging to Lake View Gardens, while Aaron’s final remark, “who let the other 3.8% in?” highlig hted that other racial or ethnic groups do not belong  or are out-of-place in this part of the city. Aaron laughed about his apparent joke while the group remained silent for three seconds. We interpreted this lack of response from the group to  indicate that Drew, Jordan, and the course instruc tor found Aaron’s remark uncouth. In summary, the group used a grid representation of space (a Wikipedia page) to reason about the racial/ethnic makeup Lake View Gardens, which bolstered their ideology that white residents are natural within this neighborhood, and other racial/ethnic groups are unnatural. Episode 2: Redlining maps and race-related ideologies of place The second episode took place during the third virt ual NE (historic NE). Before the virtual NE, PSTs read an article which explained the connection between hist oric redlining practices and present-day urban heat islands. During the virtual NE, the preservice teachers were provided a copy of the redlining map of the city and asked to read the map, explore the city in GSV. Aaron was quick to identify that Lake View Gardens wasn’t on the redlining map because “[Lake View Gardens] in the [19]30s was wilderness”. Instead, the group chose to explore a nearby neighborhood named Tarrytown, because it was similar in racial/ethnic and class composition (2.01): Line Speaker Talk ((notes, gestures, vocalizations)) 2.01 Aaron So [Tarrytown] looks similar to [Lake View Gardens] in a lot of ways. 2.02 Drew Mm hmm. Um. See like. What is this random area out here [Tarrytown] that has a ‘D’, but then there are a few areas that aren’t graded?

2.03 Aaron Um yeah, I’m curious as to what is there. I wonder if that might not have been a lumber yard or some sort of heavy industry of some sort. It is close to the Colorado river, so that is why I’m speculating.

This conversation further demonstrates the entanglement of grid epistemologies of space and ideologies about place. The group’s decision to compare Lake View Gardens and Tarrytown was inherently ideological: The group quickly agreed – without consulting any maps, data, or images from GSV – that Tarrytown was also white and wealthy, and therefore, must have always been white and wealthy (2.01). Much to their surprise, upon reading the redlining map (a grid-epistemic stance), the group realized that Tarrytow n had been ranked as a “hazardous” neighborhood in the 1930s (2.02). These two understandings, one ideological and one epistemological, conflicted with one another, and needed to be resolved. To res olve the tension, Aaron claimed that Tarrytown may have been “a lumber yard or some sort of heavy industry” be cause it is nearby a river (2.03). We interpret Aaron’s remark to be both epistemological and ideological. He read the map correctly by identifying a river cl ose to Tarrytown and using the river to hypothesize that Tarrytown may have had a lumberyard. However, he also read the map incorrectly. In class, the course instructor explained that redlining only happened to residential areas and were ranked according to the racial makeup of a nei ghborhood (i.e., not based on industry presence). A lthough Aaron may have forgotten these details, we see his efforts to explain away the tension – Tarrytown couldn’t have possibly had Black residents – to be related to his (and the group’s) ideological understanding that Tarrytown was presently and historically a white neighborhood.

In actuality, prior to the 1940s, Tarrytown was a pred ominantly African American community. In the late 1940s, African American residents in Tarrytown were forcibly removed, to make way for a growing wealthy white population in the state capital.  Because this tension went unnoticed by the course i nstructor and teaching assistant, the PSTs misreading of the maps in concert with their emerging ideologies of place caused the group to miss an opportunity to reason more critically about space.

## Discussion

This research narrates just one of many instances w here ideologies about place, when left unquestioned  by teachers or learners, limited opportunities for PST s to reason critically about space. We see pedagogi cal and theoretical lessons within this illustrative case: Pedagogically speaking, critical pedagogy of place aims to engage learners in seeing space in more critical and nuanced ways. Before people can reason  critically about space, they must identify and de-settle dominant ideologies about place. In this case, Aaro n, Drew, and Jordan constructed an ideology of plac e that framed Lake View Gardens and Tarrytown as historically and presently wealthy and white. This ideology of place could have been de-settled through questioning to allow for more critical reasoning about space. For example, we could have asked participants when they learned that a particular neighborhood was wea lthy and homogenously white and whether they see this as just in democratic society. Furthe rmore, we could have more directly asked learners how racial and class segregation relate to  perceived spatial differences. These questions cou ld work to de-settle spatial differences that have become naturalized. Theoretically speaking, a growing body of literatur e in the learning sciences investigates the entanglements between ideologies and reasoning (Phi lip & Gupta, 2020). Some scholarship examines how teachers’ ideologies about social differences (i.e., racial, linguistic, or cultural differences) relate to apti tude in school (Azevedo, et al., 2024; Daniel, Pierson, & Keifert, 2023; Philip, 2011). Other works examine the ideologies that emerge during instruction in data science (Phi lip et al., 2016) or engineering education (Gupta, et al., 2019). Our analysis adds to this literature by illustratin g new mediums through which ideologies are co-constructed: space and place.
