# Concepts, Approaches, and Outcomes in Computational Thinking and Mathematics Integration in K -12: An Umbrella Review

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Despite growing interest in integrating Computational Thinking (CT) into K-12 mathematics, the research remains fragmented. To create a cohesive overview, this umbrella review synthesizes 18 literature reviews, meta-analyses, and scoping reviews published between 2006 and 2025. Our analysis focuses on the what (concepts), how (approaches), and why (outcomes) of CT-Mathematics integration. Our findings reveal a consensus on the benefits of CT-Mathematics integration but a lack of specificity. While common concepts (e.g., abstraction, geometry) and tools (e.g., Scratch) are identified, the synergistic links between them and the pedagogical strategies for implementation are underexplored. Furthermore, learning outcomes are often studied in broader STEM contexts, obscuring the CT-Mathematics link. We conclude that future research must move beyond demonstrating if integration is effective to explaining how, when, and under what conditions CT-Mathematics integration fosters deep, synergistic learning.

In the landscape of 21st-century education, few initiatives have gained as much global traction as Computational Thinking (CT; Kafai & Proctor, 2021). Originally conceptualized as a way to help students learn mathematics (Papert, 1980) and later defined as a set of problem-solving skills derived from computer science (Wing, 2006), research on CT has grown exponentially in the past decade. A common area of growth is the integration of CT into mathematics instruction, particularly in elementary settings where such integration can lower the barrier to computer science (CS) adoption and improve mathematical understanding (Rich et al., 2020; Beck et al., 2025; Shumway et al., 2023). This integration is not arbitrary; since Papert, research has documented the alignment between CS and mathematics, including problemsolving processes such as modeling, pattern generalization, data analysis, and abstr action (Clarke-Midura et al., 2024; Rich et al., 2020). Many of these shared practices are already present in existing mathematics curricula (Rich et al., 2020). When implemented with attention to the specific synergies and differences between the fields, the domains become mutually reinforcing, offering conceptual tools that sharpen understanding of the other (Rich et al., 2020). Despite this promise, the  rapid growth of CT-Mathematics research has produced a vast yet often fragmented body of research. While numerous systematic reviews and meta-analyses have sought to consolidate this knowledge, the field still lacks a clear and consistent consensus. Key questions remain regarding what specific concepts are being integrated, how this integration is being implemented (e.g., contexts, instructional approaches), and why it is effective, including the specific learning outcomes and the synergistic potential where CT and mathematics each deepen the understanding of the other.

To address this need for a more holistic synthesis, we conducted an umbrella review, a “review of reviews.” We systematically analyze and integrate the findings from eighteen literature reviews, meta-analyses, and scoping reviews published between 2006  and 2025. The purpose of the present study is to synthesize these reviews at a deeper level and identify convergent findings, critical gaps, and provide an overview of the state of CT-Mathematics integration. Specifically, we seek to answer the following questions: (a) What is the overall focus of the current reviews on CT and mathematics integration? (b) Which specific CT and mathematics concepts are most linked in the literature? (c) What are the dominant instructional approaches used to integrate CT and mathematics? (d) What are the reported outcomes of this integration for learners?  By answering these questions, we contribute to a synthesis of the evidence, clarifying the what (concepts), how (approaches), and why (outcomes) of CT-Mathematics to inform future research, policy, and practice. The present study employed an umbrella review, a methodology designed to synthesize evidence from multiple existing literature reviews (e.g., systematic reviews, scoping reviews, or meta-analyses) into a single, comprehensive document (Grant & Booth, 2009). In contrast to reviews that synthesize findings from primary studies, an umbrella review operates at a higher level to provide a broad overview of a research field. This "review 1937 of reviews" approach is particularly effective for mapping the key concepts, instructional approaches, and outcomes across a topic, especially when a considerable number of reviews are available (Grant & Booth, 2009). This aligns with the goal of our study  to leverage this review type to examine the landscape of research on the integration of CT and mathematics in K-12 education.

This review follows the reporting standards from the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement (Page et al., 2021). The search was conducted in September 2025 across multiple databases, including the ACM Digital Library; APA PsycINFO, Education Full Text, Education Source, and ERIC (via EBSCOhost); and Education Database and ProQuest Dissertations & Theses Global (via ProQuest). We used the searching query: ("computational thinking" OR "CT") AND (STEM OR mathematics OR math OR "math class") AND (“review of literature” OR “literature review” OR “meta-analysis” OR “systematic review” OR “scoping review”). Using this query, we yielded 116 results. After removi ng 64 duplicates, two researchers independently screened 52 articles based on predefined inclusion criteria: addressing both CT and math, and their integration, focusing on K-12 settings, published in English, published from 2006 to 2025, peer-reviewed, and review papers. Reviews that targeted teachers as the sample were excluded. The database screening resulted in 14 papers with an agreement level of 96.2%. An additional 4 papers were identified through hand-searching, resulting in a final list of 18 review articles. Despite searching for studies from 2006, the earliest published review was from 2018.

After identifying articles, we first created a preliminary coding scheme based on research questions. We then read through a few articles and coded them. During this process, we revised the coding scheme to: CT definitions used, specific CT aspects linked to mathematics, specific mathematics concepts linked to CT, and the reported instructional approaches and learning outcomes. After the discussion, two researchers read and coded all the papers. Then the three authors discussed the codes and identified the themes, synthesizing across the 18 reviews. From this information, we identified three common themes: concepts, instructional approaches, and learning outcomes. The  concepts refer to the definitions and components of CT and mathematics, and their integrations. Instructional approaches cover the pedagogical methods and learning tools used to implement CT and mathematics integration. Lastly, the learning outcomes refer to the effectiveness and benefits of integration. RQ1: The overall focus of the current reviews In the included reviews (n = 18), the overall focus on research on CT-Mathematics integration is in K-12 education, with a majority focused on elementary students. While many of the reviews have a wide K-16 scope, they report a concentration of studies at the primary level. The reviews present a methodological evolution in how the integration of CT and Mathematics was examined. The earliest reviews were either scoping or systematic (e.g., Barcelos et al. 2018; Hickmott et al. 2018). Their purpose was to map the emerging field, identify trends, and to identify limitations of existing studies. They identified reliance on small studies, self-report, and a lack of assessments of learning. Recently, there has been a shift towards meta-analyses that provide quantitative evidence of impact (e.g., Yang at al. 2025; Suparman et al. 2025). These studies focused on established overall effect sizes, identifying statistically significant moderators, and making generalizable claims about the effectiveness of CT-Mathematics integration. The majority of the reviews claimed they would focus on the integration between CT and mathematics or STEM, but did not provide enough information on the actual integration, which we detail below in RQ2 and RQ3. The few reviews that did focus on integration reported that the majority of individual studies did not integrate CT and mathematics or STEM (Wu & Yang, 2022; Chan et al., 2023). For example, Yeni et al (2024) found that 40% of the studies substituted CT for the other subject. In other words, despite the plethora of individual studies and 18 reviews, the field still lacks the necessary details to understand how to integrate CT and mathematics and which topics are synergistic.

RQ2: Which specific CT and mathematics concepts are most linked? To answer how CT-Mathematics integration is operationalized, we examined (a) the definitions of CT and mathematics, respectively, and (b) how these concepts are integrated. Across the 18 reviews, the most frequently cited CT concepts were abstraction  (n = 13), algorithmic thinking (n  = 12), and decomposition (n  = 10). Two frameworks were the most widely used to conceptualize CT.  Brennan and Resnick’s  (2012) framework distinguishes between computational concepts (e.g., loops, variables), practices (e.g., debugging, abstracting), and perspectives. While Weintrop et al. (2016)’s framework, which situates CT in disciplinary STEM practices, was more prevalent in integrated  STEM contexts. On the mathematics side, a majority of the reviews focused on 1938 mathematics as a general topic (n = 7). Among those that did report on specific mathematical domains, geometry (n = 7) and numbers & operations (n = 7) were the most frequently identified. As mentioned previously, existing reviews explore CT-Mathematics integration at a surface level. Only two of the eighteen reviews provided a deeper analysis of the interaction between specific CT-Mathematics concept pairs and how the concept's interaction fits into the participants' characteristics and instructional approaches (Nordby et al., 2022; Zhang & Wong, 2024). A third of the reviews (n = 6, e.g., Yeni et al. 2024) showed the frequency of individual CT and Mathematics subdomains without detailing their overlap or pedagogical connection. This represents a missed opportunity for the field. Without mapping how CT and mathematics concepts interact, researchers cannot explain how this integration affects learning. Equally important, educators cannot apply these ideas without knowing which concept pairings are relevant to the curriculum they teach. To set clear learning goals, educators need to understand exactly how the two subjects are synergistic rather than just knowing they are. Specifically, they need to know how integration aligns with the curriculum content standards they use to guide their instruction. Reviews that ignore these details offer educators no actionable guidance. As a result, the theoretical rationale for CT-Mathematics integration remains underdeveloped, limiting its translation into classroom practice.

RQ3: What are the dominant instructional approaches?

To understand how CT-Mathematics integration is implemented in teaching and learning contexts, we investigated pedagogical approaches and learning tools. Of the reviews that mentioned pedagogical approaches, Problem-based learning and Project-based learning were the two most frequently mentioned. Based on the reviews, we classified learning tools for CT-Mathematics integration into three categories: programming languages/environments, digital and tangible robots, and unplugged activities. Scratch is cited as the most popular or frequently used tool in numerous reviews. Some studies mention Python, Java, Geometer's Sketchpad, and Lattice Land. Digital and tangible robots are the second most common category. These tools are widely used in early childhood and primary education. Bee-Bot and Cubetto are the most often cited toys for younger children. LEGO Mindstorms/EV3, Sphero, and Arduino are most commonly cited for older students. Unplugged activities, for which no digital equipment is needed (Brackmann et al., 2017), are used across all age levels, often as a precursor to plugged activities.

While most reviews (n = 14) identified a wide range of instructional approaches, they often provided these descriptions without sufficient details about how to implement them in the instructional activities. The current literature typically presents extensive lists of tools (e.g., software, robotics) and activities (e.g., programming). However, the reviews rarely move beyond simply listing these items to explain the underlying pedagogical strategies that guide their use in a classroom. Furthermore, many reviews tend to confound the pedagogical approaches with learning tools and assessment. For example, some reviews group high-level teaching under the single, broad category of "instructional approaches" (Subramaniam et al., 2022). Similarly, other reviews identified digital tangibles, apps and games, programming languages, formative or summative assessments, and other technological tools as CT tools (Chan et al., 2023). Such inconsistent and confounding categorizations of instructional approaches can mislead interpretations and distort our understanding of the conditions under which instructional approaches foster or hinder CT and mathematics integration in classroom contexts. In other words, the existing reviews provide information on what tools are used but do not provide detailed information on which CT tools work best with specific mathematical subjects, and for which grade levels. As a result, the existing reviews present a fragmented view of CT-Mathematics instruction that is disconnected from the classroom context, making it difficult for educators to implement integrated CT-Mathematics lessons in their classroom practice.

RQ4: What are the reported outcomes of CT-Mathematics integration? The existing meta-analyses (n = 6) focused on (a) the impact of the integration of CT into STEM, not Mathematics, on students’ learning, (b) the impact of CT on math learning, and (c) the relations between CT performance and overall academic achievement. Despite the 18 reviews, includ ing two for qualitative syntheses and five for quantitative syntheses of learning outcomes, the learning outcomes of the CT-Mathematics integration remain unclear because most reviews primarily investigated the effectiveness of integrating  CT into the STEM context, rather than focusing specifically on mathematics. Specifically, in research syntheses of experimental studies, Suparman et al. (2025) reported that STEM-based mathematics instruction fosters CT acquisition (g = 0.55). Similarly, Ye et al. (2022) found that CT interventions enhanced mathematics competence (g = 0.52). Lastly, Li and Oon (2024) and Yang (2025) reported the impact of CT-STEM integration on mathematics performance. Compared to control groups, the CT-STEM integration showed effect sizes of g = 0.64 and g = 0.43, respectively. 1939 These meta-analyses have supported the role of CT and its synergetic effects in STEM education contexts, but they do not offer evidence on when and how mathematics and CT should be integrated. Further, as mentioned in the instructional approaches, inconsistent subgroups (i.e., coding schemes) in the moderator analyses make it difficult to understand how and under what conditions such integration can benefit students’ learning.

## Implications and future directions

The field of CT-Mathematics integration has made meaningful progress. Existing reviews have identified the CT concepts and mathematical domains most commonly featured in integration efforts, cataloged the tools and pedagogical approaches most frequently used in K-12 classrooms, and provided initial quantitative evidence that CT integration produces positive effects on student learning (e.g., Suparman et al., 2025; Ye et al., 2022; Yang, 2025). These reviews confirm that CT-Mathematics integration is both fe asible and broadly beneficial, and provides a foundation on which more actionable research can build. However, three gaps remain. First, the reviews established which CT and mathematics concepts appear most frequently, but rarely examined how those concepts interact. A small number of reviews started this work, demonstrating that spatial and geometric reas oning are synergistic with representational CT practices, and that number and operations align with algorithmic thinking (Nordby et al., 2022; Zhang & Wong, 2024), but these analyses remain exceptions. Future research should systematically map concept-level connections across grade levels and content domains, and specify the conditions under which particular pairings are most effective. A practical priority is the development of resources that connect CT concepts to existing mathematics curriculum standards, giving educators the tools to implement integrated lessons in their classrooms. Second, the reviews documented a range of tools and identified problem-based and project-based learning as the most common pedagogical approaches, but have not explained how these elements work together in practice. Future research should move from listing resources to articulating design principles: specifying which tools support which concept pairings, for which learners, and within which pedagogical frameworks. Third, while meta-analyses have found positive effect sizes for the integration, these findings are difficult to interpret because multifaceted concepts and skills tend to be collapsed into overly simplistic categories in the moderator coding process. Future syntheses should adopt more precise coding schemes that distinguish among mathematical domains, CT components, grade levels, and instructional approaches. More broadly, future research should treat CT-Mathematics learning as a bidirectional process rather than an add-on. Taken together, these directions reflect a necessary shift in the field: from demonstrating that CT-Mathematics integration is beneficial to explaining how, when, and for whom it works.
