# Collaborative Learning and Student Outcomes: Evaluating a University Chemistry Course Design with the ICAP Framework

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
The Interactive-Constructive-Active-Passive (ICAP) framework has gained traction in the learning sciences community as a tool for understanding how students learn. It suggests that deeper learning occurs when students are engaged in generative and collaborative activities. This study evaluated the design of a university-level chemistry course and examined the relationship between ICAP engagement modes and student performance. Regression analysis revealed that collaboration had the strongest positive impact on academic outcomes.

## Introduction

Chi and Wylie’s (2014) ICAP framework posits that l earning outcomes are linked to modes of cognitive engagement: interactive > constructive > active > p assive. The framework emphasizes that generative and collaborative learning leads to deeper understandin g than passive or purely active learning. This stud y used the ICAP instructional rubric (Ha, 2022)  to assess how design features in introductory chemi stry courses predict student outcomes, including course grades and final exam scores.

## Theoretical framework

Chi and Wylie (2014) framed how students cognitivel y engage in learning via the ICAP framework. The IC AP framework hierarchy predicts that learning is commensurate with engagement mode: interactive > constructive > active > passive (Chi, 2009; Chi & Wylie,  2014). Passive engagement involves receiving inform ation without integrating or applying it (e.g., listening to lect ures). Active engagement includes manipulating mate rial (e.g., flashcards), but without generating new ideas. The constructive mode goes further, requiring learners to generate and externalize new knowledge by integrating it wit h what they already know. This aligns with Piaget’s  (1972) constructivist theory, where learning occurs throug h reflection and reorganization of cognitive struct ures. The interactive mode involves collaborative learning, w here students co-construct understanding through shared dialogue and problem-solving. Rooted in Vygotsky’s (1978) social constru ctivism, this mode emphasizes equitable participation and mutual engagement, fostering deeper learning through social interaction. ## Results

The regression analysis revealed meaningful relatio nships between ICAP engagement modes and student performance. For final exam scores, the model expla ined 34.7% of the variance. Passive mode showed the strongest positive association (β =.445), followed by constructive (β =.164) and interactive modes (β =.117). Active mode, in contrast, was negatively associated with exam performance (β = –.236). For course grades, the model accounted for 16.9% of the variance. Constructive (β =.086) and interactive modes (β =.223) were positively associated with higher grades, while pas sive mode showed a negative relationship (β = –.109). Active mode had no significant effect. To contextualize the impact of each engagement mode, the coefficients were converted to hourly effects. Each hour of interactive mode correlated with a 2.64-point increase on the final exam and a 3.30-point boost in course grades. Constructive mode added 1.56 points to exam scores and 0.54 points to  grades per hour. Passive mode raised exam performance by 1.56 points but lowered course grades by 0.24 points  per hour. Active mode was associated with a 1.20-point drop in final exam scores per hour and showed no effect on course grades.

## Discussion

Findings generally align with the ICAP hierarchy but suggest nuances in how engagement types relate to different outcome measures. Passive learning had a positive a ssociation with final exam scores, likely due to al ignment between lectures and exam questio ns. However, passive dose negatively affected overa ll course grades, which may reflect its limited contribution to deeper understanding. Active mode was negatively or neutrally associated with outcome s. This likely reflects the non-generative nature of active tasks (e.g., recall exercises). Constructive mode, involving generative homework tasks, was positively associated with both measures, consi stent with ICAP predictions. The strongest effect, however, came from interactive mode. Collaborative problem-solving in recitation sessions had the greatest positive effect on both final exams and course grades, reinforcing the value of peer-to-peer, co-generative engagement.

## Limitations

This study included a limited number of instructors  and course variations. Final exam content could no t be analyzed for generative quality, which may have influenced the predictive validity of ICAP modes.

## Scholarly significance

The study strengthens the argument that collaborative learning, particularly when aligned w ith the interactive mode of the ICAP framework, supports improved stude nt outcomes. While not causal, the associations sug gest that relatively small adjustments in instructional design—favoring constructiv e and interactive tasks —can significantly impact academic performance. Future w ork should explore how to better define and support equitable, co-generative collaboration in classrooms to maximize the benefits of interactive engagement.
