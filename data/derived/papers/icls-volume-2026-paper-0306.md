# When Understanding Breaks: Interaction Analysis of Knowledge Construction in Multilingual Science

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Across India's multilingual classrooms, science is frequently taught in a language that differs from learners' home languages, producing friction at the concept-language interface that depresses participation and understanding. Studying how breakdowns are detected and repaired in real-time is essential for equitable access to disciplinary explanations. This paper argues that microgenetic "repair" sequences constitute the observable unit of progress in multilingual science discourse. Anchored in a Grade 9 se mi-English biology lesson and complemented by student focus group discussions, the analysis identifies triggers for breakdown and traces time-stamped episodes in which understanding is restored through one-word student anchoring, peer-mediated refinement, and teacher-led reframing, alongside partial and no-repair cases. The resulting five-part typology specifies how repair unfolds in whole-class interaction and where it fails from learners' perspectives, yielding designable principles for lesson pacing, visual scaffolding, and question reframing that are feasible under time pressure and redistribute participation authority.

India is among the most linguistically diverse countries in the world, with over 1,600 spoken languages. Although states were reorganised along linguistic lines following independence, linguistic diversity persists within every state, creating classrooms w here students' mother tongues often differ from the language of instruction and textbooks (Mohanty, 2019). Multilingual classrooms are a global reality, where mismatches between home languages and instructional languages shape learning opportunities and outcomes. In science classrooms specifically, dense disciplinary terminology and abstract conceptual relationships compound these linguistic challenges, making the negotiation of meaning between languages a high-stakes pedagogical task. Semi-English instruction remains the norm in many Indian urban schools, with textbooks in English but classroom talk in students' home languages. Students encounter biological terms like xylem, phloem, photosynthesis, and transpiration in English while thinking and talking in Marathi or Hindi, causing misunderstandings that suppress participation and reinforce inequitable access to disciplinary explanation. Understanding when conceptual breaks occur at this language-content interface and what resources or strategies enable repair is critical for equitable science learning. This paper argues that microgenetic "repair" sequences (the moment-by-moment moves through which conceptual "breaks" are detected and understanding is restored) constitute the observable unit of progress in multilingual science discourse. Drawing on a 50-minute Grade 9 biology lesson in a Mumbai municipal school and triangulating classroom observation with student focus group discussions, we trace how repair emerged spontaneously through student lexical anchoring, peer-mediated refinement, and teacher-led reframing, then derive design principles to make such repair-oriented partnering intentional and equitable. This study addresses the following research questions:

1. What types of repair sequences emerge when students encounter conceptual breaks at the language-content interface, and what triggers and interactional moves characterise successful versus unsuccessful repair?

2. How do students' own accounts of these moments illuminate the conditions that enable or obstruct re-entry into disciplinary explanation?

Related literature Research on language and science learning has established that when instruction occurs in a language different from students' home language, both participation and conceptual understanding are constrained by the dual cognitive load of processing disciplinary content and navigating linguistic demands (Cummins, 2000; Lee et al., 2013). The translanguaging literature advances beyond deficit framing by showing how students' full linguistic repertoires serve as resources for meaning-making; García and Wei (2014)  argue that flexible language use supports conceptual development across content areas. Recent work has begun examining how translanguaging creates spaces for scientific meaning-making (Priyadarshini et al., 2025), yet much of this research examines designed interventions rather than naturally occurring breakdown-repair cycles in semi-English contexts (Mohanty, 2019). In parallel, interaction analysis has provided fine-grained accounts of classroom discourse, 2021 including repair sequences in language learning (Seedhouse, 2004) and scientific argumentation (Warren et al., 2001), yet these microgenetic analyses rarely address multilingual science classrooms where repair must navigate both conceptual and linguistic dimensions simultaneously. This study addresses these gaps by tracing time-stamped breakdown-repair episodes and triangulating interaction analysis with student focus group discussions. We draw on translanguaging as a theoretical framework (García & Wei, 20 14; Li Wei, 2018), which views multilingual speakers' linguistic repertoires not as separate language systems but as integrated resources for meaning-making, to understand how students use their full communicative repertoire during repair sequences in science discourse.

## Method

The study took place in a Grade 9 classroom at a Mumbai municipal school serving linguistically diverse students following semi-English instruction (Science and Math in English, other subjects in Marathi). Twenty-two students (10 girls, 12 boys) participated in a 50-minute biology lesson on photosynthesis and nutrient uptake taught by an external facilitator. Informed consent was obtained from the school and students in accordance with the institute's IRB protocol. Multi-angle video (using three cameras) and audio recording (utilising two recorders) captured classroom interactions (using three cameras) and audio recording (utilising two recorders). 13 students were invited for focus group discussions (FGD) (6-7 students each FGD, 30 minutes, conducted in Mar athi/Hindi) elicited student perspectives on language challenges and repair. We used interaction analysis (Jordan & Henderson, 1995) to identify time-stamped "break" moments where discourse stalled. Guided by translanguaging theory, we analysed how students drew upon their full linguistic repertoires (Hindi, Marathi, English) as integrated resources during repair attempts (García & Wei, 2014). We traced repair moves after breaks, coding for triggers, repair types, and outcomes. FGD transcripts were thematic ally analysed to triangulate observed patterns with student accounts. All recordings were transcribed verbatim, translated into English, and anonymised by using pseudonyms. Observations in the classroom video were triangulated with responses in FGD and field notes of the teacher and researcher. occurred frequently and were addressed through five distinct repair pathways that emerged spontaneously: one-word student anchoring, peer-mediated refinement, teacher-led reframing, partial repair, and no repair. Types of repair sequences Conceptual "breaks" in students' understanding occurred when classroom discourse stalled due to something the teacher or peers said that students did not comprehend, whether because of unfamiliar scientific jargon (xylem, phloem, photosynthesis), ideas disconnected from their everyday experiences, or other linguistic or conceptual barriers. These breaks manifested as prolonged silences, hesitant responses, or conceptual conflations (such as mixing transport structures with substances), signalling that shared understanding had been disrupted at the language-content interface. Five distinct repair pathways emerged when students and teachers attempted to address these breaks during the lesson on xylem and phloem transport. Each type differed in who initiated repair, how understanding was reconstructed, and whether students regained entry into disciplinary explanation. Peer-mediated repair When initial student responses were partial or tentative, peers sometimes built collaboratively toward coherent understanding through gesture, translation, and shared vocabulary. During a discussion about plant waste products described as "something like white, white, white" and "like gum," one student, Rashid, remained confused and whispered to a peer who had answered earlier. His peer rubbed two fingers together to indicate stickiness and provided the Marathi term Dink (plant resin) (see Fig. 2). Rashid confirmed understanding, then translated Dink (Marathi) to Gond  (Hindi) before sharing with the class, recognising classroom discourse was in Hindi. Another student added a familiar example: "It is a yellow coloured flower called Frangipani chafa, it also has this." However, students in focus groups revealed barrier s: "When we speak in Marathi, the teacher asks us to speak in English. But how can we? We don't know the English words," showing that peer-mediated repair depends on classroom norms that legitimise multilingual exchanges. After showing a video about xylem and phloem transport, she asked, "Will someone tell me what will happen in the xylem and phloem?" Silence followed (see Fig. 1-3). Recognising the breakdown, she asked, "Didn't understand?" and immediately reframed: "Now tell me, what will happen if the xylem and phloem of the plant are damaged?" A student responded, "The plant will get damaged," and when probed, students articulated: "Because they won't get 2023 any food or water" (Kyunki unko khana nahi milega paani nahi). Students in focus groups emphasised the critical role of visuals: "When the teacher said 'xylem and phloem,' I just memorised it. I didn't know what it meant until it was shown in a diagram," showing that teacher-led reframing was most powerful when combining consequenceoriented questioning with multimodal representations. Partial repair In several episodes, the class adopted a helpful term, but residual confusion persisted, and talk moved forward without fully stabilising the target concept. After showing a video, students correctly identified "sugar" and described movement as "up and down," but when probed deeper about "What is up and down?" they responded "sugar and xylem," conflating substance with transport structure. When asked, "What is sugar?" students responded Meetha (sweet), interpreting sugar through taste rather than its biochemical role. The teacher acknowledged, "Yes, because they produce sugar," but moved immediately to the next question without clarifying the scientific meaning of glucose or distinguishing everyday "sugar" from glucose produced in leaves (see Fig. 4). Students revealed ongoing confusion: "I like to learn in Marathi," noting that teachers sometimes explain new terms but other times do not, creating inconsistent support. Partial repairs clustered when facilitators accepted plausible keywords without spiral-back checks to verify relational understanding. No repair When introducing transpiration, the teacher layered multiple abstract concepts rapidly without pausing. Students responded with hesitant "Hmmmmm," signalling confusion, but the teacher pressed forward, and the discourse stalled into silence (see Fig. 5). Focus groups attributed this to overload: "When teachers speak fast in English, I don't understand." This study advances three interconnected contributions. Conceptually, positioning repair sequences as the observable unit of progress reframes learning at the language-content interface. The five-part typology shows how students, peers, and teachers co-construct disciplinary explanations through lexical anchoring, peer refinement, and teacher reframing (García & Wei, 2014; Warren et al., 2001). Successful repair demanded relational elaboration linking terms to mechanisms, often mediated through everyday experience and multimodal representation. This extends translanguaging theory (García & Wei, 2014; Li Wei, 2018) by showing that multilingual resources function not merely for code-switching but as epistemological tools for co-constructing disciplinary explan ations at moments of conceptual breakdown, creating what Li Wei (2011) calls "translanguaging spaces" where students bring together linguistic, experiential, and conceptual dimensions. Methodologically, the episode-based protocol integrating interaction analysis with student focus groups reveals not only what repair moves occurred but why they succeeded or failed. Time-stamped video identified triggers and outcomes (silence, hesitation, conflation), while FGD data exposed invisible barriers such as fear of correction and delegitimisation of home language contributions (Seedhouse, 2004). This triangulation showed that partial and no-repair episodes stemmed from pedagogical choices such as accepting keywords without spiralback checks and misinterpreting silence as comprehension. Practically, the typology yields designable principles: consequence-oriented reframing, visual anchors, peer-mediated micro-repairs, and spiral-back checks verifying relational understanding. The multimodal dimension is particularly critical; Tang et al. (2025) show how technologies afford multisensory meaning-making in translanguaging contexts, and our work demonstrates that even minimal visual scaffolds (diagrams, gestures) ground abstract scientific terminology in shared representations accessible across languages. This study reveals that five distinct repair pathways emerged when students encountered conceptual breaks at the language-content interface during semi-English science instruction: one-word student repair, peer-mediated repair, teacher-led repair, partial repair, and no repair. Analysis of breakdown-repair episodes yields designable principles that teachers candeploy under time pressure: encourage lexical anchoring from students' home languages, followed by relational elaboration linking terms to mechanism s; reframe abstract questions into consequence-oriented prompts that reduce cognitive-linguistic load; deploy visual scaffolds grounding talk in shared representations; and institute spiral-back checks verifying relational understanding rather than keyword recall alone. These findings show that naturally occurring student-peer-teacher collaboration during breakdowns can be transformed into intentional pedagogical moves. Making emergent repair deliberate redistributes epistemic authority, legitimises translanguaging as a sensemaking resource, and creates equitable pathways into disciplinary explanation for multilingual learners who might otherwise remain silent. 2024 Cummins, J.
