# Students' Online Peer Feedback Uptake in Argumentative Essay Writing

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Peer feedback studies have primarily focused on its implementation rate, resulting in a limited understanding of the peer feedback uptake process. This study aims to investigate how students take up peer feedback in the context of argumentative essay writing within an online setting in higher education. We introduced a new coding scheme to evaluate students’ uptake of online peer feedback, distinguishing between four types of uptake. Initial findings reveal that over half of the provided peer feedback was addressed, but ignoration was the most common uptake type. Students tended to accept feedback with solution features but were more likely to ignore feedback containing suggestions or metacognitive features. Our results could shed light on the relationship between the features of provided peer feedback and its uptake, contributing to the ongoing conversation on the effectiveness of online peer feedback in higher education.

## Introduction

Peer feedback can be used to improve students’ high-level cognitive processing, such as critical thinking and deep learning, and close the gap between current and desired understanding (Banihashem et al., 2022; Gao et al., 2024; Kerman et al., 2024). It can also allow students to gain a deeper under standing of the issue at hand, as well as facilitate the use of solid argumentation and reaso ning strategies (Latifi et al., 2021; Winstone & Ca rless, 2019). In recent years, a large number of studies have pointed out that online peer feedback has a positive effect  on students’ writing skills in different genres, especially for argumentative essay writing (Noroozi et al., 2023; Valero Haro et al., 2023).

However, peer feedback will be meaningless if students cannot appl y it to their  tasks (Winstone et al., 2017). Students’ engagement in receiving and implementin g peer feedback is defined as the uptake of peer feedback (Alemdag & Yildirim, 2022). Understanding how students take up peer feedback du ring revision is crucial, as research suggests that their responses to peer feedback can significantly influence the learning process, especially in tasks involving writing (Wichmann et al., 2018; Wu & Schunn, 2021). Recent studies have primarily focused on whether peer feedback is addressed, and on measuring its impleme ntation rate (e.g. Alemdag & Yildirim, 2022). However, the understanding of the peer feedback u ptake process, including how students take up peer feedback, remains limited. Furthermore, recent  research has revealed the relation ship between feedback features and the uptake of peer feedback (Kerman et al., 2022; Wu & Schunn, 2020). For instance, Wu and Schunn

(2020) demonstrated that feedback with explanation features could directly predict its implementation in the

revision process. While this relationship was recog nized, we know little about how the feedback featur es impact the uptake process of peer feedback. Hence, t his study aims to delve deeper into the feedback uptake process, investigating how students utilize online peer feed back and how students ’ uptake of peer feedback is related to feedback features in the context of argumentative essay writing in an online learning environment.

## Theoretical background

Within the peer feedback process, students benefit from the contribution of their peers, resulting in a collaborative co-construction of knowledge (Kollar & Fischer, 2010). Throughout the peer feedback uptake process, w hen students receive peer feedback and then engage with  it by comprehending the feedback, re-evaluating their performance, and making revisions, they are co-constructing their understanding and improvement in response to the feedback provided by their peer s, albeit asynchronously (Weinberger & Fischer, 2006; Wichmann et al., 2018). Weinberger and Fischer’s (2006) social mode of co-construction outlines five categories that demonstrate the extent to which learners refer to the contribut ion of their learning partners: externalization, elicitation, quick consensus building, integration-oriented consensus building, and conflict-oriented consensus building. In this study, we interpreted Weinberger and Fischer’s (2006) mode into peer feedback practices. To be specific, during externalization, learners may explicate their knowledge, which ref ers to the feedback-providing process as the role of the feedback provider. In the elicitation process, learners use their learning partners as a resource by asking questions. This eliciting action occurs in the peer dialogues or discussion process when learners have the opportunity to seek information and input from their learning partners. During quick consensus building, learners accept the contributions of their learning  partners without modification or indication. In th e feedback uptake process, feedback receivers simply accept the provided feedback, copying and pasting the new information into their revised work. In integration-oriented consensus building, learners take over, integrate, and apply the perspective of the learning partners. In the feedback uptake process, feedback receivers acknowledge the feedback providers’ feedback, and elaborate on or explain the identified issues based on the feedback. In the case of conflictoriented consensus building, learners disagree with, modify, or replace the pe rspective of the learning partners. In the feedback uptake process, feedback receivers may not accept feedback from providers, resulting in disregarding the feedback completely. This is what other studies refer to as "rejected/no t addressed" or “not implemented” (e.g., Alemdag & Yildirim, 2022). Furthermore, feedback receivers may identify the is sues that feedback providers have pointed out but do not fully agree with their suggestions. Instead, they modify the issues in their own way to address the identified concerns.

The first two categories do not pertain to the upta ke of received peer feedback but rather describe th e processes of interaction and engagement. The latter  three categories, however, indicate how feedback r eceivers respond to the received feedback, and thus were selected for this study to measure how students take up peer feedback. We used the term acceptance for quick consensus building, elaboration for integration-oriented consensus building, and ignoration and modification for conflict-oriented consensus building.

## Participants and experimental procedure

74 graduate students  enrolled in a master’s course, which included an academic argumentation skills m odule at [host institute]. The module spanned three weeks and was integrated into the  Brightspace online platform. Students were given detailed guidance on using the online peer feedback tool within the process. During the first week, students were instructed to write an argumentative essay on one of three controversial topics, which served as pre-tests. In the second week, students were required to provide nonanonymous online written feedback on peers’ two essays using the FeedbackFruits platform. Students followe d the argumentative essay criteria proposed by Noroozi et al. (2016). Recent studies have further employed these criteria to prove the reliability (Noroozi et al., 2023; Valero Haro et al., 2019). In the third week, students were instructed to revise their essays based on the feed back received from their peers. The revised essays were considered as post-tests. A total of 74 students completed the origina l essay writing and provided online peer feedback on two peers’ essays. Two students did not submit the revised essays.

## Data collection and segmentation

In total, we received 148 sets of peer feedback. Af ter excluding 8 sets from 4 students who did not su bmit their post-test essays and 1 duplicated set, 139 sets of peer feedback remained for coding and analysis. We utilized the event sampling method (Shiffman & Stone, 1998)  to segment each set of peer feedback into individual coding units, as every peer feedback  set often addressed more than one problem. An independe nt comment unit was defined as one that raises and/or solves a single issue. This resulted in a total of 894 individual feedback comments. Based on the coding scheme of Wu and Schunn (2020),  we first categorized the feedback comments into no nimplementable (summary or praise-only comments) or implementable feedback comments (revision-oriented comments that could trigger revisions). Non-implementable feedback comments (n=302) were excluded from further analysis. Subsequently, all implementable feedback comments (n=592) were coded based on the feedback features and feedback uptake.

Measurements Measurement of feedback features The features of peer feedback were measured using an adjusted coding scheme based  on previous works by Wu and Schunn (2020)  and Hattie and Timperley (2007). The coding scheme  includes: cognitive features (identification, explanation, suggestion, and solut ion), affective features (hedges (problem), hedges (solution), and mitigating praise), and metacognitive features.

Measurement of feedback uptake A new coding scheme was developed to measure the students’ uptake of peer feedback, based on the social mode of co-construction (Weinberger & Fischer, 2006).  We used  “acceptance”, “elaboration”, “modification”, and “ignoration” to code how students take up peer feedback  comments. The Compare function in Microsoft Word was utilized to identify all alterations made in th e original and revised essays. The highlighted regions corresponded to a specific feedback comment. Then, through a comparative analysis of the original and revised essays, each individual feedback comment was coded depending on how it was taken up in the revised essay.

## Results

We are able to report some preliminary results from our data. Among all the feedback comments, 38.2% of peer feedback ha s been ignored, while the remaining  were addressed in three different ways: Acceptance (16.6%), Elaboration (35.8%), and Modification (9.5%). ## Discussion and conclusions

Our initial findings provide valuable insights into  how students take up peer feedback, and how students take up peer feedback with differen t feedback features. For example, our results indicate a higher acceptance rate for feedback with solution features (i.e., specific revision advice),  implying students’ greater willingness to accept feedback offering specific advice for task-related revision. These results align with the prior study by Vander Pol et al. (2008), who found that students’ feedback, which includes specific revision suggestions, is positively related to their subsequent revision. This could be due to the higher-level cognitive demands involved in revising text, where students prioritize feedback with clear instructions for easier revision (Aben et al., 2022). Moving forward, we plan to conduct multinomial logi stic regression to delve deeper into the influence of different feedback features  on students ’ uptake of peer feedback. This will help us better understand how feedback features drive students to accept, elaborate on,  modify, or ignore  peer feedback.  This study could contribute to the broader conversation on the effec tiveness of peer feedback in higher education and offers valuable insights for researchers and educators to refine peer feedback practices.
