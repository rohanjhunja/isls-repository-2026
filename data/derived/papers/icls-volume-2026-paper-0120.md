# Shaunk: An Embodied Computing Approach to Music Learning through Culturally Responsive Design

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This design-based research study explores interdisciplinary interactions between the Arts and Computer Science in a Music classroom. We co-designed Shaunk, a robotic Dhol (a Punjabi percussion instrument), with music instructors and studied it as part of a n after-school program for middle and high school students. We conducted a mixed-methods analysis of video data, pre-and post-assessments, and semi-structured interviews to understand how embodied interaction with a musical robot shapes learning engagemen t, fluency, collaborative practice, and students' perceptions of computing. Our analysis shows increases in practice time (150%), task completion (from 60% to 95%), and engagement duration. Qualitative analysis of student interviews suggests that students appreciated the connections between cultural practice and computing. We contribute design principles for culturally responsive embodied learning environments and discuss the implications of integrating computing into authentic cultural contexts.

Digital technologies are transforming music education (Bauer, 2020). While conventional approaches such as studying sheet music and attending ensemble rehearsals are still helpful, they are no longer the sole means for students to participate in music. Tec hnological innovations such as robotic instruments and motion-sensing devices not only enhance musical talent but also expose students to programming and engineering principles, thereby creating transdisciplinary opportunities (Kapur, 2005; Leavy et al., 2023). Robotic musical instruments have evolved from experimental performances to educational tools that can facilitate collaborative learning in music classrooms (Kapur & Darling, 2010; Kusunoki et al., 2002; McCarthy et al., 2005). Further, by combining embodied activities with digital tools, educators candesign more adaptive and immersive experiences that bring together movement, technology and creativity.

Despite the potential benefits of technological advancements and embodied learning in music education, several challenges persist. One major challenge is maintaining cultural responsiveness, as many existing tools are developed with little regard for varied musical traditions and practices, thereby alienating students from minority cultural backgrounds (Woods, 2023). Another problem is the development of simple technologies to integrate into existing educational environments, as many instructors encounter o bstacles such as limited training, time constraints, or a lack of infrastructure (Phelps et al., 2024). Questions remain about whether these tools can create meaningful and sustained student engagement, particularly in ways that honour cultural identities while supporting interdisciplinary learning.

Likewise, tools that enable students to design, code, or interact with digital instruments and robotic systems may enhance music learning and help build understanding of CS concepts such as debugging, problem-solving, and systems thinking (Bain et al., 2021). Computational thinking has become an important K-12 education competency (Grover and Pea, 2013; Voogt et al., 2015), and the intersection of music and CS remains understudied. However, the intersection of music and CS is underexplored, and more research is needed to identify strategies that effectively leverage music education to inspire interest and confidence in computing disciplines, particularly among students who do not traditionally consider themselves part of STEM. Addressing these gaps requires the creation of innovative pedagogies and learning environments which are human-centred, evidenceinformed solutions that empower students while enabling interdisciplinary learning. Our study addresses these gaps through the design, implementation and use of Shaunk, a robotic Dhol co-created with Punjabi music instructors. This study investigates how music education may be enhanced in an environment facilitated by an educational robot and how this influences engagement with computer science and the development of computing literacies in non-traditional settings. Specifically, we ask the following research questions: 1. In what ways does embodied interaction with a musical robot shape engagement and learning in a culturally-situated music classroom? 2. How does the integration of a programmable musical robot influence students' perceptions of and interest in computer science?

To provide a robust theoretical foundation for this interdisciplinary intervention that combines artistic creativity and computational proficiency, this section describes four established educational theories that guide our research and help explain how students actively construct knowledge, engage with tangible artifacts, participate in cyclical learning processes, and embed th eir understanding within authentic contexts. Constructionism and learning through making Constructionism, developed by Papert (1993), builds on Piaget's (1970) constructivist theory, asserting that learning is most effective when individuals create tangible, shareable artifacts that externalize their understanding. Papert, known for his work on the LOGO programming language, demonstrated that constructing objects, such as a turtle robot drawing geometric shapes, embodies abstract concepts and fosters iterative refinement (1991). This making-based learning method has been central to the modern m aker education programs (Martinez and Stager, 2013). In this study, the robotic drum serves as the artifact through which students express their grasp of coding and music. By programming the drum to play a particular set of beats, students transform theoretical ideas, such as iteration in coding or meter in music, into a sensory outcome that they can continue to evaluate and adjust. This theory aligns closely with the study’s methodology, as the robotic drum parallels Papert’s LOGO turtle by providing immediate feedback on students’ creations. For instance, a student intending to program a Chaal beat with a 4/4 time signature but hear ing an erratic beat must debug their code, engaging in what Papert termed “learning by debugging” (1993). This iterative process not only reinforces technical skills but also mirrors authentic practices in coding and musical learning. Furthermore, constructionism underscores the motivational power of creating personally meaningful artifacts. Given music’s inherent emotional resonance, students programming a drumbeat they enjoy will likely persist through challenges, enhancing engagement and learning efficacy (Papert, 1991).

Embodied cognition and musical learning Embodied Learning suggests that cognition is tied to bodily engagement with the world, enhancing understanding through movement and sensory input (Leman & Nijs, 2017). This perspective, rooted in Vygotsky's (1978) sociocultural theory, emphasizes that lear ning is mediated through tools and social interaction. As students manipulate the drum and interact with peers in a dynamic space, they ground abstract concepts in physical actions, making the experiential cycle more immersive and compelling. This multisensory approach accommodates diverse learners by providing a tangible entry point to reflection and experimentation. In musical contexts, physical engagement activates cognitive processes differently than abstract symbol manipulation. When students watch Shaunk's drumsticks move, hear its strikes, and coordinate their own bodily actions to match, they engage in multimodal learning that integrates visual, auditory, and kinesthetic information. This embodied approach may be compelling for skill acquisition that involves precise timing and coordination (Kolb, 1984). 1078 Communities of practice and situated learning Learning is not an individual process but a community of practice in which learners participate in real-world activities alongside more experienced practitioners (Lave and Wenger, 1991; Wenger, 1998). The Bhangra academy in the context of this research is a community where learners engage in a legitimate peripheral participation-progressively becoming less peripheral and more central in the activity of the Punjabi folk music. The Shaunk introduction into this environment does not lose the authenticity of t he cultural practice and extends the community to incorporate the competency of computational literacy as a valued one. This places learning in contextualized contexts rather thandecontextualized learning. Culturally-sustaining pedagogy Culturally sustaining pedagogy (CSP) focuses on teaching methods that support and affirm students' cultural and linguistic identities rather than assimilating them into mainstream standards. In the Learning Sciences, CSP has been applied to conceptualize learning settings that rely on community knowledge and student agency (Bateman, 2020). For example, Luna (2020) demonstrated the role of video-club professional learning communities in helping teachers observe and develop culturally based classroom practices that deepen their understanding of students' cultural resources. Similarly, Hickey, Lam, Luo, and Quick (2023) proposed design principles for culturally sustaining educational assessment (CSEA) and applied CSP to the field of assessment by appreciating various methods of knowing and expressing knowledge. Taken together, these publications make CSP one of the central frameworks for equity-based design in research on the learning sciences, where pedagogy, assessment, and participation are not substitutes for cultural practices. Shaunk is a CSP in practice, as it relies on knowledge from the community and on teaching methods that are more culturally appropriate.

## Positionality, methodology and participants

[Author 1] has  been a Punjabi Folk Dance (Bhangra) Instructor for the past 13 years in North America. His expertise in this field naturally sparked interest in music education, and the connection between CS and music set the foundation for this study to explore music as  a pathway to CS. We use a percussion instrument called the Dhol. This double-headed drum is a landmark in the energetic world of Bhangra. Originating in the Punjab region of South Asia, it is often used at celebratory events such as festivals, gatherings, and Bhangra performances. The Dhol is played using two sticks, Tilli and Dhagga (see Figure 1). The Tilli is a slim, flexible stick used on the treble side, and the Dhagga is a thicker, sturdier stick used on the bass side. Design-based research We used design-based research, in which the goals of “designing learning environments and developing theories or ‘prototheories’ of learning are intertwined” (The Design-Based Research Collective, 2003, p. 4). Sandoval and Bell (2004) argue that two prominent affordances of design-based research are the development of a “better theoretical understanding of the learning phenomena addressed by an intervention and knowledge of useful and generalizable design principles” (p. 200). Design-based research involves  iterative cycles of design, implementation, analysis, and redesign, with the explicit goal of producing both practical innovations and theoretical advances (Brown & Campione, 1996; Easterday et al., 2014). In this project, the music education classroom is the learning environment, and the prototheories are our hypotheses about how the robot would make particular Dhol enactments and about whether learning is more or less productive. We engaged in iterative cycles of design, enactment, and analysis (Anderson & Shattuck, 2012; Zheng, 2015) to understand how the design of Shaunk would enable use, understanding, and interaction with the music instructors, thereby anchoring design decisions and analysis throughout the work. To address our RQs, we use a mixed-methods approach in which we concurrently collect rich quantitative and qualitative information to inform descriptions of how Shaunk is used, and we utilize a pre/post design to measure changes in students’ engagement and learning. We use a nested datacollection structure in which the unit of study is a timed interaction conducted either by the instructors (Phases 1 and 2) or by the students (Phase 3). We collected quantitative and qualitative data through visual observations, audible cues, pre-and post-survey results, and one-on-one interviews to address our two research questions and triangulate findings across analysis techniques.

Phase 1: Development and refinement of Shaunk-the musical robot Shaunk houses a regular Dhol in a custom-built wooden frame with wheels for easier movement around classrooms (see Figure 1, right). A 3D-printed Dhagga and Tilli were then attached to the wooden frame, which was connected to two servo motors powered by an Arduino Uno. Shaunk also has visual and auditory feedback nodes to enhance the learning process. Visually, a red and a blue LED are attached to Shaunk and light when the 1079 Dhagga and Tilli strike the Dhol, respectively. The other visual cue is seeing the movement of the Dhagga and Tilli strike the Dhol. The audible feedback for participants is the sound of Shaunk as a whole when it strikes the Dhol. This design style is based on the concepts of tangible and embodied interaction in educational robotics, in which physical manipulation and multimodal feedback facilitate learning (Bravo et al., 2021). Phase 2: Exploration of music–cs connections During this study, we invited ten CS students of different ethnicities living in North America to learn music rhythms through clapping. We played various rhythms with Shaunk, asking participants to clap along to the beats. Following the session, participants reflected on the computing concepts they recognized or applied during the activity. A total of 20 distinct concepts were identified, including looping, machine learning and debugging. 70% of participants found that looping was a key concept recognized when connecting music studies and CS education. Two participants reflected on how complex concepts such as event-driven architectures and threading could be introduced through music. All student participants identified multiple connections between learning music and computing and showed a positive attitude towards learning from music robots. After students participated in the study, they said things like, “... Combining music and CS is sticky, you keep repeating it in your head … I would fall in love with Robotics.” This exploration was highly engaging and interactive. It gave us preliminary insights into how embodied, rhythm-based activities can strengthen conceptual bridges between music and computing. It also helped us refine Shaunk’s multi-modal feedback design and build intuitive interaction interfaces for Shaunk to support music learning at the academy in Phase 3.

Phase 3: Classroom enactment and evaluation of Shaunk A local Bhangra academy in Brampton, Ontario, Canada, teaches Punjabi Folk Dance to students of all ages. This academy was transformed into a culturally resonant, engaging, and innovative environment where student learning can take place. We designed the environment to have four working stations, with 2 PCs on each, to support smallgroup interactions among 19 participants. Eight Dhols were distributed around the classroom to make it accessible, creating a dynamic environment and encouraging active learning. This embodied environment allowed participants to move freely in the class and collaborate with one another (see Figure 2, left). This is not something a traditional Dhol class would have, as it is more student-teacher-focused than student-to-student. Our robotic Dhol, Shaunk, is also placed in the environment for the participants to use when instructed. This unique approach to the study's focus on embodied and collaborative learning helps break from conventional ways of teaching music and CS.

Figure 2 Embodied Environment Study 1-learning to play Dhol in an embodied environment We set up the dance academy with four workstations arranged in a U shape and two PCs at each station. In the class, we had eight Dholis to learn with, along with extra wooden Dhaagas and Tillis on the tables for participants to practice the beats. Twenty p articipants first completed the Pre-Survey on computers to gauge CS interest and knowledge of Dhol playing. Of these 20 participants, 17 had never played Dhol before, and none could play a simple beat before this study. The session began with a brief introduction outlining the purpose of the study and providing participants with an overview of Dhol playing techniques. The Tilli is the skinnier wooden stick used 1080 for the treble side of the Dhol. The Dhagga is the bigger wooden stick used for the bass side of the Dhol. Ge defines a Dhagga strike on the Dhol, Na is a Tilli strike, and Dha is a Dhagga and Tilli strike. These are called Bols and are essentially vocabulary terms for playing Dhol. The participants are then introduced to the beat they will be learning, called Chaal, which is played as Dha Na Na Na Dha Dha Na. Our Dhol Instructor begins the lesson by having participants practice each bol on the Dhol. Since there were twice as many participants as Dhols, they shared the Dhols, and extra Dhagga Tillis were also used to practice on tables. Students slowly worked up to playing the whole Chaal beat with the instructor. Once the basics were taught, Shaunk was placed in front of each participant to follow the beat (see Figure 2, right). The students had a few feedback points to mimic the actions of Shaunk, such as the movements of the Dhagga and Tilli, the sound emitted by the strikes, and 2 red and blue LEDs to rep resent the strikes of the Dhagga and Tilli, respectively. After, the students had the chance to work with Shaunk, and they replayed the beat without Shaunk. Study 2-Learning to code The second part of this study looks at learning to code with the help of Shaunk. Participants paired up and returned to their stations to work on the PC. The students opened the Arduino IDE with a stripped-down version of the code that runs Shaunk preloaded. The facilitator gave a brief lesson on the Arduino environment, what it is for, and what it cando for the study. The structure and critical components of the code were presented in a high-level overview. The demonstration introduced two custom functions: dha() and na(). These were designed to replicate specific Dhol strikes, dha() being the bass side and na() the treble side. This explanation enabled participants to understand how the Arduino environment could be used to control the Dhol’s movements and incorporate technology into the musical experience.  The students were tasked with coding the Chaal beat by calling dha() and na() in the correct order and with the correct syntax. Students also had the opportunity to change the speed variable in the code to make Shaunk’s striking speed faster or slower. When satisfied with their code, participants would upload it to Shaunk by connecting the USB to their PC. Semi-structured interviews After the study was completed, students were asked to volunteer for a short interview to discuss their experiences. Four students volunteered and were interviewed in private. We asked six questions to gauge the study's effectiveness, identify shortcomings,  outline future steps, and assess interest in computer science and music education. This also allowed participants to provide feedback on Shaunk itself. For our first research question, we are interested in understanding the impact Shaunk had on engagement and learning in the Music classroom. To do this, we integrated Shaunk into a traditional Dhol learning class, where we observed notable increases in student engagement and collaboration. Students also reported that Shaunk supported their learning, with all participants indicating in the post-survey that the robot helped them learn to play the Dhol (see Table 1).

## Interview findings

To answer our second research question on how Shaunk influenced students' perceptions of and interest in computer science, we analyzed data from our survey. As seen from Table 1, while there was only a 10% increase in student interest in taking CS courses, Shaunk was successful in helping students gain a broader understanding of the field and gain a new interest in CS.

During our semi-structured interviews, participants shared their amazement at the interdisciplinarity of this endeavour. “Singh” (14 years old, no coding experience) shared: “I never thought our culture could be merged into technology like this, and this is the first time I’ve heard an instrument turn into a robot.” This same student said, “My whole perception of what Computer Science is has changed.” While changing student perceptions was not the purpose of this study, building computing literacy definitely helped clear misconceptions that CS was not “only about games and coding.” As the instructor pointed out, we “definitely broke many stereotypes around CS.” During multiple student conversations, it became evident to us that students appreciated the impact of merging culture and technology and considered it “huge for the community” (“Mo”, 15 years old, basic coding experience). “Mo” further pointed out that “this is a proud moment for the entire community as it is the first time a robotic Dhol has been introduced to the Punjabi community.” Participants described their learning experiences, including the emotions associated with working with a robot and its affective and practical implications. “Mia” (15 years old, no coding experience) mentioned several “fun moments” associated with coding,  including feeling like she was “in a....” Several other participants agreed, describing the presence of Shaunk in the classroom as “useful,” “fun,” “purposeful,” and “superb.” In addition, when asked about their coding experiences, they described the sense of “control” and “confidence” that Shaunk provided.

Another student, “Rohit” (15 years old, no coding experience), said, “Before, I was thinking about doing Law, but now I am considering Computer Science.” Our pleasant surprise came from “Ria’s (15 years old, no coding experience) email that night to our lab, saying that she would like to be involved in projects like this and wants to help out in our lab as much as possible.

Through a design-based research study, we demonstrated the potential of Shaunk as an embodied, culturally situated learning robot that bridges musical and computational thinking. By integrating a robotic Dhol into traditional Bhangra learning settings, stu dents experienced learning that was physically, socially, and culturally grounded. The rhythm-based interactions promoted active engagement and organic collaboration. The tangible presence of the robot allowed students to connect bodily movement with computational ideas such as looping and sequencing, both in the CS students' study in Phase 2 and the music classroom study in Phase 3. This builds on research in embodied cognition showing that learning is enhanced when abstract concepts are anchored in tangible experiences (Leman & Nijs, 2017; Planey & Lindgren, 2020) and aligns with constructionist principles, in which learners build understanding through the creative making of music and code (Papert, 1993). For us, it was also important to highlight how culturally responsive pedagogies can change perceptions of what counts as computer science. The use of Shaunk in the music classroom helped participants connect code 1083 to music and reframe computer science as creative and community-centred. From a design-based research perspective, Shaunk’s iterative development led to several design principles for embodied robotics. These include:

1. Integrate multimodal feedback to connect sensory and conceptual understanding;

2. Design for collaboration between different learners such that the robot supports organic groups.

3. Center cultural authenticity over technological novelty to sustain engagement, familiarity and identity;

4. Balance scripted guidance with improvisation to keep it authentic.

These principles inform both the refinement of Shaunk  and the broader design of embodied, culturally grounded learning environments.

Future work will entail making Shaunk a much more complete Dhol player (Dholi), with various beat-playing abilities embedded and listening capabilities to engage more with the environment in which it is present. A few ways to improve Shaunk include making it much louder than it currently is. In this study, participants had to rely on visual cues to match the beat due to ambient noise. This is because the noise in the classroom was much louder than we anticipated, with eight Dhols, the instructor, and Shaunk playing simultaneously. Another improvement will be with the frame structure of Shaunk to make it more transportable. The frame can benefit from an upgrade by using a much more solid metal frame. Additionally, adding more visual feedback may help students stay on beat. In the current version of Shaunk, there are only two lights available for students to track, along with the visual cues of the Dhagga and Tilli strikes. For future implementations, these lights should become more prominent, and the addition of a monitor screen will also add more layers to Shaunk's multimodal feedback system.

## Significance of this Work

The most unexpected reward of our study is hard to measure, but days after the study, students are still talking about it, especially since this is a first for the community. We were excited to take CS into a non-traditional environment, and the continued engagement we see with Shaunk post-study is proof that culturally responsive, embodied design can generate enduring engagement and learning connections.  We share this research to contribute to three conversations in the learning sciences: Embodied Learning Environments: Interactions with Shaunk help clarify how physical artifacts mediate learning in domains that require precise timing and coordination, such as music learning (Halverson & Sawyer, 2022). The multimodal feedback loops Shaunk enabled appeared crucial for skill development, suggesting design implications for other domains as well.

Culturally-Sustaining Pedagogy in Computing: We demonstrate one approach to embedding CS learning within cultural practices. The deep desire of our first author to give back to the community is what motivated this work. Our co-design process and community embeddedness were essential, and the research-practice partnership for such work is ongoing, never complete.

Informal Learning Environments:  After-school settings offer flexibility for embodied, collaborative, culturally situated learning that is difficult to achieve in formal schooling. This work paves the way to start understanding how to leverage these spaces for interdisciplinary learning. The learning sciences must continue grappling with how to create equitable access to computing education, not just by adding diversity to unchanged structures, but by transforming what counts as computing contexts and who defines what constitutes valuable computational practice.
