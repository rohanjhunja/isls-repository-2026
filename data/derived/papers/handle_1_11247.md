# Narratives of Change: Fostering Agency toward Sustainability

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study explores the role of narratives in empowering young people to engage with sustainability issues, with a focus on fostering agency through narrative-based learning. Using data collected via the SenseMaker tool, this research examines how micro-narratives reveal students' attitudes, personal agency, and reflections on climate change. Findings suggest that narratives enhance understanding, providing a lens through which students envision their roles in sustainability and address complex, real-world challenges.

## Introduction and theoretical background

We live in times of unprecedented change, with majo r economic, environmental, geopolitical, societal a nd technological challenges that coincide and amplify each other. As the Icelandic writer Andri Snaer Mag nason wrote: “we are facing with changes much greater than what our mind is used to, more challenging than any of our previous experiences” (Magnason, 2019) – changes that deeply ask us to rethink our roles as agentic humans in this world. Again, as Magnason: “time is running and even if Nature has left geolog ical speed of change, our reactions seem to be still on a geological scale”. This is also combined with the perspective raised  by the South Korean-born Swiss-German philosopher Byung-Chul Han, who supports the idea that the accelerati on of social rhythms has produced, in everyday life, a widespread cult of urgency capable of eroding the possibility of control by individuals, who are forced to measure themselves against an epochal trait of uncertainty and ungovernability of the future (Han, 2019). Howe ver, as Bruno Latour (20 20) stresses: “The great advantage of being faced with the Anthropocene is that we no longer have to deal with a natural problem, in front of which we would be without strength and resources, but we are faced with socia l decisions which we can (easily) oppose”. We have the responsibility and potential to build our futures and this requires unprecedented actions. We face changes of epochal significance and, despit e the growing attention from many corners as well as the voice of the youth in the squares, calling the adults to respond to their responsibility, science at school still seems to fail in being supportive for students' mak ing sense of these demanding socio-scientific questions. The fact that almost half of national curricula all ove r the world don't refer explicitly to climate chang e issues (UNESCO, 2019) is a representative example of the d elay that formal  education has with respect to societal challenges. This clashes against recent reports (e. g., OECD, 2018; Bianchi et al., 2022) suggesting th e need to promote students’ awareness of present and future issues, preparing them for dealing with the complexity of reallife challenges. In the context of complex problems such as today’s sustainability and climate change challenges, narratives become a primary and crucial means for integrating scientific knowledge, authentic inquiry, and critical thinking skills needed to pursue solutions,  and the goal-oriented, affective and active engagement in action (Tasquier. Knain & Jornet, 2022). Why do narratives matter? As Schwab and Malleret (2 021) argue, as human beings “we are storytelling creatures”, and the stories we tell are our fundamental tool of communication and transmission, and serve as a way of understanding others (D’Orto & Tasquier, 2025). Narratives are how we make sense of life. Most importantly, compelling narrati ves have the power to inspire us to act (Schwab & M alleret, 2021). So, people make sense of the world and give meaning to life as reflected in the everyday micro-narratives (or small stories) that people tell in their social i nteractions (Vander Merwe et al., 2019). But why a re micronarratives so important? Schwab and Ma lleret (2021) supports the idea that the constellat ion of important interrelated small stories can coalesce around a bi gger central story. Micro-narratives contribute more to participative modes of sensemaking than big stories  do. Furthermore, these fragme nts collectively disclose identities, motivations, and attitudes.  They addre ss a broad spectrum of issues aiming to shed light on what’s coming and to offer some clarity on our options in terms of a collective response (Schwab & Malleret, 2021). Thanks to micro-narratives, the young can have the opportunity to f igure out who they are and envision plausible futures for themselves and their surround ings through making use of cultural resources in so cial relationships with the people who take part in thes e worlds.  This includes aspects such as personal and politica l agency, the extent to which they experience despair versus hope and detachment versus empowerment regarding the present and future, and their narratives relate d to climate change and transformatio ns to sustainability. This last is the hypothesis on which we based the empiri cal research, and the activities described in this extended abstract that guess to answer the following questio n: T o what extent can narratives provide insights into the current attitudes of young people about their sense  of agency in relation to climate change and sustai nability challenges?

## Context, design of the study and methods

This study is framed within the Horizon2020 SEAS project (Science Education for Action and Engagement towards Sustainability), whose overall aim is promo ting scientific literacies and skills necessary to address complex sustainability challenges by embedding a transformative and agentic dimension  into scientific literacy and, especially, into teaching/learning climate cha nge and sustainability (Tasquier, Knain & Jornet, 2 022). To incorporate this dimension into science, the projec t refers to the heuristic model of the three spheres of transformation (O’Brien & Sygna, 2013), whic h defines transformation as a process taking place ac ross three interacting spheres: i) a practical sphere which includes both technical and behavioural chang es that contribute to the solution of climate change and sustainable i ssues; ii) a political sphere which highlights the systems and structures that facilitate or hinder transformations in the practical sphere; iii) a personal sphere, which highlights the importance of individual and collective worldviews, values, beliefs and paradigms that are at stake and which drive people’s motives for practical and political action. In the SEAS project approach, embedding a transformative and agentic dimension into scientific literacy means that school science has to be re-conceptualised and re-configured to act in and across the three spheres, with the overall goal that both individuals and the ir institutional contexts change as participants and gain agen cy over their learning processes, and over the institutional and political conditions that context ualise those processes. This is recognizable as an individual/collective movement that puts into a dyn amical relationship the agentic role of both the in dividuals toward the system and the system toward the individuals. In this study, we analyse the data collected throug h the use of a specific tool, called SenseMaker and developed by Prof. Dave Snowden on the Cynefin sens emaking framework (Snowden & Boone, 2017), which was re-adapted within the project with the aims of: e ngaging young people in sustainability challenges; keeping track of emerging narratives of change during scien tific learning, as well as fostering imagination and scenario building through the use of micro-narratives (Barelli et al., 2022; Levrini et al., 2021). SenseMaker links micronarratives with human sensemaking, in this way decision makers are able to listen deeply and capture the dreams, fears, concerns, ideas, trends, and early warning s igns for changes within a group of people. They can  identify patterns and themes in the stories and identify collectiv ely held beliefs and contradictions. Methodological ly, SenseMaker collects narratives from respondents and  combines these with quantitative data and pattern visualization.

This paper presents some findings related to studen ts’ micro-narratives collected through the SEAS SenseMaker questionnaire that reveals some kind of individual/collective dynamics activated by the activities and the impact on students’ ability to imagine future s cenarios and take agency toward climate change and sustainability. The data were analysed through by a group of five researchers through qual itative analysis for cross-checking, corroborating evidence and evaluating the  extent to which all evidence  converges. Given the sample (about 100 questionnaires) and research issues, we assume the approach of reflexive thematic analysis where a mixed inductive/deductive operative way of looking at the data and highlighted themes is used (Braun & Clarke, 2019).

## Findings

Analysis of micro-narratives from Italian and Norwegian students highlights several key differences in how young people approach sustainability and climate issues, influenced by their cultural contexts. Italian students frequently emphasize personal responsibility, detailing individual actions such as recycling, conserving water, or purchasing sustainable products. This focus on individual agen cy reflects a strong internalisation of environment al responsibility but also reveals a frustration with the lack of collective or family support. Many Ital ian students expressed a desire for more community involvement, indicating a gap between their own efforts and the larger societal context (Tasquier, Knain & Jornet 2022).

In contrast, Norwegian students showed a stronger f ocus on community-driven actions, often participating in local environmental initiatives su ch as park cleanups and community campaigns. This emphasis on collective responsibility reflects Norway’s cult ural context, which values community engagement and environmental stewardship. Norwegian students often  viewed their actions as part of a broader societal  effort, underscoring the importance of collective agency in tackling climate challenges. Furthermore, they demonstrated greater awareness of the systemic factors affecting  climate change, such as corporate influence and go vernment policies, and emphasised the need for coordinated, large-scale action. The narratives reveal that students in both groups are deeply aware of climate issues and perceive the necessity for broader systemic change. However, Italian students tend to approach climate action through personal habits, whereas Norwegian students often describe their efforts as embedded within community initiatives. These differences reflect the influence of cultural values and environmental contexts on students' conceptions of agency. More specifically, Italian students often emphasize d personal responsibility, focusing on individual actions such as recycling, conserving water, and purchasing sustainable products. This focus on individual agency reflects a deeply internalised sense of environmental responsibility, where change is seen as starting at the personal level. Many Italian students described these eco-conscious habits as small but meaningful contributi ons to sustainability. However, this individualised approa ch to climate action  also surfaced frustrations. Students frequently expressed disappointment with the lack o f support from family members and society, particul arly in the face of intergenerational conflicts regarding e nvironmental values. For instance, several students  share d experiences of family members who downplayed the im portance of actions like recycling, leading to feel ings of isolation in their environmental efforts. This frus tration suggests a gap between students' personal c ommitments and the collective engagement t hey wish to see in their communities. Despite these  challenges, Italian students’ narratives reflect a strong desire for more community involvement, highlighting the need for social structures that support sustainable practices.

Norwegian students, in contrast, demonstrated a str ong emphasis on community-driven initiatives and collective responsibility. Many students described participating in local environmental campaigns, suc h as park cleanups and community projects, reflecting Norway’ s cultural emphasis on environmental stewardship and societal engagement. This collective approach under scores a belief that tackling climate issues requir es coordinated efforts beyond individual actions. Norw egian students often positioned their personal cont ributions as part of a broader societal commitment to sustainability, seeing themselves as members of a community working towards common goals. Additionally, Norwegian narra tives revealed a greater awareness of systemic fact ors impacting climate change, such as corporate influence and governmenta l policies. Many students articulated the view that while individual actions matter, large-scale changes are essential for significant impact.  They stressed the importance of international cooperation and  called for accountability from governments and corp orations, recognising the limitations of personal efforts in the face of global environmental challenges. The differences in Italian and Norwegian students’ narratives highlight the role of cultural contexts in shaping young people’s perceptions of agency and re sponsibility. Italian students primarily focus on i ndividual actions and express frustration with th e lack of collective support, indicating an interna lised, personal approach to sustainability. Norwegian students, on the other  hand, emphasize collective efforts and display a b roader awareness of systemic influences, underscoring the value they place on community and coordinated action. Both groups recognise the limitations of individual efforts alone and the necessity for broader systemic change, though they approach these issues from different starting points. In sum, these findings suggest that fostering agenc y in young people requires acknowledging both individual and collective dimensions of action. Educational efforts should consider these cultural nua nces, promoting a balanced approach to agency that integrates personal responsibility with community engagement and systemic change. By bridging individual actions wit h larger social and political frameworks, educators  can empower students to engage more effectively with cl imate challenges in ways that resonate with their cultural values and experiences (Barelli et al., 2022; Tasquier, Knain & Jornet, 2022).

## Discussion

The findings from the micro-narratives of Italian and Norwegian students provid e valuable insights into young people’s attitudes toward their sense of agency in addressing climate change and sustainability challe nges. Narratives serve as a powerful tool for exploring how students perceive their roles within the complex landscape of environmental issues, highlighting both individu al and collective dimensions of agency. Through the  stories students tell, we gain a nuanced understanding of how cultural contexts shape their approaches to climate action, and how personal experiences and societal influences contribute to their evolving perspectives on sustainability. Italian students' narratives reveal a strong sense of individual responsibility, often rooted in perso nal, everyday actions like recycling or conserving resou rces. These stories reflect an internalised commitm ent to environmental stewardship but also expose a gap in collective support, with students frequently expres sing frustration over family or societal inaction. This individualised focus illustrates how narratives can  uncover a sense of isolation that may hinder broader climate engagement, suggesting th at while students feel responsible, they may struggle to translate this into larger-scale impact without community reinforcement.  In contrast, Norwegian students’ narratives emphasize community involvement and collective responsibility, painting  a picture of agency that extends beyond individual ac tions to include collaborative efforts. Their stori es of participating in local cleanups or advocating for systemic change reveal an understanding of sustainability that is deeply embedded within a social framework. This col lective approach suggests that Norwegian students v iew their actions as part of a coordinated societal res ponse, pointing to a more integrative perspective o n agency that aligns individual efforts with community goals. Nar ratives thus provide a window into how young people conceptualize their role in larger environmental mo vements, revealing differences in agency that are c ulturally influenced. Moreover, the narratives highlight students' awareness of the systemic barriers to meaningful climate action. Norwegian students, in particular, expressed frustration with political and corporate inaction, indicating a recognition that individual and local  efforts may be insufficient without structural chan ges. This awareness of systemic constraints points to a critical understanding of the broader forces shaping environmental ou tcomes, suggesting that students’ sense of agency is not li mited to personal or  local scales but extends to a desire for influence on larger societal and global issues.

Overall, the use of narratives allows educators and  researchers to access the multifaceted attitudes o f young people towards climate change, providing insi ght into how they perceive their capacity for actio n within different contexts. Narratives capture the spectrum of agency from personal to collective and reveal the interplay between cultural values, social dynamics, and environmental awareness. By uncovering these layers of meaning, narratives offer a rich foundation for designing ed ucational approaches that not only promote scientific understanding but also foster empowered, context-sensitive agency among students in relation to sust ainability challenges.

## Conclusion

This study highlights the transformative potential of narratives in fostering young people’s understanding of sustainability and agency. Through the use of micro-narratives, students engage in reflective processes  that connect personal actions with broader  socio-environmental contexts, enabling them to grasp the complexities of climate change beyond isolated facts. This narrativ e-based approach empowers students to see themselves as active participants in shaping sustainable futures, bridging the gap between knowledge and action. The findings underscore the importance of integrating socio-cultural contexts into sustainability education. Italian students tend to adopt individualised approaches to  climate action, often facing frustrations due to l imited collective support. In contras t, Norwegian students demonstrate a preference for community-oriented efforts, emphasizing the value of collective responsibility.  Recognising these cultural distinctions allows edu cators to design interventions that foster both personal and community-based agency, equipping students to navigate climate challenges in ways that align with their values and social realities. Ultimately, narrative-based learning within projects like SEAS demonstrates its effectiveness in promoting agency among young learners. As science education evolves to address the demands of a rapidly changing world, integrating narratives with socio-scientific inquiry becomes crucial. This approach n ot only enhances  students' scientific literacy but also cultivates a generatio n capable of tackling the complex, interconnected c hallenges of climate change and sustainability with critical awa reness, empathy, and a sense of agency. By linking individual and collective ac tions within a broader social and political framewo rk, narrative-based education provides a pathway for students to envision and work toward a more sustainable and equitable future.
