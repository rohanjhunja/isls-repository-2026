# Adaptive Equity-Oriented Pedagogy Predicts Student Achievement in Higher Education: Evidence from a Multi-Site Study

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study investigates whether equity-oriented teaching competencies predict college students’ success. In pedagogy courses, 143 graduate and undergraduate student instructors (hereafter “instructors”) completed the Adaptive Equity-Oriented Pedagogy (AEP) Competency assessment after teaching lessons. This portfolio-based assessment asked instructors to document observable AEP competencies and evaluated how instructors planned, taught, reflected, and adapted to address students’ equity barriers. This previ ously validated AEP competency assessment has demonstrated strong reliability, exhibits no gender bias, and correlates with researchers’ independent review of instructors’ teaching methods and materials. At two R1 institutions, multilevel regression modeling suggests that higher AEP competency scores significantly predicted greater student achievement in 14+ STEM and Social Science courses, controlling for instructors’ course, class size, teaching experience, minoritized status, and gender. Furthermore, addressing the pernicious belief that equity undermines rigor in curriculum, this study provides evidence suggesting that centering equity through AEP can improve instructor and student success.

Minoritized students face disproportionately high attrition in STEM majors (Bottia et al., 2021), partly due to lowquality pedagogy such as unclear expectations, misaligned assessments, limited faculty support, and practices that exacerbate equity barrier s (Phuong, 2022; Seymour & Hewitt, 1997; Weston, 2019). In addition, instructional challenges such as unclear expectations, misaligned assessments, and equity-related barriers to learning have also been documented in social science and humanities college courses (Chick et al., 2012; Phuong et al., 2017a). While active learning, culturally responsive teaching, and universal design for learning are well-supported by research (Gay, 2010; Hammond, 2014; Ladson-Billings, 1995; Rose et al., 2006), many faculty and teaching assistants (TAs) at R1 institutions often receive little preparation in equitable pedagogy and typically teach as they were taught (Davis, 2009; Theobald et al., 2020). Many STEM instructors also disengage from professional development when it lacks relevance or recognition (Wieman, 2017). To explore how equitable pedagogies can be supported and made beneficial early on, we examined graduate and undergraduate student instructors (e.g., TAs, tutors, hereafter “instructors” for simplicity). These instructors participated in a pedagogy course on Adaptive Equity-Oriented Pedagogy (AEP). Instructors designed lessons, taught, and assessed students under faculty supervision. AEP was selected as the foundational framework since it has been shown to significantly raise average achievement by over a letter grade for all students (Phuong et al., 2017a; Phuong & Nguyen, 2019).

As part of AEP pedagogy courses offered at two R1 universities, instructors were supported in developing AEP competencies. We use multilevel modeling to test whether these AEP competencies predict student achievement, measured here as standardized learning  gains, across disciplines and institutional contexts. We explore the following research question: Do instructors’ AEP competency scores predict standardized student learning gains, controlling for student-and instructor-level covariates? Conceptual framing: Adaptive equity-oriented pedagogy AEP is a framework for adapting instruction to address equity barriers to learning based on student data, including formative assessments, classroom observations, and student surveys (Phuong et al., 2017b; Phuong et al., 2021). AEP draws on foundational theories in the learning sciences to support instructors in identifying and addressing equity barriers to learning. Grounded in sociocultural perspectives, AEP emphasizes that learning is socially, 850 culturally, and emotionally situated (Engle & Conant, 2002; Hand & Gresalfi, 2015; Lave & Wenger, 1991; Nasir, 2002). Effective instruction, therefore, attends to students’ identities, lived experiences, and the design of learning environments (Gutiérrez & Rogoff, 2003; Nasir & Hand, 2008; Philip & Azevedo, 2017). AEP also builds on the concept of teacher noticing, positioning it as a critical mechanism for interpr eting classroom interactions, identifying inequities, and adapting instruction responsively (Levin et al., 2009; Sherin & van Es, 2009). AEP operationalizes these learning sciences principles through six interrelated teaching competencies:

1. Clarify learning outcomes, establish clear expectations, and incorporate equitable course policies.

Instructors clearly communicate learning outcomes and prerequisite knowledge while implementing equitable course policies (e.g., grading for equity, flexible deadlines) to reduce barriers to learning (Feldman, 2019; McCallum, 2013). Instructors clarify expectations and demystify the hidden curriculum through rubrics, resources, and exemplary student work when appropriate (Winkelmes, 2014). When such expectations are unclear, bias can arise in educational settings and assessment.

2. Align formative assessments and teaching with learning outcomes.  Learning activities and formative

assessments are aligned with course learning outcomes to further support clear expectations, create equitable access to disciplinary learning, and to avoid privileging students with more prior preparation or access to greater resources (Fink, 2013; Hammond, 2014; Phuong et al., 2017b).

3. Identify students’ strengths/competencies, interests, and learning needs.  Instructors engage students’

assets, interests, and funds of knowledge in pedagogy to foster deeper engagement and motivation (Moll et al., 2006). Research shows that students’ funds of knowledge can be engaged through experiential learning and project-based learning, which can promote culturally responsive learning for students from marginalized groups (Barron, 2006; Gay, 2010; Hammond, 2014).

4. Understand equity barriers to learning. Engaging in equity-focused teacher noticing (Patterson Williams

et al., 2020; van Es et al., 2017; 2022), instructors use student data (e.g., surveys) to notice and understand equity barriers. Equity barriers such as stereotype threat, cultural misalignment, and exclusionary classroom norms are actively recognized and addressed, enabling students to feel valued and capable of engaging fully with disciplinary content (Lee, 2003; Rosebery et al., 1992).

5. Adapt teaching to address barriers.  Informed by teacher noticing, instructors interpret student work,

reflections, and classroom interactions to adjust instruction in real time, scaffold learning experiences, and meet emerging student needs (van Es & Sherin, 2008).

6. Iterate: reflect upon pedagogy to support continuous adaptation and growth.  Through AEP, instructors

engage in ongoing critical reflection to assess not just the intent, but the impact of their teaching on students’ learning and experiences. They continuously adapt instructional strategies and prior AEP practices, drawing on design-based research principles that emphasize iterative improvement in response to students’ evolving needs and contexts (Brown, 1992; Schön, 1987). Instructors recognize that pedagogy, assessment, and education systems are racialized and shaped by broader systems of power (e.g., structural racism, myth of meritocracy) (Kendi, 2019). Grounded in the understanding that there is no single story of student experience (Adichie, 2009), they use student learning data and feedback to better understand their learners, and respond to systemic inequities they face such as racism, sexism, and ableism.

Teacher noticing, identifying equity barriers, and reflective adaptation.  In this study, we extend the concept of teacher noticing in higher education to explore how college instructors notice, reflect on, and respond to equity barriers in their classrooms. Noticing involves attending to, interpreting, and adapting to classroom interactions (Sherin & Star, 2011), and is rooted in reflective practice (Schön, 1987). Building on van Es et al. (2017, 2022), who foreground equity in K –12 teacher noticing, we examine how higher education instructors identify and address forms of sexism, racialization, and other equity barriers, while drawing on students’ assets and sociocultural contexts (Levin et al., 2009; Patterson Williams et al., 2020; van Es & Sherin, 2008). Our study contributes to the learning sciences by analyzing how reflective tools (e.g., written reflections focused on addressing equity barriers) and student data are used to enact AEP, offering a model of equity-focused teacher noticing and instructional adaptation that can strengthen disciplinary learning, engagement, and belonging in postsecondary STEM, humanities, and social science courses. Instructional impact and evidence base.  Building on this conceptual foundation, we examine how AEP supports instructors in identifying and continuously adapting evidence-based strategies (e.g., culturally responsive pedagogy, universal design for learning) that effectively reduce equity barriers to learning (e.g., stereotype threat, 851 imposter phenomenon, assumed background knowledge). Experimental research has shown that, compared to active learning courses, instructors applying AEP increased average student achievement by over a full letter grade for all students (Phuong et al., 2017b; Phuong & Nguyen, 2019). AEP also reduced stereotype threat and improved students’ sense of self-efficacy and community (Phuong et al., 2017a). These findings suggest that prioritizing equity through AEP can help students reach rigorous learning outcomes at a greater rate than standard active learning.

Advancing professional development research in higher education Our project addresses persistent gaps in professional development research related to equitable college teaching in STEM and the social sciences. While studies have examined how professional development programs promote the adoption of active learning stra tegies (Derting et al., 2016; Ebert-May et al., 2015; Wheeler & Bach, 2021), far less research has focused on how these programs influence the adoption of equitable pedagogical practices over time in STEM and across other disciplines. For example, existing studies have primarily explored how STEM-focused professional development influences instructors’ equity-oriented beliefs and practices, typically through self-report, pre-and post-program surveys (Harrison-Bernard et al., 2020; Metevier et al., 2010; O’Leary et al., 2020; Parker et al., 2016; Rooney et al., 2020). However, these five studies have not directly examined the impact of equitable professional development or the equitable teaching competencies developed in the professional development programs on student achievement outcomes. To move beyond self-report data, our study uses a validated measure of equitable teaching and applies multilevel modeling to assess whether instructors’ AEP competency, developed in a pedagogy course, predicts student achievement across college-level STE M, humanities, and social science courses.

## Participants

In this IRB-approved study, 143 participating instructors were enrolled in a pedagogy course on AEP offered at two research-intensive universities, both of which are Asian American and Native American Pacific Islander-Serving Institutions and emerging Hispanic-Serving Institutions. About 33% of instructors identified as female, 64% as male, and 3% as gender non-conforming. Approximately 15% of instructors identified as minoritized (Black, Latinx, Native American, or Pacific Islander). Instructors designed, taught, and assessed lessons for groups of students, meeting either in course sections or at scheduled times. These groups typically ranged from 3-40 students. The dataset included 526 participating students. Participating instructors represented 23 different course content areas across STEM, social science, and humanities disciplines. STEM courses included computer science, data science, statistics, and electrical engineering. Social science and humanities content included psychology, education, public health, disability studies, music, decolonization, and civic engagement.

## Measures

Adaptive Equity-Oriented Pedagogy Competency (AEPC) assessment. The pedagogy courses require instructors to complete a validated AEPC assessment. The AEPC assessment (scale 0–42) contains reflection questions that ask instructors to provide evidence demonstrating AEP competencies. Through a teaching portfolio, AEPC ass essments elicit evidence about teaching philosophies, written reflections on teaching and assessment practices, teaching videos, and evidence of student learning and teaching effectiveness. A rubric based on the AEP elements in the conceptual framing section was used to score responses to items, requesting descriptions and evidence of AEP practices implemented. See Table 1 for more details. Importantly, although portfolios included artifacts related to student learning (e.g., examples of student work), the scoring rubric emphasized instructors’ decision-making, reflection, and data-informed adaptation rather than students’ achievement outcomes. The AEPC assessment embeds teacher noticing by prompting instructors to identify and address equity barriers as they attend to, interpret, and adapt to student learning needs (Sherin & van Es, 2009).

AEPC validation.

The AEPC assessment was previously validated for use with instructors, including graduate student instructors (Phuong, 2022; Phuong et al., 2022b; Phuong et al., 2025). Based on partial credit Rasch modeling (Masters, 2010), the AEPC assessment has high reliability (0.94), strong validity based on Wilson’s (2005) validity framework, shows no gender assessment bias, has high inter-rater reliability, and correlates with researchers’ 852 independent review of instructors’ classroom teaching methods and pedagogical materials (Phuong, 2022; Phuong et al., 2022b; Phuong et al., 2025). Previous research demonstrates that instructors can use the AEPC assessment to demonstrate competency and progress in applying equitable teaching practices. This assessment approach helps instructors document AEP practices and reflect on how they plan, teach, and adapt to address equity barriers. Additionally, the AEPC assessment does not exhibit the gender and racial bias often found in course evaluations (Boring et al., 2016; MacNell et al., 2015; Phuong, 2022; Phuong et al., 2022b; Stark & Freishtat, 2014). Students’ gain scores.

For the student outcome variable, we analyzed gain scores from pre-to post-assessments on course content. As required in the pedagogy course, each participating instructor developed equivalent pre-and post-assessments, leveraging common items and/or equivalent rubrics in each assessment. When applicable, the pedagogy course provided validated assessments from previous studies that instructors could also use or adapt. Instructors who taught the same course collaborated to coordinate and revise assessment questions. To support content validity, facilitators created activities where instructors aligned assessment questions with key learning outcomes and concepts for their instructional context.

Because courses varied in content and used different assessments, raw achievement scores were not directly comparable across disciplines. Instead, for each student, the raw gain from pre-to post-assessment was divided by the standard deviation of the pre-assessment scores among their instructor’s students. This approach converts growth into comparable units (i.e., standard deviations of the pre-assessment baseline within each instructional group), offering a standardized metric for cross-course comparison. Course GPA (scale 0-4) was not the outcome, since GPA is a less sensitive measure of growth and does not fully isolate the mechanisms that drive learning (Arum & Roksa, 2011; Kuh & Ewell, 2010; Rojstaczer & Healy, 2012). Course GPA could also be subject to manipulation in a pre-and post-GPA study (e.g., instructors could give everyone an A or make grading easier on the post measure of GPA). Depending on the context, GPA can be difficult to use as a direct measure of learning because grades may reflect differences in grading policies, instructor discretion, course norms, departmental expectations, and other non-achievement factors. By examining 853 gains on equivalent pre-and post-assessments, we model what predicts growth in students’ understanding and application of concepts, reducing the possibility that achievement gains in our study are due to grade inflation. To gauge fidelity of implementation (FOI), we randomly sampled 15% of instructors at each institution. Researchers scored instructors’ teaching and pedagogical materials with an AEPC rubric to obtain an instructor FOI score. Due to collinearity and conceptual overlap between AEPC and FOI scores, AEPC was retained as the primary predictor in our models.

## Data analysis

To answer our RQ, we used multilevel modeling to test predictors at the instructor level while accounting for the fact that students are nested within instructors. Specifically, the model examined whether instructors’ AEPC, developed during the pedagogy course, predicted students’ standardized gain scores at the end of the course. The outcome variable is the standardized student gain score. We regressed standardized student gain scores  on student characteristics at level 1  (e.g., gender), and instructor characteristics at level 2  (e.g., AEPC assessment scores, minoritized status, gender, years of teaching experience, years of tutoring experience, the course they teach, the number of students in the course the instructor teaches (i.e., class size)).

We controlled for the course each instructor teaches, since faculty may use different grading policies (e.g., curving, limited support), assessments, and pedagogies, all of which can influence teaching in small group sections and achievement (Pace & Middendorf, 2004; Seymour & Hewitt, 1997). The institutional affiliation covariate was entered into the initial model but was omitted by the statistical software due to collinearity. We suspect that since unique courses were offered at only one institution, controlling for courses also controlled for institutions. Post-estimation diagnostics indicated no evidence of problematic multicollinearity among the remaining fixed effects. We included random intercepts for instructors to account for between-instructor variation and the clustering of students within instructors. Models were estimated by maximum likelihood with robust standard errors to protect against misspecifications of the random part of the model and the residual covariance structure.

We begin by reporting descriptive statistics to contextualize our findings. Across instructors (n = 143), AEPC scores averaged 15.10 (SD = 5.62). Standardized student learning gains averaged 2.57 (SD = 2.46). On average, instructors reported 2.06 years of tutoring experience (SD  = 1.76) and less than one year of teaching experience (M = 0.88, SD = 0.96).

Our results show that higher AEP Competency (AEPC) scores were significantly associated with greater student learning gains across the courses in our sample at both R1 institutions.  Per unit increase in AEPC, there is an estimated mean increase in student gains of 0.35 standard deviations of the pre-assessment baseline (p <.001), after controlling for variables in the model (e.g., instructors’ class size, teaching experience, tutoring experience, gender, minoritized status, student gender). Given variation across assessments and the use of groupspecific standardization, we interpret coefficient magnitudes conservatively, emphasizing the direction, statistical significance, and robustness of the association.

After accounting for instructor AEPC, covariates such as student gender, instructor gender, minoritized status, teaching experience, tutoring experience, and class size were not significant predictors of standardized learning gains. While average learning gains varied across course contexts, we did not find significant AEPC-bycourse interaction coefficients in the model, suggesting no clear evidence that the association between AEPC and student learning gains differed by course. This pattern suggests that the positive association between instructor AEPC and student learning growth was relatively consistent across the various courses and disciplinary contexts represented in the study.

Sensitivity analysis Because gain scores may be shaped by students’ starting points, we estimated an additional model that included students’ course-standardized pre-assessment scores as a baseline covariate. We interpret this covariate as a conservative adjustment rather than as a substantive predictor, since pre-assessment performance is related by construction to the gain scores.

We also conducted diagnostic and sensitivity analyses to examine whether results were driven by small instructional-group pre-assessment standard deviations or extreme standardized gain values. Specifically, we estimated models excluding observations with pre-assessment standard deviations below 0.50 and 1.00, as well as models trimming and winsorizing standardized gain scores at the 1st and 99th percentiles. Our AEPC results were not sensitive to these model variations. For example, the significant regression 854 coefficient for AEPC remained relatively consistent (0.3 3 vs 0.35) when the course-standardized pre-assessment scores were added to the model. These instructor reflections help contextualize the quantitative findings and offer insight into how AEP can support student learning gains. One instructor described how AEP elements supported student achievement growth: “I learned a lot, especially around using AEP practice elements to teach scientific inquiry […] They were essential to achieve the positive growth that was seen.” Another instructor said: “I relied heavily on the AEP methods because many of the principles of AEP agreed with my own teaching philosophy and I had found that based on my experiences

A different instructor described a shift from reactive, moment-to-moment responsiveness to a more proactive, data-informed approach to lesson planning, explaining, “I now think that true equity-oriented teaching requires intentional, proactive design through the alignment of learning outcomes, formative assessments, and scaffolding strategies that anticipate and address systemic barriers before they arise.” Another instructor shifted their perspective and now pays more attention to the issues of equity: I used to not pay enough attention to the issue of equity. I sometimes talked to students who had mastered the content and worked with them on hard problems/projects, and ignored some of the other students. This research aligns with prior findings showing that, compared to instructors in the bottom 25% of AEPC growth scores, instructors in the top 25% had more inclusive definitions of equity, showed less deficit thinking, used multiple data sources to adapt teaching, and shared responsibility for learning (Phuong et al., 2023; Phuong et al., 2025).

Connecting to Learning Sciences Literature on Teacher Noticing.  These shifts in instructor thinking reflect equity-focused applications of teacher noticing in higher education within the learning sciences. Focused on moment-to-moment interpretation of classroom interactions (Sherin & Star, 2011; van Es & Sherin, 2008), noticing has increasingly been theorized as a dynamic process involving reflection, interpretation, and pedagogical adaptation informed by sociocultural context and equity concerns (van Es et  al., 2022). This study applies these ideas to a new context by examining how college instructors used AEP to move from reactive to proactive noticing, leveraging student data, prior knowledge, and theories of equity to identify barriers and redesign instruction accordingly.

This work complements prior noticing research by demonstrating the benefits of applying equity-focused noticing in higher education settings, particularly through structured, reflective practices. The AEPC framework integrates written reflections with teac hing videos when applicable, student data, and peer feedback to examine how instructors perceive and respond to equity-related barriers. Unlike self-report surveys, interviews, or observations alone, the AEPC portfolio-based assessment asks questions where  instructors need to reflect on, synthesize, and cite evidence of applying AEP competencies after reviewing multiple artifacts and reflections written over time. This approach provides an equity-focused lens on developing reflective practitioners  (Schön, 1987), highlighting how instructors reason about and implement equitable pedagogy (Phuong et al., 2022a). The 855 AEPC assessment provides a concrete tool for developing, applying, and assessing equity-oriented noticing as an instructional mechanism that supports student learning in multiple disciplinary college contexts.

## Limitations

This study is limited by variation in assessment design across contexts and reliance on group-specific standardized gain scores. In addition, participating instructors were graduate and undergraduate students teaching small course sections, which may not generalize to large lecture settings. Nevertheless, previous studies showed that AEP is effective in larger courses in different disciplinary contexts (Phuong et al., 2017a; Phuong & Nguyen, 2019).

## Implications for STEM teaching and learning

This study addresses long-standing measurement limitations, as prior work on faculty development often relies on course evaluations, self-report or observational measures that capture instructor behavior without linking it to student achievement (Addy et al., 2022; Harrison-Bernard et al., 2020; Metevier et al., 2010; O’Leary et al., 2020; Parker et al., 2016; Rooney et al., 2020; Smith et al., 2014; Wieman & Gilbert, 2014). Building on the Teaching Practices Inventory (Wieman & Gilbert, 2014), COPUS (Smith  et al., 2017), and the Protocol for Advancing Inclusive Teaching Efforts (PAITE) (Addy et al., 2022), we move beyond self-reports to directly connect documented AEP practices with student learning gains.

This study contributes to the learning sciences by examining how novice instructors begin to develop equity-focused noticing as a core instructional competency (Patterson Williams et al., 2020; van Es et al., 2022). Drawing on the AEP framework, the study explores how instructors engage in responsive planning, reflection, and instructional adaptation to identify and respond to students’ diverse learning needs. We are inspired by the notion of professional vision (Goodwin, 1994), which refers to developing ways of seeing phenomena that reflect growing levels of expertise. The AEPC assessment supports instructors in expanding what they can see, attend to, and address with regard to equity barriers in their teaching. For example, instructors using AEPC became more attuned to how factors like food insecurity, assumed background knowledge, hostile STEM climates for non-male students, stereotype threat, and imposter phenomenon shaped students’ learning and success outcomes (Phuong et al., 2022b; Phuong et al., 2023; Phuong et al., 2025). This AEPC approach also aligns with research on relational noticing, which highlights how teachers’ awareness of students’ emotional and contextual experiences informs instructional decisions (Mendez & van Es, 2024). Building on Schön’s (1987) concept of the reflective practitioner, our study frames college instructor learning as an iterative process of interpreting classroom dynamics and adapting practice to create more inclusive instruction. Our finding of no significant statistical interaction between AEP competency and course taught has important equity implications. It suggests that fostering equitable teaching practices through AEP is not only effective in isolated cases or subject-specific settings, but may serve as a broadly applicable strategy for improving student learning in diverse classroom environments. Such consistency across course types strengthens the case for integrating AEP into institutional professional development and evaluation systems as a scalable approach to advancing equity in higher education. By equipping instructors with adaptive, student-responsive pedagogical tools, institutions can promote more equitable outcomes across a wide range of disciplines. AEP tools can be beneficial to centers for teaching and learning and instructor professional development programs serving specific and multiple disciplines (Kearns et al., 2018).

Challenging the pernicious belief that equity undermines rigor in curriculum, this study offers evidence that centering equity through AEP can improve instructor and student outcomes. Such evidence is needed in light of challenges related to faculty and community resistance to equity efforts within the United States. Our findings underscore that equitable teaching is not ancillary to teaching effectiveness, but a core instructional competency that fosters learning environments where all students can thrive.  This study further provides empirical evidence that college teaching in multiple disciplines can be restructured to center care, proactively address systemic barriers, and iterate in response to students’ lived experiences, advancing the field’s conception of effective teaching as adaptive, equity-oriented, data-informed, and deeply relational (Nguyen et al., 2021; 2022; 2023; 2024).
