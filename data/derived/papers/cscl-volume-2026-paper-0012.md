# Biased Chatbot Interaction as Preparation for Collaborative Learning

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Twenty-five teachers engaged in individual preparation with chatbots deliberately designed to exhibit biased stance s on the learning topic of AI adoption in higher education before engaging in collaboration with their human peers. Through intermediate reflections, we probed into how everyone consolidated their learning from the initial human-AI collaboration, whether and how this affected reasoning during groupwork, a nd how everyone consolidated their learning post human-human collaboration. Results showed that while teachers recognized and critiqued biases of the chatbots, their group discussions deepened understanding of ethical concerns and the complexity of bias ed views, leading to a more nuanced and criteria-driven endorsement of AI for education. Overall, our design fostered reflection and perspective-taking, although some teachers remained resistant to changing their views based on feasibility concerns.

What can we do to seed productive discourse in learning (Vogel et al., 2017)? How may we increase the likelihood that collaborative learning triggers rich processes like argumentation and mutual regulation (Dillenbourg, 1999) required for collaborative facilitation (Nokes-Malach et al., 2015; Rajaram, 2021)? Learning sciences scholarship has long grappled with these central questions as means to design more effective scaffolding for collaboration. We propose that interaction with biased chatbots prior to groupwork can serve as one such scaffold to unlock unique learning affordances. By allowing for generation of partially-formed suboptimal ideas, forcing deep elaboration of the bias, and having learners experience conflict with personal viewpoints, this learning design can create a fertile ground for surfacing internalized tensions and reconciling different, often contradictory viewpoints later. That is precisely the foci of our present work, where by aligning human-AI and human-human collaboration with principles of preparation, struggle, and argumentation, we create opportunities for teachers to deepen their understanding of the light and dark sides of AI adoption in higher education, a critical topic in current discourse.

## Theoretical background

Our study is informed by three theoretical lenses from the learning sciences – individual work as preparation for collaboration (Lam & Kapur, 2018), guided failure (Sinha et al., 2021), and learning through argumentation (Asterhan & Schwarz, 2016)  – while addressing design gaps in the use of chatbots for collaborative learning. Former work has shown that engaging learners individually before groupwork  surfaces diverse, partially formed ideas that can enrich collaboration (Lam & Kapur, 2018; Mende et al., 2021; Chen et al., 2024). When this individual work involves structured struggle, such as encountering incorrect or biased information, learners benefit from guided failure  – grappling with suboptimal ideas and viewpoints along with their underlying epistemic tensions under supportive scaffolding has been shown to foster deeper understanding (Sinha et al., 2021; Sinha & Kapur, 2021; Thorgeirsson et al., 2022). Failure in this sense is not an endpoint but a catalyst, particularly when learners are prompted to question, critique, and refine their reasoning, rather than passively accepting ideas. These preparatory processes set the stage for argumentation, in which group members advance and interrogate the credibility of claims,  exchange reasons, and consider counterarguments, thereby strengthening both conceptual knowledge through sensemaking and epistemic cognition through an examination of the reliability and credibility of knowledge sources (Chinn & Clark, 2013; Asterhan & Schwarz, 2016). Meanwhile, recent research on educational chatbots within collaboration has largely positioned them as scaffolding agents that can facilitate collective efficacy  (Weng et al., 2024), socio-emotional support, real-time feedback (e.g., interdependence, belongingness), and dialogic engagement (Zhang et al., 2024; Kovari, 2025; Kim & Lim, 2025). The majority of these chatbots are typically integrated into the group discussion itself, functioning as a tutor, mediator or peer, rather than creating a prior safe space for learners to surface their individual reasoning or biases ahead of discussion with human partners. While valuable, this design pattern overlooks the possibility of using chatbots as means of preparation for collaboration, rather than as co-participants in it. Here, our teacher participants individually interacted with biased chatbots before group discussion, which allowed them to surface assumptions (preparation), confront partiality (guided failure), and subsequently engage in peer argumentation informed by prior critical reflection. By shifting the role of chatbots from “for collaboration” to “preparation for 23 collaboration”, we examine this under-explored design possibility of using chatbot-mediated individual reflection (human-AI collaboration) to prime richer, more critical peer argumentation (human-human collaboration).

## Participants

We engaged N = 25 primary, secondary, and postsecondary school teachers from Singapore as part of regular coursework at a teacher training institute. The majority of participants had completed a postgraduate diploma in education. Informed, written consent was obtained from n = 20 of the 25 participants. The study was approved by the ethics commission of Nanyang Technological University (IRB-2024-1081).

## Study design

We introduced a novel multi-phase study design (Figure 1), where everyone started out by self-reporting their perceived baseline levels of AI literacy using a five-point Likert scale.  This 21-item survey was adapted from previously validated scales (Lintner, 2024) and captured six complementary subdimensions of (i) knowing and understanding AI (e.g., “When I am unsure about something presented by AI, I check whether it is true or not”), (ii) using and applying AI (e.g., “Depending on the situation, I can choose suitable AI platforms”), (iii) evaluating and creating AI (e.g., “I can evaluate the accuracy and reliability of AI-generated responses”), (iv) ethical dimension of AI (e.g., “I can identify potential ethical issues related to AI use”), (v) social impact of AI (e.g., “I consider the societal effects of AI advancements”), and (vi) detecting AI (e.g., “I can tell if I am interacting with an AI application or a human”). ## Study Design and Relevant Measurements (Mx) Within Each Phase

Subsequently, the focal topic of class discussion was introduced – Is the use of AI / genAI / LLMs justified in educational contexts? Participants were then randomly chosen to interact with one of three custom chatbots that we had designed via backend prompts and a curated knowledge base – TechBot, MarketMind, and EthosBot

– for up to 30 minutes. Drawing on Selwyn (2024), our rationale was to design sufficiently distinct and orthogonal

perspectives, specifically in the form of a technical proponent, a corporate proponent, and a social critic of AI for education. Thus, TechBot  was nudged to maintain an enthusiastic stance towards AI by focusing on t echnical details, engineering advancements, and positive outcomes of AI in educational settings. MarketMind was nudged to maintain a business-minded stance towards AI adoption by focusing on aligning educational outcomes with workforce needs, market demands, and corporate practices. EthosBot was nudged to maintain a cautious and 24 critical stance towards AI by focusing on advocacy for the societal impacts of AI for education (e.g., social justice, equity, etc). In addition to specific instructions relevant to their conversational contexts, all three chatbots were given common guidelines to maintain their personalities, play devil’s advocate when prompted to adopt views on AI that differ from their personalities, and not reveal their personality under any condition. Further, following best practices in prompting for learning (Sharples, 2023; Mollick & Mollick, 2024), all chatbots were given general instructions to vary the conversational rhythm by mixing explanations and probing questions, refrain from presenting long blocks of text and instead aim to have a good back-and-forth, and finally not do the thinking for the human partner but rather build on what they know. Some example interactions were provided as part of the backend prompt for affording extrapolation of expected response styles in the AI’s conversational turns. ChatGPT 4o was used as the design platform for the present work. Authors can be contacted for complete backend prompts. In the next phase, all participants individually completed a 20-minute pre-reflection where they answered three sets of questions. First, their understanding of the stance of the chatbot  (M 1), collected via open-ended questions focusing on the central issue discussed with respect to the discussion topic, the chatbots’ perspective on the issue, along with which of its arguments seemed most convincing in supporting that perspective. Second, their defense of the stance of the chatbot  (M 2), collected again via open-ended questions that probed students on how they would defend the chatbot’s reasoning against criticisms including strategies like refuting or downplaying opposing views and counterarguments. Third, their initial stance on the discussion topic  (M 3), gathered via an open-ended question evaluating how has trying to adopt and defend the chatbot’s perspective influenced their understanding. Participants could refer back to their chatlogs when answering these reflection questions. Everyone subsequently moved to a groupwork phase  (45 minutes). Here, we allowed teachers to form groups of three based on their seating arrangement in class, which organically led to N = 2 groups where everyone had previously interacted only with TechBot, and N = 5 other groups with prior interactions spanning any two (n = 4) or all three chatbots  (n = 1). One group had to be excluded from all analyses due to a lack of consent. We used a worksheet to  scaffold th is collaborative discussion. Every group member first  summarized their own chatbot’s perspective briefly (10 minutes). This was followed by analyzing arguments put forth by each group member (25 minutes), specifically by articulating core claims, evidentiary basis (reasoning), underlying implicit and explicit assumptions, and counterarguments (limitations)  for each line of argument  within a table. We laid out norms of this discussion by indicating that everyone was expected to respectfully challenge ideas and critically analyze arguments, with the goal of exploring different perspectives on an issue  rather than simply winning. Finally, the group as a whole was nudged to note down primary areas of agreement as well as disagreement  (10 minutes), implicitly laying out the expectation that achieving consensus was not necessary.  Participants were required to use different font colors in the worksheet to mark their individual contributions clearly. The final phase of our study design comprised a second post-reflection activity (20 minutes), where everyone answered three sets of questions. First, benefits of interacting with peers (M4), collected via open-ended questions that focused on valuable insights gained from groupwork (e.g., because of a viewpoint that challenged initial understanding) and specific ways in which their understanding evolved due to the discussion. Second, benefits of engaging with chatbots individually prior to collaboration with peers (M 5), gathered via an open-ended question that probed teachers for whether and how their individual AI-assisted preparations contribute d to the stance on the discussion topic (M6) via an open-ended question asking for elaboration of key reasons and evidence supporting their current stance post-groupwork. We ended with a debrief of the entire activity sequence.

## Research questions

RQ1a: How did participants describe their understanding of a biased chatbot’s stance on the learning topic? RQ1b: How did participants describe their attempts to defend the biased chatbot’s stance on the learning topic? RQ2: What was the impact of collaborating with human peers on the evolving understanding of participants? RQ3a: What was participants’ experience of engaging in AI-assisted individual preparation prior to collaboration? RQ3b: How was participants’ own stance on the learning topic affected after interacting with AI and their peers? To answer our research questions focused on human-AI collaboration (RQ1), human-human collaboration (RQ2), and the overall effectiveness of our learning design (RQ3), AI-assisted thematic analysis was employed with varying qualitative data sources as inputs – M 1 for RQ1a, M 2 for RQ1b, M 4 for RQ2, M 5 for RQ3a, and a comparative analysis of M3 and M6 for RQ3b. Initially, generative AI was used to inductively identify themes by segmenting at the speaker-level to capture each teacher’s unique perspective while preserving enough context. A novel three-part prompting framework, drawing on  Sinha (2026), was used, where we started with an (i) 25 exploratory phase, crafting deliberate instructions for the AI to come up with a larger set of key themes in the data (e.g., 5-7) with supportive evidence and justification, followed by, (ii) a focused refinement  phase, again crafting deliberate instructions for the AI to reconsider all themes by thinking through if they were exclusive enough, evaluate and justify the possibility of keeping a smaller more parsimonious set of themes that best explain the data, and subsequently (iii) collaborative refinement, where the smaller set of AI-identified themes were manually evaluated and reorganized by the lead researcher, as a content expert, for validity so as to ensure that the themes were conceptually coherent, theoretically relevant and interpretively meaningful.  A codebook based on the verified themes was then created to involve two independent human raters (blind to the individual-level participant demographics), who applied themes to 20% of the data deductively. Teacher reflections sometimes contained multiple clauses, inherently allowing for double coding – thus, in the case that more than one code was applicable, following one common practice for resolving ambiguities (Chi, 1997), the more dominant code best representing the core meaning of the response was systematically picked. Inter-rater reliability, computed between the expertvalidated themes and thematic coding from two-three independent raters accounting for their dominant code, was deemed acceptable for the various data sources corresponding to RQ1a,  RQ1b, RQ2, RQ3a and RQ 3b (Krippendorff’s 𝛼𝛼 = 0.81, 1, 1, 0.85, and 0.73). Disagreements were resolved by discussion, after which the remaining data was human-coded. GPT 4 o, accessed via Azure OpenAI service, which allowed safeguarding participant privacy by making prompts and anonymized data unavailable outside the analysis context, was used. Conversation logs during initial chatbot interactions comprised M = 17.31 (SD = 11.52) turns, with the average conversation length per turn being M = 195.05 (SD = 116.06) words for the chatbot and M = 23.9 (SD = 20.17) words for the human conversational partner. However, despite this observable human succinctness and AI verbosity,  application of a simple conversational quality metric evaluating  the level of critical analysis in human turns (0 – human accepts AI feedback at face-value without question or reflecting on the AI feedback, e.g., using ok, yes, thank you; 1 – human occasionally questions or reflects on AI feedback without providing a new perspective, e.g., simply reflecting/responding to a question posed by AI; 2 – human regularly assesses AI feedback critically, asks for clarifications or justifications, and provides a new perspective that was not originally prompted by AI; 3 – humandemonstrates deep critical analysis of AI responses by pushing for deeper explanations and seeking an understanding of the rationale behind its suggestions, e.g., by questioning AI feedback), drawing on Sinha (2025a), suggested that participants showed responsible human-AI collaboration. This was seen in the majority of our participant turns (60.6%) being coded into levels 2 and 3, a s coded by two independent raters after achieving Krippendorff’s 𝛼𝛼 > 0.7. Taken together, this suggest s thoughtful engagement during our designed chatbot interaction activity.

Group worksheets during the later peer interaction were coded for two objective and observable measures to evaluate the fidelity of collaboration. First, equality of participation was quantified using the Gini coefficient, which is a widely used measure of statistical dispersion – in our context, this measure would capture how far the distribution of the number of times each group member contributed in the argumentation sub phase with either a claim, reasoning, assumption or counterargument deviates from perfect equality (min. 0, max. 1). On average, the Gini coefficient for our data sample was M = 0.04 (SD = 0.06) across the N = 7 groups, indicating high equality of participation. Second, we also looked at evidence for transactivity during the argumentation subphase by noting down instances in group worksheets where multiple font colors (proxies for different individual contributions) were used as part of (i) either the same core argument thread (e.g., if member A stated the claim, member B stated the assumption for that claim, and member C stat ed the counterargument  for the same claim, three transactive contributions would be counted), or, (ii)  the same claim, reasoning, counterargument, or assumption (e.g., if member A and member B both contribut ed to fleshing out a particular claim, one transactive contribution would be counted). We found that the presence of this higher-order collaborative skill was rarer, at least when we made inferences only from the group worksheets, as only two of the N = 7 groups showed evidence of building on each other’s ideas rather than merely moving their own arguments and perspectives forward. Memos from our in-class observations, though, did suggest participants to be quite active during this argumentation sub-phase, and we posit that time constraints may have prevented transactivity to be accurately reflected in the group worksheets. Understanding of the chatbot’s stance (RQ1a) Our thematic analysis results for RQ1a suggested three core themes  for participants’ foundational understanding of the chatbot behaviors. First, recognition of the chatbot bias, defined as reflecting on how directional biases are 26 presented in the chatbot’s stance either in terms of ethical evidence, economic logic, or techno-optimism, and exemplified via quotes like (i) “Actually at first it felt very efficiency and effective stance but if I would say something which is most convincing would be the Bots perspective being the ‘Evidence-Based Efficacy’ claim – specifically that ITS and AI writing assistants show measurable improvement in student learning outcomes”, (ii) “I don't find the arguments strong because the AI cannot understand what my subject discipline and work requires. It keeps coming from the economic perspective and doesn't take into account other perspectives like ethics and assessment of students”, and (iii) “AI kept trying to monetize the idea, marrying that to the efficacy of the product…starting small at a classroom level, because it is lowest cost (effort), highest learning environment”. Second, recognition of the conditions for making AI a valuable tool, defined as making judgments on how AI is used and whether it is anchored with an intentional pedagogical purpose, and as seen in quotes like  (i) “However, when AI is doing more – teacher’s role will slowly ‘disappear’ and the child might just want to communicate with AI instead of having interaction with human...AI can become a trusted companion…But only teacher can help children translate that trust into real-world relationships”, (ii) “Over-reliance can erode thinking, creativity, and responsibility...AI should support, not replace, human thinking in education...When designed intentionally, they can prompt critical thinking and clarify complex ideas, helping both students and teachers engage more meaningfully with learning”, and (iii) “The teacher must and can adapt alongside with AI...The teacher becomes an experience creator – making use of AI to further supplement areas which may be limited by human ability (e.g., personalization or instant feedback to every student)”. Third and finally, recognition of cultural and contextual specificity for evaluating AI’s role, defined as attempting to question how localization, values and national identity may conflict with AI’s embedded norms, and exemplified via verbalizations like (i) “Quoting the chatbot: I believe Singapore absolutely needs to invest in building its own AI tools for education – but not just for the sake of being self-sufficient. It’s about protecting our values, students, and teachers from being shaped by someone else’s idea of what learning should be” and (ii) “To ensure ethical and pedagogical alignment, educators, students, parents, and education authorities must all be involved in shaping how AI is designed and used in schools. We must prioritize tools that serve learning and inclusivity – not just efficiency”.

We implemented a novel pedagogical approach in authentic higher education classrooms where interaction with biased AI chatbots was designed to prime  teachers for engaging in groupwork with their peers. As a learning design contribution, we provide a pedagogical framework to bring together the complementary affordances of human-AI collaboration and human-human collaboration, while offering targeted reflection and critiquing 29 opportunities to optimize the learning potential from each phase. As a theoretical contribution, we highlight the mechanism of designed cognitive conflict (instantiated in the clash between the biased AI stance and a peer’s counter-bias) and perspective-taking (analysis of multiple flawed viewpoints) to intellectually challenge learners. that teachers recognized the embedded bias we had deliberately designed  for, sometimes finding it compelling, but often challenging their chatbots’ narrow framing or assumptions. Teachers further endorsed a collaborative, augmentative role for AI – where AI acts as a tool, co-teacher, or assistant, and teachers retain final authority. They used an application-oriented lens to judge whether AI’s use is justified and saw pedagogical intentionality and human oversight as essential to making AI use effective. Teachers also demonstrated a nuanced recognition that AI is never value-neutral and culturally-aligned design and governance are key for justified implementation in their context. Beyond simply recognizing the bias, though, we also used reflection question s that pushed teachers to consider various ways of defending the stance of their biased chatbots and making it appear credible. Our rationale was to trigger  critical evaluation, perspective-taking, and deeper processing of how the chatbot’s bias manifests, and have teachers focus on elaborating  a strong defense for that specific viewpoint (by finding evidence and logical-sounding justifications). Results for RQ1b, which focused on supporting the chatbot’s stance while likely filtering out and/or downplaying contradictory information, showed that t eachers conceived their chatbot to be defendable when its advocacy on AI adoption c ould be reframed as professionally responsible, pedagogically beneficial, or operationally pragmatic. Simply put, their chatbot’s stance on AI as a tool (rather than a threat) could be defended when foregrounding how AI appeals to institutional values or classroom impact. In addition to what the chatbot argued, teachers also highlighted how its rhetorical strategy (e.g., responsiveness and openness) often rationalized or softened the bias  put forth. Teachers further focused on normative (valuebased) justifications for the chatbot’s stance based on future-oriented and societal concerns, invoking ethical obligations for preparing students for bias in AI, ensuring cultural or cognitive independence, and protecting moral agency. In summary, these analyses for RQ1a and RQ1b, beyond serving as extended manipulation checks, suggested that our probing questions (administered  through measures M 1 and M2) successfully problematized participants’ understanding. This was crucial to align their personal critical reflections with the biased perspectives of their individual chatbots before beginning groupwork. Maximizing this divergence of opinions was intentional. Moving on to evaluate the impact of groupwork on teachers’ evolving understanding (RQ2), we found in post-groupwork verbalizations (measure M 4) that despite not achieving consensus, peer discussion s may still have played an important role in sharpening o r reinforcing ethical and human-centered concerns inherent in the learning topic, causing epistemic movement  in (or, confirmation to) teachers’ prior beliefs. When teachers were forced to reconcile information from their peers, this presumably moved them beyond simply understanding their own assigned bias to comprehending how different biases can lead to conflicting and equally flawed conclusions. Groupwork thus afforded teachers the opportunity to deconstruct this initial bias as they confronted their peers with similarly well-articulated defenses for opposing, biased positions.  When evaluating their experiences engaging with our learning design of individual work as preparation for collaboration (RQ3a), teachers articulated that they used the chatbot as a learning partner, sometimes transformative and sometimes confirmatory, but always in relation to their own conceptual stance on the learning topic (measure M 5). They elaborated on the dialogic impact of individual preparation too (e.g., how ideas from the chatbot were operationalized into group talk). Few teachers, however, expressed skepticism towards the learning design.  Finally, when looking at how everyone’s stance on the topic was affected (RQ3b) after working with the chatbot (measure M3) and subsequently after integrating learning from the groupwork (measure M 6), we found that for the majority of teachers, their revised positions articulated a more balanced, conditional, and criteria-driven stance relative to earlier, less-specified remarks that may have been driven primarily by interactions with our biased chatbots. The majority moved from tentative or tool-focused takes to qualified endorsement of AI with boundaries, and surfaced principled ethical criteria as prerequisites for AI adoption. Given the moderate-to-high AI literacy in our sample, we interpret these enhancements as epistemically enabled rather purely chatbot exposure-driven. However, for a minority, there still remained a tendency for (partially) rejecting change in stances on the learning topic based on feasibility grounds. For educators, an important implication stemming from the present work is to not give learners AI to find answers, but use AI’s inherent flaws (e.g., their tendency towards bias) to our instructional advantage (e.g., prime conflict / start a debate) in the service of building critical thinking skills and discouraging undue dependence on technology (Sinha, 2025b). Our limitations stem from working with a small teacher sample with relatively high levels of AI literacy. We also focused on an engaging learning topic  of AI adoption in higher education. Future work across other topics is therefore warranted to assess the generalizability and depth of reflections engendered by our proposed learning design.
