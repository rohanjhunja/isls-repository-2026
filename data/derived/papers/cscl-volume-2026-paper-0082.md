# Collaboration Quality in Computational Modeling: Implications for Middle -School STEM+C Learning

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Collaboration is recognized by NGSS as an important 21st-century skill, yet its role in middle-school STEM+C remains underexplored. This study examines how students collaborate while building computational models (CM) of scientific processes and how it relates to learning outcomes. We coded 16 hours of activity from eight dyads across Meier et al.’s nine collaboration dimensions (e.g., mutual understanding, dialogue management, information pooling) and analyzed links between collaboration quality, model performance, and assessment scores. Findings highlight the dynamics of CM collaboration and the need for scaffolds that promote productive interaction and teach collaboration as a transferable skill.

Computational modeling, where students create models of scientific processes by translating scientific knowledge into computational form, has been shown to foster STEM+C learning (Grover and Pea, 2013; Hutchins et al., 2020a). This requires students to identify variables and their relationships, which can be challenging for middle school students still building foundational knowledge (Basu et al., 2016). However, most studies focus on secondary and university students who already have STEM+C backgrounds (Ma  et al., 2022). Collaboration enables students to share information and develop a shared understanding (Snyder et al., 2025), aligning with the social-constructivist view of learning as interaction-driven rather than as individually acquired (Vygotsky, 1978). From a CSCL perspective, productive collaboration involves the co-construction of knowledge through discourse, coordination, and reciprocal engagement (Roschelle & Teasley, 1995). Thus, in computational modeling, learning depends not only on participation but also on the quality of interaction as the students negotiate ideas and build shared representations of scientific processes. The NGSS recognizes both collaboration and computational modeling as essential 21st-century skills (NGSS, 2013). Several CSCL studies analyze collaborative discourse in middle‑school STEM+C contexts. Hong et al.

(2025) found that frequent idea sharing and negotiation were associated with greater gains. Studies focusing on

computational modeling emphasize information pooling and shared understanding (Snyder et al., 2024; 2025), framing collaboration as a transferable skill (Cohn et al., 2025). Notably, these studies focus on older learners, and research on younger students remains underexplored (Ma et al., 2022). Prima ry‑level studies using block‑based coding (e.g., Ardito et al., 2020) focus on computing rather than modeling scientific processes, missing the affordances of collaboration in open‑ended, interdisciplinary problem solving. Thus, a significant gap remains in understanding how middle-school students collaborate to co-construct knowledge as they jointly model computational representations of scientific processes.

In this paper, we analyze 16 hours of video and conversation from eight pairs of middle school students working together in a STEM+C learning environment. Our analysis is guided by a social-constructivist CSCL framing in which learning is mediated through discourse-based processes of shared meaning-making and coconstruction (Vygotsky, 1978; Roschelle & Teasley, 1995). Consistent with this framing, we use Meier et al.’s

(2007) Rating Scheme as an analytic framework to operationalize these interactional processes in students’

collaborative work to address two exploratory research questions: (1) Which dimensions of collaboration characterize middle school students’ work during science-based computational modeling tasks? (2) How does collaboration quality relate to learning outcomes, including modeling performance and assessment scores? WRC curriculum unit The WRC curriculum (Basu et al., 2022) comprises 15 lessons delivered over three weeks of one-hour in-person classes, integrating pen-and-paper activities with computer-based tasks. Focusing on Earth science concepts, such 509 as conservation of mass, rainfall, absorption, and runoff, students examine the problems caused by urban water runoff. They perform hands-on investigations to explore how different surfaces absorb rainwater and produce runoff. Students develop conceptual models to calculate absorption and runoff, then implement them as computational models (CM). In the CM environment, teacher-assigned pairs build models using a block-based, domain-specific modeling language (DSML; Hutchins et al., 2020b). DSML combines domain components (rainfall, surface materials as inputs; absorption, runoff as outputs) with computational thinking practices (decomposition, algorithmic thinking) and computing concepts (variable initialization, conditional logic). After the CM activity, stu dents undertake an engineering challenge to reduce schoolyard runoff within budget and accessibility constraints. Learning outcomes are evaluated through a post-test covering Science, Engineering, and Computational Thinking, complemented by formative assessments. ## Participants and data

This study took place in a middle school (ages 10–12) during science lessons in the southeastern United States. A total of 104 students (51% male, 49% female; 67% White, 14% Black/African American, 11% Asian, 8% Hispanic/Latino) across four class periods participated, taught by two teachers. The study was approved by Vanderbilt University’s IRB and the school district, with informed consent from parents and students. We collected students’ assessment data, along with time‑aligned audio, video, screen captur es, and interaction logs from the CM environment. Instructional approaches differed: Teacher 1 (one class period) employed a scaffolded, step‑by‑step method; Teacher 2 (three class periods) used a flexible, student‑driven approach. We focused on Teacher 2’s classroom to explore authentic collaboration in open‑ended problem-solving and its connection to learning outcomes. Absences during CM sessions or assessments led to incomplete datasets, resulting in a final sample of eight pairs, with two one-hour sessions, formative assessments, and post-tests. To analyze the video data, we segmented it using time-aligned interaction logs at each model test run. This yielded 80 segments (M=10, SD=5.2), lasting 15 seconds to 13.5 minutes (M=3:00, SD=2:56). Segments were deductively coded to map onto Meier et al.’s (2007) nine collaboration dimensions using the codebook described in Table 1. Meier et al.’s scheme was selected because it operationalizes collaboration as observable interactional processes, including shared understanding, reciprocity, coordination, and joint task engagement. These interaction processes can be linked to social constructivist theory constructs that emphasize the use of language (communication) as a mediating tool to develop shared understanding, co-construct knowledge, and coordinate shared tasks to facilitate collaborative learning. This makes the framework well-suited for examining how discourse quality mediates learning in collaborative computational modeling. The codebook was iteratively refined by two researchers, resolving discrepancies through discussion, establishing inter-rater reliability (Cohen’s κ = 0.95). Each code was mapped to a discrete event, and a rationale was recorded. To answer RQ1, we conducted frequency analyses and computed descriptive statistics on the assigned codes to identify which aspects of collaboration were most prominent in students’ conversations. For RQ2, we computed a Collaboration Quality Score by adding the percentage frequencies across collaboration dimensions, and analyzed its relation to students’ CM, formative assessment (FA), and post-test CT scores:

Frequency analysis of the 80 coded segments revealed distinct patterns in students’ collaborative behaviors during the computational modeling activity (RQ1). Students exhibited strong task-oriented behavior (see full table in Supplementary Materials (1)), with Individual Task Orientation observed in 81.3% and 92.5% for Student 1 and Student 2, respectively. A substantial proportion of student dialogue involved Information Pooling (30%), particularly during the initial stages of model construction, as students frequently consulted their notebooks for instructions to guide their work. Evidence of Sustaining Mutual Understanding appeared in approximately 25% of our dataset, reflecting efforts to maintain shared meaning. In contrast, Dialogue Management  (10%) and Reaching Consensus (8.75%), which are central to productive collaboration, were relatively infrequent. Task Division was also rare (11.25%), as students were encouraged to build the computational model together. However, students frequently alternated control of the laptop, reflecting Technical Coordination (20%). Time Management was also seldom observed (6.25%), perhaps because the teacher and researchers externally regulated pacing.  The codes Dialogue Management, Reaching Consensus, Task Division, and  Time Management  each appeared in fewer than 10 segments; hence, they were excluded from subsequent analysis. RQ2: How does collaboration quality relate to individual learning outcomes? To examine how collaboration quality relates to learning outcomes, we calculated Pearson’s correlations between the collaboration dimensions (Sustaining Mutual Understanding, Information Pooling, Technical Coordination, Reciprocal Interaction,  and Individual Task Orientation) and the three performance measures: CM score, debugging task score, and post-test CT score.  See details in Supplementary Materials (1). 511 Among pairs that completed the CM, at least one partner was always engaged (Individual Task Orientation = 100%). The average Individual Task Orientation was strongly correlated with CM score: r=0.69. Sustaining Mutual Understanding and Reciprocal Interaction were also positively associated with CM score (r=0.43 and r=0.34, respectively), whereas Information Pooling (r=0.13) and Technical Coordination (r=-0.06) had minimal influence. The aggregated Collaboration Quality Score (see Methods) was moderately cor related with CM score (r=0.42), highlighting the role of collaborative processes in science-based modeling tasks. Good collaboration was also conducive to promoting students’ individual learning, both in the debugging task (r=0.58), reflecting a more immediate, domain-proximal association, and in the post-test (r=0.54), reflecting a longer-term association. Sustaining Mutual Understanding  was strongly correlated with average debugging score (r=0.73) and post-test (r=0.59). Similarly, Information Pooling (r=0.49 with debugging;  r=0.51 with post-test) and Reciprocal Interaction (r=0.58 with debugging; r=0.50 with post-test) were also positively associated. In contrast, Task Orientation  showed only a moderate association with assessments: r=0.34 for debugging and r=0.24 for the post-test. These findings reinforce that shared sense-making processes: Sustaining Mutual Understanding, Information Pooling, and Reciprocal Interaction, are tightly coupled with conceptual learning in collaborative computational modeling activity.

## Case study: The Podcast Boys

This case study examines a pair of students, “Podcast Boys”, an intriguing instance where low collaboration quality coincided with notable immediate, domain-proximal learning, but limited transfer to post-test questions. During the CM activity, the students humorously pretended to record a podcast of their work, speaking aloud to an “audience” rather than engaging in Reciprocal Interaction. Only S2 was engaged throughout (Individual Task Orientation = 100%) and completed the model alone (Technical Coordination = 0%). The brief instances of Sustaining Mutual Understanding occurred only at the beginning and end of the activity, both times when the students explained their progress to the imagined audience. As such, both students performed equally on the Debugging Task (6 points each), suggesting a shared understanding of the  final model; however, this did not transfer, as S2 scored 9 points on the post-test, while S1 scored only 3 (see our Supplementary Materials (1)). While the students’ “podcast” approach fostered continuous verbalization and externalization—a practice that can support metacognitive awareness (Schraw & Dennison, 1994)—it fell short of genuine collaboration, which requires reciprocal contributions, negotiation of meaning, and shared problem-solving (Roschelle & Teasley, 1995). This case highlights that externalization alone is insufficient and that effective collaboration requires mutual engagement and the co-construction of understanding.

Our findings extend the growing body of research on collaboration by illuminating how middle school students engage in and benefit from collaborative practices during science-based modeling tasks. Theoretically, this study deepens social constructivist accounts of CSCL by illustrating how collaboration quality is manifested through observable discourse processes rather than participation frequency. The prominence of Sustaining Mutual Understanding, Information Pooling, and Reciprocal Interaction, and their strong associations with learning outcomes, reinforces the idea that conceptual learning in computational modeling emerges through socially mediated sense-making. Our results emphasize that effective collaboration extends beyond technical coordination and task division; it involves reciprocity and the co-construction of ideas (Roschelle & Teasley, 1995; Snyder et al., 2025). The relative absence of higher-order collaborative behaviors —such as Reaching Consensus  and Dialogue Management—highlights that at the  middle school level, collaboration skills require explicit cultivation (Barron, 2003). This calls for designing explicit scaffolds for promoting collaboration in the learning environment through teacher prompts, interface features, or task structures. This study is not without limitations. The multimodal data collection enabled fine-grained analysis, but introduced data loss: missing streams or student absences during the longitudinal sequence of lessons or assessments, rendering the dyad’s data unusable. Consequently, only eight dyads were retained. Our findings are also correlational and exploratory, aimed at characterizing collaborative processes and their relationship with learning outcomes. However, our preliminary results point to embedding structured opportunities for students to practice and refine their collaborative strategies within computational modeling tasks to support STEM+C learning, with collaboration serving as both a process and an outcome of learning in technology-enhanced learning environments. Furthermore, with recent advances in LLMs and generative AI, we candevelop agents to support the development of effective collaborative processes that, in turn, scaffold learning.
