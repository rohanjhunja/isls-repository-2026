# Value and Challenges in Using a Collaborative Critical Reading and Learning Analytics System: A Cross-Case Analysis of Two High Schools

**Conference:** ISLS 2023

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
WiREAD+ is an augmented web-based collaborative critical reading and learning analytics environment that was developed to scaffold and engage students in collaborative dialogue around online texts. This paper reports on the trial of WiREAD+ for Grade 9 students in two high schools – School 1 (S1) with prior experience and School 2 (S2) that was new to the intervention design. We report on a cross-case analysis of the two schools, focusing on perceived ease of use and usefulness for learning, and reflect on the value and challenges of enacting WiREAD+ across schools of varying prior experience. Drawing from the findings of this cross-case analysis, we consider how we can support the wider adoption and deployment of the system across schools and settings by highlighting obstacles that new participants who might trial such new learning analytics systems might face.

## Introduction

In today’s digital age with a proliferation of fake news and dis/misinformation, th e ability to read critically is an essential skill for students. In CSCL, one of the research priorities (Wise & Schwarz, 2017) is to develop learning analytics tools to provide learners with process feedback on their collabor ative learning. WiREAD+ is an augmented web-based collaborative critical reading and learning analytics environment that scaffolds and engages students in collaborative dialogue around online texts (Koh et al., 2022; Tan et al., 2017). Using a design-based research process with teachers, specialists from the education ministry, education researchers and web developers as co-designers, WiREAD+ was trialed in seven schools. Of which the da ta from two high schools (secondary schools) are reported in this paper. The two schools had the whole Grade 9 (Secondary 3) level use the system as part of their English Language curriculum but one had prior experience with the inter vention design while the other was new to it. The guiding research question for this study is: to what ext ent do the augmentations in WiREAD+ improve its scalability readiness (operationalized in terms of perceived ease of use and usefulness for learning) across multiple schools and contexts? We investigated the ease  of use and perceived usefulness for learning of students and teachers in the two schools and performed a cross-case analysis using the quantitative and qualitative data collected. Key implications on the usefulness of such tools, as well as the value and challenges, especially for new participants, are reported. Notably, the tool e ncouraged collaborative knowledge building amongst the students and increased their sense of learner agency. Not withstanding, challenges exist in the initial usability learning curve as well as providing guidance and support for all students to use the system. The WiREAD+ intervention design WiREAD+ is an intervention design underpinned by four embedded learning and pedagogical fr ameworks: the multiliteracies pedagogical framework, dialogic learning, assessment for lea rning, and computer-supported collaborative learning (See Tan et al., 2017). The techno-pedagogical system can be  summed up in terms of two key learning affordances: (i) multimodal social dialogic learning with metacognitive  prompts, and (ii) dynamic visual learning analytics dashboard.

Our study is part of a larger project, and this paper focuses on two high schools – S1 and S2 who both decided to use WiREAD+ for their whole Grade 9 level. S1 was the partne r school of the project and had been involved in prior studies of a similar intervention before the current study. S2 was a new school that was interested to trial the current intervention design. See Table 1 for enactment details of the current study for S1 and S2.

## Methodology

To investigate the ease of use and perceived usefulness of students and teachers, quantitative and qualitative data was collected and analyzed. For the quantitative data, a self-report questionnaire pre-and post-intervention was CSCL 2023 Proceedings  © ISLS 234 administered to measure students’ (i) perceived ease of use of the system (post-only; 1 item) (Brooke, 1996), (ii) overall perceived usefulness of the system (post-only; 8 items), and perceived usefulness for learning in terms of (iii) deep learning (8 items) (Biggs, Kember, & Leung, 2001) and (iv) critical reading ability (9 items) (Tan et al., 2017). These scales demonstrated good internal reliability, with Cronbach alpha score s ranging from.87 to.98. The quantitative data were analyzed via descriptive and inferential statis tics. Sample items are: ease of use (5point scale, Strongly disagree to Strongly agree): I thought WiREAD+ was easy to use; overall usefulness (7-point scale, Strongly disagree to Strongly agree): WiREAD+ is useful for learning at my own pace; deep learning (7point scale, Never to Always true): I find new texts interesting and spend extra time trying to learn more about them; critical reading ability (7-point scale, Never to Always true): I know how to explain my personal responses. ## Quantitative analysis: Descriptives and t-tests

For ease of use, students from S1 tended to agree with the statement (M=2.7, SD=.91) more than students from S2 (M=2.29, SD=1.02). Similarly, the overall perceived usefulness rating was higher for S1 (M=5.46, SD=1.06) than for S2 (M=5.00, SD=1.15). Thematic analysis The thematic analysis focused on the value and challenges of the system in relation to the ease of use and perceived usefulness of WiREAD+.

Initial usability learning curves While students from both schools found WiREAD+ “straightforward” (S1 student) or “organized” (S2 student), many highlighted an initial learning curve to know how to navigate the system and make use of the functions. An S1 student shared, “After some time and some troubleshooting, I was able to figure out how to use [WiREAD+] quite easily and how to navigate the site with ease”. S2 students, perhaps bein g newer to the system, shared that they wanted more technical help from their teacher although a few others s aid they could manage on their own. Teachers in both schools, while generally agreeing that the platform was easy to use, also raised concerns over this initial learning curve for both the students and themselves. Teachers suggested adding a help button/chat for technical support, particularly for remote learning and to provide time for students to learn how to use the system. Uneven student dashboard usage but dashboard encouraged self-evaluation Students had different experiences with their student dashboard. Many students appreciated the student dashboard and the information provided on their progress for self-evaluation. S1 students highlighted that it is “helpful because it gives a summary for what I have done during the lesson” and another elaborated that it provided “an insight into which few tags I constantly used and which ones I can try to e xpand and try using the next time”. Some S2 students were unaware how to use it for self-evaluation due to le ss emphasis by their teachers. Nevertheless, several S2 students explored it on their own and used it to refle ct on their learning. For instance, a S2 student shared “while I was [replying] … I decided to check it [das hboard] … to provide me with …  an effective overview of what I've been missing out on … and where to actually improve...”. Another S2 student also said that it was “easy to see the data".

Critical thinking promoted though tagging Several students and all teachers in both schools felt that the tagging function of WiREAD+ of critical lenses and collaborative talk types helped to promote and organize students’ thinking. Students were mindful of what tags to use and highlighted that the tags “open us to different ways of creating our opini ons” (S2 student). Similarly, a student from S1 shared that the tags “make me think more... it can broaden my perspective”. Still, some students, especially S2 students, indicated that they were unsure of or confused about tags. Some chose the easiest or familiar tags, and several students did not clarify with the teacher about wha t the tags meant. We had noted that all teachers explained the tags to students at the beginning and some even provide d printed out examples for the students. Teachers shared that they observed mindless use of tags by some  students and that more guidance is needed to support these students.

Collaborative knowledge building but with selected peers In both schools, collaborative knowledge building amongst students in their discussion wa s apparent. Students replied to each other and their interactions helped them to better understand the  content and gain “alternative perspectives”. One shared that WiREAD+ was a “very good platform to collaborate with classmates and share viewpoints, opening up to others and getting to understand the context of an article or situation better.” Build-ons frequently occurred too. For example, a student reflected that they “could share or somewhat elaborate more on the response that they [peers] give...".  Still, some students admitted to replying to selected peers only e.g., close friends, as they were more comfortable with them. Students also confessed that, with their friends, there was less disagreement and some were reluctant to voice a countering opinion to their friend. A suggestion for improvement was to allow for anonymous replies.

Increased student agency as well as requiring further teacher facilitation Interestingly, in both schools, students reported higher learner agency and some students appreciated the teacher’s feedback given to them in the system through their comments, likes and badges. Students highlighted that lessons are “more self-directed rather than being taught by a teacher, because you have to annotate and learn by ourselves rather than like listening to the teacher teach.” Still, students maintained that the teacher’s role was key for them. ## Discussion and conclusion

The cross-case analysis of the schools reveals some differences related to the ease of use and usefulness perceptions of participants. Some challenges were expected as students  in S2 were new to WiREAD+. This unfamiliarity explains some of the difficulties reported in technical use, the  lesser use of the learner dashboard, and students’ confusion in tag usage. These challenges are also reflected in S2’s quantitative results – S2 usability scores were lower than S1, and the delta change in deep learning was non-significant, and critical reading ability decreased significantly (possibly due to the confusion about tag use). What was less expected was the larger set of commonalities found across both schools which included the recognition of the value in the system in facilitating collaborative knowledge build ing, increasing student agency, and promoting critical thinking. This suggests that the underpinning design of the intervention has benefits. S1 students also showed higher usability scores and gains in pre-post self-reports, which could be due to the longer time of enactment (1 more session) as well as greater familiarity with the tags. These findings suggest that further lesson time is needed for participants to overcome the challenge s in using WiREAD+ to reap the potential gains.

Drawing from the findings of this cross-case analysis, we consider how w e can support the wider adoption and deployment of the system across schools and settings by highlighting obstacles that new participants trialing such new learning analytics systems might face. Longer onboarding time c an be planned for new participants and the use of the learner dashboard can be more scaffolded in the lesson. Further research can tease out learner dashboard preferences and effectiveness, as well as the te acher’s role a nd tool use. Besides the limitation of not having a control condition, and brief details due to wordage in this paper (though supplemented by further references), we have reported on the perceived usability and usefulness of an innovative learning analytics system in CSCL. As found from our study, the participants were actively engaged with the collaborative knowledge building process and we hope that such techno-pedagogical advances will cont inue to contribute towards the development of the critical thinking skills in our students.
