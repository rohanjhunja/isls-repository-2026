# Developing Productive Classroom Dialogue Using Analytics-Supported Video-Based Approach for Teacher Learning

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study investigates the role of a video-based teacher professional development approach supported by a visual learning analytics tool, Classroom Discourse Analyzer (CDA), for helping teachers to understand and to enrich their classroom dialogue in the teacher education context. Data were drawn from three teacher educators and their pre-service student teachers (n = 52) spanning over six months on enriching classroom dialogue. Teacher educators met regularly in six teacher meetings to discuss and reflect on classroom dialogue using video clips enriched by CDA visualizations. Coding of classroom talk using accountable productive talk moves indicates changes in teachers’ increased use of productive classroom dialogue moves. Qualitative analysis of teacher reflection and discussion illustrated how CDA supported teachers in noticing their own and student discourse, using dialogue moves as the framework for reflection, and developing deeper meanings of dialogue using CDA as objects of inquiry. Implications of the designed analytics-supported video-based teacher professional development approach are discussed.

## Introduction

Developing productive classroom dialogue is a major research theme and gaining significance in light of changing global and educational contexts for dialogical educ ation (Mercer et al., 2019). While research on clas sroom dialogue is well established (Howe & Abedin, 2013), teachers often meet challenges in orchestrating productive classroom discourse in teaching and learning (Chen et al., 2020). Compared with the traditional initia tionresponse-follow-up (IRF) pattern (Sinclair & Coulthard, 1975), productive classroom dialogue requires teachers to encourage students to engage in dialogic interac tion-practice by sharing, build-on, arguing, elabor ating and reflecting in the classroom discussions. To help te achers conduct productive classroom dialogue, resea rch into teacher professional development (TPD) has given su bstantial attention to using classroom videos to su pport teacher learning and teacher collaboration has played a vital role in these video-based TPD programmes (Beisiegel et al, 2018; Shrien & van Es, 2009). With the advances in technology, the use of video-based TPD supported by teacher collaboration has been recognized as a major approach for teacher learning in the learning sciences. This paper presents an analytics-supported video-based a pproach that aims to help teachers and students fos ter productive classroom dialogue.

The social-cultural theory emphasizes social interaction as a key mediator to support learning (Pifarré & Staarman, 2011). Increased attention has been paid in recent years to the notion of productive classroom dialogue for mediating joint understanding. For example, Mercer (2008) identified exploratory talk as productive dialogue through which students engage in collective knowled ge construction in the form of critical and constru ctive building on each other’s ideas. Michaels et al. (2008) discussed accountable talk, which is a productive form of discourse emphasizing accountability to the learnin g community, accurate knowledge, and rigorous think ing. Productive classroom dialogue, as a form of talk, is well examined to be effective for students’ learning (Howe et al., 2019; Murphy et al., 2009). Given the importan t role of productive classroom dialogue, an overarc hing question is proposed for educators that how to help teachers learn and foster productive dialogue in classrooms. There has been considerable research interest in strategies training for teachers in the TPD programs, in turn, to orchestrate productive dialogue with students in th e classrooms (e.g., Chen & Chan, 2022; Chen et al. 2020; Pehmer et al., 2015).

Although video-recording of classroom dialogue is a  central tool in TPD to help teachers to reflect on classroom teaching, challenges still exist due to the large amounts of classroom dialogue data that teachers must process, especially to identify useful video clips and episodes. Different approaches have been taken to address the problem such as teachers selecting their video clips and collaborative discussion among teachers (Sherin & van Es, 2009). In recent years, researchers have developed visualization and analytics tools to help teachers notice and identify salient classroom events. Specifically, the Classroom Discourse Analyzer (CDA), a visual learning analytics tool (Chen, 2020), was developed to visua lize classroom dialogue using analytics data. Using  CDA, teachers can navigate and synchronize selected clas sroom events (video clips) with classroom talk (tra nscripts) and productive dialogic moves (dashboard analytics)  (Figure 1). CDA has different affordances, includi ng ICLS2022 Proceedings 863 © ISLS multiple representations, interactive visualization, and contextualized evidence. The CDA platform pro vides strategies for solving the difficulties teachers meet in reflecting on and analyzing classroom dialogue, including those related to large datasets, coding, visualization, and tracking and comparison (Chen, 2020). Note: Bubble size represents the number of words in a turn Current research on classroom dialogue spans different educational levels and subjects and has mostly examined in-service teacher learning from K-12 leve ls. It is also important to examine the development  of a dialogical approach among teacher educators, as the y are modeled for pre-service teachers whose experi ence would aid future school transformation. In particul ar, the focus in teacher education is often placed on subject matter knowledge and pedagogical and teaching skills, rather than collaborative and dialogical learning. Jurow et al. (2012) discussed the need for incorporating a l earning sciences lens in teacher education, noting that a dialogical approach would be fruitful. This study was conducted in a teacher education setting to examine teacher change towards developing productive classroom dialogue. Specifically, its goal was to integrate the development of a visual learning analytic tool to help teachers  orchestrate academically productive talk moves in classroom teaching. Specifically, two research questions were addressed: (1) Did the teacher educators increase their use of academically productive talk moves, and what characterized these productive dialogic moves? And (2) How did teacher educators employ visualization and analytic s information from CDA to promote their understandi ng of academically productive talk in the classroom?

## Research context and participants

Data were drawn from three teacher educators and their preservice student teachers (n = 52) who participated in a six-month programme for developing productive cla ssroom dialogue for teacher development. The group consisted of eight university teachers who met once  a month to share their practices in teaching a pre-service teacher education course on Educational Inquiry. Other teachers also joined the discussion at different times, but these three teachers opted to make a more focused i nquiry into their own practice, including video ana lysis supported by CDA. Altogether, six teacher meetings were held, including discussing the classroom dialo gue scheme adapted from the accountable talk (Table 1) (Michaels et al., 2008), teachers sharing video clips of their classroom teaching, and using CDA to examine and interpret classroom dialogue. Data sources and analysis ICLS2022 Proceedings 864 © ISLS Teacher educators’ classroom dialogue moves were examined using two classroom videos obtained from each of the three teachers during the early and late phases of intervention (TPD discussion on classroom dialogue). There were six classroom videos, with each class lasting 120 minutes. The classroom transcripts were analyze d and coded using the adapted “academically productive talk” (APT) scheme (Chen et al., 2020; Michaels et al., 2008; Resnick et al., 2010). Table 1 shows the five main talk types: sharing their own ideas (elaborating), deepening their reasoning (reasoning), listening carefully to  one another (listening), and engaging with others’  ideas (thinking with others). In addition, based on a theory-and data-driven approach, we added a new type – reflecting with others (reflecting). A second rater coded 20% of the data and the inter-rater reliability was.87  (Cohen’s kappa; Cohen, 1968). Teacher discussions in TPD mee tings were transcribed and qualitative analyses wer e conducted to examine salient events and themes on how CDA supported teacher learning. The CDA-analytics-enriched discussion of students’ dialogue patterns enriched teachers’ noticing. ## Conclusion and implications

The study has examined how a group of teacher educa tors improved their use of academically productive talk moves after participating in an analytics-supported, video-based, teacher professional development pro ject. Video-based teacher professional development is now  a major approach to teacher learning in the learni ng sciences (Chen et al., 2020; Sherin & van Es, 2009). Concurrently there is also considerable research development in using analytics information to support teaching and learning in classrooms (Wise et al., 2021; Wise & Schwartz, 2017). This study integrated the use of video-suppo rted TPD with visualization and analytics informati on to address the issue of overwhelming data and selectio n problems in using classroom videos. Specifically, the use of video-based analytics approach is developed in t he area of helping teachers employ productive class room dialogue and the use of visualization and analytics would be particularly helpful. Results of the study using coding of classroom dialogue indicate that teachers increased their use of academically productive talk moves after the intervention, especially for elaborating and thinking with others  talk types. At a more general level, teacher educators and student teachers changed the amount of total talk in the classroom with student teachers increasing their engagement in classroom talk. Analysis of teacher reflections and discussions at teacher meetings identified several key themes, including how CDA can support t he noticing of current and changing teacher and stu dent practices, facilitate their understanding and enactment of dialogical moves via providing a systematic framework, and identify how different CDA representations provide shared objects of inquiry for developing deeper collective inquiry into the nature and meaning of productive dialogue. This study contributes to the literature on video-based supported teacher learning highlighting the r ole of visualization and analytics-supported reflection, with learner-generated information (classroom talk) provided to participants for them to reflect on their ongoin g inquiry for making changes to their current practice. The integration of technology and pedagogy and analytics in CDA helps provide different possibilities. Many studies show the important role of classroom dialogue and t his study also shows how the approach may support t he development of dialogic practice. This study is of particular significance, in investigating how teachers could be helped to overcome video data selection problems by harnessing analytics information on their classroom dialogue and providing objects of shared inquiry for understanding and enacting dialogic practice.
