### Abstract

This study developed an object-based learning approach that utilizes dark heritage sites and nuclear experiments for cross-disciplinary learning. ## Introduction

Peace museums, which relates to  dark heritage, can offer as learning  materials to foster ethical thinking in education for peace, playing a crucial role in the design and establishment of a sustainable, peac eful society. The author presents a case study of object-based learning among Japanese students using dark h eritage sites and objects from nuclear experiments. This study examines the pedagogical potential of object-based learning with dark heritage and objects, including radio-exposed objects, to foster ethical thinking for a peaceful society. Dark heritage, objects, and object-based learning The term dark heritage refers to locations such as postwar concentration campsites (e.g., Auschwitz) or nuclear power plants after accidents (e.g., Chernobyl). Sim ilar terms, including contested heritage, complex heritage, and negative heritage, have been used to describe such sites (Koskinen-Koivisto & Thomas, 2016; Thomas et al., 2019). Dimitrovski et al. (2017) argue that he ritage interpretations in historical contexts vary across countries and regions. Thus, it is impossible to claim that heritage conservation universally regarded as a social or public treasure. In addition to the heritage sit es themselves, the museums, monuments, and memorial parks associated with them are included as dark heritage sites. Recently, there has been a growing trend towards using dark heritage as learning material to foster ethical thinking in education for peace. Object-based learning is a powerful pedagogy in which lear ners physically engage with  museum objects. It enables learners to obtain both subject-specific and cross-disciplinary knowledge, including observational, practical, and other transferable sk ills through multisensory engagement with museum collections and objects (Chatterjee & Hannan, 2015). A s the largest object on Earth, heritage can also be included as learning material in object-based learning. Dark heritage studies have recently become an emerging field of critical cultural heritage research and have gained increasing attention. (Koskinen-Koivisto & Thomas, 2016; Thomas et al., 2019). In addition, object-based learning has been demonstrated as an efficacious approach for cross-disciplinary learning, fostering encounters, collis ions with, and tolerance for differing perspective among students from different  disciplines, thereby achieving cross-disciplinary understanding (Chatterjee & Hannan, 2015; Simpson, 2023). However, with a few exceptions, object-based learning as a cross-disciplinary learning method has yet to be widely incorporated into the Japanese educational system (Tanabashi, 2021, 2022).

## Pedagogy and methodology

Peace museums in Japan include the Hiroshima Peace Memorial Museum, the Nagasaki Atomic Bomb Museum, and the Okinawa Peace Memorial Museum. Addi tionally, although not situated in a war field, the DaigoFukuryu Maru Exhibition Hall (Figure 1), which depicts the suffering of victims of nuclear experiments, can also be considered such a museums.

Nuclear war, represented by the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki, w as both intentional and aggressive. In contrast, nuclear experiments may not always be deliberate or aggressive, but are often conducted to enhance nuclear weapons performance and carry the inhumanity of being used for human experimentation to assess the effects of nuclear radiation. For example, the Bikini Incident, —known for the radiation exposure of the Daigo Fukuryu Maru, a tuna-fishing ship—was an unethical human experimentation that disregarded  humandignity and rights at the nuclear experiment site on Bikini Atoll in the Marshall Islands. The indigenous people of the islands were not only forcibly displaced from their homes but were also subjected to experiments to measure the effects of radiation on the human body. The exposure to radiation faced by indigenous people near nuclear experiment sites, as well as surrounding residents and workers, is particularly concerning because they are among the most vulnerable members of society. This risk also extends to workers in uranium mines or factories.

Conversely, advancements in nuclear engineering hav e played an important role in  the quality of human life, particularly through nuclear power gene ration and nuclear medicine used in diagnostic radi ology. Fostering ethical thinking in education for peace is crucial to addressing the complexities of nuclea r engineering.

## Practice and results

In this study, object-based learning was developed with curators, focusing on dark heritage sites and objects in the Daigo Fukuryu Maru Exhibition Hall. The exhibition showcased  artifacts related to the hydrogen bomb damage, the crew's medical condition, nuclear conta mination in the Pacific Ocean, and the global impac t of nuclear experiment. Discussions were conducted with 18 students from diverse disciplines, including natural sciences, humanities, and social sciences, between May and September 2024.  The author surveyed students’ feelings, understanding and satisfaction through object-based learning using these artifacts. Some of the results are as follows.

## Conclusion

It is essential to provide opportunities for cross-disciplinary learning using dark heritage sites and objects to consider and acknowledge humandignities and rights  through reflection of human behavior. To design and establish a sustainable, peaceful society, this approach is necessary for engaging with across diverse disciplines to prompt to foster ethical thinking amid the uncertainty of the Anthropocene.
