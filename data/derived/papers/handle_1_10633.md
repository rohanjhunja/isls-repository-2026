# Preparing Graduate Students to Enact Equity-Oriented Pedagogies for Science Outreach Programs

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Informal Science, Technology, Engineering, and Math (STEM) outreach programs have been promoted as a mechanism to redress under-representation of minoritized students in STEM fields. However, few studies have investigated how to support program leaders and implementers, who are often graduate students, to teach science for equity and justice. In this design-based research study, we explore how professional development (PD), grounded in situated teacher learning theories, facilitated life science graduate students’ development of a multilevel, equity-oriented pedagogy for a science outreach program with minoritized youth. Through qualitatively analyzing PD video recordings, interviews, and artifacts for evidence of graduate students’ attention to micro, meso, and macro levels of equitable science education, we found that situated PD challenged their baseline conceptions of equity work and aligned their multilevel pedagogical praxis. Our study contributes to scholarship on how to support informal science educators’ learning towards equity-oriented pedagogies.

## Introduction

Informal STEM outreach programs for youth have been promoted as a mechanism to redress under-representation of minoritized students in STEM fields (Alcéna-Stiner & Markowitz, 2020). Though developed by STEM faculty, these programs are often implemented by graduate students (Ufnar et al., 2012). Studies show positive outcomes for graduate students teaching in STEM outreach pro grams. Laursen and colleagues (2012) identified thr ee benefits: (1) specialized knowledge and pedagogical  skills neede d to succeed in STEM; (2) direct involvement with professional practices, like developing teachi ng statements; and (3) personal investment in the p rofession, particularly through developing confidence as scientists and educators. Similarly, Kompella and colleagues (2020) found that graduate students who participated in a youth science workshop reported improved science communication skills, which they attributed to simp lifying their research to effectively teach it to y ouths. While research does invest igate how professional development (PD) supports gr aduate students as teaching assistants (e.g., Gallardo-Williams & Petrovich, 2017; Rivera, 2021) or outreach facilitators (e.g., Thiry et al., 2007), equityand justice-oriented PD for graduate-student (outreach) educators is under researched (Sarju & Jones, 2022). Equitable STEM education that redresses educational  injustices requires a shift from traditional pedagogies. For over a decade, P-12 science education has prioritized learning that engages youths in investigating phenomena, rather than receiving lectures o r following recipe-like experiments (NRC, 2012). This approach differs from pedagogies graduate students often exp erience, which conflate high-quality teaching with transmitting knowledge to uneducated students (Gardner & Parrish, 2019). These transmission-based pedagogies promote inequitable learning, especially for minori tized youth, by limiting students’ agency for meani ngfully constructing and applying knowledge (Freire, 2005). Further, equity-oriented science educators must grapple with their own identities and histories vis-à-vis STEM, and the historical and institutional factors that create educational injustices (Morales-Doyle, 2017). We draw on Burgess & Patterson Willia ms’ (2022) framework for realizing equity-oriented science education that identifies factors across three levels: (1) a micro level encompasses intrapersonal and interpersonal factors like identi ty, language, and relational dynamics; (2) a meso level encompasses classroom factors like climate, instruc tion, and curriculum; and (3) a macro level encompasses broader sociopolitical factors including institutio nal, axiological, and ideological influences. We ap ply this framework both to our analysis of participant learning and PD design. Our PD design leverages situated theories of learning in which learning is applied to a real-world context (Putnam & Borko, 2000). We define teacher learning as changes in (1) conceptualizing how students lear n; and

(2) professional practices of teaching and curricul um design within a co-constructive learning community (Horn

& Little, 2010). Our PD design is guided by the need for educators to take concrete steps towards applying theories about justice to pedagogical practices, rather than only addressing their beliefs (Shah & Coles, 2020). Altogether, PD that aims to develop science educators’ equity-oriented pedagogies must support them to anticipate and disrupt educational inequities within everyday pedagogical activities. This study investigates how intentionally designed PD workshops can support graduate students in developing equity-oriented science pedagogies across micro, meso, and macro levels. Specifically, we address the following question: how does situated PD design  facilitate graduate students’ development of an eq uityoriented pedagogical praxis for a science outreach program for minoritized youths? We present cases of  two graduate students across two years of part icipation in the PD, and analyze their interviews, participation, and curricular designs to understand how their equity-oriented pedagogies complexify over time.

## Methods

We leverage design-based research (DBR; Cobb et al., 2003), an iterati ve interventionist approach that investigates processes of learning and means to support it. DBR allowed us to articulate the PD guiding principles and iteratively refine the design for facilitating and analyzing participants’ learning. Participants were graduate students in a life science program (public, R1 university in New England) who wanted to teach minoritized youths in a science outreach program coordinated by Adler.

Each PD iteration (Sum22 and Sum23) was a 5-week summer workshop series guided by three main principles: (1) learning is social, situated in sociopolitical contexts, and distributed across individuals and artifacts;

(2) youths learn science through investigating phen omena and refining thinking through discussions; and (3)

equity-oriented teaching leverages youths’ assets and disr upts oppressive institutional factors. Each series ended in a capstone to design, implement, and refine a 13-week equity-oriented life-science outreach curriculum. The first iteration (Sum22, seven participants) focused  primarily on elicit ing youths’ ideas, recognizing their conceptual and linguistic resources, and engaging them in authentic science practices like explanatory modeling. The second iteration (Sum23, seven participants, five returning) focused primarily on developing science lessons that connected to the lived experiences and long-term goals of youths and their communities. Graduat e students also completed a science equity audit to assess how existing lessons successfully built on communities’ histories and efforts towards liberation (Braaten et al., 202 2), addressing questions like “How are stories of h arms or consequences of STEM portrayed in your curriculum?”.

Data come from three streams. First, we recorded 45-minute semi-structured interviews throughout the first and second year focused on participants’ experiences with teaching and their understanding of equity-oriented science teaching; these were conducted before the s tart of the PD and soon after. Second, we video-recorded weekly PD sessions where participants engaged in di scussions, curriculum co-design, and teaching rehearsals. Third, we collected artifacts created by participants, like co-designed lesson plans. Of all the participants, here we focus on two critical cases (Yin, 2012): Cynthia, a  woman with “identities that intersect over privile ge” (Interview1, Sum22), and Tino, a man from a “majori ty minority US territory” (Interview1, Spring23). W e rely on these cases to trace how the learning activities  and their participation in both cycles of the PD s upported Cynthia and Tino to develop their equity-oriented science pedagogical praxis. Through invest igating how the design supported their learning, our findings can y ield internal generalizations (Maxwell, 2021) about  how and why informal science educators learn, especially biologists. Light and Suárez qualitatively analyzed (Miles et al., 2013) data for how participants’ pedagogical praxis (i.e., theory, practice, critical reflection; Freire, 2005) attended to equity at three levels (i.e., micro, meso, macro; Burgess & Patterson Williams 2022). For our analyse s, we operationalized praxis as follows: (a) participant conceptualizations of equity were coded as theory; (b) use of critical knowledge for pedagogical rea soning was coded as practice; and (c) critique of theory/practice was coded as critical reflection. Also, equity at the intra-or interpersonal level was coded as micro, equity at the classroom level was coded as meso, and the broader societal level was coded as macro. We coded data independently for evidence of chang e, and discussed the effectiveness of the codebook and our interpretations of participants’ learning.

## Findings

Tino and Cynthia wanted to teach science in the out reach program because of their commitment to expand ing access to science. Initially, they wanted to commun icate information to novice youths, particularly th ose from populations with little exposure to science. Tino drew from prior outreach experiences with youths at home, where he fostered community engagement in turtle conserva tion through connections to cultural practices like  eating turtle meat and eggs during celebrations. He said, “what really excites me is when I do outreach and actually see people get interested” (Interview1, Spring23). Simi larly, Cynthia wanted “to engage scholars at a youn g age, especially scholars who are from under-resourced communities” and help them connect to sci ence (Interview1, Sum22). Cynthia and Tino thought the disconnects be tween science and under-served communities were the product of sociopolitical ideologies. Cynthia thoug ht science appeared inaccessible and irrelevant to youths, and even worried that her privileged identity could rei nforce stereotypes about who belongs in science. Si milarly, drawing from his teaching assistant experiences, Tino was emphatic on the need to be honest with undergraduates that many early prominent evolutionary biologists w ere eugenicists; he worried that if students learne d this on their own, it could further marginalize them from science and contribute to imposter syndrome. These exchanges suggest Tino and Cynthia both began  with micro-level theory: conceptualizing equity work as expanding access to science by communicatin g scientific information in an accessible and inter esting way to youth whose identities are underrepresented in science. The two graduate students also started with macrolevel theory: recognizing the oppressive societal ideologies th at shape perspectives of who belongs in science, and the need for scientists and science educators to redress those histories by advancing inclusion and accessibility in science. Importantly, these two levels were some what unaligned, as both participants wanted to disr upt assumptions about who can be a scientist, while con currently positioning themselves as transmitters of scientific knowledge with power to unlock the doors of the academy. Tino and Cynthia developed lesson plans (PD Sum22) that applied their baseline conceptions of equity and reflected the PD curriculum design principle of  learning science through engaging in  science prac tices. Cynthia’s lesson introduced multiple ways of representing observational data and encouraged youths “to come up with creative ways to represent their findings” (Le sson5, Fall22) thus leveraging youths’ resources. I n Tino’s descriptive statistics lesson, youths calculated mean, median, and mode of the color distributions of bags of candy, turning a potentially inaccessible subject into a r elevant hands-on activity. These examples depict how Cynthia and Tino applied theory of meso-level equity by developing lesson plans that made s cientific concepts and skills relatable and accessible. Still, in practice participants struggled to balance curricular goals for content and skills with youth agency, in some cases reverting to lectures to transmit information that youth reported felt “too much like school” (Program Notes, 2022; PDsession4, Sum23).

Cynthia and Tino took important steps towards compl exifying and aligning their multilevel praxis of equity, supported by the redesigned activities (PD Sum23), such as performing an equity audit of scien ce curricular materials (Braaten et al., 2022). When a uditing his Fall22 lesson, Tino noticed his origina l design portrayed scientists only as white men and presented the role of statistics for lay-people as the creation of curious factoids (Tino_EquityAudit, Sum23). Through this audit, Tino also realized that an equity-oriented lesson should develop youths’ data literacy applied to social jus tice by way of reflecting on “why median can be bet ter to describe a pattern than mean, using income inequality” (Tino_EquityAudit, Sum23). His refined lesson plan was “less telling… more active” and included an activity that supported “students to recognize the potential consequences of unfair data representation, using a  situation that is relevant to them” (PD session4, Sum23; Lesson1, Sum23). Cynthia audited a lesson titled “Engineering a Bee” that engaged youths in designing a robotic bee to pollinate crops and mitigate the risks to agriculture posed by declining bee populations. Through the audit, Cynthia noticed that “missing from this activity ar e the ideas that it i s important to conserve bees as opposed to replacing them” (Cynthia_EquityAudit, Sum23), which  she saw as an instantiation of Eurocentric axiolog y: valuing more-than-human species based on the labor that can be extrac ted. In response, Cynthia created a new lesson that explored the negative impacts of the An thropocene on animals and celebrated diverse environmentorganism relationships based on “the idea that diet  niches shape morphology” (LessonPlan, Sum23). It i ncluded an activity that helped youths connect their indivi dual food preferences and cultural foodways to anim al adaptations (PDSession5, Sum23). In both cases, par ticipants critically reflected  on how meso-level science instruction and lesson plans challenge or reproduce macro-level hegemonic axiology.

## Conclusion

STEM graduate students involved in science outreach programs for minoritized youths need support in developing and implementing equity-oriented science pedagogy. Our preliminary findings  show participants’ initial intentions and experiences align with micro-and macro-level equity conceptions, consistent with a previous report (Sarju & Jones 2020). Through PD and outreach experiences of designing, implementing, and revising lessons to connect science to youths’ lives, participants deve loped a pedagogical praxis that aligned with Burges s and Patterson Williams’s (2022) definition of equity-oriented science education. Specifically, Cynthia and Tino drew on different lived experiences vis-à-vis science to conceptualize and critique a common educational injustice (i.e., minoritized youths are excluded from science). They  even identified two different mechanisms for the e xclusion (i.e., racist origin stories, extractive nature-culture relations) and a common one (i.e., science is disconnected from youths’ everyday lives). Despite following separate, though compleme ntary, pathways, both Tino and Cynthia took important steps towards defining, developing, and enacting an equity-oriented pedagogical praxis through co-designing outreach lessons that focused on youths’ assets and engaging in science practices (PD Sum22), and connected to broader justice goals (PD Sum23). We a rgue that PD iterations, specifically the emphasis on investigations and performing equity audits, prompt ed Tino and Cynthia to develop a critical pedagogic al praxis with greater alignment among the three levels of eq uity and across theory and practice. This work exte nds the power of situated educator learning to bridge theory and practice to the context of equity-oriented PD for science outreach. Specifically, situated PD can help participants expand their initial conceptions of equity and develop a multi-level equity-oriented science pedagogical praxis.
