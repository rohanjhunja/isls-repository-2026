# From Fun to Ownership: Rethinking Epistemic Engagement in Multilingual Science Learning

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Despite increased recognition of affect in science learning, terms like “fun” remain tied to informal contexts, reinforcing binaries that separate joy from seriousness and emotion from intellect. Using sociocultural theory, particularly Vygotsky’s notion of perezhivanie our study explores how multilingual students' understandings of “fun” in a STE(A)M summer academy challenge these divides. Drawing on six years of interview data, our findings show that students often described “fun” as emerging from engaging in meaningful, intellectually demanding work that fostered epistemic agency. Our study contributes to science education research by developing an understanding of affective engagement as central to identifying moments of rigorous and agentive learning.

Over the past decade, work in science education has challenged the longstanding assumption that affect is separate from rigorous disciplinary engagement (Jaber & Hammer, 2016). Emotions such as curiosity, frustration, and satisfaction are now recognized as  integral to how students engage in inquiry, navigate uncertainty, and build disciplinary relationships in science learning (Jaber et al., 2021). Recent work has further centered emotions like joy as foundational to justice-oriented science education (Scip io et al., 2025). Varelas (2025) extends this conversation by theorizing emotions as political and entangled with power, agency, and the heterogeneity of knowing and being. Although prior research has elucidated the complex ways affect manifests within epi stemic practices and informs processes of inquiry and knowledge construction, limited attention has been given to how students themselves articulate and make sense of their affective experiences in engaging with epistemic work, revealing a gap in centering learners’ own perspectives within disciplinary contexts. Attending to these voices is particularly important when examining multilingual and marginalized youth, whose perspectives on learning are often silenced or filtered through dominant discourses of e ngagement. We take up the construct of critical response-ability to underscore the importance of attending to multilingual youths’ voices as sites of epistemic agency. As discussed elsewhere (Kayumova & Tippins, 2021), critical response-ability builds on b ut extends Barad’s (2007) ethical notion of response-ability toward pedagogical relations that affirm learners as knowers. This conceptual stance emphasizes how educators’ recognition of students’ epistemic and affective contributions, grounded in practice s of relational accountability, creates the conditions for learners, particularly those from multilingual and marginalized communities, to participate as valued contributors to knowledge-making. When students describe their experiences of “doing science,” whether that involves reading, discussing ideas, engaging in investigations, or solving problems, we conceptualize these accounts as perezhivaniya  (Vygotsky, 1994; Veresov, 2019). Perezhivanie helps us see how students make sense of science as something felt as well as reasoned, how they narrate curiosity, frustration, or satisfaction as part of their meaning-making. To interpret how these lived experiences become recognized and valued within classroom communities, we draw on critical response-ability as a complementary stance (Kayumova & Tippins, 2021; Barad, 2007; Gutiérrez, 2018). Critical response-ability highlights the ethical and relational dimensions of learning—the ways teachers and peers listen, acknowledge, and respond to students’ ideas as worthy of attention. For multilingual and marginalized students, such recognition can be especially significant: it signals that their ways of knowing, speaking, and feeling have a legitimate place in disciplinary conversations. Together, perezhivanie and critical response-ability offer complementary perspectives for understanding how multilingual youths’ accounts of their experiences of engaging science learning reveal both the personal and relational dimensions engaging in disciplinary work. Guided by these perspectives, our study asks:

1. What do students’ descriptions of “fun” reveal about the relational, emotional, and epistemic conditions

under which science becomes meaningful?

2. How do these expressions signal moments of critical response-ability, when students experience

themselves as intellectually respected, agentive, and included as knowers? 1737 In this paper, we focus on one emergent theme, meaningfulness and ownership, and specifically ask how experiences of “fun” signal these as key conditions for agentive science learning among multilingual youth.

## Research design, method, and analysis

This study draws on qualitative data from a larger mixed-methods project examining asset-based learning within a community-engaged STE(A)M program in Northeastern, U.S. (Kayumova & Kahveci, 2023). The program, codesigned with local educators and multilingual youth, included a two-week summer academy that brought together middle school students who spoke Portuguese, Spanish, Haitian Creole, French, and English. Across six years (2018–2024), the broader project collected 80 semi-structured interviews exploring students’ learning experiences and identity development in science.

All interviews were transcribed and analyzed using iterative coding and thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2006). During initial open coding, we noted that participants frequently used the term “fun”  to describe moments of learning, prompting a more focused analysis. From this larger corpus, we selected 11 interviews in which students invoked the notion of “fun”  in ways that suggested more than simple enjoyment. Drawing on critical response-ability and perezhivanie as our theoretical sensitivities (Strauss & Corbin, 1990), we approached the data inductively, using both categorizing and connecting strategies (Maxwell & Miller, 2008). During open coding, we identified each occurrence of “fun”  and its surrounding context. We developed a set of descriptive codes capturing when and how students used “fun”  in relation to specific activities, emotions, or forms of participation (e.g., curiosity, collaboration, challenge, problem-solving). These instances were treated as potential perezhivaniya, moments where affect and disciplinary engagement intertwined in students’ talk about science. Next, we conducted axial coding to examine patterns across participants, focusing on what fun appeared to signify in context: what students described as fun, what conditions made it possible, and how they described its connection to meaning or learning. Through iterative memoing, peer debriefing, and engagement with literature on affect, learning, and epistemic agency, we refined these patterns into themes that reflected both the “affective-cognitive” (perezhivanie) and relational (critical response-ability) dimensions of students’ accounts. Four overarching themes emerged through collaborative discussion and memoing; this proposal focuses specifically on meaningfulness and ownership. Analytic rigor was ensured through iterative engagement with the research team, reflexive memoing, and sustained dialogue with scholarship on affect, agency, and recognition. Our analysis of students’ talk about “fun” revealed that enjoyment often marked moments of meaningful, self-directed, and intellectually demanding engagement in science learning. Students did not equate fun with ease or play; rather, they described it as the satisfaction of tackling challenging problems, generating ideas, and creating things that felt real and worthwhile. In this sense, fun  indexed both affective and epistemic investment, moments when doing science felt rigorous, relevant, and theirs. Students often characterized experiences as fun where they could make decisions, pursue questions that mattered to them, and see their ideas taken seriously. These accounts clustered around four recurring dimensions of their experience: ownership and autonomy, competence as epistemic confidence, respect as recognition, and inclusion and belonging. Students’ experiences of “fun” signaled perezhivaniya, moments when affect and cognition converged in purposeful activity, and acts of critical responseability, when learners were recognized and positioned as legitimate contributors to knowledge-making. Given the focus of this short paper, we center on one theme —meaningfulness and ownership—which most clearly illustrates how students enacted agency and shaped their own learning through meaningful, self-directed engagement.  More specifically, we provide an exchange with Lucius, a multilingual middle school student, who described building an air filter during the STEAM program as “fun” and “another level.” His reflection captures how “fun” can signal both joy and rigor —how ta ckling a demanding task became a moment of authorship and epistemic agency.

In response to a question about how he felt, Lucius describes the activity as “fun” and “another level”—terms he repeats and amplifies as the interviewer prompts for elaboration. After initially saying “fun” and “excited,” Lucius goes on to explain: “This one is more fun because you get to know how to create a filter… and we create it… with our own minds… our ideas.” Here, Lucius’s notion of ‘fun’ is closely tied to his epistemic participation. The phrase “get to know how” functions as a colloquial way of expressing this participation, connecting his sense of fun to the process of learning how to do something tangible.  However, this phrasing does not explicitly assert an epistemic claim. It simply highlights that the activity involved learning a process and that this learning was a source of enjoyment. The epistemic dimension of Lucius’s stance becomes more explicit in his subsequent statement: “we create it… with our own minds… our ideas.” Rather than framing school as boring simply because of the task type (e.g., “papers and books”), Lucius’s comments suggest that the relational and epistemic positioning he experiences  in school may be less agentic. This resonates with broader discourses of student voice and agency (e.g., Oldfather, 1993), and aligns with literature that conceptualizes ownership not just as choice, but as a recognition of students as knowledge constructors (Apple & King, 1990; West, 1994). This also aligns with Goodman's (1986) assertion that meaningful engagement emerges when students perceive activities as personally relevant, chosen, and valuable to themselves, rather than merely tasks performed to sa tisfy external demands such as teacher expectations. This moment can be read as a moment of epistemic agency (Miller et al., 2018), where he asserts the authority to construct and evaluate knowledge on his own terms. Lucius uses the pronoun "we" to emphasi ze collective authorship and resist individualized or teacher-centered knowledge construction. This emphasis on authorship and choice resonates with prior research showing that students often associate “fun” with opportunities for self-direction and creativity. As one student in Mathers’s (2008) study explained, “Sometimes you can make up your own things and... they can be about anything you want or things you like” (p. 80). Mathers noted that too often classroom learning is devoid of such opportunities for choice, limiting students to answering questions posed by others—teachers, textbooks, or worksheets —rather than questions of their own making. These insights parallel West’s (1994) finding that “fun was not simply a label” students “used for playing and goofing off, but an expression of positive feelings about the kinds of learning situations that facilitated their goals” (p. 7). Lucius’s repeated phrasing, "with our own minds… our ideas," contrasts what is not theirs, positioning himself and his peers as active creators of knowledge, reflecting epistemic agency (Miller et al., 2018). This moment illustrates how discourses of “fun”  are entangled with resistance to normative schooling and with aspirations toward more agentive forms of learning. Rather than treating “fun” as simply affective feedback, we read it as a stance that indexes alignment with certain values—autonomy, authorship, and being taken seriously as a learner. As Mathers (2008) argues, fun and hard work do not have to be mutually exclusive; rather, fun may actually encourage higher levels of engagement and effort. Lucius’s case illustrates this connection: what he describes as “fun” involves challenge, collaboration, and intellectual investment. His experience exemplifies how joy and rigor can coexist in meaningful, agentive learning. From a sociocultural discourse perspective, this moment reveals not 1739 only how students feel, but also how they position themselves within broader educational narratives about who gets to “do” science and how. From a critical discourse perspective, his statement becomes a small but powerful re-authoring of his epistemic agency. This reminds us of research on epistemic affect, where emotions such as excitement emerge from moments of sensemaking, design, and application (Jaber, Dini, & Hammer, 2021). Lucius’s talk thus offers a window into how multilingual students experience agency and belonging not simply through activity type, but through their positioning as legitimate epistemic actors. These interpretations challenge the tendency to overlook “fun” as a meaningful part of learning. In educational contexts, “fun” is often regarded as incompatible with academic rigor, as it tends to be associated with play or non-serious activity rather than with meaningful learning (Mathers, 2008). Yet our data shows that “fun” can emerge from moments of authentic authorship, curiosity, and ownership in epistemic practice. This study reframes “fun” as an epistemic affect that reveals deeper processes of ownership in science learning. Rather than representing trivial enjoyment, students’ descriptions of “fun” illuminated moments when they experienced agency, authorship, and recognition —when their ideas mattered and they could shape the learning process. Lucius’s statement, “we created it with our own minds, our ideas,” exemplifies how joy and agency coexisted within meaningful inquiry, challenging assumptions that separate emotion from rigor. Through the lens of critical response-ability, such ownership is relationally produced: students act with agency when they are recognized as capable contributors and their perspectives are respected. Perezhivanie further explains how this ownership is lived affectively, as curiosity, struggle, and joy become intertwined in the process of meaningmaking. Together, these frameworks position “fun” as the emotional trace of ownership—an affective marker of students’ belonging and intellectual investment in science.
