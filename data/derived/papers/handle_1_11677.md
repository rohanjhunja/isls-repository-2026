# Querencia, Pueblo Revival Architecture and Material Agency in a Summer Camp for Youth

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
We describe contrasting cases in a middle school STEM summer camp to investigate how learners negotiate their agency with different materials to make connections to the land – querencia – using visual content analysis and material agency. We found that students created anatopic buildings with few alterations when using templates but made locally-authentic designs when using less structured materials, shedding light on authentic ways to learn.

## Introduction and research purpose

Querencia, deep connections with the land and community, has the potential to help minoritized learners to learn and feel like they belong in STEM spaces (Bang et a l., 2014). In New Mexico, one of four minority-majority states, Pueblo Revival architecture connects deeply to the land with the styles of historic Pueblo peoples (Traugott, 1998). We are guided by the research questions: How might providing different materials – structured templates, cardboard & cardstock, and perforated cardboard – in a STEM coding and crafting camp support middle s chool learners to create building and house mode ls that align with the architecture of their commun ity? What are the forms of designed materials that enable learners to draw from their everyday knowledge and querencia?

## Theoretical framework

Querencia is the fostering connections to and memor ies of the land. While place-based learning focuses on the ecology and culture, querencia focuses on stories of the land, and responsibility to it, in alignment with Indigenous way of knowing (Dunbar-Ortiz, 2007). Design problems, such as projects in STEM camps, are ill-structured, meaning they have multiple satisfactory solutions (Jonassen, 2000) and so must be framed (Svihla et al., 2021) which leads designers to exercise their agency. Mul tiple agencies exist simultaneously, including the camp facilitator, the learners, the materials, and the people and places affected by the problem (Knappett & Malafouris, 2008). This post-humanist stance foregrounds relationships between l earners and materials, in turn shaping the problem frame and potential solutions. To understand these relationships, we use the being, doing, and becoming framework (Gravel & Svihla, 2021). Being materials are used without modification, ac ting as a coercive and unexamined force. Doing materials are selected by the designer for a specific purpose that aligns with their form. Becoming materials are fundamentally changed and used differently than was originally intended.

## Methodology

We used design-based research to investigate how access to different materials allowed for negotiations with those materials to design with querencia. We report on th ree camps, 2 in 2023 and 1 in 2024. Participants ra nged from 11-15. Each camp used microco ntrollers and culminated in a design challenge. The  first asked for solutions to problems related to a large annual event in New Mexico – the Balloon Fiesta; the second for solutions to problems in Albuquerque; and the third solutions to natural disasters in New Mexico. All three centered on radio communication. While each design challenge was diff erent, all of them asked learners to design specifi cally for New Mexico, to solve a local problem, and to make it something that people in New Mexico would use. However, each camp had different materials. The first had precut/scored cardboard to make peaked-roof houses. The second had flat pieces of cardboard. The third had die-cut shapes with pre-punched grid holes. We collected video/audio recordings of camp time, learner worksheets, and de sign artifacts in line with approved IRB protocol. We developed a coding scheme to align with the design features of Pueblo Revival architecture. We identif ied elements of Pueblo Revival architecture as having a  square footprint  and silhouette, earth colors, parapets, and the overhanging struts that cross the length of the building, called vigas (Traugott, 1998). We identified anatopic features, including peaked roofs, gable windows, no n-natural building materials (like white), and rectan gular silhouettes.

## Results and discussion

We consider all the instances where learners created houses or other buildings as part of their final camp designs. In camp 1, one learner made a house and tunnel that illuminated when cars drove through it in the dark during the Balloon Fiesta. The house, made from a cardboard template, is authentically ad obe-colored, it has a peaked roof and a gable window which are not authentic to the a rea; the house is connected to a tunnel, something that does not exist in New Mexico. This design incorporated being materials that the learner did not contextualize. Another learner in camp 1 used a white cardstock template, resulting in the same peaked roof house, to make a place to park bikes at the event. He cut cardboard to match the shape of the back wall and peaked roof to accommodate the size of the microcontroller. Again, the learner did not contextualize the materials, suggesting the template was a being material, while the cardboard acting as a d oing material, replicating the anatopism of the pea ked roof house while providing adequate space for his mechan ism. In these cases, the designs were anatopic in t hat they did not reflect the place or learners’ own lived ex perience in that place, with the materials having h igh agency over the form of the solutions.

In camp 2, while cardboard and cardstock were provided, they were freeform rather than templates. One learner crafted a home security system using cardbo ard with a flat roof and both a square profile and silhouette, generally reflecting Pueblo Revival sty le. The cardboard was a doing material, with attributes that let him meet his goals. Another learner made school active shooter security doors. She used cardstock to make her school, and while it had a square footprint and flat roof, the cardstock was white, deviating from Pueblo Revival norms, which are locally common in school buildings. Both students created structures that reflected the general characteristics of the buildings in their communities, yet neither created doors or windows in their structures, nor vigas, parapets, or other more detailed features of Pueblo Revival architecture. In camp 3, one learner made a system to lock down the house in the case of dust storms or tornados using pre-cut, perforated cardboard pieces connected with pipe cleaners before using cardboard strips to make slats. She inserted dowels through the pre-cut holes so they spanned the structure and overhung the walls (vigas), and added a flat roof like that in Pueblo Revival buildings. In addition, the square walls and silhouette and the color matched these design features. Another learner created a ho use with an alarm system for flooding, also selecti ng the perforated cardboard to make a square building befo re inserting straws across the roof to make vigas. In both cases, the perforations were intended – by facilitators-to make it easy to connect the structures together (which they did do); recognizing that the perforations als o made ideal inserts for vigas sugg ests that in this case, the cardboard was a becoming material, reflecting querencia. Although some of the precut cardboard was in triangles, suggesting peaked roofs, none of the learners selected this option. While the blank cardboard and cardstock of camp 2 might intuitively be the least coercive (and have the least agency) in that it was least altered by the f acilitators, with the skills and tools the learners  had it became coercive in practice. Learners could not make the types of holes needed to make vigas (as more than one learner struggled to make larger holes with the safety scis sors provided). However, this might not have been c oercive in contexts with different architectural histories and may have been overlooked without considerations of the unique context of New Mexico. Overall, we found that the f orm of the materials available to learners impacted  their agency, in turn, shaping the ways learners built mo re or less authentic architecture when designing  for New Mexico communities. Even authentic design challenges with inauthentic materials can produce anatopic designs, and lower agency materials may still be coercive. The Querencia displayed in these houses reflect not just the way that buildings are designed now, but also a history  of the place that is tied deeply to the realities of the land and the people who have lived there. This helps ground learners and connect their knowledges to the knowle dges of the place around them.
