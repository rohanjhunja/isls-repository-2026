# From Initiative to Impact: Modeling How Collaborative Initiative Shapes Problem -Solving in STEM Simulations

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study examines how collaborative learning unfolds in HoloOrbits, a WebGL-based computer-supported collaborative learning (CSCL) environment designed to teach planetary motion. Using Epistemic Network Analysis (ENA), we analyzed how students initiated (collaborative intiative; CI) and responded to one another’s contributions (uptake; UP) during group inquiry. We found m ost initiatives were procedural, focusing on planning, clarification, and task management, while adaptive and idea-generating initiatives occurred less frequently. Groups where students’ contributions were taken up and extended by peers engaged in more sustained, collective reasoning; whereas others showed more confirmatory or fragmented discourse. These findings position collaborative initiative as a relational construct, with value that lies not in the correctness of individual contributions but in how peer responses take up and build on them to support collective sensemaking. The study underscores the importance of designing CSCL environments that scaffold both student agency and responsiveness, enabling individual contributions to become shared understanding.

Keywords: Computer-Supported Collaborative Learning; Collaborative Initiative; Uptake; Epistemic Network Three-dimensional interactive simulations can support spatial reasoning and shared problem-solving in CSCL environments, and technologies such as WebGL have made them increasingly accessible directly through web browsers without additional software (Hennig et al., 2013). Yet fostering productive group work in these settings remains a persistent challenge: students often struggle to coordinate actions, integrate multiple viewpoints, and build shared understanding—especially in simulation-based settings that impose both conceptual and interface-related cognitive demands (Moro et al., 2023; Sun et al., 2020). In such environments, knowledge building depends as much on peer uptake as on individual contributions. This study therefore examines two interrelated constructs: Collaborative Initiative (CI) and Uptake (UP). CI captures spontaneous, unprompted contributions through which students advance group activity—such as proposing strategies, sharing divergent ideas, or inviting contributions  (Chu-Carroll & Brown, 1997; Rajarathinam & Kang, 2025). UP refers to how peers respond to and build on those contributions (Barron, 2003; Chan et al., 2019). Unlike participation or engagement, which describe the extent and quality of involvement in a task (Fredricks et al., 2004), CI and UP together capture the interactional processes through which ideas were introduced, negotiated, and developed into shared understanding. In this sense, CI  represents directional moves that reorient the group's epistemic or task focus —the micro-level mechanisms through which students exercise epistemic agency (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2006; Zhang et al., 2009) and enact unscripted, self-initiated regulation beyond what structured frameworks anticipate (Järvelä & Hadwin, 2013; Chu-Carroll & Brown, 1997; Howard et al., 2017).

Examining CI alongside UP, this study investigates how their interplay shapes whether individual contributions become, or fail to become, shared understanding in collaborative inquiry. Two research questions guided our analysis: RQ1.

What types of collaborative initiative behaviors emerge as students interact with a science simulation during collaborative inquiry?

RQ2.  How do peers respond to students’ collaborative initiative moves, and how do these interaction patterns differ across groups?

This study investigated collaborative processes within HoloOrbits, a WebGL-based CSCL environment. The platform guides students through inquiry-based tasks related to planetary motion and Kepler’s Laws. Its manipulable 3D interface afforded direct parameter experimentation —a design feature that, as we discuss later, 449 shaped the forms of initiative that emerged. Eighteen undergraduate students (13 male, 5 female) with no prior physics or astronomy background were randomly assigned to six groups and completed a 50-minute, four-part inquiry sequence. Facilitators provided procedural support only; all sessions were recorded and transcribed. Drawing on Chu-Carroll & Brown (1997) and Rajarathinam & Kang (2025), we  distinguish task initiative (steering group progress through planning, proposing, or directing action) from dialogue initiative (managing communication flow, clarifying meaning, or inviting peers to contribute). Six CI sub-facets were operationalized (see Table 1). Not every utterance was coded as CI or UP; codes applied only when a contribution met each construct’s definitional criteria.  Student Uptake (UP) was coded using Barron’s (2003) framework: Accept (A) for agreement, Discuss (D) for acknowledgment with elaboration, Reject (R) for dismissal without rationale, and Ignore (I) for absence of acknowledgment. “What do you all think, which focus should we pick?” We segmented all transcripts into utterances —defined as a single speaker’s contribution bounded by a change in speaker or sentence completion —and coded each for CI and UP. Across six groups (~400 utterances each; ~2,400 total), CI behaviors accounted for approximately 38% of coded utterances (n = 912), distributed as: PSP (41%), EPS (24%), SHC (19%), SDI (7%), DS (5%), IC (4%). Three coders established inter-rater reliability (κ >.85 across all categories) through two iterative calibration rounds; discrepancies were resolved collaboratively before coders continued independently.

Epistemic Network Analysis (ENA) quantifies the co-occurrence of discourse codes within conversational contexts (Shaffer et al., 2016; Ruis et al., 2019; Siebert-Evenstone et al., 2017). In ENA networks, nodes represent discourse codes and edge weights (line thickness) reflect co-occurrence frequency within a sliding conversational window—thicker edges indicate codes that cluster more often. We used a four-line sliding window with the utterance as the unit of analysis. RQ1 mapped CI sub-facet co-occurrence patterns; RQ2 modeled CI and UP together to examine how initiative moves were received across groups. RQ1. Collaborative initiative behaviors during simulation-based inquiry RQ1 aims to identify the types of collaborative initiative (CI) behaviors students exhibit as they engage in inquiry using the science simulation. Specifically, it explores how students take spontaneous, unprompted actions —such as proposing strategies, exploring ideas, or inviting contributions —to advance group problem-solving and knowledge building. By mapping these behaviors, RQ1 seeks to characterize the forms of initiative that drive collaboration within a CSCL environment. The aggregate ENA plot (Figur e 1, left) shows that most student initiatives clustered around PSP, EPS, and SHC. This pattern reflects a predominantly procedural orientation to collaboration—consistent with prior findings that task-regulatory talk dominates simulation-based inquiry 450 (Mercer, 1996; Roschelle & Teasley, 1995). SDI and DS were less central, while IC occupied an intermediate bridging role.

Group-level comparisons reveal distinct profiles of adaptation and divergence. Group 1 (Figure 1, center) showed an adaptive initiative profile: DS co-occurred strongly with SHC and IC, indicating that students flexibly revised plans while actively soliciting peer involvement. Group 2 (Figure 1, right) reflects a divergent initiative profile, with SDI co-occurring strongly with EPS and SHC: students frequently introduced speculative framings that prompted exploration rather than task execution.

RQ2. Peer responsiveness to collaborative initiative across groups RQ2 explores how CI moves were responded to by peers across groups, focusing on patterns of uptake and their variation. While RQ1 examined the kinds of CI moves present in student discourse, RQ2 shifts attention to how those moves functioned within group i nteractions, specifically, whether they were acknowledged, elaborated, or left unaddressed.  The aggregate ENA network of CI and UP (Figure 2) shows strong co-occurrence between Accept (A) and Discuss (D), reflecting baseline acknowledgment patterns across all groups. Group centroid positions reveal meaningful variation: groups positioned near uptake nodes (A, D) tended to affirm contributions; groups positioned near CI sub-facets showed more generative initiative-taking. Group 6 (Figure 3, right) displayed the most integrated CI-UP coupling: PSP co-occurred strongly with Accept (strength =.61) and Discuss, suggesting sustained cycles of proposing, acknowledging, and elaborating consistent with productive knowledge building (Chan et al., 2019; Sc ardamalia & Bereiter, 2006). Group 4 showed similar but weaker patterns (PSP– A strength  =.32), where contributions were acknowledged but less often extended. Group 3’s discourse centered on a PSP –A–D triad (PSP–A =.79; A –D =.47) with limited diversification across CI sub-facets.

Group 1 showed a similar pattern. Beyond these patterns, Group 2’s profile was distinct: divergent ideas were accepted but rarely developed, producing a creative yet fragile dynamic in which novel contributions circulated without gaining collective traction.

Across groups, taskrelated initiatives (PSP, EPS, SHC) dominated, reflecting a procedural orientation to collaboration. More relational moves (IC, DS, SDI) were less frequent but consequential: groups that deployed these moves alongside responsive uptake showed richer collaborative dynamics, while procedurally focused groups often settled into confirmatory cycles.

Our findings support a relational view of CI: initiative gains significance through peer uptake. This aligns with knowledge building theory (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2006; Zhang et al., 2009), where ideas must receive elaborative uptake to contribute to collective understanding rather than remain individual assertions. The contrast between adaptive and divergent initiative also resonates with work on epistemic agency in CSCL (Järvelä & Hadwin, 2013), where unscripted, self-initiated moves are understood as mechanisms through which students shape shared knowledge outside standard regulatory sequences. The value we attribute to CI is not correctness, but contribution to collective sense-making: an initiative becomes epistemically productive when it is acknowledged, extended, and integrated into the group’s ongoing reasoning. The platform’s affordances shaped these patterns: HoloOrbits’ manipulable 3D interface supported exploratory manipulation and EPS behaviors, while SDI and IC were comparatively rare. This suggests the task and interface scaffolded execution more thandivergent thinking or peer invitation, highlighting opportunities for future CSCL designs to incorporate prompts that better support adaptive and divergent initiative. As an exploratory study with six groups from a single institution, these findings are not generalizable. Future research across larger datasets and CSCL contexts can further examine how peer responsiveness shapes learning. Together, CI and UP reveal interactional mechanisms that participation-based frameworks often overlook—how student agency becomes shared understanding or dissipates in fragmented discourse.
