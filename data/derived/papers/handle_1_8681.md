# Using Interest and Identity to Design for Mentor Persistence

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Mentorship is identified as a successful approach to supporting youth in out-ofschool learning. However, less attention has been paid to how to develop and support a mentor workforce. This study examines how to design supportive infrastructure that encourages mentors to persist in mentoring by providing opportunities for interest and collaborative identity development. Drawing on two case studies constructed from interview and interaction data, we demonstrate how these processes unfolded in a summer mentoring practicum.

## Introduction

Mentors and mentorship are identified as successful  approaches to supporting youth in out-of-school le arning (e.g., Barron, Gomez, Pinkard, & Martin, 2014). Les s attention has been paid to how to develop and sup port a mentor workforce, especially a diverse, STEAM mentor workforce. This study examines how to design supportive infrastructure that encourages mentors to persist, despite day-to-day difficulties. We explore this question in the context of STEAMbassadors, a summer mentoring practicum co-des igned by community partners in twoneighboring midwestern cities, with the goal of dev eloping community college students into a diverse, out-ofschool, STEAM mentor workforce, serving historically oppressed neighborhoods. Mentors participated in shared core training but were then placed, based on their interests, at different sites and given additional training to lead specific camps. They were assigned to sites in pairs or groups and participated in peer reflection sessions. We argue that these layers of supportive infrastruc ture were consequential for mentor persistence, because, based on prior research, we conceptualize interest and collaborative identity development as supportive of persistence (e.g., Azevedo, 2011; Eccles, 2009; Hidi, 2006). Conceptualizations of persistence as a  personal characteristic (i.e., “grit”, Duckworth, Peterson, Matthews, & Kelly, 2007) have gained significant tr action. However, we align ourselves with critics of this approach (e.g., Zembylas, 2021) who argue that focusing attention on individual grit misdirects attention toward “fixing” individuals rather than fixing inequitable ecosystems. We chose, instead, to focus on the designed ecosystem of STEAMbassadors, the interactions made possible by that ecosystem, and how these layers of context supporte d interest, identity, and persistence processes. We  propose that mentors supporting youth in exploring topics of shared interest would support mentor persistence. Based on research on relational work among learners (e.g., U ttamchandani, 2021), we also propose a supportive r ole for peer-to-peer interactions among a cohort of mentors collaboratively developing their mentor identities.

## Method

In Summer 2021, 30 mentors participated in our rese arch. These mentors were part of the second STEAMbassadors cohort, who conducted their work one year into the Covid-19 pandemic. This meant that many pandemic restrictions were still in place, leading to hybrid or outdoor-only camps and low youth parti cipation. We collected video of Zoom semi-structured pre-and post-camp interviews, and observations and video of group Zoom reflection sessions. We coded the video for themes related to interest, identity, difficulty, and persistence. For the reflection session data, we also conducted interaction analysis on moments identified as relevant to these themes, to examine how mentors were supporting one another by responding to each other. Finally, to illustrate themes in our dataset, we identified two focal mentors and conducted case analyses. These mentors were selected because their individual stories are rich and representative of other mentors and also because they came together in a significant interaction in a reflection session that represents how mentors supported one another.

## Findings

Dante was a Latino male attending college to become  an engineer. Prior to joining STEAMbassadors, he h ad worked as a summer camp counselor. He was also interested in becoming a DJ. Consequently, Dante was excited that, at his practicum site, there was a DJing station, run by a professional DJ. In his end-of-camp interview, when we asked him about time when he had helped a young person develop an interest, he said, “I would say i t was with the DJ, where we had this one girl who was really into it, and she liked learning more about it from our DJ.” This positive reflection contrasted with Dante’s critical reflections on day-to-day frustrations at his practicum site. He said that the drop-in camp model made making mea ningful connections with youth difficult, that outd oor ICLS2022 Proceedings 1996 © ISLS recreation activities like skating competed with th e STEAM learning stations for youth attention, and that the overall number of campers was low. However, he also described having close relationships with fellow mentors. Kiara was a Latina female college student. She had many interests (art, science, math, acting), but was considering majoring in either business or computer science, with a focus in cybersecurity. Thus, she was excited to be assigned to a cybersecurity camp, a nature camp, and a hip hop history camp. She framed the cybersecurity camp as a positive experience learning alongside youth, since she hadn’t yet taken a cybersecurity class. However, she mentioned challenges, such as low attendance and participation. She also mentioned frustrating interactions with peers, after she was asked to manage fellow mentors at her site, who were older and more experienced than she was. She said, “I don't really know how to explain it, but they wouldn't really give--do the tasks that I would give them or they’d agreed to.” In contrast, Kiara also served as a peer reflection session facilitator and described this experience positively. She noted that in one p articularly productive session, “[mentors] were abl e to communicate with us the struggles and where that frustration was coming from, so I think that was great.” Kiara said she was glad to be able to support and validate other mentors’ struggles and learn that it was okay to struggle. In the reflection session that Kiara cited as produ ctive, there was a consequential interaction betwee n Kiara and Dante. It demonstrates how shared interests and experiences and opportunities for collaborative identity development with fellow mentors encouraged them to persist. In this session, Dante explained a struggle, saying, "[W]e came up with a great idea, we're going to do a leaderboard and everything, and they would get gift cards, and that just fell apart immediately." Kiara responded, “So what went wrong exactly, because I know gift cards, we have gift cards too, and that definitely get som ething going, at least the participation wise. So w hat do you think...made it fall apart?” Dante responded that the idea had not progressed ov er the weeks of his camp, and expressed frustration about that. At this point, other mentors asked questions and made suggestions. Dante and another mentor from his camp provided more details. Moments from this discussion were fraught, as Dante voiced several tensions. The tone of the conversation chan ged, however, when Kiara encouraged Dante to think about what a positive session might look like. This led Dante to focus on positives, including connecting with the girl who was interested in DJing and time spent with coworkers. Kiara’s empathy for Dante and her challenge to him proved critical for him to focus on these positives and their role in shaping his mentoring identity and experience.

## Discussion

We have documented here the supportive role that in terest and collaborative identity development playe d in encouraging mentor persistence in STEAMbassadors. Although both Dante and Kiara experienced frustratio ns, both ultimately viewed the practicum positively. For Kiara, the opportunity to learn about cybersecurity alongside youth and help her fellow mentors articulate their struggles and successes outweighed her frustrations. For Dante, the opportunity to help youth explore DJing and form close bonds with fellow mentors also made the experience worthwhile. In both cases, reflecting as a group allowed mentors to bond over shared experiences and to collaboratively develop their mentor identities, rather than being isolated, as is sometimes the case for mentors who are the only educators at their organizations.  These interest and collaborative identity developm ent opportunities designed into the infrastructure of t he program allowed mentors to work through difficul ties and persist. Educators and researchers candraw on these findings to design intentional supports for mentors, including having mentors work with youth on topics of interest, giving them opportunities to be positioned as leaders, and making sure that they have the support of a peer cohort with whom to share and develop collaboratively.
