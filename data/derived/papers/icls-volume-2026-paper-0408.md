# Co-Intelligence Instructional Coaching: A Concept Analysis of Human–AI Collaboration in Teacher Professional Development

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Artificial intelligence (AI), a major driver of educational transformation, can complement instructional coaching to strengthen teacher professional development. While instructional coaching is effective, its scalability is limited by the shortage of qualified coaches. This study introduces Co-Intelligence Instructional Coaching, a hybrid concept combining AI with human expertise. Through conceptual analysis, it identifies the antecedents, attributes, and consequences of this model, providing a foundation for future inquiry and practice with added relevance for underserved contexts.

Artificial intelligence (AI) is increasingly recognized as a transformative force in education; however, its integration into teacher professional development (PD) has been comparatively underexplored. In a review of 95 studies on AI in education, Tan et al. (2024) reported that only 35% examined AI’s role in teachers’ PD. Although AI can provide facilitation and virtual coaching, barriers include insufficient resources, limited expertise, and concerns over its impact on critical thinking (Aguilar-Cruz & Salas-Pilco, 2025). Addressing these limitations, Yang et al. (2024) emphasized that community support and mastery experiences strengthen AI self-efficacy, and in line with this, Duan and Zhao (2024) found that AI interventions foster autonomy and self-directed learning. Although AI presents both opportunities and challenges in PD, instructional coaching remains a key strategy for enhancing classroom management, instructional delivery, and reflection (Knight, 2009); however, it is resource-intensive and constrained by shortages of qualified coaches (Mai & Richert, 2020). In response to these challenges, AI-assisted coaching has been shown to improve stress management and readiness (Movsumova et al., 2020). At the same time, its lack of empathy and lived experience limits its ability to reduce stress and foster meaningful connections (Passmore et al., 2025a, b). Consequently, scholars advocate hybrid approaches in which mentoring provides emotional and reflective support while AI contributes by offering scalability and personalization (Birescu, 2025; Singh, 2025). Building on this perspective, the present study aims to conceptualize co-Intelligence instructional coaching, a hybrid framework designed to integrate human and AI strengths, address their limitations, and guide future applications of AI-assisted PD in education. Methodological approach Following the procedure of concept analysis (Walker & Avant, 2011), this study conducted a literature review to propose the construct of Co-Intelligence Instructional Coaching. A comprehensive search of Scopus and Google Scholar guided the selection of sources. The search strategy used the following title-based Boolean string: ("instructional coaching" OR "teacher professional development" OR "teacher education") AND ("AI" OR "artificial intelligence" OR "AI coaching" OR "artificial intelligence coaching") AND (teacher OR educator OR instructor). This search, conducted in accordance with the PRISMA framework, initially yielded 654 studies, of which 23 were identified as directly relevant. These studies were analyzed to identify antecedents, attributes, and consequences, which were synthesized into a definition and conceptual framework. Co-Intelligence Instructional Coaching concept Building on Knight’s (2009) definition of instructional coaching as a partnership-based, collaborative approach that offers intensive and differentiated support for teachers, this study compared delivery models, including faceto-face, non-face-to-face, online, blended, and e-coaching to highlight their respective benefits and limitations, and to explore how integrating AI capabilities with human expertise extends traditional instructional coaching into a co-intelligent framework. From this analysis, the concept of Co-Intelligence Instructional Coaching was proposed, followed by a conceptual framework outlining its antecedents, attributes, and consequences. Face-to-face coaching, based on situated learning theory, highlights real-world contexts, cooperative learning, and cognitive apprenticeship (Crawford et al., 2021). In contrast, non-face-to-face coaching enables interaction without physical co-location, typically through remote or digital means (Kim & Lee, 2023). A more 2653 specialized type, online coaching, draws on collaborative constructivism and the zone of proximal development, offering inquiry-driven guidance through online  communication with a more knowledgeable partner (Stenbom, 2015). Moving toward integration, blended coaching combines  instructional, consultative, collaborative, facilitative, and transformational approaches to foster reflection and leadership capacity   (Ezaki, 2015). Another form, e-coaching, employs intelligent systems capable of observing, analyzing, and learning from user behavior to offer guidance (Kamphorst, 2017). The most technologically advanced, AI  coaching, builds on e-coaching through machine-driven autonomy, delivering scalable, data-driven, and personalized support (Graßmann & Schermuly, 2021). However, AI lacks empathy and lived experience, reinforcing the argument that it should support rather than replace human expertise (Passmore et al., 2025a; Singh, 2025). While existing coaching models each offer distinct strengths, they also present inherent limitations when applied in isolation. To address this gap, this study introduces the concept of Co-Intelligence Instructional Coaching as a partnership-based approach that integrates human coaches’ empathy, cultural awareness, and relational expertise with AI’s scalability, availability, consistency, and data-driven insights to enhance and sustain professional development. Within this framework, AI coaching can be integrated across diverse delivery modelsface-to-face, online, blended, and other emerging forms. Attributes, antecedents, and consequences The analysis revealed structural, pedagogical, and individual antecedents shaping Co-Intelligence Instructional Coaching, including systemic conditions such as equitable access, digital literacy, and ethical standards (Birescu, 2025), along with the establishment of AI tool standards for quality and trust (Passmore, 2025b). At the institutional level, leadership support, resource allocation, and alignment with reform efforts ensure sustainability (Sumner, 2011), while teacher readiness is shaped by AI literacy, attitudes toward technology, and trust in AI systems. Regarding attributes, human-driven and AI-driven elements were identified. Human coaches contribute relational expertise, reflective support, empathy, cultural sensitivity, and experiential knowledge (Sumner, 2011; Caneva et al., 2023; Passmore et al., 2025b), whereas AI agents provide scalable, data-driven, affordable, and consistent feedback, particularly valuable in novel or sensitive contexts (Movsumova et al., 2020). Finally, three levels of consequences were identified: teacher, instructional, and organizational. These include enhanced teacher agility, compassion, and purpose (Birescu, 2025), improved student learning (Sumner, 2011), and more sustainable, equitable PD. AI coaching functions to emulate human practices, provide support, train coaches, and analyze interactions to generate knowledge (Terblanche, 2024), while leveraging adaptive learning to personalize support and increase efficiency (Graßmann & Schermuly, 2021). Additionally, technology-driven collaboration in rural schools can further address educator needs, improve student outcomes, strengthen teacher retention, and support meeting teachers’ professional needs, enhancing working conditions in schools and retaining high-quality teachers in rural areas (Randolph et al., 2025) (see Figure 1). ## Conceptual Framework for Co-Intelligence Instructional Coaching

This concept analysis introduced and clarified the construct of Co-Intelligence Instructional Coaching, highlighting its potential to enhance teacher professional development by combining the relational strengths of human coaches with the scalability and data-driven capacities of AI. Through this  approach, the study identified the antecedents, attributes, and consequences of this hybrid model, offering a comprehensive framework for understanding its role in teachers’ PD. The findings suggest that while human coaches remain central for fostering empathy, contextual sensitivity, and reflective growth, AI can complement their work by providing personalized feedback and expanding access to professional support. By advancing conceptual clarity, this study provides a foundation for future empirical investigations to implement and evaluate Co-Intelligence Instructional Coaching, ensuring that technological innovation complements the humandimensions of teacher learning.
