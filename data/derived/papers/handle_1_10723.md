# Unraveling Learner Self-Efficacy in the Electronic Making: Insights from Interaction Analysis, Self-Reports and Interview

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Makerspaces serve as collaborative environments nurturing creativity and hands-on learning. Despite their potential to cultivate twenty-first-century skills, concerns persist regarding diverse participation and sustained engagement, particularly in STEM fields. Motivation, notably self-efficacy, emerges as a crucial determinant of participation and persistence. While existing studies employ surveys and longitudinal analyses, they fail to capture nuanced changes during task engagement. This research proposes the integration of interaction analysis alongside interviews and surveys to provide a comprehensive understanding of self-efficacy dynamics within makerspaces. By addressing the "why" and "what" behind self-efficacy changes, we aim to shed light on the intricate interplay between motivation and makerspace engagement. The study endeavors to answer the research question: How does interaction analysis enhance our understanding of self-efficacy dynamics in makerspaces?

## Introduction

In the rapidly evolving education and innovation landscape, makerspaces have emerged as dynamic environments that foster creativity, hands-on learning, and technological exploration. Makerspaces, which serve as collaborative hubs for makers (individuals working in makerspaces) to create, innovate, and engage with various mate rials, offer a promising avenue to bridge the gap between theoretical knowledge and p ractical application. Supporters of the maker education movement believe that makerspaces can serve as educational hubs, offering students from pre-K through 12 an opportunity to acquire a diverse se t of twenty-first-century skills (Doorman et al., 2019). Makerspaces have been critiqued that it has been un successful in involving diverse participants especi ally for sustained period of time (Barton et al., 2017). Par citipation and persistence in STEM has been linked to noncognitve factors especially motivation  (Alex & Aman, 2019).  Since makerspaces are gaining popularity in educational contexts, there is an increasing need t o examine the composition of makers and their motiv ational beliefs (Mersand, 2021). Of the many related constructs of motivation, self-efficacy has been liked to persistence and participation. Given that makerspaces are interest driven spaces, it  is even more important to analyse participation through the lens of self-efficacy. In this context, a particular emphasis on self-efficacy is warranted, given emerging research that underscores the relati onship between participants’ self-efficacy and sustained engagement and persistence in STEM (Marra et al. 2018). Self-efficacy is commonly studied via cross-sectional surveys and statistical analyses, offerin g insights into population characteristics. However, these met hods limit understanding of variability and influen ces on change. To address this, longitudinal ana lyses are emerging, yet fail to capture fine-grained changes during task engagement (Chien et al. 2020; Hilton et al. 2020; Brubaker et  al. 2019; Andrews, Borrego & Boklage, 2021). However, a few subsets of studies supplemented surveys with interviews and  observations (Hilton et al. 2018; Supraja et al. 2022). Many researchers developed and validated survey instruments, often drawing guidance from Bandura's scale (Shu & Huang, 2021; Sinha et al. 20 20; Andrews, Borrego & Boklage, 2021). Qualitative analyses, such as content or thematic analysis, were also utilized in conjunction with observations and interviews (Hui & Gerber, 2017; Supraja et al. 2022). From the se studies it was found that self-efficacy can be improved with intervention and the development of self-efficacy over a period of time can be aided by public validation and modeling others’ work ((Hui & Gerber, 2017). While the above-mentioned studies capture ‘how self-efficacy changes’, the ‘why’ and ‘what influences the change’ remains unanswered.  These static self-efficacy assessments track pretest-to-posttest changes but miss fine-grained changes during task engagement (Schunk, & DiBenedetto, 2021). To address this, we use 'interaction analysis' as  an analytical approach in conjunction with self-report survey and interview data to acquire in-depth insights into how participants' self-efficacy shows itself in the setting of the makerspace. With the aforementioned analytical approach into consideration to look at the self-efficacy of learners in workshop-based making settings, the study is directed by the following research question: RQ: How does the use of interaction analysis in com bination with interviews and self-report surveys enhance our grasp of self-efficacy and its dynamics in the context of makerspaces?

## Theoretical framework

Albert Bandura's self-efficacy theory forms a fundamental pillar of under standing human behavior and motivation. At its core, the theory revolves around  the concept of self-efficacy, which represents an individual's belief in their capability to accomplish specific tasks or achieve certain outcomes. Bandura posits that self-efficacy is a powerful determinant of actions, as it influen ces the goals individuals set, the effort they inve st in, and their resilience in the face of challenges (Bandura, Free man & Lightsey, 1999). This theory suggests that individua ls with higher self-efficacy are more likely to approach tasks with enthusiasm, persistence, and a proactive attitude. They view obstacles as surmountable and are more likely to engage in behaviors that lead to successful outcomes (Bandura, 1993).

Traditional methods of studying self-efficacy, often reliant on surveys and interviews, are pervasive in educational psychology. However, makerspaces, roote d in distributed cognition, challenge this approach, advocating for a more situated methodology. Socio-cognitive theory, central to self-efficacy research, underscores the triadic reciprocal relationship  between the self, behavior, and environment. Recogn izing environmental influence, we leverage interaction analysis, pionee red by Jordan and Henderson (1995), to explore self-efficacy within makerspaces. Grounded in ethnomethodology, i nteraction analysis scrutinizes how individuals int eract with each other and their surroundings, acknowledging the social and contextual nature of human knowledge. By adopting this approach, we aim to gain a comprehensive understanding of self-efficacy dynamics in makerspaces, bridging the gap between traditional educational ps ychology methods and the unique environmental conte xts of makerspaces.

## Method

The study took place in a college popup makerspace,  engaging 11 participants (8 boys, 3 girls) aged 12 to 15. Posters facilitated recruitment. Over two days, participants explored breadboard sets, paper circuits, and electronic textiles, fostering hands-on experimentation. On day two, they selected mater ials and crafted artifacts to nurture creativity. Ethical consent was obtained. Data collection involved pre-activity Motivated Strategies for Learning Questionnaire (MSLQ) surveys and post-activity interviews. The MSLQ, using a 7-point Likert scale, assessed motivational factors. Interviews, lasting 7 to 10 m inutes, delved into participants' decision-making processes, values, and intentions. Conducted one-on-one by the researcher, interviews were audio-recorded, and participants had access to their artifacts during the session. T o ensure comprehensive documentation, the entire st udy was video recorded, capturing real-time engagement and behaviors.

The study followed the case study methodology to get a detailed view of the making processes (Merriam, 1998). The audio and video recordings of the procee dings were repeatedly attended to by the authors and conducted the operations of interaction analysis (J ordan & Henderson, 1995). The audio and video recor dings were transcribed, followed by content logging and s egmentation. The content logs and segments were clo sely analyzed by looking at how participants engaged in the process of making as they used the resources provided. The interpretations arrived from the analysis were connected and checked with the self-reporting from the MSLQ and interviews. The authors created analysis memos from video analysis, self-reports and interviews, followed by discussions on differences and similarities in i nterpretation in multiple cycles. For the analysis,  data sources related to two participants-Kurenai and Kimimaro (pseudonyms) were selected as Kurenai reported high self-efficacy and Kimimaro reported low self-efficacy respectively among the 11 participants. It is ex pected that contrasting cases can reveal valuable insights regarding self-efficacy.

## Findings

Episode 1 In this specific episode, Kurenai engaged with a breadboard, connecting a red LED to a battery source, a resistor, and a switch. The red LED was effectively incorporated into a closed circuit with correct polarities aligned to the battery, showcasing Kurenai's confidence and profic iency. However, when attempting to add a yellow LED  in a parallel connection, she initially faced challenges. Af ter 3 to 4 minutes of persistence, Kurenai successf ully rectified the connections and expressed self-affirmation with a "thumbs up" gesture. This not on ly demonstrates her adeptness in circuit assembly but  also underscores her self-efficacy in overcoming obstacles. Visual documentation in Figure 1 illustrates the sequence of actions, emphasizing the positive moment of accomplishment. The interview data analysis of Kurenai revealed tha t she had very little knowledge about electronics and that few of her misconceptions were  clarified. Below is a snippet from the interview d ata related to the description arrived with the interaction ana lysis of episode 1.  “First when I made this connection here I connected the wire here and the second wire I have joined here, t hen I feel that it is not working. Then my thought...that this wire had to be connected to the  switch…then this wire was removed and connected to  the switch…at the last moment.” This narrative unfolds as a testament to the participant’s self-efficacy, revealing a resilient approach to problem-solving. Kurenai is found to acknowledge the failures in getting the yellow LED lit up and the troubles she faced in connecting the wir es. T he reporting can be connected back to the analytic interpretation that was presented earlier.

Episode 2 In this episode, the participant completed her artifact and cleaned the worktable. She arranges things on the table in such a way that the completed artifact takes cen tre stage, and the rest of the materials are sorted and moved to worktable corners. With the help of a mobile phone, she took pictures and recorded the video of the artifact and its functioning as she switched on different switch es with the connected LEDs lighting on and off. She  stopped the recording and then wrote “sources of light” as the name for the artifact with a smile and continued her way of documenting the artifact by clicking pictures and r ecording videos. In the interview, Kurenai was aske d about how she felt after the making and about the final a rtifact, to which she responded:  “Well, I am feeling proud of myself”. These words from the participant reveal a sense of achievement and pride as she faced multiple difficulties and solved the same while making the a rtifacts. Also, Kurenai reported that she created t he artifact - “sources of light”- by taking inspiration from the workshop room which was fully illuminated with lights. “When I was sitting outside, I thought of what I should be making. Then I looked inside the room and found all the lights... And I asked myself. why don't I work on lights? ”. After completing the video documentation of her art ifact, the participant meticulously reviewed the wire connections and ensured its proper functioning. In the video, Kurenai is observed rehearsing detailed narrations about he r artifact, demonstrating a gr owing sense of reassurance and personal connection. This preparation reflects her motivation to excel in the making process and share her creation with fellow learners, underscoring the significance of personal engagement in makerspace activities. Episode 3 In this particular episode we observe Kimimaro engaging with electronic textiles for 2 minutes before moving on to the breadboard. In the interview when asked abou t why he chose to create this artifact, he replied: “I did not want to use textiles at all, because it was very ti ring and boresome and I always messed it up. I trie d 10 or 12 times before, but I could not attach a single LED. So I joined these two modules [breadboard and paper circuits] and made this ”. When probed further on why he chose to use them, he  responded by saying: “I knew two materials, breadboard-I used before also. And this paper circuit also I u sed before”. From these excerpts, he was familiar with using two out of the three electronic-making components introduced to him in the previous day's workshop. His responses provide us with a potential link to low self-efficacy with few materials. He candidly admits avoiding the use of e-textiles due to perceived challenges, expressing frustration, and describing it as tiring and bothersome. The interplay between material choi ce, past experiences, and his expression of difficulty sheds light on the potential association between Kimimaro ’s creative decision and low self-efficacy in dealing with certain materials.

## Results and discussion

The study is aimed at exploring the possible ways in which learners’ self-efficacy can be investigated while they engage in electronic making. The data sources included video recordings of making, self-reported surveys and interview were collected as part of a maker worksho p. The study examined the self-efficacy dynamics of eighthgrade students in an electronic makerspace. By focu sing on two specific contrasting cases of making, the study used the techniques of Interaction Analysis in conjunction with self-reporting from surveys and interviews to take a deeper look into how learners expressed self-efficacy in diverse ways. With the inputs from the MSLQ survey, the video recordings of the making process were ana lyzed, and further evidence was insp ected from interviews jointly.

Analysis of the Motivated Strategies for Learning Q uestionnaire (MSLQ) indicated consistently high levels of self-efficacy in Kurenai's workshop skills mastery, echoing findings from interaction analysis episodes. These included Kurenai's video documentation of her work and triumphant gestures upon overcoming challenges, corroborated by her personal connection with the artifact revealed in interviews. Such validation experiences align with literature on self-efficacy development (Hui & Gerber, 2017), painting  a comprehensive portrait of self-efficacy encompassing cognitive, emotional, and behavioral dimensions within makerspaces. For Kimimaro, MSLQ responses depicted fluctuating s elf-efficacy, particularly regarding complex materials. Video analysis unveiled his aversion to electronic textiles, opting for familiar materials,  indicative of past struggles. Interviews further elucidat ed his avoidance of electronic textiles due to perc eived challenges, shedding light on the nuanced interplay between mat erial choices and self-efficacy. This understanding underscores Bandura's socio-cognitive theory, emphasizing reciprocal interactio ns between behavior, cognition, and environment (Bandura, Freeman, & Lightsey, 1999). Future research should explore diverse participa nts' motivational aspects, beyond self-efficacy, incorporating multifaceted data sources t o comprehensively understand motivation in creative learning environments.
