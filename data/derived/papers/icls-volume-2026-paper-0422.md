# Camp Cyber: Developing Girls’ Interest and Self-Efficacy in Cybersecurity using Generative AI

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This mixed-methods study examines how a week-long residential cybersecurity camp integrating generative AI (GenAI) tools influenced underrepresented minority girls’ cybersecurity knowledge, self-efficacy, and interest. Thirty-three middle-grade participan ts engaged in cybersecurity lessons and created GenAI informational videos for local nonprofits using ChatGPT and HeyGen AI. Pre-and post-tests revealed significant gains in knowledge and self-efficacy. Qualitative analysis showed growing confidence and real-world application. Findings suggest GenAI-integrated informal education builds technical knowledge and confidence among underrepresented youth.

The growing demand for cybersecurity professionals has increased the need to broaden participation in cybersecurity education, yet girls and underrepresented and underserved racial and ethnic minorities (UUREM) continue to face limited access to meaningful  learning opportunities (CSIS, 2025). Informal environments may help address these inequities by providing flexible, hands-on, and collaborative learning experiences that are often difficult to implement in formal school settings (Milton et al., 2023). At the same time, generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) offers new ways for students to create, communicate, and apply disciplinary knowledge through authentic work. This study examines a week-long residential cybersecurity camp in which UUREM middle-grade girls learned foundational cybersecurity concepts and used GenAI tools (ChatGPT and HeyGen AI) to create educational videos for local nonprofits. By combining cybersecurity learning, GenAI-supported media production, and service-learning, the camp represents an innovative informal learning model designed to support both technical skills and interest development. Given the importance of interest and self-efficacy for sustained participation (Hidi & Renninger, 2006), this study asks: How does participation  in a week-long residential cybersecurity camp integrating GenAI-based video creation influence UUREM girls’ interest, content knowledge, and self-efficacy in cybersecurity?

This mixed methods study (Creswell & Guetterman, 2019) explored changes in interest, self-efficacy, and understanding of cybersecurity topics among middle grade UUREM girls (n=33) attending a week-long residential STEM summer camp in a large southwestern U.S. city. Over four days, campers received cybersecurity and GenAI instruction, worked in groups of 4-8, and developed educational videos for local nonprofits on cybersecurity topics aligned with each organization’s mission. Throughout the week, they engaged in iterative prompt refinement with expert feedback on clarity and accessibility. On the final day, campers used ChatGPT (GPT-4o) and HeyGen AI (Avatar IV) to create 60 second educational videos integrating cybersecurity concepts, ethical considerations, and universal design principles. Pre-and post-surveys measured changes in cybersecurity self-efficacy, interest, knowledge, and career aspirations. Video recordings of two groups yielded 18 hours of observational data documenting participant interactions. We used two instruments: the Cybersecurity Engagement and Self-Efficacy Scale, Version 2.0 (McGill, 2021) and the Cyber Knowledge and Interest Scale (Asif et al., 2025). Quantitative analysis consisted of paired t-tests examining pre-to post-test changes across measured constructs, while qualitative analysis used content logging of video data to identify evidence aligned with the theoretical constructs measured in the surveys.

Paired t-tests revealed a statistically significant and large increase in participants’ cybersecurity knowledge scores (d=1.20, p<.001), indicating that the week-long camp substantially enhanced participants’ self-reported understanding of cybersecurity. Complementing this finding, video data showed that campers increasingly applied concepts across modules. For example, one 9th grade camper shared, “Practical takeaways, we have to check our password and make sure that we’re entering strong and correct passwords,” while other campers added ideas such as “strong passwords,” “safe-social media behavior,” and “not sharing personal information,” showing application of cybersecurity concepts in everyday contexts. Cybersecurity self-efficacy refers to campers’ confidence in their capability to successfully carry out cybersecurity tasks (Thomas et al., 2024). We examined six domains  in the survey: computer use, cyber threat identification, networking, problem-solving, system administration, and web management. Paired t-test results revealed a statistically significant increase in cyber threat identification self-efficacy, with a large effect size (d=1.02, p<.001). Significant improvements were also observed in web management (d=0.55, p<.01) and networking (d=0.49, p<.05). Complementing these results, video data showed campers moving from labeling threats to making actionable judgments. One 9th grade camper explained that they should not give identity information to people who are not verified as trustworthy, while other peers extended this into concrete strategies such as “think twice before sharing,” “ask what the information is for,” and “verify the requester.” Campers also applied these ideas in phishing scenarios and embedded them into their GenAI video scripts. These behaviors indicate growing task-specific confidence in identifying and responding to threats, aligning with the significant gains in threat identification and web/credential management self-efficacy.

## Significance

These findings contribute to understanding how emerging technologies can be leveraged in informal STEM education to address cybersecurity workforce diversity challenges (CSIS, 2025; Francis et al., 2024), though sustained engagement beyond short-term interventions may be necessary to maintain momentum in interest and career aspirations (Hidi & Renninger, 2006). The study also provides critical insights for designing inclusive cybersecurity education that builds both competence and confidence in populations historically excluded from the field (Buchanan et al., 2021; Francis et al., 2024).
