# Balancing Agency and Accountability to Support Learning in Playful, Embodied Science Activities

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Student agency is important to support in learning environments, as it enables students to control their learning and can lead tonew understandings of content. Yet, there are many questions about when and how to hold students accountable for content learning while still fostering their agency. This paper examines data from a first-grade classroom, in which students embodied bees in the STEP (Danish et al., 2020) mixed-reality environment in order to learn about nectar collection and pollination. We analyze the ways in which student agency and accountability interacted in the embodied learning space and led to engagement with science content. We describe patterns in how student agency can support sensemaking as students navigate different sources of accountability and we discuss productive ways to balance agency and accountability in embodied learning environments.

## Introduction and framing

Student agency has long been seen as an important p art of science learning. It provides new roles and voice to students (Barton et al., 2010), and enables students to actively generate disciplinary knowledge (Cavagnetto et al., 2020). While agency has been defined in several ways across education and psychology, we find it productive to view agency from a sociocultural perspective. We build on sociocultural theories of learning, which highlight how cultural ideas and artifacts mediate learning (Enge ström, 1987; Vygotsky, 1978) to view student activi ty as individuals-operating-with-mediational-means (Wertsch, 1993). Agency, then, is not something that stud ents possess or do not, but instead can be conceptualized as the mediated actions that students have opportunities to, and choose to, enact within a classroom activity system. In this view, it is important to consider what features of the classroom environment support or limit students’ ability to exercise agency and how they do so. In our work on embodiment, we have found that activities where students use gestures, body positions, and movement  to represent and explore their ideas can provide new opportunities for, and constraints on, student agency (Danish et al., 2020). Students often have the freedom to move  however they want, yet their peers’ motion or the software environment also constrain the impact that their movement has on the system being explored. The goal of this paper is to examine the ways in w hich particular features of an embodied learning activity influenced student agency and supported student lea rning. We focus specifically on understanding the r ole of accountability. Agency and accountability are often dichotomized as  opposites, in which agency is good and accountability, structure, or teacher authority sho uld be minimized. However, in an academic setting, students often need to be held accountable to disciplinary practices and content, and so the question shifts to how agency and accountability should be balanced in the classroom in order to foster learning (e.g. Danish & Enyedy, 2006). In embodied environments in particular, students ac t and interact in ways that are different from many  science classrooms (Danish et al., 2020; Lindgren & Johnson-Glenberg, 2013), which necessitates new understandings of how agency and accountability function. Therefore, we ask the following research questions:

1) How do students take up agentic roles in embodied learning experiences?

2) How do interactions between agency and accountability in embodied activities influence the learning of

scientific content?

## Participants and data sources

This work examines data from the Science through Technology Enhanced Play (STEP) project (Danish et al., 2020). In this project, 11 students from a small pu blic school in the midwestern United States partici pated in collective embodied activities in a mixed-reality environment, in which motion tracking software captured student movement and depicted students on a screen as bees in a field (see Figure 1). This analysis focuses on the group of students participating in a series of eight lesson plans focused on understanding honeybees, nectar collection, and pollination (see Danish et al., 2020 for similar studies). The classroom teacher and several researchers served as instructors of the unit.

Analysis We began by reviewing video footage including scree n recordings of the STEP simulation of the whole le sson sequence, and logged moments where agency or accoun tability were salient. Based upon this first pass, we selected moments for further analysis in which inte ractions between accountability and agency seemed t o be productive for the learning of scientific content. We iteratively reviewed these moments by articulati ng and refining conjectures about the role of agency and a ccountability in supporting learning. In the presen t analysis, we present patterns that we observed across the lessons, and then focus on one activity that occurred on the sixth day of the sequence where students learned about po llination. We selected this illustrative example, i n which student agency seemed to positively impact learning, because we were interested in highlighting the po tential value of student agency in this kind of embodied de sign. Note that while episodes had varying levels of student agency, many had similar moments and we selected th is particular episode because of the many interacti ons between agency and accountability that were concentrated in one sequence. We use interaction analysis (Jordan & Henderson, 1995) to consider how students interacted with each other, the instructors, and the simulation, and the ways that the activity oriented students to behave in agentic ways or notice different sources of accountability.

## Findings and discussion

Patterns across the lesson sequence Our first research question asked how students take  up agentic roles in embodied learning experiences.  Across activities and days, we noticed a stark contrast between the limited agency that students were afforded in teacherled discussions about content (where students could  choose to answer a question or not), and the more flexible agency that they were afforded in the embodied activities (where students had agency to act, speak, and model in ways that they decided). In the embodied activities, one way that students acted agentically was by ma king decisions about where and how to move in the space. In some cases, students’ movement choices were influenced by the way that the system responded to their behaviors. For example, some students chose to crouch down, but when they did so, they lost the nectar that they had collected because the system could no longer see them. As a result, students stopped choosing to behave in this way and stood up tall. In addition to the ways in which students decided to move, they also had agency to call out and direct other students’ actions. That is, as students watched others engaging in movement, they often took it upon themselves to hold their peers accountable to the content, particularly through comments like “get the nectar to the hive!” Students that were directed by other students had agency to listen or not, prompting them to navigate sources of accountability in relation to their own goals. Our second research question asked how interaction s between agency and accountability influenced the learning of scientific content. In this designed sp ace, we found that there were several competing sou rces that students needed to be accountable to, including the instructors’ suggestions, other students’ suggestions, and the software. The presence of these competing accountabilities gave students the agency to determine which ones to follow, and why. We argue that in exercising this a gency, students explored and made sense of the unde rlying rules that are a part of accountability, leading to learning of the target content. Illustrative case: Agency, accountability, and learning about pollination In this case, we focus on the agency on one particu lar student, Tom (though other students also acted in agentic ways), and how that agency enabled the class to learn more about the process of pollination. Prior to this episode, students had just finished a round of embodied activity in which they acted as bees to visit flowers, and then saw that the next spring, most of the flowers died, but one was replaced with many other flowers because it had been pollinated. Another student argued that Tom c ould actually move wherever he wanted (line 4), whi le an instructor gave him permission to enact agency by following his choice of accountability source. In doing so, Tom was able to engage in sensemaking around the behavior of bees. After Tom returned to the hive, an instructor sent Jade into the embodied space. (3) Jade: Oh. (moves to a yellow flower)

(4) Instructor 2: The two big yellow ones are the ones we’re going to. That was what Tom had suggested.

Accountability played a positive role in this exchange in that reminders to follow the rules ensured that students would be able to see the results of Tom’s plan. In line 4, an instructor held Jade accountable to the original plan, and specifically positioned Tom’s ideas as a valuab le contribution. In addition to instructors enforci ng accountability, Tom and Caleb made suggestions to the embodied participants. The instructor’s comment on line 7 reinforced the norm that students have agency to make suggestions by ratifying Tom’s comment from line 6. There were several components of students’ agency in relation to accountability that led to engagement with the scientific concepts. Tom’s agency to sugge st an idea, to choose to be accountable to the rule s of the simulation rather than to the teacher, and to hold students accountable to trying his idea all helped students to learn about how pollination works. ## Implications and conclusion

In our analysis, we found that embodied activities broadened the types of agency available to students as compared to traditional teacher-led discussions. That is, students enacted agency through choosing where and how to move, holding their peers accountable to ideas and conten t, and navigating competing sources of accountabili ty. This work suggests that accountability does not necessarily have to limit student agency. Having multiple s ources to be accountable to (instructor(s), the other students, and the rules o f the software/technology) and being accountable for multiple ideas (in this case, for enacting a polli nation plan and for collecting nectar) enables students to understand that rules are not just for following, but for making sense of. When students had agency to make sense of competing sources of accountability, they gained nuanced understandings of content. Ther efore, designers of learning activities should consider how to create an environment where multiple, competing sources of accountability are present, and where students are given agency to both act as and to navigate these sources.
