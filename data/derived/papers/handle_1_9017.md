# Sociopolitical Discourses Within Student Farms and Gardens

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
We identified 46 university-based student farms or gardens’ (SFG) mission statements from around the U. S. and used sociosemantic analysis to describe and compare the individuals involved, the actions conducted, and the objects involved. Two discourses emerged from our analysis: (a) a depoliticized framing of SFGs as sites for producing food for higher education communities; and, (b) a politicized framing of SFGs as sites for engaging with issues of social and environmental justice within the broader community.

## Introduction

This research is part of a broader project to under stand the learning that occurs amongst participants  at a university-based student farm/garden (SFG). At many junctures in this project, we noticed tensions between the purposes of student farm workers (e.g., growing food to donate to community members) and the purposes of the university personnel (e.g., selling produce at local markets or using produce in promotional events). We see these tensions as arising from conflicting discourses regarding the purposes of university SFGs. Discour ses are “particular way(s) of representing some part of the (physical, social, psychological) world” (Fairclough, 2003, p. 17). For example, SFGs candraw on various discourses to inform their daily work (ex. community service, food security, university pride). We see these various d iscourses and the conflicts between them as importa nt to understand to make sense of day-to-day participation in SFGs. To that end, we have ‘zoomed out’ from our local context to gather a broader understanding of the various discourses which inform SFGs. In this paper, we collect mission statements from 46 SFGs at land grant universities around the U. S. and ask the following three research questions:

1. Who is involved? (ex. students, professors, 'the community’, or others?)

2. What do they do? What actions do participants of these SFGs engage in? Towards what or who?

3. What objects or ideas are involved? (ex. Science learning, skills, tools, produce, etc.?)

## Data Analysis

To analyze the 46 SFG’s mission statements, we drew  on a CDA technique called sociosemantic analysis (van Leeuwen, 1993) which involved three steps. First, we read each mission statement and annotated three features: participants (people or institutions involved in the SFG), processes (actions of the people or institutions in the SFG), and objects (materials, ideas, or social relations related to the SFG). Second, we developed a codebook to capture the different themes amongst participants, processes, and objects. Third, we create a frequency count for each theme and reflect on the variation amongst the  set of SFG mission statements. In the next section s we summarize the themes among participants, processes, and objects included in SFG mission statements. We close with a discussion about the two discourses we see surrounding the purposes of SFGs. Who are the Participants?

Participants fell into three primary groups among the 46 SFG mission statements: (a) the garden/farm itself; (b) participants from the university or college; and, (c) participants from beyond the university or college. The most frequently mentioned participant was the student farms or gardens themselves. All 46 of the mission st atements referenced the farm or garden as a social actor. 25  of the 46 mission statements only include the SFG as a participant, and make no mention of other participa nts in their mission statement. The second most fre quently mentioned participants were social actors from the university or college in which the SFG is situated.  Students were included as participants in 14 of the 46 (30.4 %) mission statements. Faculty and Staff were inclu ded as participants in 2 of the 46 (4.3%) mission statements. Far less frequent, were mentions of social actors outside the boundary of the university or college. Families wer e included as a participant in 1 of the 46 (2.2%) mission statements. Food banks or non-profits were included as particip ants in 2 of the 46 (4.3%) mission statements. ‘Community’ was included as a participant in 10 of the 46 (21.7%) mission statements. And ‘Society’ wa s included as a participant in 1 of the 46 (2.2%) mis sion statements. In these instances, SFGs are posit ioned as organizations whose purposes extends beyond to soci al actors who are not directly involved with univer sity or college.

We focus on processes as a way to see how SFGs delegate actions to different participants involved in the SFG. In this poster, we describe the directionality of processes. By directionality, we mean which social actor conducts the action of a process. Three directionalities emerged from the data: (a) processes conducted by the SFG towards other participants; (b) processes conducted by othe r participants; and, (c) processes conducted by the  SFG and other participants together. Most process directions were those conducted by the SFG towards other social actors. All 46 SFG mission statements included some processes conducted by the SFG. Far less frequent were instances where social actors other than the SFG conducted pr ocesses. Nine SFG mission statements included proce sses conducted by participants outside the SFG (19.6%). Three SFG mission statements included processes conducted by the SFG together with other participants (6.5%).

What are the objects?

Student farms or gardens provide produce (material), knowledge about sustainable practices (mental and material), and friendship (social). We focus on objects as a w ay to see what services are rendered available by S FGs. The objects identified from the mission statements can be classified in four categories: (a) nature and food; (b) social objects; (c) education; (d) and justice. The primar y objects involved in SFG mission statements were r elated to nature and food. Nature and food objects are any object that is provided from the natural (as opposed to cultural) realm. 39 of the 46 (84.8%) mission statements include some mention of nature and food. For example, Dilmun Hill at Cornell University “seeks to empower studen ts through active engagement in ecological agricult ure”. Social objects were also frequently mentioned in SFG mission statements. Social objects were any object related to community, partnerships, collaboration, and students. 30 of the 46 (65.2%) mission statements inclu de some mention of a social object. For example, the Community Gardens at Auburn University “provides access to local produce through community partnership”. Educational objects were also frequently mentioned in SFG miss ion statements. Educational related objects are those related to learning, knowledge, and interest. 31 of the 46 (67.4%) mission statements mention education as an object to be provided by the SFG. For example, The Gardens at Texas A&M University “engage the community in a living teaching environment.” Justice related objects were the least frequently mentioned category of objects. Justice objects related to sustainability, environment, sovereignty, and hunger. 22 of the 46 (47.8%) mission statements include justice related objects. It stands to note that the majority of these SFG mission statements (19 of the 22) addr essed sustainability and the environment (environme ntal justice); whereas, only 3 SFG mission statements mentioned made any mention of objects related food sovereignty and hunger (social justice). Perhaps most stark, was the complete abs ence of any discussion of race or racism amongst the 46 SFG mission statements.

Two Emerging Discourses We have begun to notice the contours of two differing discourses about the purposes of SFGs. One discourse portrays SFGs as politically neutral and bound to the space of the institution. This discourse is characterized  by mission statements which: (a) draw a distinct boundary between the SFG and the broader community; (b) ascribe most social actions or processes to the garden itself (and therefore fewer social actions ascribed to other participants); and, (c) focus on delivering objects (whether natural, social, educational, or justice-oriented) to participants in t he garden or to community which the garden serves. A second, far le ss frequent discourse portrays SFGs as politically engaged and embedded within larger communities, regions, and cu ltures. This discourse is characterized by mission statements which: (a) embed the SFG in broader communities; (b) ascribe social actions to a range of participants; and, (c) discuss objects as being exchanged amongst participants who  are both a part of the SFG and a part of the commu nity. Furthermore, this discourse focused more explicitly on justice as a product of SFG (ex. food security, food sovereignty) rather than solely generating produce, social exper iences, or educational experiences. Now that we bet ter understand the landscape of discourses which inform SFGs around the U.S., we aim to document the discourses which are employed in our local research context (whether in advertising materials, or in interaction). We do this to exa mine tensions that arise between participants on the student farm to trace how competing discourses mediate learning and interaction.
