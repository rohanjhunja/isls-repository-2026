# Effects of Difficulty-Based Strategies on Finnish Vocabulary Learning: Sympathetic Arousal and Recall

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Acquiring vocabulary is an essential but daunting part of learning a new language, therefore impacting students’ motivation. Words vary in difficulty (e.g., similarity or dissimilarity to known words). This study compares different wordlist strategies for learning easy and difficult words in an experimental design. Participants will study Finnish words (encoding) and their English translations and test their learning (recall). To understand the most effective strategy, participants’ self-efficacy, perceived cognitive load, and sympathetic arousal will be measured.

## Introduction

Learning a new language is a long process in which acquiring vocabulary is as essential  as it is challenging (Hoshino, 2010), thereby impacting students’ motiva tion and persistence.  Accordingly, there  is a  need to investigate how to optimise this process, by understanding the causal relationship between working memory and vocabulary learning and how learning strategies  could make this process most effective  (Lee et al., 2020). Wordlists, organised in groups such as synonyms, antonyms, c ategories, and themes (Hoshino, 2010), have been used as a common and popular vocabulary learning st rategy to promote, organi se, and structure  vocabulary acquisition (Nation, 2016).

In addition, words vary in their difficulty, which could be understood in many ways, such as frequency of use, lexical characteristics, semantics, phonetics, or familiarity  (Li & Koda, 2022). While those factors could be subjective, another way to distinguish familiarity could be the contrast between loan words. These are words that have their origin in other languages  (Orcasitas-Vicandi, 2020)  f or instance, the Finnish word sauna has entered many other languages, including English, as a loan word. Similarly, many English words have entered the Finnish language with minimum changes (e.g. teknologiaa from technology). From the classical work of Bandura (1982), it is known that one practical and effective way of improving self-efficacy—one’s judgment of their ability to carry out a task—is via the increase in their performance. Based on this, easy words (here operationalised as loan words from English language) could boost performance, because of their familiarity. Accordingly, we hypothesi se that alternating easy and difficult words, could increase selfefficacy and by extension, motivation to learn the language. To test this hypothesis, we compare the effectiveness of three wordlist learning strategies combining easy and difficult wo rds, with the wordlist structured as 1) block of easy words followed by a block of difficult words, 2) alternating easy and difficult words, or 3) randomly arranged easy and difficult words. In addition to students’ performance as measured by their recall of the learned words, we will measure their self-efficacy, cognitive load, and sympathetic arousal. To evaluate the most effective wordlist, it is impo rtant to understand the cognitive load each strategy may exert on the learners. Cognitive load is defined as the measure of how much mental effort a learner needs to complete a task (Paas & van Merriënboer, 1993). Acc ording to Paas et al.  (2003), the understanding of performance and cognitive load constitutes a reliable estimate of the mental efficiency of an instructional method. In addition, Liu (2024) points out that reducing  the external cognitive load (the design structure of a task), and managing cognitive load, improves vocabulary learning and memory retention. Measuring perceived cognitive load during the learning process, therefore, will give insights into each strategy’s features such as  complexity, time pressure  and overall effectiv eness as the efficiency of instructional condition s is considered high if performance is attained with little mental effort (Paas et al., 2005). Nielson et al., (2005), in a wordlist memory consolidation experiment, infer that hormonal release during arousal enhances memory encoding. In th e present  study, a novel data modality, sympathetic arousal, the physiological activation connected to cognitive and affective processes (D awson et al., 201 7), measured via electrodermal activity (EDA), will be used. EDA, defined as the variations in the electrical properties of the skin due to sweat gland activity (Boucsein, 2012), is a promising physiological data modality (Pijeira-Díaz et al., 2019). This data modality  has been used to explain the relationship between arousal and perfor mance according to the Yerkes-Dodson Law. This law, also called the Inverted-U curve states that performance increases with arousal up to a certain point (optimal arousal) after which performance decreases due to impairing anxiety or the reduction in the attention to cues (Pijeira-Díaz et al. 2019). However, there is little evidence supporting this influential curve hence in this study we explore this learning constructs to better understand this law. While there is ample research on the  impact of motivation and vocabulary learning strategies (Sun & Fang, 2021; Xu et al., 2022), there is a dearth of research exploring the phenomena using multimodal data, which would enable a stronger understanding of the most effective strategy. Multimodal data offers new possibilities for studying students’ cognitive and affective processe s (Järvelä et al., 2021). Furthermore, whereas wordlists have been explored from various angles  (Schmitt, 2008), the word arrangement in these wordlists has not been sufficiently investigated. This study therefore uses multimodal data in form of self-report questionnaires, log data, and physiological data to understand the impact of wordlist design structure on vocabulary learning, and shed light on the relationship between word difficulty, cognitive load, self-efficacy, arousal, and performance while learning Finnish as a foreign language, with potential for extension to other languages.

## Research questions

1. What is the best wordlist strategy based on word difficulty for Finnish vocabulary learning?

2. How do difficulty-based strategies relate to self-efficacy in Finnish vocabulary learning?

3. How do difficulty-based strategies relate to cognitive load in Finnish vocabulary learning?

4. How do difficulty-based strategies relate to sympathetic arousal in Finnish vocabulary learning?

a) Is there an optimal level of arousal for vocabulary learning?

b) Is there a detrimental level of arousal for vocabulary learning?

## Methods

Design The research follows a within-subject experimental design  in a randomised controlled trial to compare groups using different vocabulary learning strategies. Participants are non-Finnish speaking adults (N = 100) tasked with learning 30  Finnish words. Relevant demographic data  will be collected, including age, gender, prior Finnish studies, other spoken languages, and time living in Finland. They will first study the Finnish words and their English translations (memory encoding) and then will be tested for their retention (memory recall). The hour-long wordlist experiment will focus on learning Finnish words under three conditions; of 1) blocked, 2) alternating and 3) randomly arranged easy and difficult words.

The Gorilla Experiment Builder (Anwyl-Irvine et al., 2020) will be used as an online lear ning platform as well as to collect log data during both learning and testin g phases. The experiment will be conducted in temperature-controlled computer classrooms, which is important when measuring EDA (Boucsein, 2012). Participants’ self-efficacy will be measured  using a customised  General Self-Efficacy Scale questionnaire (Schwarzer et al., 1995) at the beginning and  after each recall session. T he Paas (2003) scale will be used to measure perceived cognitive load for each word throughout the experiment. Sympathetic arousal will be indexed by EDA, which will be measured with  EDAMove4 sensors (Borovac et al., 2021)  continuously from beginning to end, including a baseline. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) will be used to compar e the three difficulty-based Finnish vocabulary learning  strategies in terms of word recall, self-efficacy, cognitive load, and sympathetic arousal.

## Points of discussion and expected impact

It is expected that different wordlist strategies may have a significant impact on vocabulary learning.

1. The alternating wordlist strategy may enhance vocabulary retention due to a balance in cognitive load

and possibly sustained self-efficacy. The block strategy may lead to one part of the task being too easy, while the  difficult part m ay cause potential  cognitive overload, higher (beyond optimal arousal) and ultimately low performance.

2. Understanding cognitive load, self-efficacy, and physiological arousal, as well as their interaction in the

context of Finnish vocabulary learning outcomes will inform best practices on wordlist strategies that could be extended to other foreign language learning settings.

3. The findings aim to inform learners, teachers, lear ning designers, and computer-assisted language

learning (CALL) technology developers on effective vocabulary sequencing that promotes self-efficacy and balanced cognitive load, ultimately enhancing motivation and learning.

4. Finally, this study will contribute to the understanding of the physiological dimension of vocabulary

learning processes,  largely understudied to date, hence contributing to improved vocabulary learning strategies and improved learning outcomes.
