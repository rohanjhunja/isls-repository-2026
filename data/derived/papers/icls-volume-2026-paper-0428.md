# Playful Learning: Debugging in Secondary STEM

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Research demonstrates that playfulness can support learning. Yet, in secondary settings, play is understudied; instead, playfulness is often discouraged as leading to “off-task” behavior. Here, we propose that playfulness can (a) be generative for STEM learning in ways that are unanticipated by the student, teacher, and curriculum, and (b) encourage persistence in students’ explorations. To illustrate these affordances, we present a case in which a 6th grade student, Hunter, playfully engaged in STEM modeli ng. We argue that his playful stance is a resource for STEM learning. Objectives, purpose, and literature review Research demonstrates that playfulness can support learning. A playful mindset allows an individual to “see situations and activities as containing possibilities for leading, exploring, and enjoying” (Mardell et al., 2023, p. 17). Playfulness encourages exploration by promoting enjoyment while reducing the seriousness of consequences (Bateson, 2014; Bruner, 1983; Pellegrini, 2011). In early childhood, play is recognized as a developmental necessity that supports learning and socialization (Paley, 2007; Piaget, 1945; Vygotsky, 1978). The benefits of playfulness extend to adulthood—in professional science, play enables creativity, innovation, and discovery (Bateson, 2014; Erickson, 1976; Feynman, 2010; Maurois, 1959; Watson, 1968). Yet, in secondary school settings, play is understudied (Dean et al., 2025; Johnston et al., 2023; Parker et al., 2022). In such settings (e.g., middle schools, high schools), playfulness is often discouraged as leading to “off-task” behavior. A significant body of research instead focuses on helping children develop skills and dispositions that enable them to stay “on task” by setting goals toward objectives and evaluating their progress toward their goals (Näykki et al., 2014; Ng et al., 2024; Ochs et al., 2024). When secondary STEM education research does consider classroom play, it often focuses on guided play (e.g., structured interventions, such as games; Hirsh-Pasek et al., 2020) that scaffold toward specific learning objectives (e.g., Kanter et al., 2011). Yet designing guided play can be challenging, because aiming toward specific goals can violate principles of play (e.g., that play is enjoyable/voluntary, or that play prioritizes means over ends; Pellegrini, 2011). Rather than focusing on play as an intervention, we instead consider how adopting a playful stance can be a resource for secondary STEM learning. We propose that playfulness can (a) be generative for STEM learning in ways that are unanticipated by the student, teacher, and curriculum, and (b) encourage persistence in students’ explorations. To illustra te these affordances, we present a case in which a 6th grade student, Hunter, playfully engaged in STEM modeling activities. We focus on his interaction with his computational model about guppies (fish) and their environment, and we trace the way that his playful stance supported generative learning about science and computational thinking, despite Hunter’s repeated encounters with potentially frustrating bugs. We argue that his playful stance is a resource for STEM learning and debugging.

## Method

This work is part of a larger design study (Cobb et al., 2003) aiming to integrate computational thinking and modeling into an NGSS-aligned 6th grade STEM curriculum (for details, see Pierson et al., 2023). These data were collected during a 9-week ecology unit designed to support students’ understanding of the flow of energy and matter in an ecosystem through engagement in model-based inquiry, including diagrammatic, physical, and computational modeling. Students used a visual programming tool (StarLogo No va [SLN]) to create their computational models.

The teacher (Ms. S) and the first author co-designed and co-taught all of the lessons for the project. Lessons centered on the phenomenon of guppies’ interactions in their ecosystem (with plants and predators or prey).  Here, we focus on a class session in which students had two tasks: (1) setting up their “biospheres” (closedsystem physical models), including adding live guppies and snails to their biospheres, and (2) adding to their computational models by using step-by-step instructions to build sliders (to set starting population sizes) and a graph (to represent population sizes over time) and by creating an algae breed in their models by using their existing guppy set-up code as a prototype. After Hunter had completed the computational modeling tasks of adding sliders, graphs, and algae to his model, he pursued his own goal (creating a predator). During the episode we analyze here, Hunter had created 3 breeds: “guppy,” “guppy-tester-2,” and algae. 2713 For our analysis, we transcribed video recordings of Hunter interacting with his computational model. Then, we identified an episode to analyze more closely. We selected this episode because it included both building and exploring a feature within his model (predation) and because it demonstrated how a playful stance supported both science learning and computational thinking. Here, we present findings from a 15-minute episode focused on resolving a bug introduced by Hunter’s program and a bug embedded in t he SLN system. To more closely analyze this episode, we created a multimodal transcript that included Hunter, his computer, his teacher, and any peers he interacted with. We then analyzed his goals and interactions line-by-line, considering the role of playfulness at each turn in supporting learning.

We analyzed a 15-minute episode in which Hunter worked to introduce predation into his computational model. This work might be considered off-task, because it occurred after he had completed the assigned tasks of creating sliders, a graph, and algae, and during a time when he was supposed to be working on his physical biosphere model. However, this exploration and creation (motivated and sustained by Hunter’s playful stance) created further learning opportunities for Hunter about computational thinking (control flow) and science (population dynamics).

In this episode, Hunter demonstrated playfulness by leading  (setting a goal: predation), exploring (debugging with a combinatorial approach, rapidly moving through combinations of blocks and population sizes), and enjoying (laughing, showing pride in his work). His approach was generative for computational thinking and science: his combinatorial exploration enabled him to recognize a solution inspired by his previous code and to begin to consider the impact of population size on population dynamics (a theme explored later in the class session). His combinatorial exploration allowed him to reflect on the  larger program he created, rather than focusing in on a specific block or sequence, supporting learning about the logic within this specific visual programming language (broadly, learning about control flow). Moreover, his exploration with different population sizes seeded further exploration that was generative for learning about popul ation dynamics later in the class session. When adjusting the populations from 1 to 100, he saw that the fish immediately ate the algae, whereas the algae population decreas ed more slowly when there was only one of each fish. This experience was foundational as he later explored different population sizes of predators, prey, and plants and made observations about rate of change using the graph (broadly, learning about population dynamics and ecosystems). We thus propose that Hunter’s playful stance was not only an affordance for learning in this context, but could also be a resource for STEM learning moving forward. His playful stance also may have contributed to persistence: he was motivated by a self-determined goal, and his combinatorial approach to debugging his code prevented him from becoming frustrated as he moved quickly through potential solutions.

## Implications, significance, and conclusions

Our findings offer implications for both researchers and educators. First, research on computational thinking and debugging seeks to understand how and why learners persist in particular contexts. Hunter’s playful stance offers one example of a resource th at might help students persist in potentially frustrating debugging contexts. Second, research about playful classroom learning focuses largely on elementary contexts, suggesting that there is limited research about what playful learning could look like in middle school STEM and why it might be valuable. Our self-directed play can also be a pathway to generative and meaningful learning in secondary classrooms. Still, our prior research details the challenges of supporting such heterogeneity-seeking learning (Pierson et al., 2023). Recognizing and supporting students’ diverse questions and lines of inquiry, even within a specific STEM topic, could be overwhelming for teachers and is rarely supported by curricula. Applications of this approach to playful learning also raise questions about feasibility and equity: while Hunter’s stance and skillset resulted in many windfalls for learning, other students  may not initially have the resources to identify and pursue such lines of inquiry, and so proposals to shift instructional designs toward playfulness needs to be accompanied by research to support inclusive participation. Further research is alsoneeded to understand how teachers could structure and facilitate such environments so that (a) students can share their findings in a way that benefits their peers, and (b) all students have opportunities to make “discoveries” that can be shared with the class. On e approach might involve looking for smaller grain-size creative tasks (for example, rather than large goals like “create predators,” identify low-hanging prompts like “find ways for agents to interact”). Despite these challenges, in secondary classrooms, where self-regulation is often emphasized over playfulness, there is great opportunity for researchers and educators to explore how to help students adopt playful stances that encourage exploration and discovery, both to support their learning in classrooms and new contexts. 2714 Bateson, P.
