# Emotion Expressions as Negotiations of Critical Technology Tool Use in Small Group Collaborative Design

**Conference:** ISLS 2023

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In a technology-rich learning environment where children collaboratively designed games from scratch, the learning processes are mediated by the technology tools and the socioemotional interactions. This study focuses on the group conflict around sharing the critical tool of iPad and examines the roles that emotional expressions play in mediating the interactions during the conflicts. Findings revealed that both negatively and positively activated and expressed emotions could hinder individual members from equally participating in the group design process, which indicated that to develop children’s socioemotional competence, facilitators need to observe beyond the displayed behavior such as dominance and disengagement and understand the emotional experience of learners to better support students’ reflection on emotional experiences.

## Introduction

Collaborative processes provide students with opportunities to develop competencies, but  only to the extent to which students participate and engage in the collaborative processes as part of a learning community. A more equitable learning experience where all group members contribute as valued par ticipants could not be realized through adding technology resources alone. Technological tools are value-laden and have direct impacts on the collaborative processes (Sørensen, 2009). The current study was carried out in such a learning environment where children engaged in design activities with the support of various physical and digital technologies. In the design process, solutions and knowledge are created through conceptualizing, sketching, and  building with materials. Human intentions and tools form a relational process, which indicate that w e need to understand how they constrain and enable each other to create knowledge (Mehto et al., 2020). As such, s mall group collaboration is an intertwining process constructed by individual members with the use of media tional means and cannot be reduced by the summation of individual contributions (Borge & Mercier, 2019). One key aspect is individual members’ affective state and emotional expressions which often have imme diate effect on other group members and the overall group process. Just like conceptual idea development in design groups, affective states could also converge or diverge (Duffy et al., 2015). Not surprisingly, positive socioemotional inte ractions are associated with positive effects, while negative socioemotional interactions with negative e ffects (Törmänen et al., 2021). Collaborative design in a technology-rich learning environment features a synerge tic interplay between humans and materials. Socioemotional interactions could impact the conflicts around tool use. This study is informed by two methodological frameworks of emotional expressions (Hufnagel & Kelly,

2018) and academic emotions (Pekrun, 2016). Hufnagel and Kelly (2018) explicated the four  dimensions of

emotional expressions, i.e., aboutness, ownership, frequency in discourse event, and emotion type, to reflect how emotions are “interactional, contextual, intertextual, and consequential” (p. 906). The “aboutness” reveals that an emotional expression reacts to a specific event in situ, and in the context of this study, the use of tools and artifacts in the collaborative design process. Pekrun (2016) posited that emotion in learning s ettings consists of multiple components and developed a taxonomy of academic emotions falling under two dimensions of a ctivation (activating/deactivating) and valence (positive/negative), giving rise to four broad groups of emotions relevant to the learning experience. These two frameworks provided an analytic lens through whi ch emotional expressions could be examined in terms of their roles in negotiating the opportunities in the uses of critical technology tools. Research purpose and question In the game design activity with Bloxels, the digital tool of iPad as the crit ical technology tool where final decisions of design products were made became a center locale where powe r relations were established, and negotiations were undertaken. Group mem bers’ negotiations around using this critical tool play a vital role in individual members’ participation in the design process and their learning opportuniti es around game design. In a collaborative learning space with young children’s laughter and tears, emotional expressions were both indicator and moderator in such negotiations. We ask the following question: how did group members express emotions as negotiations of iPad use in game design?

## Methods

The current study is part of a multiyear after school program in an element ary school in the Northeastern US. Three groups of 4-6 graders were engaged in game design using Bloxels through three main phases with multiple ways to participate in the process. First, teams used grid paper or the Bloxels kit to create game elements such as characters. Second, teams imported game elements to the iPad application. Te ams used the iPad Bloxels application to scan their designs and further modify their designs in the app. F inally, teams need to link game elements to complete the game. Each session lasted around 75 minutes, with about 10 minutes of introduction and 65 minutes of design. Three cameras were mounted on tripods beside each table to capture group activities. Video recordings started at Lesson 3 when we obtained copies of consent forms f rom parents as well as oral consent from students. Three groups agreed to participate in our study: G1, G3, G4. G1 cons ists of three 5th grade boys (Chris, Carter, and Mitch, all pseudonyms). G3 consists of two 4th grade boys (Eric, Theo), who were in our club before, and a third 5th grade girl (Aubrey). G4 consists of three 4th grade girls (Serena, Evelyn, and Sadie). Guided by Interaction Analysis (Jordan & Henderson, 1995), we first segmented th e video recordings into 2-minute chunks and documented content logs. Focusing on the social and materia l aspects, content logs documented how group members interacted and used tools as they made progress with designs. After content logging, the first author coded the segments for “resources sharing” issues around the use of iPad. The other two codes included “no building,” where there were whole class discussion or in vestigation and groups were not building their game, or “good,” when group members smoothly shared resources and ide as. Third and final step was zooming into places with resources sharing issues and transitions into and out of those problematic episodes, and to examine group members emotional status in relation to the tool use and group work engagement.

The f indings present the occurrences of resources sharing issues, coded in orange with number 2 in Figures 1, 2, and 3, as embedded in the overviews of three groups’ activities. “No building” segments are coded as number 0 and c olored in blue, while “good” collaboration segments are coded as number 1 and colored in green. The figures show a pattern of occurrences of resources sharing issues each session, and we then present detailed analys is of the emotional status when group members negotiated the sharing of iPad.

## Discussion

The common issue around sharing technology is the few members’ dominant uses of the technology tool while marginalizing others in the process. Thus, it is critical for each m ember tonegotiate opportunities to participate equally in the process. In the current study, emotional expressions are examined as means of negotiation in using the critical tool of the iPad in the process of game design. Our findings show  a nonlinear relationship between emotional expressions and the resource sharing issues. Confirming previous liter ature that showed positive CSCL 2023 Proceedings  © ISLS 284 socioemotional interactions are associated with positive effects, while nega tive socioemotional interactions with negative effects (Törmänen et al., 2021), G1 in our study had an overall negative c limate over the three sessions of game design. Mitch was constantly in an activated, negative emotional state, showing anxiety and fear, which was explicitly in verbal and nonverbal behavior in Lesson 5, where Mitch explicitly stated other members would mess up their design, and insisted on refusing to participate in the investigation, i.e., refusing other groups to play their game, even though neither Carter nor Chris showed resistance to the inves tigation activity. In reaction to Mitch, Carter was more in a deactivated yet negative state by disengaging from the group, with occasional confrontation to fight for opportunity to design in the iPad. Chris was reacting in similar ways to Carter. Interestingly, an overall positive emotional climate could also be a hinder to equa l participation in the design process, as shown in G4. All three members were excited about the technology and design challenge. They started drawing the characters and enemies right away and were excited to see their character walking and jumping in the iPad application. However, an external factor, i.e., a priori relationship be tween Sadie and Serena, positioned the third member Evelyn in the peripheral place, barring her from equally participating. As the unequal participation structure continued where Evelyn needed to kneel or stand in between Sadie and Serena to have visual access to the screen, Evelyn had to refrained from the activated, positive emotional state and express rather negative emotional state tonegotiate for a more equal access. The two contrasting group dynamics between G1 and G4 showed emotional expressions in themselves mediated group and individual inte ractions and played a critical role in negotiating opportunities in participating in the design process (Hufnagel & Kelly, 2018). We believe there is a need for explicit reflections on the emotional experiences and for intentional effort in regulating emotional expressions as negotiations for learning opportunities. To facilitate s uch reflection and regulation, teachers need to look closely for the emotional state and address the potential e motional threat that contribute to the issues relating to equal participation.

In G3, technology resources were shared and moving among all three members without conflict. Design ideas were welcomed, discussed, and extended. There were collectively a ctivated positive emotions such as excitement. The collaborative processes in G3 showed an intricate web of  effects among the socioemotional, artifactual, and conceptual processes. The emotional expressions are negot iating the participation opportunities and are consequential in conceptual engagement in design. This shows a direction for future research to include the examination of content negotiations and see the roles that emotional expressions play. To promote social and emotional learning, there is a need to further examine how emotions are expressed in the process of learning and how they are consequential to other dimensions such as conceptual learning. Only with such understanding can teachers play a critical yet positive role in engaging all learners, rather  than inadvertently further marginalizing those who are already marginalized by others in their group.
