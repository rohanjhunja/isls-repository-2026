# “Feeling That You're Not Alone”: Impacts of a Youth-led Community of Learners in Computer Science

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper explores how a high school group of women and nonbinary students construct a Community of Learners around Computer Science (CS) that provided support and emotional healing following experiences of discrimination and exclusion in male-dominated tech spaces. Through observations of club meetings and interviews with youth, this paper seeks to answer the following research questions: 1) What motivates students to form a Community of Learners and how do they build it? 2) How does this Community of Learners ultimately impact its members? This research begins to address a gap in literature regarding student-driven Communities of Learners, and how young people understand the value of collaborative CS learning and engagement of practices within social contexts.

## Introduction

A quick internet search of “computer scientist” image s depicts individual people working alone at a comp uter. One could argue that this is because a computer keyboard and mouse have been designed for use by only one pair of hands. However, a stereotype also persists that computer scientists are not social and do their best work alone. Yet, computer scientists actually do most of their work in collaboration with others. While K-12 education efforts have sought to emphasize this when teaching computational thinking and programming skills (e.g., through “pair programming” methods, with the inclusion of “collaboration” among K-12 Computer Science Education Standards, etc.), many C S courses and programs continue to expect students to program alone, be tested and submit their projects individually, and learn in situations disconnected from others. But what happens in a context where young women and non-binary students refuse this “solo” approach to learning CS, and create their own Community of L earners where they can share, collaborate, and enga ge with computing to fight for equity, diversity, and  social justice together? And what happens when this  student-led Community of Learners is built as a space of healin g and protection in the face of adversity in male-dominated tech spaces? While some studies have described Communities of Learners that support student-driven inquiry and agency (e.g., Watkins, 2017; Renzulli, et al., 2021; Theobald, et al. 2017; etc.), not many explore wh at sociocultural approaches to teaching and learning l ook like in a student-created and student-led Community of Learners specifically. This study fills this gap in research,  sharing how student learning and agency came to li fe when students were the ones designing their own Com munity of Learners that built a feeling of connection and belonging.

## Research questions

This study seeks to answer the following research questions:

1) What does a student-led Community of Learners involve?

a) What motivated students to form their own Community of Learners, and how did they maintain it?

2) How did this Community of Learners impact its youth members?

Theoretical and methodological approaches This work builds on sociocultural theories emphasiz ing that learning does not happen alone in a vacuum, but through human social exchanges with one another and  the artifacts we create, contextualized within spe cific historical moments shaped by human cultural practic es and perspectives (Vygotsky, 1978). Traditionally in Western cultures, however, educational spaces have been organized counter to this notion, with teachers standing at the front of a room, lecturing to students seate d at individual desks in rows facing teachers  as blank slates (Freire, 1970). Sociocultural theories help us see how learning occurs through shared experience, social interaction across diverse levels of understanding, and how ideas are transmitted beyond the simple “pendulum swing” between control and freedom of teachers vs. learner s (Rogoff, 1994; Vygotsky, 1978; Lave & Wenger, 199 1). Thus, people learn through participation in social communities while constructing their identities in relation to those communities over time (Wenger, 1998; 1999).

Our examination of youth-led CS learning is also informed by the concept of Communities of Learners. A Community of Learners can be described as a colle ctive of individuals who hold common values and bel iefs and are actively involved in mutual learning throug h shared sociocultural activities (Lave & Wenger, 1 991; Rogoff, 1994). Learners rely on each other and shar e responsibility for learning, contributing resourc es and perspectives, and maintaining a respectful environm ent. Some Communities of  Learners follow a knowledge-building approach, where learners construct new cognitive artifacts through shared goals, group discussions, and collective ideas (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2007). Ot hers focus on structured interactions with well-designed sequences directing collaborative inquiry activities (Biela czyc & Collins, 2005). The student-led Community of Learners described in this paper leans more toward the first category.

## Methods

This study took place in a student-created and led Girls Who Code (GWC) club  at a public school in California. GWC is a nonprofit working to close the gender gap in technology and change the perception of what a person in tech looks like and does. One of its key interventi ons is “clubs,” a free program for 3rd-12th grade students to build CS skills and join a community of supportive peers. The club described in this study was unusual in that most are created/run by adults, but this club was created by a pair of young women who, after  participating in a summer GWC program online, decided that other women and non-binary students in their community could benefit from having a place to socialize while learning CS. The two students were 11th graders when they formed this club, and they chose to have the club meet weekly for 35 minutes during a lunch period in their host teacher’s classroom. The host teacher was not actively involv ed in planning, organizing, or implementing the clu b, but provided the space and institutional support required for the club to “officially” participate in GWC. Data sources – including observations of club meetings and intervi ews with club members – were collected in Spring 2023 over a period of three mon ths. Five observations, that resulted in both video/audio recordings and fieldnotes, were conducted on Zoom, with the entire classroom space and all students visible. In these observations,  students discussed issues of underrepresentation, equity, and ethics s urfacing in a graphic novel they chose to read, called Power On! (Ryoo & Margolis, 2022). This book follows the expe riences of four high school friends in different schools, illustrating institutional inequities in access to computing education, how a lack of diversity in computing has resulted in racist and sexist biases in tech and AI, and how youth can take lead in addressing issues of inequity and underrepr esentation in their school s. Following these discussions, nine of seventeen total students were interviewed (based on availability during a busy spring semester). Data sources were analyzed by the entire research team (authors 1-3) through qualitative thematic content analysis to examine students’ perspectives and expe riences with the club and book. First, the research  team used Atlas.ti software to analyze data with a list of codes informed by sociocultural theory related to social interactions, context (space/place), belonging, identity, etc. The entire research team read every data source together, discussing differences in coding until reaching consensus. At the same time, the team developed an inductive list  of codes based on patterns in student discussion or behavior; these included codes such as “CS Views” with subcodes like “AI,” “ethics,” “gender,” “racism,” etc. Following every round of coding, the research team met  with GWC leadership (authors 4-7) three times to discuss codes and emerging findings, blending researcher and practitioner perspectives around the Community of Learners. This collaborative approach ensured that GWC leadership could weigh-in on typical and atypical club practices and norms, while also shap ing understanding of student experiences that the research team may have had less proximity to.

## Findings

A Community of Learners: Space for shared experience, values, and support When asked why they created th eir student-led club, Alexand ra and Karen (pseudonyms) explained that they wanted to engage in CS learning in a safe space where women and non-binary students could share about their experiences and feel supported. After attending a GWC summer camp the year before, Alexandra thought, “More than like the content being interesting, I felt it was really important to have community …I could definitely learn that stuff [html] like just online…[but] it was really important…having that support and seeing those people that are in the same boat as you. ” Not only did Alexandra value peer support, but she felt this community critically enabled learning for people who met discrimination in other computing spaces at their school.  She noted  the community “was really integral to go through the code” because solving GWC challenges with like-minded peers was more meaningful.

Club members described joining their GWC club for similar reasons. Maria described wanting a place where people understood her identity and experiences: “[I] like to go, because I know that it's like other girls like me who are interested in computer science, and…it's kind of nice to be surrounded by people who have the same interest as you and whatnot.” Asher remembered having conversations with Alexandra about their school’s maledominated CS club that neither felt comfortable joining “because it was literally all guys…and it wasn't bec ause they were mean or anything. They were just really s tubborn in that, like, programmer way: ‘Well, you c an't tell me to change my code. I know better than you.’ And it was very patronizing to be around that…and there were like 30 of them, and I was like: ‘No, I'm good.’ So  I kind of disconnected from that programming club,  and like the second I heard [Alexandra and Karen were] making a [club], I was like: ‘Sign me up! Put me on that list!’” In these ways, the club established a space where t he students could feel a connection with others fro m similar experiences and perspectives, a feeling of belonging that was missing elsewhere, and a space t o collaboratively make sense of computing together  in ways that felt like a Community of Learners, enc ouraging deeper engagement with CS.

Student-driven practices supporting the Community of Learners But what were the specific student-driven practices that encouraged connection, collaboration, shared learning, and belonging?  After founding the club, Alexandra and Karen made m any important moves to develop a Community of Learners that created the kind of “sup port” and feeling of being with “people in the same  boat as you.” These practices included: 1) Choosing to engage in discussion around issues of underrepresentation, equity, and ethics, and 2) Sharing and encouraging discussion of personal experiences with these topics to help members understand that they are not alone.

S pecifically, when Alexandra and Karen heard about the GWC opportunity  to receive free copies of Power On!  to discuss in their club, they immediately signed u p. When asked how they chose to lead book discussions that brought up issues of bias and discrimination in computing, Alexandra highlighted a central move to “draw on peoples’ responses to get peoples’ opin ions…so different people could share their voices.”  Karen noted that it was “important to ask how they've experienced various sorts of bias and exclusion in their own lives. Just because I feel like it was important to build a sense of connection, like the feeling that you're  not alone in experiencing these things.” This approach to creating connection and safe space  in the Community of Learners for personal stories was central to what club members identified as most  valuable when discussing issues of equity, ethics, and underrepresentation in computing. The two student leaders facilitated discussions like a book club among friends, eliminating any sense of age or CS ability hierarchy.  Additionally, Meredith explained how she appreciated that Alexandra and Karen would start conversations with their own personal experiences, “Because a lot of the times the things they said would remind me of something I had experienced…[but] didn't manage to articulate. So when they stepped up I was like, ‘Oh, thank you!’” Marla  agreed, noting how important it was to hear “thing s that are actually happening to people that you know, not in the book. It’s real life.” Impacts of the Community of Learners: Inspiration to address local inequities Following participation in the club and discussing issues of equity, underrepresentation, and ethics in tech surfaced in Power On!, a number of students articulated wanting to address inequities they saw in education and computing locally. More specifically, students wanted to build other Communities of Learners as we ll as find ways to use technology to have positive social impact. Particip ating in this Community of Learners inspired Karen and Alexandra to create a summer club for elementary students. They wanted younger students to have spaces  of shared learning and support  at their local library too.  Savana was also inspired to help younger children h ave a similar positive Com munity of Learners  experience and established a girls/non-binary STEM club at a local elementary school so “they can see themselves represented in the field.” She shared that their high school robotics team and physics classes are “primarily male and not very diverse” and she thinks this starts in elementary school because they “don’t have much exposure to those top ics.” So she wants to “raise awareness for all the fields of STEM.” Through these efforts, all three young women began to develop a sense of agency and empowerment to build on their experiences toward improving the educational experiences of others. Additionally, this Community of Learners inspired members to think about how to address inequities in tech that were discussed in the club. Meredith shared that she started “thinking more about how computer science can help other people, like being used as a form of  protest or being used to create art.” Computer sci ence was no longer simply a class to her as she felt compelled to think about how to mak e “a huge impact” and “think about what other things I cando with it.” Karen also shared how hearing other club members’ e xperiences in the Community of Learners while discussing the graphic novel gave her a new sense o f responsibility to educate others and “help other girls.” For Marla, participating in the Club and discussing equity issues in tech inspired her to want to “try and fix some of the problems that you sort of see arising from AI biases.” She said she wasn’t sure how to do this yet, but wanted to figure it out. In  these ways, participating in the Community of Lear ners had a positive influence on students’ sense of agency and desire to make an impact with what they learned.

## Discussion and conclusion

This paper explores why and how young women and non-binary students created their own Community of Learners and the impacts that Community had on memb ers’ own ideas and actions for the future. The find ings illuminate how young people recognize the value of learning in collaboration with like-minded others and see the importance of holding space for one another’s experiences and perspectives with a shared domain of interest, joint activity and information sharing, and shared repert oire of practice applied to  personal actions (Lave & Wenger, 1991; Rogoff, 1994).

While the club provided space for healing from negative experiences as women and non-binary students in computing, this was made possible by sharing about and discussing issues of equity, ethics, discrimination, and power in computing. Participation in these discussi ons deepened young peoples’ engagement with learning CS, motivating a sense of purpose that could have positive community impact. An important future direction for this research could be to further explore what young people do to take  action on their newfound convictions with computing. This work was limited by the short period of time  in which observations were made and the fact that not all students were available for an interview. A longer study of this group could be valuable for understanding the variations in perspectives across Community members and longitudinal impacts of participation on students.
