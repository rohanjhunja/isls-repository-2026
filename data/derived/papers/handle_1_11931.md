# Crafting Computational Thinking with Rope Weaving

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study explores how integrating computational thinking (CT) with weaving can make CT more accessible and engaging. Nineteen undergraduates participated in a rope weaving activity to recreate patterns, test structures, and connect math to tangible materials. Analysis of reflections and video data revealed students applying CT practices like pattern recognition and algorithmic thinking. Findings highlight the value of tactile, craft-based activities in supporting CT learning and bridging abstract concepts with real-world, embodied experiences.

## Introduction

Building on prior literature on computational think ing (CT) in collaborative learning environments (Huang & Parker, 2023), this study explores how weaving can serve as a tangible, accessible approach to computer science (CS) education. Weaving’s historical connections to  computational innovations like the Jacquard loom a nd core rope memory (Essinger, 2004; Schaffer, 1994) i llustrate its deep-rooted relevance to computational thinking. Its structured, patterned nature offers a meaningful me dium for exploring CT practices such as pattern recognition, algorithmic thinking, decomposition, and abstraction. While robotics and programming activities are widely used to teach CT (e.g., Bers, 2018; Resnick et al., 2009), they can feel abstract and inaccessible to some  learners. Weaving, in contrast, provides a concrete and tactile entry point. Prior work with tangible tools like Arduino and tabletop games has demonstrated the potential of ph ysical computing for improving CT skills (Kafai & B urke, 2015). Projects like Lee and Vicent’s “Looming Code” show how weaving can bridge physical and digital realms by translating patterns into code (Lee & Vicent, 2019). While their focus was on digital translation, we center the embodied experience of physical manipulation to exa mine CT directly. Using a constructionist framework (Papert, 1980; Harel & Papert, 1991), we designed a  rope weaving activity where 19 undergraduate stude nts recreated patterns with colored ropes to simulate w eaving.  The rope weaving activity embodies this approach, allowing students to construct knowledge by physica lly creating woven patterns, making abstract concep ts like matrix representations and cloth separability concr ete and explorable. This hands-on approach encourages personal discovery and experimentation, aligning with the constructionist emphasis on self-directed learning. The collaborative nature of the activity also highlight s the social constructionist dimension, where share d problemsolving fostered the application of CT practices. We analyzed  reflections and video data to investigate how students demonstrated CT practices and transitioned  from physical manipulation to algorithmic design. We ask:

(1) What types of CT practices do students demonstrate during the rope weaving activity, and how do they apply

these practices? (2) How do students transition fro m physical manipulation to algorithmic design when determining cloth separability?

## Methods

This study was conducted in Spring 2023 in an under graduate Informatics course in California focused o n computing, robotics, matrix algebra, and textile arts. Nineteen students participated, working in small groups (3– 4 students) based on diverse backgroun ds in programming, engineering, mathematics, and th e arts, as identified through pre-course surveys. The rope weaving activity, developed by the researc h team (Yankova et al., 2024), spanned two sessions and involved one hour of hands-on work. Students used ropes in two colors to simul ate weaving patterns, exploring cloth separability and matrix-based representations. The activity introduced concepts from Grünbaum and Shephard’s (1980) algorithm to de termine if a woven structure separates into two lay ers. Students first created patterns using ropes, then ph ysically lifted one set (“A”) from the other (“B”) to test separability (see Figures 1 and 2). They later discussed how to translate this process into a computational algorithm using binary representations, aligning with constructionist learning principles. Data sources included pre-and post-tests, video recordings from 360-degree cameras (25 minutes per group), and written reflections. The assessments, scored on a 0 –2 rubric (10 points total), measured students’ unde rstanding of cloth separability and their application of algo rithmic thinking. A paired-sample t-test was conducted to examine learning gains between the pre-and post-test scores. Video and written data were used to su pport qualitative analysis of how students engaged with computational thinking during the activity. ## Results

A paired-sample t-test showed significant improvement from pre-test (M = 2.95, SD = 2.42) to post-test scores (M = 6.11, SD = 2.82), t(18) = 5.66,  p  < 0.001, Cohen’s  d = 1.22, indicating that the rope weaving activity supported students’ understanding of cloth separabi lity and strengthened computational thinking (CT) p ractices. Students demonstrated CT skills such as pattern rec ognition, algorithmic thinking, and debugging throu gh iterative testing and rope manipulation. In Group 4, Priyank classified the rope strands int o “A” and “B” sets based on the projected matrix, stating, “The two middle ones are ‘A,’ and the first and fourth ones are ‘B’” (Figure 3). His classification prompted Kai and Hu to begin arranging the ropes on the floor to match the patte rn. After their initial setup, Kai suggested, “Let’s just try it,”  leading the group to lift the ropes and test the st ructure’s stability. When the weave separated, Hu pointed wit h his foot and noted, “This one isn’t matching what’s on the screen,” helping the group identify the error. They repeated  this process, making small adjustments and re-testing—embodying a hands-on debugging loop that reinforced pattern recogniti on, algorithmic thinking, and visual-spatial reasoning.

Across groups, students translated virtual weaving patterns into physical arrangements and decomposed problems into smaller parts. These embodied experie nces supported abstraction, as students connected p hysical patterns to matrix-based representations of woven structures. Kai reflected, “Initially, I struggled to visualize what the 0s and 1s meant, but with help, I learned to fi gure it out just by looking at the matrix math. ” Jess echoed this shift in understanding, explaining, “ We identified threads held down at only one interse ction, which allowed us to determine which threads were critical to the str ucture.” Reflections also showed how students transitioned from trial-and-error to more formal reasoning, designing algorithms to determine cloth separability. Some students noted how their algorithms mirrored the group’s physical strategies, while others proposed generalized solutions. One student emphasized, “The benefit of using an algorithm is that the process can be automated, making it faster and more suitable for complex tasks,” while another acknowledged that “physical manipulation doesn’t work well for random patterns, which require more trial-and-error.” These reflections illustrate how students navigat ed between physical exploration and abstract computati onal reasoning, using tactile engagement to inform and deepen their understanding of CT practices.

## Significance

Findings show that students demonstrated CT practic es through rope manipulation, highlighting the value o f tactile approaches. The significant improvement fro m pre-to post-test in students’ understanding of weaving concepts and cloth properties indicates how hands-on engagement with physical materials supports learning. This approach bridges the gap between abstract mathemati cal concepts and real-world applications, making complex ideas more accessible and tangible. By incorporatin g matrix math into the rope weaving activity, stude nts connected mathematical representations with physical outcomes, applying CT  practices such as pattern recognition, algorithmic thinking, decomposition, a nd abstraction. This study demonstrates how tangibl e materials enhance CT learning by making abstract ideas more concrete through physical manipulation and visual representation. Overall, this study has significant implications fo r the design of educational activities aimed at enhancing CT. The findings provide valuable insight s for educators to create more engaging learning ac tivities that integrate matrix algebra and CT practices.
