# Automatic Analysis of Transactivity in Collaborative Problem Solving Dialogues: The Relationship of Novelty and Reference

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Recent research has proposed a two-dimensional model for transactivity, encompassing the elements of novelty and reference. In this study, we further operationalize this model by identifying three distinguish levels for each dimension and we examine the relationship of novelty and reference in a case study. Specifically, we employ ChatGPT for the automatic coding of 12 chat dialogues of collaborative problem solving and examine how novelty and reference change over time and when and how the dialogues become transactive. Results indicated that both novelty and reference are volatile, with isolated peaks or short transactive sequences within a dialogue without a gradual build-up. In addition, it was evident that it was easier for students to reach higher levels of reference with novelty levels lagging. The study provides preliminary insights into the nature and dynamics of transactive dialogue and offers a way to instructional designers for supporting transactivity in collaborative learning.

## Introduction

Transactivity is  a crucial element during collaboration and is gener ally defined as building upon previous contributions of others (e.g. Teasley, 1997; Weinberger & Fischer, 2006). Researchers have explored multiple ways to operationalize the levels of transactivity  often intersect ing with collaborative learning processes  as learners rely on their peers as information resources engaging in paraphrasing or building on peers' contributions (Fischer et al., 2013; Demetriadis et al., 2018).  Gätje and Jurkowski (2021) have suggested that stud ying the linguistic features of transactivity can likewise e xpand our understanding characteristics of collabor ation. Exploring the internal dynamics of transactivity can thus reveal valuable insights. Recent research proposed a model for transactivity with two core elements: novelty and reference (Vogel et al., 2023). Introducing novelty underlines the importance of new concepts and ideas instilled into the discussion, while reference to previous part s of the collaboration ensures an interconnected dial ogue. The benefit of such a model is the opportunity to then study the interplay between these two elements, which may vary within differing settings of collaboration. For example, novelty may be prominent in brainstorming, whereas reference might be prominent during consensus building or reaching a c onclusion. Settings incorporating discussion of arguments could encompass a mixture of both (Vogel et al., 20 23; Weinberger & Fischer, 2006).  Identifying this interplay would allow the instructional designers to provide adaptive support and facilitate transactive dialogue. Basing our approach on the novelty-reference model on Vogel and Weinberger (Vogel et a l., 2023), we propose that these elements are not binary variable s but rather scales with distinguishable levels. In  the present, we operationalize our definition of the novelty-reference model and test it in a preliminary case s tudy of collaborative problem solving, providing empirical evidence on how such an approach could be implemented and what the insights are for the nature of transactivity in dialogues  of computer-supported collaborative learning (CSCL) settings. In doing so, we also explore the potential of ChatGPT (using the GPT-4o large language model-LLM) in automatically analyzing transactivity by applying a multi-layered validation process that tries to address

the inconsistency and ambiguity that can characterize ChatGPT outputs. Based on the above, the present study explores the following two research questions:

• RQ1: What is the relationship between novelty and reference in collaborative problem solving dialogues?

• RQ2: How does transactivity, as depicted by a funct ion of novelty and reference, evolve during

collaborative problem solving dialogues?

## Method

Data Our data consisted of 12 recorded chat dialogues of  the colMOOC project (Demetriadis et al., 2021) with a total of 590 chat messages  (M = 49.16, SD  = 18.00). In the project, Bachelor’s  students of a programming course attended an on-site, three-hour lecture on computational thinking (i.e., 3x45 minutes). During the third hour, the participants were randomly grouped into dyads and w ere asked to collaboratively solve a problem using computational thinking (e.g., define a decision process to decide which study program to enroll into). Participants’ collaboration occurred via an online chat tool  – N.B., while the project activities included a collaborative conversational agent, the agent was not present for that lecture. Operationalization of novelty, reference, and transactivity Table 1 presents our model. We perceive novelty not as a binary variable (present or not) but as the level at which a new concept has been introduced and integrated within the dialogue. Similarly, we distinguish between a direct reference to previous dialogue (e.g., verbatim or via a prono un) and a reference that encompasses a discussant's own words (e.g. rephrases or elaborations on what has been sa id), suggesting that the discussants have a better grasp of the dialogue and the discussed concepts.

Table 1 Scales of Novelty, Reference, and Transactivity Novelty  Description 0 (absent) Nonew concept is being mentioned in the message. 1 (low) A new concept is mentioned, but is not linked to what has been discussed previously OR a link between previously discussed concepts is created or modified. 2 (high) A new concept is mentioned AND is directly linked to previously discussed concepts. Reference  Description 0 (absent) No reference to concepts discussed previously. 1 (low) A reference to previously discussed concepts is made verbatim or via a substitute word (e.g., pronoun, adjective, etc.).

2 (high) A reference to previously discussed concepts is made by rephrasing or further elaboration. Transactivity 0 (absent) Novelty OR reference is absent (0).

1 (low) Both novelty AND reference are low (1).

2 (medium) Novelty OR reference is low (1) while the other is high (2) (i.e., 1-2 or 2-1). 3 (high) Novelty AND reference are high (2).

## Data analysis

Two independent raters coded the novelty and reference levels of two randomly selected chat logs using Table 1. Weighted Cohen’s κ for novelty (κ =.69) and reference (κ =.66) indicated a moderate, but sufficient agreement. Their scores were subsequently averaged and used as  the baseline in validating automatic coding by Cha tGPT (using GPT-4o LLM). Three prompting techniques were used (zero-shot, chain-of-thought, and tree of thoughts) on the same two chat logs. Each prompt included a requirement for an explanation of each code assigned to each message. This helped us in evaluating the output, b ut according to studies also improved performance (e.g. Ashwin et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2024). For each prompt, we calculated (a) its test-retest reliability coefficient, prompting each dialogue twice, and (b) its agreement against the raters’ averaged baseline using weighted Cohen’s κ (we similarly averaged two consecutive prompt outputs).  After several design iterations, the zero-shot prompt was deemed the most appropriate for automatic analysis, reaching excellent and moderate agreement for test-test reliability for novelty (κ =.85) and reference (κ =.58), respectively, and moderate agreement with raters (novelty: κ =.53; reference: κ =.49). This was not surprising as Clavié et al. (2023) reported that zero-shot prompts focused on reasoning often outperform more complex methods.  The zero-shot prompt was then used to code all 12 dialogue chats.

The analysis focused on the frequency that each lev el of novelty, reference, and transactivity appeare d in the dialogues, and the transition of the dialogues to different levels of transactivity.

## Results

Figure 1 presents (a) the average frequency of the levels of novelty and reference within all 12 dialogues, and (b) the levels of novelty and reference of all the messages of a selected dialogue  (i.e., 50 messages in this case). Novelty was, on average per dialogue, absent in 56% of the chat messages, low in 29%, and  high in 15%, while the respective percentages for reference were 20%, 55%, and 25% for absent, low, and high, respectivel y. Similarly, transactivity was absent in 62% of the chat messages, low in 13%, medium in 16%, and high in 9%. Further analysis showed that transitioning from a m essage with some level of reference but no novelty (i.e., 0-1 or 0-2) to any level of transactivity (i.e., 1-1, 1-2, 2-1, and 2-2) occurred 16.10% of the times, while the opposite (i.e., transitioning from 1-0 or 2-0) occurred only 1.75% of the times, suggesting that it was significantly more common for the dialogue to become transactive while there was already some level of reference in the previous message.

The examination of the Markov chain suggested that there is not a particularly dominant sequence, with the absent state (0) being by a large margin the mo st frequent. Specifically, it was more likely to return to (0) or return there from any other state (0 0: 65%; 1 0: 51.39%; 2 0: 55.56%; 3 0: 63.04%).  The steady-state probabilities for the Markov chain underlined how common the absent state was compared to the other states (0: 61.81%; 1: 12.58%; 2: 16.31%; 3: 9.30%). In addition, from all the states we would consider transactive, high (2-2) was the least probable, while low (1-1) and medium (1-2 and 2-1) were more probable and comparable to each

other. Looking back at Figure 1a, it is also apparent that the majority of messages with medium transactivity levels had low novelty and high reference (1-2), while the percentage of messages that had the opposite combination (2-1) was negligible (3%).

Figure 2b presents a typical example of what we could see in all the dialogues. There were three messages (out of 50) with high levels of transactivity (i.e., 4, 6, and 22), several other isolated messages with low or medium transactivity, and only one sequence of messages where transactivi ty remained at the same level (messages 8-910; medium). In other words, transactivity seems to appear as isolated peaks followed by gaps of non-transactive dialogue.

Another interesting finding is that transactive see ms to appear very early. Prior to the analysis, we expected that participants may need to have enough contributions, sharing their individual understandi ngs and discovering the norms of their communication, befor e they could start engaging and building upon each other’s contributions. On the contrary, the data presented vividly that transactivity in the case study we examined was not affected by the timeline of the dialogue, as peaks of transactive messages appeared without an identifiable pattern throughout the timeline.

## Discussion

RQ1: Relationship of novelty and reference The data analysis showed that, on average per dialogue, 38% of the messages demonstrated a level of transactive dialogue. Looking at the two dimensions we examined in this study, novelty was evident in 44% of the messages and reference in 80%. Even though there is no universally accepted measure of how much transactivity is enough for productive collaborative learning,  we can claim that in general, the participants tried to add new concepts and ideas to their dialogues, and by referring back often they also had very interconnected dialogues. Looking at the relationship between novelty and reference, the first interesting findin g is how prevalent reference is. One could argue that indeed the participants did a great work linking their new messages to what has been discussed. However, we could also identify a f undamental difference in the definitions of these t wo dimensions that may have resulted i n the figures we saw. As the dialogue progresses, t he reference landscape grows as the participants have more things to refer  back to. On the contrary, adding new concept s and ideas is harder in a mature discussion. A newly introduced c oncept raises the novelty tally once before it beco mes yet another topic the participants can refer to. Examin ing our model in Table 1, it is meaningful to wonde r whether “further elaboration” on a previously discussed concept should also merit  novelty points. The question then is “What is novel?”. In their novelty-reference model for transactivity Vogel and Weinber ger (Vogel et al., 2023) refer to novelty as the introduction of new ideas t hat would lead to collaborative construction of kno wledge. So, is providing more elaboration of a previously discu ssed idea also a way of injecting novelty into the dialogue? According to the academically productive talk frame work (Michaels & O’Connor, 2015), deepening one’s reasoning and engaging with each other’s reasoning are two distinct goals. While the former refers to argumentation and elaboration, the latter is closer  to the definition of transactivity that we use in this study. As such, we could argue that “further elaboration” cou ld have a different meaning depending on whether th e participants elaborate on their own or their peer’s previously introduced ideas. As such a path forward would be to modify our model, taking into account individual  contributions instead of looking at the dialogue a s a whole. This could provide a better picture of self-reference and genuine novelty. A major assumption in the operationalization of our model is that novelty and reference should co-occur. Even though Vogel and Weinberger (Vogel et al., 202 3) talked about alternations of novelty and referen ce, they also mentioned that these could co-occur, expecting, however, that this would not happen at  the same level for both dimensions. Our model distinguishes between different level combinations and the data analysis has indeed confirmed that having messages that have at the same time high levels of novelty and reference are not common. RQ2: Transactivity evolution As mentioned earlier, h ighly transactive messages appeared isolated. There were no clear patterns or gradual build-ups, for transactivity to evolve  as the Markov chain showed that the dialogue returned to m essages where transactivity was absent. In addition, peaks of transactive dialogue appeared  throughout the dialogues. This contradicts our expectations and previous studies (e.g., Gätje & Jurkowski, 2021; Vogel et al. 2023; Weinberger & Fischer, 2006) suggested about the transitioning from a high novelty phase early in the dialogue to a high reference phase while previously discussed ideas need to be evaluated. We can only hypothesize that perhaps the nature of the task and the study setting that followed a classroom lecture prepared or prompted the participants in such a way that transactive dialogue was possible e arly on.  However, if the observed lag  in novelty  is generalizable, it underscores the need for instructional designers to explicitly foster it (e.g. Vogel et al., 2023).

## Limitations and conclusions

Relying on ChatGPT for the automatic coding of the dialogue poses a great risk for the study. Utilizing generative artificial intelligence and examining its potential in education and resear ch is an interesting current topic. Acknowledging the inherent limitation of the techno logy, we applied a multi-layered validation approach that used raters as the baseline. However, it was also apparent that the coding task was challenging for the raters who only achieved moderate agreement, thus deeming the performance of ChatGPT sufficient.

In conclusion, this study provided preliminary empirical evidence on a scaled transactivity model based on novelty and reference. The results of the study suggested that reference was prevalent compared to novelty and that transactive dialogue appear ed throughout the timeline and in isolated peaks. Neve rtheless, the outcomes of the study can help the instructional designers operationalize and identify novelty and reference and provide adaptive support to students to help them maintain high levels of transactivity in their collaborative activities.
