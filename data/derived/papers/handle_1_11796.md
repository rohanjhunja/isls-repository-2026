# Teachers of Students with Visual Impairments’ Role in Supporting Nonvisual Spatial and Graphical Literacies for Blind and Low Vision Students

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Teachers of students with visual impairments (TVIs) are a specialized subset of special education teachers who differentiate instruction and learning media for students whoneed low vision or nonvisual accommodations. Oftentimes, these strategies involve tactual, auditory, and kinesthetic modalities. Recent US standards and policy documents (e.g., CCSSI, 2010; GAISE, 2020; NGSS, 2013) promote the use of visuals to support comprehension, analysis, and communication of core content in all curricular areas. For blind and low vision (BLV) students, the very modality meant to make information easier to grasp is a barrier to learning. Additionally, the ability to make sense of visuals is not trivial even for sighted children (Roth and McGinn, 1997). In this paper, we report an analysis of seven interviews conducted with three BLV access technology specialists and four TVIs as they discussed the teaching strategies and tools they used to support BLV students’ sensemaking of information typically presented visually.

## Introduction

Visual representations are a cornerstone of modern education, playing a critical role in facilitating comprehension, analysis, and communication across disciplines. Rec ent standards and policy documents (e.g., CCSSI, 20 10; GAISE, 2020; NGSS, 2013) emphasize the importance o f both understanding and creating visuals to enhanc e student learning. For example, the NGSS Performance  Expectation 3-ESS2-1 for Elementary School requires students to “represent data in tables and graphical displays to describe typical weather conditions expected during a particular season” (2013). These documents assume  creating visuals supports comprehension, helping a ll students convey complex ideas and process information (Bobek & Tversky, 2016). Graphicacy (Aldrich & Sheppard, 2000; Balchin & Coleman, 1966)  broadly refers to the ability to convey and interpret relationships through visual formats. Though often presented as a fundamental human skill, its visual nature poses challenges for blind or low  vision (BLV) students, as traditional instruction often la cks accessible alternatives. Even sighted students require structured support to develop these skills (Roth & McGinn, 1997).

Purpose and rationale BLV students cannot always access visual informatio n. Best practices for equitable access require prov iding materials in a BLV student’s preferred modality sim ultaneously with sighted peers. However, even with access, BLV students may not receive the same instructional support (e.g. explicit instruction, scaffolding) as their sighted peers in developing reasoning skills for nonvisual representations. Educators may mistakenly assume vi sual impairment limits engagement with visuals, restrict ing sensemaking opportunities and future academic or career paths.

While significant research explores how BLV student s can access information presented visually (Aldrich et al., 20002; Rosenblum et al., 2021; Zeb ehazy & Wilton, 2021), much of this research has fo cused on new technologies for providing alternative and time ly access (Beal & Rosenblum, 2018; Fan et al., 2022). However, access alone does not ensure comprehension or sensemaking, as even sighted children require structured instructional support to interpret and create compl ex visual representations (Lee & Wilkerson, 2018; R oth & McGinn, 1997). Vygotsky’s (1978) theory of the Zone  of Proximal Development (ZPD) suggests that studen ts learn best when guided by structured support, often  referred to as scaffolding (Wood et al., 1976). Sc affolding provides a temporary  framework that helps students develop skills they w ould not yet be able to achieve independently. Yet, instructional practices for BLV students often lack this necessary scaffolding, pa rticularly in the development of tactile graphicacy, or the abili ty to  interpret and create nonvisual representations of information.

For BLV students, instructional materials are often  differentiated through the tactile modality. Converting visual information into a tactile graphi c is not an exact translation. Figure 1 shows a sid e-by-side comparison of a visual (a) and tactile (b) vers ion of the reproductive system of a plant (BANA, 20 22). While the tactile graphic provides an essential, legally requ ired nonvisual alternative to visual representation s, it is not a perfect replication. The tactile sense does not allow for the same resolution as vision, so information density must be reduced. In Figure 1b, the number of petals and stamen is reduced, and textures are added to denote the different parts of the plant.

Teachers of students with visual impairments (TVIs) pl ay a critical role in ensuring these graphics are accessible and meaningful by using their pedagogica l knowledge to help students explore and make sense  of the diagrams. Only recently has the visual impairments field (VIF) recognized the need for a structured, s caffolded approach to teaching tactile graphicacy (Sapp & Hat len, 2010). Best practices for teaching tactile gra phicacy are still not well developed, leaving many TVIs underpr epared to teach this critical skill effectively. Addressing this gap is crucial to enabling BLV students to engage w ith tactile representations of information and deve lop the reasoning skills necessary for academic and career success. Given the critical role of TVIs in scaffol ding tactile exploration and sensemaking, this study seeks to better understand how these specialized educators support BLV students’ engagement with nonvisual representations of information. We address the following research questions:

1. What is the role of teachers of students with visua l impairments in teaching sensemaking surrounding

2. What teaching practices do teachers of students wit h visual impairments use to structure experiences

## Background

According to the World Health Organization (WHO), a pproximately 2.2 billion people globally have a vis ion impairment or blindness, with at least 1 billion of  these cases being preventable or unaddressed (WHO,  2019). Visual impairment refers to a significant reduction in vision, including conditions such as visual acuity loss, field loss, or neurological impairments, that cannot be corrected with glasses. The WHO World Report on Vision (2019) projects a rise in visual impairments due to aging,  lifestyle changes, and lack of eye care —particularly amongst low-and middle-income countries and underserved populations such a s women, migrants, indigenous peoples, people with certain kinds of disabilities, and in r ural communities. While visual impairment affects a  substantial portion of the global population over 50, young students with visual impairments often face education challenges that require specialized support. “Vision plays a c ritical role in every facet and stage of life” from  learning to walk, read, to participating in school and work (WHO, 2019). While world-wide statistics on visual impairments amongst children is difficult to aggregate, WHO (2019) estimates 19 million children under 15 years of age have a visual impairment. Such numbers denote visual impairment as a low-incidence disability in educational settings, meaning that occurrences are rare or low in numbers. Visual impairment is a high-needs condition requiring specialized teachers to support access to general e ducation (Miyauchi, 2020). Import antly, BLV students often require structured, guided instruction —scaffolding—to help them meaningfully interpret and engage with information that is typically conveyed through visu al means. TVIs play a critical role in this process, ensuring that students receive the appropriate level of support to work within their ZPD as they develop independent access and learning strategies.

## Blindness and low vision: Current teaching methods and modalities

Research, especially that of contemporary Western s ocieties, suggests that vision is the most widely u tilized and therefore studied perceptual sense due to its highly developed, complex processing abilities. (see Hutmacher, 2019 for a summary). For BLV students, education and daily experiences o ften rely on a combination of remaining vision and other senses such as auditory, tactile, and kinesthetic. Effective training in these sensor y systems is essential for BLV learners to develop concepts and  navigate their environment (Sapp & Hatlen, 2010). Effective listening and tactile discrimination skills are cru cial to concept development, defined in the VIF as "the organization of information into generalizations ab out the world" (Wormsley & D’Andrea, 1997). In part icular, hands-on, experiential lea rning is critical to BLV students (Spungin et al., 2017) and the introduction of tactile exploration must be deliberately scaffolded to support their ability to process and interpret information. Tactile and kinesthetic input can provide BLV stude nts with information about objects they come in contact with and use. In the visual impairments field, t his process of mapping the physical properties of a n item to its name is known as developing a concept. As su ch, effective use of the hands for tactile exploration is key. When possible, providing BLV students with real-life examples and objects (realia) for full develop ment of concepts is preferred (Wormsley & D’Andrea, 1997). When the real item is not available due to size (e.g. a plant cell or the planet Jupiter) or danger (e.g. fire or a tiger), the use of 3D models or tactile graphics becomes essential. However, using tactile graphics effectively require s structured instruction and guided practice, reinf orcing that this is not an automatic skill but one that must be  intentionally scaffolded by educators. Tactile gra phics—raised line drawings or embossed images —can also convey information symbolically such as ba r charts, chemical representations, and ma ps through a nonvisual modality. However, the conve ntions that are used in visual representations often leverage visual metaphors (su ch as red=hot and cold=blue; the use of small to in dicate something is farther away) that do not easily translate for BLV learners. Understanding and using a tactile graphic for learning depends on an individual’s experiences  with tactile graphics, the type of materials used (simple outline, textured 2-D illustration, 3-D objects), and the person’s level of visual experience (Bara et al., 2018). The role and preparation of TVIs Teachers of students with visual impairments (TVIs)  are a specialized subset of special education teac hers who differentiate instruction and learning media for students in early intervention programs (birth to 3) through 12th grade whoneed low vision or nonvisual strategies (Spungin et al., 2017). This includes direct instruction, concept development, accommodations, and material modificat ions that provide access to the general education curriculum. Their main role is that of an access specialist. TVIs support their BLV students’ concept development and sensemaking related to the impact of visual impairment by supporting the integration of new vocabulary (e.g. phases of the moon), accessible materials (e.g. tac tile graphics), and sensory development (e.g. effec tive use of touch).

While TVIs are knowledgeable about best practices s urrounding nonvisual access to the general education curriculum, they do not have the same pedagogi cal and content knowledge as general education teachers. Instead, teacher preparation programs for  TVIs fo cus on how to support BLV students’ access needs, conceptual development of information, and teaching  of an additional set of curricula called the Expanded Core Curriculum (ECC). The ECC is a set of nine skills —sensory efficiency, assistive technology, ori entation & mobility, social interactions, recreation & leisure, independent living, self-determination, career education, and compensatory access—developed exclusively for the unique learning needs BLV students have to compensate for vision loss (Sapp & Hatlen 2010). Many of these are as require structured scaffolding from TVIs to ensu re that BLV students can progress toward independent mastery. Within the general education classroom, a TVI’s role is that of a consultant to work with the classroom teacher on ways of making content accessible to BLV learners. Nonvisual spatial literacy and tactile graphicacy Many frameworks attempt to theorize sensemaking of spatial and symbolic information including visual, information, and/or data literacies, but most emphasize how this is done via the visual modality. This work draws from two areas which are less contingent  on the visual presentation of information: spatial thinking and graphicacy.

Spatial thinking is a combination of cognitive skil ls consisting of knowing spatial concepts, using representational tools, and applying processes of reasoning (National Research Council, 2006). It includes actions in space, reasoning about how the world works, and using spatial tools like maps or diagrams  (Metoyer  et al., 2015). Tools for conveying spatial characteristics include mental images, tactile (e.g. tactile graphi cs and maps) and visual (e.g. pictures and diagrams) media, auditory (e.g. sonification or description), and kinesthetic (e.g. 3D manipulatives and models) forms (NRC, 2006). Wakabayashi & Ishikawa (2011) developed a conceptual schema to clarify spatial thinking and its relationships (See Figure 1a). The top layer culminates in spatial literacy, defined as an individual’s abilities or attitudes to think spatially in an appropriate way (Wakabayashi & Ishi kawa, 2011). Embedded within spatial literacy is graphicacy (See  Figure 1a). Balchin and Coleman (1966) define grap hicacy as the collection of skills surrounding the use of symbolic or visual representations of information and the ability to create and interpret these representations. Balc hin (1976) linked graphicacy to visual-spatial abilities and denoted it as the first mode of communication, deve loping before articulacy, literacy, and numeracy (See Figur e 1b). According to Balchin (1976), early childhood is an ideal time to teach children how to understand and create representations of information.

Tactile graphicacy is a subsection of graphicacy focused but on the spatial quality of the thing that needs to be represented rather than the often researched and used visual modality (Aldrich et al., 2002, Ros enblum et al., 2021; Zebehazy & Wilton, 2021). Though tactile formats best convey spatial concepts, tactile literacy requires explicit instruction (Siu & Presley, 2020). By comb ining the use and creation of symbolic representati ons of information denoted in graphicacy and the different  modalities and tools spatial thinking states these representations can be conveyed through with a stru ctured sequence of scaffolded learning experiences,  we address the ability to develop nonvisual spatial literacy and tactile graphicacy  in BLV students. The TVI’s role in this process is essential, yet research on how b est to support TVIs’ instructional methods remains limited. By systematically scaffolding instruction in tactile l iteracy, educators can ensure BLV students are not merely accessing but actively making sense of complex spatial and graphic information.

## Methods

To better understand and support the role TVIs play  in their BLV students’ development of nonvisual sp atial literacy and tactile graphicacy, the first author interviewed four TVIs and later three blind professionals. The first author’s experience as a prac titioner, gaps noted in VIF-specific research surrounding STEM subjects, and research in the Learning Sciences focused on sensemaking informed questions for TVI interviews. An initial focus was placed on data literacy as a topic that emerges within academic domains (e.g. mathematics and science), is a common element of general literacies (fire maps, understanding public communication), and heavily relies on the visual modality. The four TVI interviews explored (1) practices for teaching tactile graphics use, (2) the TVI’s role in supporting nonvisual exploration and sensem aking of informational representations, and (3) cha llenges and gaps in developing nonvisual spatial literacies. Coding of the TVI interviews informed subsequent interviews with three blind professionals, which examined their educational exp eriences with visual information, including supports and challenges, and its implications for t heir professional and personal lives. All seven int erviews were coded and analyzed to develop four themes focused on supporting TVIs in teaching tactile graphicacy. Data sources The first author identified and interviewed all sev en participants using a convenience sample; the VIF  is small and the first author has served as a practitioner in the VIF for over 15 years. All participants were interviewed via Zoom video conference for approximately 60 to 90 minutes. Interviews were recorded and transcribed for analysis (see Data Analysis Procedures).

The four TVIs had a strong interest in tactile grap hicacy and nonvisual sensemaking. Isabel and Natalie (all names are pseudonyms) were former Orientation and Mobility Instructor (O&M) students of the first  author and each had over seven years of experience in the VIF. O&Ms work with BLV travelers to support safe, efficient, and purposeful travel including the development of body, directional, and environmental concepts, and spatial thinking (Sapp & Hatlen, 2010). Isabel was a multip le-subjects general education teacher in elementary sc hool and Natalie was a math tutor before pursuing their TVI, then O&M credentials. Katherine and Melanie we re suggested by a colleague of the first author due to  their years of experience in the VIF. Katherine ha d over 20 years of experience and began her career in the VIF  as an O&M while Melanie had over 25 years of exper ience and worked as a Guide Dog Mobility Instructor (GDMI) before becoming a TVI. Of the three BLV participants, Myles was a former student of the first author, Kenneth was a well known instructor in the VIF, and Delaney was suggested by  the same colleague who suggested Katherine and Mel anie. All three BLV participants had congenital visual im pairments, TVIs throughout their education, and fir sthand experience with inaccessible information. Delaney a nd Kenneth were born with usable vision while Myles  has been totally blind since birth. All three participa nts identify as blind and use non visual modalities to access the world around them including braille, tactile media, access technology, and auditory media. Additionally, all three blind participants currently or previously worked a s access technology (AT) specialists, though in dif ferent capacities. Delaney worked as an AT specialist at a  library, Kenneth as a retired AT teacher for BLV s tudents, and Myles as a digital accessibility specialist. Re sponses to interview questions surrounding their pe rsonal experiences in the education system,  professional lives, and daily activities were used to validate findings from the TVI interviews and identify gaps where further teaching supports can be developed. Role of the researchers The first author has been a practitioner in the VIF  for over 15 years while the second and third autho rs were introduced to the VIF through the first author in the last five years. The first author was responsible for designing and conducting the interviews while the first and s econd authors coded transcripts and implemented ana lysis. Collaboration with other TVIs and the first author’s personal teaching experience shaped the interview questions. Additionally, the first author’s closeness with the VIF and pedagogical knowledge surrounding nonvisual teaching strategies and techniques reflects an important perspective and understanding of the phenomena addressed in this paper. Collaborations with researchers from outside  the VIF including the second and third authors wer e sought in an attempt to reduce researcher bias and to provide expertise into the learning sciences’ focus on sensemaking.

## Data analysis procedures

All seven interviews were coded using an iterative process as outlined by Miles, Huberman, & Saldaña (2014). The first author coded Katherine’s interview through Deductive, Hypothesis Coding which was developed based on theory/predictions of potential find ings (i.e. student learning modalities & definition  of key terms). The first and second authors then worked together to code the  remaining TVI interviews. Inductive codes emerged during the first coding process and were applied during a second round of coding. Code types included Descrip tive Coding denoting participant teaching strategies (i.e. explicit teaching & adaptations), and in vivo coding utilizing participant language to create codes related to sup port strategies, needs, and barriers (i.e. “buildin g skills” & “I want”). In the third round, TVI codes were applied to the BLV interviews and new codes emerged surroun ding the experiences of the BLV interviewees (i.e. early access & non-academic use). Throughout the iterative process of data analysis, codes were added, revised, and re moved as patterns were noted and themes emerged. Ma ny of the initially identified deductive codes were remov ed or consolidated to focus on comparative and cont rastive analysis across multiple interviews and the instructor versus user experiences. Four major themes were identified and are discussed in the findings section.

## Major findings

Major themes discussed in all seven interviews included (1) pedagogical practices (2) the expanding role of TVIs, and systematic changes that need to be made in the (3) general education and (4) special education tea cher preparation programs. Within these themes, intervie wees noted a number of challenges surrounding the t eaching of nonvisual spatial literacy and tactile graphicacy. For example, time to prepare materials and teach concepts was often limited or nonexistent. In addition, while TVIs are not expected to be the primary instructor responsible for STEM or other core curriculum, this is often necessary in practice. Theme 1: Pedagogical practices In all seven interviews, pedagogical practices spec ific to BLV learners consistently emerged as a cent ral theme. Three key sub-themes were identified: (1) use and limitations of representational tools, (2) concept development, and (3) explicit BLV-teaching strategies. All four TVIs and the three blind profe ssionals emphasized the tactile and auditory modalities as primary channels for con veying information. Natalie (TVI) described obtaini ng representational tools from the American Printing H ouse for the Bli nd (APH) such as a foam double helix with distinct textures for each allele. However, she not ed “I wish it came apart,” highlighting the need fo r greater adaptability and accuracy of tools. In this case, students lack access to the computer simulation program on DNA replication their sighted peers use to understand a nd recreate this phenomena. When pre-made tools were unavailable or time constraints prevented locating and ordering tools, she created materials by hand. These scenarios highlight that TVIs need both pedagogical  knowledge specific to blindness and a strong under standing of subject-specific standards. They also point to a lack of em phasis in supporting BLV students in their own creation of representational tools to deepen spatial understanding of key concepts. BLV-specific pedagogical practices including concept de velopment and explicit teaching strategies, were consistently emphasized by all interviewees. A ll four TVIs emphasized the importance of pre-teaching and previewing materials—especially complex ones—for BLV learners. These strategies allow TVIs to introduce new vocabulary and subject matter in advance, ensuring students are prepared when these topics are later presented in the classroom. Melanie (TVI) stressed the necessity of providing materials “really tailored to their learning needs and their visual impairment,” reinforcing the diverse learning modalities and sensory integration abilities of BLV students. Each lesson must be individualized to accommodate these variations. Beyond pre-teaching, TVIs discussed the systematic approach required for building skills and navigating tactile graphics. Katherine (TVI) noted the contrast between sighted and BLV students in interpreting graphs: “It’s quicker visually for a kid to understand and to be able to see the intersection of a row and a column and use that to get a data point,” whereas BLV students must tactually track across rows and columns to identify intersections. Though this process requires time and practice, eng aging with representations tactilely fosters deeper  spatial understanding, reinforcing the link between height and quantity in graphical data. Melanie (TVI) emphasized the cognitive effort required for tactile graphic sensemaking, explaining that her students “really need to explore, and they need time to explore the whole thing,” which i s why she pre-teaches concepts and tactile exploration techniques before students encounter them in class.  Delaney (BLV) summarized nonvisual sensemaking succinctly: “It comes down to equipment, expectations, and prior learning.” TVIs must be well-versed in available tools, proficient in teaching their use, and confid ent in their students' ability to process and inter pret information non-visually.

Theme 2: An expanding role TVIs play in the development of nonvisual graphicacy In many of the interviews, the role of the TVI expa nded beyond access specialist into the realm of con tent specialist. Natalie (TVI) mentioned the need to know math and science standards so she could pre-teach concepts before classroom lessons, and Isabel (TVI) mentioned her students’ use of the abacus being when she (rather than the classroom teacher) teaches  them “Number sense, one-to-one correspondence, how to add and subtract, take away, basic skills in kindergarten and preschool.” While Natalie’s background is in mathematics and Is abel was a multiple-subjects elementary teacher before becoming a TVI, many TVIs are less comfortable with STEM content. Yet, they need to know enough to portray i nformation correctly and collaborate with the class room teacher when they do not have the necessary content knowled ge. According to Isabel and Katherine (TVIs), collaboration with the classroom teachers is a must, including having conversations and checking the t eacher’s guide. TVIs must know upcoming lessons to prepare accessible materials, pre-teach concepts, and support student learning.

As Myles (BLV) put it, “I think TVI's role from K-12 is to help the students gain the skills that they need to narrow the gap of performance between them and t heir sighted peers.” For example, when discussing h ow to present data, Melanie (TVI) asked “So am  I the keeper of the data? Do I say what they get and what they don't get?” Melanie was discussing the ethical decisions TVIs make to reduce information so it can be access ible yet not reducing it to the point of limiting her studen ts’ sensemaking and deci sion making abilities. Additionally, Isabel (TVI) brought up personal bias when working with her students saying that “You can't use your o wn knowledge or your own emotions on how you view thin gs. You have to make it more fact and concrete abou t what you're seeing.” Both TVIs highlighted their ex panding role beyond access specialists to include responsibilities traditionally associated with content specialists making collaborations even more important. Theme 3: Systematic changes within general education All four TVIs emphasized the need for systematic ch anges in general education to better support their ability to teach tactile graphicacy and enhance BLV students’ learning outcomes. A key concern was time: the time required to prepare materials, teach tactile concepts, and a llow students to explore tactile graphics in advanc e. Delaney (BLV) highlighted this challenge, stating, “Tactile graphics take more time to make than a description does.” Yet, many TVIs lack sufficient material preparation time. Natalie (TVI) explained that she often doesn’t “get anything until the morning of,” leaving her with just a 60-minute period to create 3D assignments for her blin d student’s classes that same day. Melanie (TVI) underscored the link between preparation time and material quality, noting, “The quality depends on how much leeway we're given. When given enough time, there can be some spectacular materials.” Most TVIs work itinerantly and manage full or even overloaded caseloads. Without adequate lead time, the quality of materials suffers, and concepts cann ot be effectively taught in advance. This limits BL V students' ability to engage in sensemaking and crea tes inequitable educational experiences. In such ca ses, effective communication and collaboration between TVIs and ge neral education teachers, as discussed in Theme 2, becomes critical. Unfortunately, many general educa tion teachers lack an understanding of the unique l earning needs of BLV students or how TVIs support these learners. Melanie (TVI) captured this issue, stating: “I feel like teacher preparation programs don't really go over the special needs and accommodations. I think that there should be more of that, like how if you have students in your c lassroom that need accommodations, how do you make that happen? Or who do you align with? Where do you get the help? Not that the teacher has to do it all, but how do you find out how to get that help to present [information] to the students so that everybody gets it?” The accessibility of general education materials, d etermined at both the classroom and district levels, significantly impacts TVIs’ ability to prepare reso urces. Isabel (TVI) described spending extensive ti me training general education teachers on what is or is not accessible and encouraging them to adopt alternative ways to present materials so that BLV students can have equ itable learning opportunities. Natalie (TVI) echoed  this concern, explaining that some teachers fail to send her any materials at all. She also criticized schools and districts for adopting inaccessible curricular programs, stat ing, “They shouldn’t be using things that are inacc essible without alternate media. Period.” Accessible curric ula would let TVIs focus more on teaching critical concepts, sensory development, and integration than adapting materials. Theme 4: Systematic changes within TVI preparation programs While TVIs focused on systemic changes to support B LV students in general education, Delaney, Kenneth,  and Myles emphasized the need for reforms in TVI prepar ation programs. Kenneth (BLV) advocated for tactile graphicacy to be central to TVI training, stating, “Tactile graphics should be part of their training as a major—not just a tool set, but a learning process.” Myles (BL V) reflected on the need for training that addresse s “how to approach data visualizations and think of them as a  blind person.” Both highlighted the importance of TVIs learning to create accessible materials and teach c ritical concepts in sensory development, spatial li teracy, and graphicacy.

A shared priority among the three blind participant s was ensuring early and consistent access to tacti le graphics. Kenneth (BLV) pointed out that the VIF of ten prioritizes teaching braille as a code rather t han reading and writing literacy, and graphicacy. He explained, “We never introduced pictures and spatial relationships at an early enough age to help students acquire that kind  of information at the same developmental period as  sighted kids.” Delaney (BLV) highlighted the disparity between sighted children, who are immersed in visual information, and BLV children, who depend on intentional efforts by their support systems to provide access to tactile graphics. She stressed the need for proactive exposure, notin g, “We really have to make a special effort —and the people supporting us have to make a special effort—to get enough graphics under our hands.” These gaps in early instruction have long-term effects. Myles (BLV) shared that limited expos ure to tactile graphicacy influenced his decision to pursue liberal arts, avoiding fields requiring spatial literacy. This lack of access not only shapes career pa ths but also affects the ability to interpret perso nal data, such as financial or health trends. Delaney (BLV) also highlighted the l ong-term consequences of insufficient early instruction, stating, “If we weren’t given graphical literacy in struction early  on, we might undervalue graphical communication and say, ‘I don’t need to see those s tats or that chart; I don’t understand it, and I do n’t need it.” Delaney also emphasized the importance of teaching BLV students to create their own representations and navigate inaccessible systems while Natalie (TVI) ec hoed this as a skill her students are lacking in, e mphasizing the need to equip TVIs to teach this critical layer  of tactile graphicacy so as to fosters independenc e and greater equity in spatial literacy

## Discussion and implications

Lack of timely access to information in an accessible format disadvantages individuals in education, careers, and daily life. To support BLV students’ development in spatial literacy and tactile graphicacy, TVIs must be equipped to explicitly teach these skills, general educators need to embrace a collaborative approach, districts must prioritize accessible curricula aligned with Universal Design for Learning (UDL; CAST 2024), and teacher preparat ion programs must address these needs comprehensively. The TVIs interviewed emphasized their commitment to curricular accessibility but acknowledged significa nt challenges in scaffolding instruction and foster ing their students’ sensemaking. Without TVIs fully prepared to teach these essential skills, how can BLV students systematically develop and apply them within or bey ond the education system? Without adequate training, can TVIs effectively guide students in navigating, inte rpreting, and advocating for accessible graphics or  using their learning in future contexts? The level of support BLV students receive in developing tactile graphicacy and spatial literacy should not depend on chance but on a well-prepared, collaborative educational system. Spatial literacy and graphicacy are not exclusive t o the visual sense (Aldrich et al., 2002; NRC, 2006). Understanding how to design tactile graphics that support nonvisual concept development and teach sensemaking through nonvisual modalities are crucial.  Embodied learning and other "learn by doing" framew orks could offer valuable insights into nonvisual learning processes. These approaches emphasize active engagement, which aligns with scaffolding techniques that support BLV studen ts as they gradually build competence in tactile graphicacy. Such research alignments would also serve other sensory-and neuro-diverse students who benefit from descriptive language, accessible materials, and tangible learni ng tools. These align with UDL principles, which, t hough imperfect, provide a foundation for more equitable educational practices benefiting all learners. More research is needed to explore how BLV students  develop nonvisual spatial literacy and tactile graphicacy, as well as how TVI preparation programs teach pre-service teachers to scaffold these skills effectively. Future studies could investigate how early exposure to tactile graphics affects long-term spatial reasoning in BLV students, or how co-designed materials by general educators and TVIs im pact student independence and self-advocacy. As noted in the Methods section, all four  TVIs taught travel skil ls either as O&Ms or GDMI. Further research into how TVIs, O&Ms, and dual-certified teachers support nonvisual spatial litera cy is essential to identifying gaps in pedagogical training and instru ctional scaffolding. Additionally, engaging general  educators who have BLV students in their classrooms could ide ntify needed supports, clarify the role of TVIs, and reveal gaps in general education teacher preparation programs surrounding the unique learning needs of sensory-diverse students.
