# Effects of Narratives on Undergraduate Student Understanding of Fundamental Concepts in Biology

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Undergraduate students frequently struggle to understand biological concepts. Embedding conceptual information in a historical narrative holds the potential to support their learning. We developed learning materials comparing narrative or expository texts and assessed studentsÕ conceptual understanding via post-tests. Results showed that students, who read the narrative text, understood the concept of randomness better. No difference was found for the enzyme energetics concept. ## Introduction

Recent studies have shown that university students often fail to fully understand biological concepts,  including enzymatic energetics or stochastic processes (i.e.,  randomness) on a molecular level, and retain incor rect conceptions despite instruction (Champagne Queloz et al., 2016). Along with university lectures, textbooks, written  as expository texts, are a major source of information for natural science undergraduate students. Despite  their ubiquitous use in natural science education,  there are also caveats regarding presenting concepts abstract ed from real-world contexts. For example, theoretic al and empirical evidence argues for more context-dependent, enculturating and social forms of learning and t eaching (Brown et al., 1989). In contrast, narratives naturally provide a social and cultural learning environment that may reduce the abstractness of concepts (Avraamidou & Osborne, 2009). This could allow learners to better relate new ideas to prior experiences and social contexts (Dahlstrom, 2014). Further, narratives may facilitate the activation of prior knowledge (including knowledge about related concepts from different disciplines) and in addition to that the integration of new concepts into the pre-existing knowledge framework instead of learning isolated knowledge fragments (Dahlstrom, 2014). Eventually, using narr ative elements in educational texts could prompt dissatisfaction with incorrect student explanations more efficiently, ultimately supporting conceptual change from prior explanations to scientifically accepted theor ies. However, recent research findings report contr adicting evidence regarding the effectiveness of using narrative-embedded theory elements to improve the understanding of biological concepts in higher education: learning with narratives resulted in higher (Dai et al., 2021), but also lower (Wolfe and Woodwky, 2010) post-intervention performance in contrast to learning with expository texts. Therefore, the present study aims to investigate:

1. What are the effects on understanding when using narratives compared to expository text materials?

2. How do the different text materials affect the situational interest towards the topic affected?

We hypothesize that narrative text materials lead to greater conceptual understanding than expository ones, and that students in the narrative intervention group show increased situational interest in the topic.

## Methods

Sixty-one first-year undergraduate natural science students (20.1 ± 1.6 years old; 61.6% female, 38.4% male, 0% non-binary) from a highly ranked Swiss university p articipated in the study. The universityÕs ethics c ommittee approved the study before implementation. Participation was voluntary, and no compensation was paid. Text materials covered the concepts Òenzyme energe ticsÓ and ÒrandomnessÓ. These topics were selected as they were shown to constitute frequent difficulties in understanding for natural science students (Champagne Queloz et al., 2016). The conceptual information wa s embedded in a historical context for the narrativ e text materials. The expository text focused solely on co nceptual information. The validity of the text mate rials was analyzed with experts in biology and literature. Th e narrative text was slightly longer than the expos itory one (narrative: 112 sentences, expository: 79 sentences), but displayed similar text characteristics, incl uding typetoken ratio (TTR) (narrative TTR: M = 0.39, SD = 0.12; expository TTR: M = 0.39, SD = 0.16) and Flesh readability indices (narrative: 37.02, predicate difficult; expository: 41.56, predicate difficult). A single-item question on a five-point Likert scale was used to measure situational interest.

Participants were randomly assigned to read either  the narrative text (n = 31) or the expository text (n =

30) before the students took the post-test and were then asked to answer the questions covering putative learning

mechanisms. An ANOVA was conducted to determine post-test performance differences. Differences regarding learning mechanisms were assessed by performing a Kruskal-Wallis test.

## Results

The ANOVA revealed that students in the narrative group performed significantly better in the randomness test questions (F(1, 59) = 4.337, p =.0492, d = 0.51, 95% CI = [-0.01, 1.04]). In con trast, no statistically significant performance difference was found for the enzyme energetics concept (F(1,59) = 0.365, p = 0.548, d = 0.15, 95% CI = [-0.36, 0.67]). Thus, we found partial evidence supporting the hypothesis stating that learning with narrative texts could lead to greater conceptual understandin g. Concerning situational interest, students in the  narrative group reported a significantly higher interest in the topic than students from the expository group (H(1) = 4.52, p =.033, BF10 = 9.80). Hence, we found evidence in favor of the hypothesis regarding higher situational interest in the narrative intervention group compared to the expository intervention group.

## Discussion and conclusion

Prior research has demonstrated the need for novel teaching approaches in biology at the university le vel. Embedding conceptual information in narratives could constitute an effective way to convey biological concepts than expository texts. In the present study, the use of narrative learning materials resulted in a significantly better performance on a post-test related to questions on the concept of randomness compared to the expository materials. Moreover, situational interest after the intervention was significantly higher in the narrative group. Demonstrating a greater interest could thereby go hand-in-hand wi th greater cognitive activation and more active eng agement with the materials. No differences were found for the enzyme energetics concept, potentially because a relatively low number of questions was used to infer studentsÕ  understanding of this concept, and a ceiling effec t was observed. Hence, extending the number of conceptual  questions, as well as adding questionnaires invest igating potential learning mechanisms, could help better un derstand whether and how the different text sorts a ffect understanding.

In conclusion, our results provide support for the effective use of narratives to enhance understanding of natural sciences in higher education. Given that a well-grounded scientific understanding is crucial t o discuss, evaluate, and eventually solve current real-world p roblems, it will be essential to find ways of impro ving the learning of core biological concepts. Our study might thus inspire other developments in this area.
