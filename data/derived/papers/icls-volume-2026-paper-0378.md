# Unpacking Modeling-Based Learning in Kindergarten: A Micro-Level Discourse Analysis

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This case study explores how Modeling-based Learning (MbL) unfolds in a kindergarten science classroom through a micro-level analysis of the 24 children's discourse (aged 5-6) during a 35-minute whole-class discussion on water diffusion on a napkin. Findings indicate that participating children c ould engage in complex mechanistic reasoning when supported through structured MbL cycles and close teacher facilitation. The study highlights the importance of teacher monitoring different scalar-level mechanistic reasoning and scaffolding the MbL discourse.

Modeling is widely  recognized as a core  practice in science and engineering (e.g., Grandy & Duschl, 2007; Nersessian & Patton, 2009), and  modeling-based learning (MbL) has gained support as an effective approach to science education (e.g., Gilbert & Justi, 2016; Halloun, 2016;  Osborne, 2014; Schwarz et al., 2009; Lehrer & Schauble, 2010). In science education, MbL involves constructing, evaluating, revising, and using models to explain and predict phenomena (NRC, 2013).

Models developed through MbL can take diverse forms, including everyday language, drawings, diagrams, gestures, dramatic play, 3-D structures, computer simulations, and mathematical equations (Gilbert, 2004). Model development necessitates  the representation of elements relevant to  the phenomenon and the corresponding underlying mechanisms that exhibit  how these elements interact  (NRC, 2012). In this sense, mechanistic reasoning involves explaining phenomena by identifying the entities involved, their actio ns, properties, organization, and the causal relationships between them  (Louca et al., 2011). Russ et al. (2008) described a framework for  mechanistic reasoning: descriptions of the target phenomenon, identification of the setup conditions, entities, their  activities, properties, organization, and chaining. MbL also involves analogical reasoning, which enables learners to present predictions or explanations for an  unknown system while drawing information from a second known system. May et al. (2006) proposed a framework for analogical reasoning that includes identification, generation, evaluation, and use of analogies. This case study investigated the kindergarten children’s capacity to participate in MbL practices and the teachers’ support.  It investigated an MbL 35-minute whole-class discussion with a group of 24 children exploring water diffusion on a napkin. Their discussion focuses on the mechanism underlying the phenomenon, interpreted through the children’s second set of personal models and the collaborative development of a revised model. This descriptive case study (Yin, 2017) was conducted with 24 children aged 5 to 6 years at a public kindergarten school in Cyprus. One videotaped lesson was transcribed and analyzed to generate rich descriptions of children’s engagement in MbL and the teacher’s facilitation. The analysis followed two complementary methodological approaches. First, children’s conversations were examined to explore their thinking and learning, following discourse-based research (e.g., Criswell et al., 2021), thereby enabling detailed accounts of the MbL conversation's context and content.  Second, the discourse was systematically cod ed using four analytic frameworks. The first, by Russ et al. (2008), categorized instances of seven mechanistic reasoning components. The second applied a modified version of May et al.’s (2006) scheme of four  analogical reasoning components. A third analysis coded discourse for the four MbL phases described by Louca & Zacharia (2015) (investigate the phenomenon, construct, evaluate, and revise a model). Lastly, a fourth analysis was based on Louca & Zacharia’s

(2012) study, coding what the teacher responded to (mechanistic reasoning, analogical reasoning, or  MbL

elements), and how she responded (with prompts, clarifications, evaluation and restatement of ideas or reasoning). The lesson analyzed below occurred after four prior lessons in which children investigated the phenomenon and developed, evaluated, and revised an initial  model. At the outset, the teacher directed attention to  the fact that in children’s second models, the diffusion of water on a napkin was explained by suggesting that the number of water “drops” increased over time. The teacher identified two obstacles. First, the children used the word “drop” to refer both to the visible, initial drop placed on the napkin and to the water molecules within that drop. This 2560 ambiguity hindered their ability to distinguish between  macroscopic observations and molecular-level explanations. This lack of precision contributed to a second difficulty: the “initial drop” was a macroscopic event, whereas “drops” (i.e., molecules) represented  a lower scalar level description (Krist et al., 2019). Despite that, this idea originated from a familiar analogy from everyday childrens’ experiences with living organisms. Although this analogy can be productive, it must be applied at the appropriate scalar level. Without separating these levels, many children remained at the macroscopic level, unable to articulate mechanistic reasoning. The analysis (Figure 1, Part 2) shows that the children nevertheless attempted to engage mechanistically by identifying entities involved in the phenomenon and their activities.

Figure 1 Figure 2 Children’s MbL Discourse Analysis To clarify distinctions  between the initial drop and the water molecules, the teacher repeated the experiment. Subsequent discussion (Part 3) focused on children’s observations and was coded as a description of the target phenomenon. T hen, the teacher guided children toward interpreting these observations (Parts 4– 5). Mechanistic reasoning increased in richness, including references to the phenomenon, the setup conditions, entities’ activities, and chaining. As the discussion progressed (Parts 6–9), children’s contributions demonstrated both mechanistic reasoning (describing the target phenomenon, setup conditions, entities, their activities and organization, and chaining) and analogical reasoning (proposing  analogies). In terms of the MbL cycle, Parts  7– 9 reflect model construction. Several minutes later, in response to the teacher’s continued probing for a plausible explanation, a child suggested  that the initial water drop consists of smaller “ droplets”—a newly introduced word—that detach and move outward in all directions. Part 10 shows how the group spent time refining this idea by exploring the activities of these newly conceptualized entities. In Parts 11–14, the teacher introduced dramatic play as a new modeling medium. Children embodied water droplets, arranged in concentric circles, and moved outward sequentially over time to represent diffusion.

Overall, Figure 2 shows that  children contributed 60% of the total discourse lines. Of these, 88.2% involved mechanistic reasoning and 11.8% analogical reasoning. The teacher contributed 40% of the discourse. Of her contributions, 77.5% addressed  children's mechanistic reasoning, and 22.5% addressed  other MbL elements. Notably, none of the teacher’s contributions responded to analogical reasoning  directly. Half  of her discourse consisted of restating children’s ideas (50.2%), while 35.6% involved prompting children to think in certain directions. Evaluation of children’s contributions accounted for 12.9% of her talk, primarily providing feedback urging children to differentiate between “ drop” (macroscopic) and “drops” (microscopic). The remaining 1.3% provided clarifications.

This case study offers  evidence that kindergarten children can meaningfully engage in MbL when supported through structured MbL cycles and teacher scaffolding. This aligns with research identifying modeling as a core scientific practice (e.g., NRC, 2013; Schwarz et al., 2009) and supports claims that young children have  the cognitive capacity to construct and refine models as reasoning tools (NASEM, 2022; Louca & Zacharia, 2025). The children demonstrated engagement in mechanistic reasoning (Russ et al., 2008), consistent with perspectives that model development centers on representing underlying mechanisms (Ke & Schwarz, 2019). The analysis highlights challenges in mechanistic reasoning across scalar levels (Krist et al., 2019), as linguistic ambiguities constrained children’s mechanistic explanations. However, teacher scaffolds—especially the use of dramatic play as a modeling medium—helped  children articulate and refine their ideas.
