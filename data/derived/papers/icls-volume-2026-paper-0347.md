# Co-design as Transformative Pedagogical Spaces: what zooming out teaches us about learning to co-design together

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This symposium brings together Learning Scientists and practitioners engaged in co-design practices embedded in diverse traditions (participatory design, human-centered design, youth participatory action research, design-based implementation research, and research practice partnerships) across diverse geographies. In different ways, these collections of papers challenge two artificially imposed constraints on how many co-design spaces are (a) designed, as short, condensed, irregular, or infrequent activities, and (b) studied, as analysis primarily focused on the interactions happening within the formal co-design activities themselves. By zooming out temporally and analytically, we make visible co-design processes that are deeply context-dependent, relational, political, and ethical practices, ones that researchers and participants construct a nd hone together over longer units of time. Through our collective discussions, we show how co-design spaces are unique spaces of being, doing, and becoming, for researchers and participants alike. Objective of the session In recent years, collaborative design has emerged in the learning sciences as a key method supporting the development of learning artifacts (Yoon et al., 2016; Gomez et al., 2018). Like other design-oriented disciplines, co-design in the learning sciences seeks to surface participants’ lived experiences and needs to directly inform the design of some learning artifact. Unlike other design-oriented disciplines, co-design in the learning sciences has focused on the relational, social, and dynamic learning processes that occur within co-design spaces (Mawasi et al., 2023; Penuel, 2019; Philip et al., 2022). This burgeoning line of research from the learning sciences complicates how we design, analyze, and assess co-design spaces, particularly when time constraints often necessitate that these co-design sessions occur over short or infrequent periods. Extrapolating concrete design decisions from co-design spaces can be exceptionally difficult given the fluidity, tensions, and outright contradictions that emerge (Chang & Philip, 2025). Here, rather than narrowly conceptualizing the telos of co-design spaces as surfacing fixed designs, we build on Rogoff’s conceptualization of “learning as a process of transformation of participation” (1994) and argue that co-design spaces must be understood as sites where participants and researchers dynamically learn to co-design together. In this way, we understand co-design as a deeply context-dependent, relational, political, and ethical practice that researchers and participants construct and hone together; design decisions thus emerge as an ever-evolving byproduct of these practices (Bang & Vossoughi, 2016; Vossoughi et al., 2021). Past work in the learning sciences offers several starting points for exploring co-design spaces as sites of mutual learning about co-design itself. McKercher (2020) argues that building conditions for co-design workshops 2274 require intentional power sharing, relationship and trust building, and creating safe spaces for participants. The relational nature of co-design spaces presents a key challenge, as co-design engagements (in practice) typically occur over a short, condensed time period (10s of hours). Such tensions, even when unintended, raise important questions for the Learning Sciences community regarding what co-design entails and how to educate participants and researchers in co-design. Prior work has begun to conceptualize co-design as co-researching with participants and youth (e.g., Clark et al., 2022), while educating learners about the research process. While past work gestures towards the deep relational work that happens in co-design processes, our understanding of how those relational processes unfold is often constrained by two artificially imposed constraints on how many co-design spaces are (a) desi gned, as short, condensed, irregular, or infrequent activities, and (b) studied, as analysis primarily focused on the interactions happening within the formal co-design activities themselves. This symposium brings together five projects that seek to zoom out from these constraints in different ways and conceptualize co-design as a pedagogical space that generates educational possibilities (Freire, 1976) for transformation and shifts in interactions, dynamics, relations, roles, and knowledge, while positioning both researchers and participants as learners of co-design. The projects offered here are hardly the only projects in the learning sciences that thoughtfully challenge those constraints; we seek to make a humble contribution by bringing together a highly diverse group of co-design projects that engage with those constraints and possibilities in creative ways. By exploring these constraints and possibilities, we collectively explore different approaches we have taken in exploring this argument, by focusing on five examples of what we consider a form of co-design research work. Across these projects, we take care to highlight the conditions for co-design, describing the process of mutual becoming and what is transformed in the process, emerging issues in the co-design process (e.g., relational trust, context, power, shifts, ideologies, axiologies), and the tensions or possibilities between desired outcomes and what was eventually achieved. This focus enables us to make the following contributions: (1) We bridge between various co-design projects in the field to recognize the multiplicity of approaches to co-design, and to refine our conceptualization of co-design. (2) We outline key challenges for conceptualizing co-design spaces in the learning sciences for both technology-mediated environments and work with educators, practitioners, and learners using participatory methods. (3) We examine the kind of learning co-design environments afford to participants and, researchers in their collaborative learning process. (4) We examine the ways co-design can be a potential space for creative and constructive exploration and deconstruction and construction of ideas, among participants and researchers with nonecessarily defined outcomes needing to emerge-enabling possibilities for critical thinking, liberatory education, and fluid outcomes that create new possibilities for groups and that could be similar to group work in psychology treatments. Overall, the symposium provides guidance on addressing co-design not only as a methodological approach, but also as a learning environment design that enables diverse possibilities for learning for both participants and researchers. Each of the five projects here identifies different ways to zoom out from the typical frames used to study co-design spaces, thereby illustrating different ways that researchers and/or participants learn from each other in ways that enrich the co-design process: (1) Chang et al. zoom out from one-off co-design intensives to show how researchers and youth participants negotiate the aims of co-designing AI-for-education tools over a three-year period. (2) Mawasi et al. zoom out from the formal co-design activities and show how a team of researchers/designers, themselves operating from the margins, learn from each other in order to create co-design spaces around ethical AI that respond to the political contexts of Arab/Palestinian youth. (3) Romero & Lee zoom out from simplified descriptions of participant needs to demonstrate how decision junctures during long-term co-design processes, both big and small, shape how researchers and participants participate in co-designing math and computer science curriculum. (4) Takeuchi et al. zoom out from recent history to demonstrate how researchers and participants learn to attune themselves to the agency and teachings of the Land through mutual learning and imagining, spanning approximately 12,000 years of land stewardship by indigenous communities. (5) Ehrenfeld et al. zoom out from the seemingly convenient touchpoints between researcher and academic during co-design and demonstrate how mismatched rhythms of co-design create pedagogical and ethical tensions when researchers partner with math educators. Collectively, these projects illustrate heterogeneity in co-design research enactment across geographies, topics, and methodological choices of design-based research, and demonstrate how co-design can serve as a productive framework across disciplines, including AI in Education, Mathematics Education, and STEM Education. Together, these contributions assert the need for teams t o co-design for co-design—to continually 2275 examine and refine the very processes and relationships that make co-design possible. ## Researcher and participant identity attunements in the figured world of co-

design Michael Alan Chang, Angel Osorio Pizarro,Thomas M. Philip Statement of purpose We aim to demonstrate how, over a three-year period, researchers and participants collectively shifted the aims of a co-design space, from envisioning how AI can support collaboration to healing from systemic injustices experienced in K-12 schools. Collaborative design between researchers and youth participants is an approach used to develop AI for education (AI-Ed) tools that are responsive to the needs and lived experiences of educational stakeholders (Ravi et al., 2025). When it comes to thinking about the learning that happens in AI-Ed co-design contexts, the focus is typically on researchers teaching participants about basic AI literacy competencies (Holstein et al., 2019; Long & Magerko, 2020), in the hope that this socio/technical understanding will prompt participants to raise issues that they may not have initially considered (Giacomin, 2014). This limited conceptualization of learning in AI-Ed co-design spaces has the side effect of ensuring that the valued outcomes prioritized by researchers for the co-design space remain intact, e.g., considering how AI should be applied to a particular type of learning practice that is assumed to be valued by researchers and participants alike. Here, we present a threeyear co-design workshop on AI-Ed where youth participants and researchers collectively engaged each other in learning about AI literacy, the local high school context, and the aims of the co-design effort itself. Within this longer-term, more expansive context, we ask: how do co-design youth participants and researchers come tonegotiate the valued outcomes of AI-Ed co-design contexts? We build on Chang & Philip (2025) who conceptualized co-design spaces as a type of figured world where the valued outcome is to envision new figured worlds. Here, we understand each new instantiation of a co-design space as a new, fragile world where both researchers and participants enter in with narrativized identities from other, more familiar worlds (Holland et al., 2001). For instance, researchers might bring in identities as facilitators from other co-design spaces that include different actors from different contexts, while youth participants might draw from identities offered to them in classrooms or interactions with everyday AI-based devices (e.g., Amazon 2277 Alexa). Extended engagement mediated by co-design structures and artifacts allow researchers and participants to collectively author and revise co-design identities, thereby re-mediating subject-subject relations to support collective envisioning of new possibilities for AI-Ed. These co-designed identities have the potential to subvert historical and cultural conventions that sideline some voices from the design of AI-Ed tools (Bang & Vossoughi, 2016; Chang et al., 2024).

Data sources We present audio and video recordings from a three year  co-design effort with high school aged, predominantly youth of color (all except one), with the initial aim of envisioning expansive possibilities for AI-supported collaboration. Of the 30 participants we started off with in year one, eight participants engaged in at least two years of the workshop. We produced content logs of transcribed discussions from all three workshops and thematically coded transcripts in order to understand how both researcher and participants understood the aims of the co-design space and their own role in accomplishing those aims. Over the three years of the workshop, participants’ and researchers’ engagement with the dynamically constructed figured world underwent a dramatic transformation. In the first workshop, researchers initially structured the codesign space as a more conven tional pedagogical space where we sought to create opportunities for youth participants to explore sociopolitical issues surrounding the use of AI. Similarly, many participants drew on familiar identities offered to them from schooling, and most commonly d evised uses of AI tools that made incremental improvements within the existing context of schooling. Researchers later adopted more unfamiliar identities as speculative educators, which created the context for participants to step into other familiar, context-specific roles outside  the traditional grammar of schooling, such as their experience protesting their local high school administration for not listening to students on issues specific to their shared schooling experience. As the researchers adapted th e aims of the workshop alongside participant needs (i.e., from AI-supported classroom collaboration to creating the conditions for student voices to be heard), one student participant in the start of year three described the co-design context as a space fo r student healing — one that he had never found inside the context of schools. Here, over time, co-design structures created by researchers that made salient figured worlds that at first glance have very little to do with AI-Ed (e.g., student cooperative houses, Black Panther Liberation Schools) played a key role in mediating student participants and researchers’ evolving identities within the codesign space.

## Implications

Most commonly, each individual co-design space is a short engagement that is most unfamiliar to participants and researchers, even ones experienced in other  co-design spaces. Long-term co-design efforts such as the one described in this proposal help to make visible the fragility of the identities researchers and participants bring into co-design spaces; this fragility means that more familiar identities can complicate co-envisioning processes, but also offer opportunities to improvise new possibilities that may not surface in other more conventional contexts. Exploring knowledge building in co-design efforts for co-design learning activities Areej Mawasi, Amani Rohana, Jool Farraj Statement of Purpose Learning scientists actively engage in designing artifacts, including technologies, hands-on materials, processes, and instructions. Co-design has gained attention as an approach that supports collaborative knowledge construction and participation of diverse audiences (e.g., teachers, youth, communities). However, organizing for co-design is a challenge in itself, as it requires a design process that bridges research goals, pedagogical moves, and the needs and hopes of intended collaborators (e.g., Mawasi et al., 2023). In this work, we draw on our efforts to co-design a Storyline learning unit aimed at engaging Arab/Palestinian youth in Israel with critical perspectives on AI and emerging technologies. Between 2023 and 2025, the three  authors co-designed a storyline  and learning activities in  which youth discuss, test, and build ideas, tools, and artifacts to develop critical AI literacies (Atias & Mawasi, In Review), ethical thinking, and responsible innovation (e.g., Mawasi et al., 2022). 2278 When collaborative processes for knowledge construction are intentionally organized toward a design purpose, participants in such settings can engage in collective knowledge building whose impact extends beyond the temporal moment of a single meeting or a single cycle of design events (e.g., Penuel et al., 2022). The purpose of this paper is to examine the possibilities and constraints of small-scale collaborative design within design teams, focusing on co-design in a one-on-one team structure and how knowledge building unfolds through artifacts across time. We argue that such a design process can be both rigorous and generative, enabling transformations and shifts in both the design and the thinking of the designers. These shifts may be reflected in their design decisions, how the design unfolds over time, who they become as designers/researchers through the process, and the ideas they continue to build on and with. Considering the contextual and temporal boundaries of co-design, this proposal addresses the following questions: (1) How might we continue to build on each other’s knowledge across different points in time? (2) What is transferred and learned through design artifacts (e.g., meeting notes, recordings, learning materials, diagrams, brainstorming no tes)? (3) What are the key elements that support the consequentiality of this co-design process amidst the involvement of newcomer(s)?

## Data sources and findings

To address these questions, we draw on data sources from our co-design meetings which extended between 2023-2025. These meetings included developing artifacts such as slides, storyline goals, and brainstorming notes that

captured our meaning-making process as co-designers. In this data, we examine how our collective knowledge efforts unfold through shifts and decisions that occurred throughout our co-design process. Specifically, to understand these shifts and decisions, we examined four key artifacts from our co-design meetings (2023-2025): initial brainstorming notes, affinity diagrams (e.g., Hanington & Martin, 2019), mindmaps, and meeting summaries. These documents provide insights into how discourse and decisions evolved in response to contextual considerations, temporal boundaries of co-design (e.g., resource constraints, sociopolitical context, and perceived evolving community needs), and our interactions to co-construct the learning materials. By focusing on shifts in language, vocabulary, and decisions, we navigated the complex realities impacting our work, including our diverse and intersectional positionalities. We suggest that despite  limited capital in this co-design process, our positionalities, interdisciplinary expertise, trust, and commitment to the design goals created space to design with a “context matters” mindset, designing during challenging sociopolitical realities, and building knowledge across multiple disciplines (e.g., AI & digital literacy/STEM education, Architecture, and Anthropo logy). This interdisciplinarity served as a necessary condition for co-design from the margins, enabling collective knowledge construction and the deconstruction of established frameworks that have shaped our practice, especially in this under-studied context. This ongoing process builds a web of meanings and influences that becomes visible in the artifacts we create and continue to build on.

## Implications

Arab/Palestinian youth in Israel experience structural inequalities that are reflected in unequal access to quality education, persistent digital divides, and the daily impacts of ongoing conflict and violence on their lives (Sa'di, 2016). As we co-design, our insider standpoint can bring a critical lens to how such structural inequalities are understood, addressed, and experienced by Arab youth. This paper offers insights into how co-design operates within a politicized, small team, and nondominant context. This specific case, illuminates how structural inequities shape our design thinking and collaborative dynamics as researchers and designers. Takeuchi, Deborah Dutta, Kori Czuy, Tatenda Mambo, Mathew Swallow Statement of purpose The process and results of co-design can shift hierarchical power relations that have historically been perpetuated in research, by honouring voices, stories, and ideas of the participants as agentic contributors (Ishimaru et al., 2018; Mawasi et al., 2023). With deep engagement with critical historicity, co-design approaches can orient researchers toward “desettling” (p. 308) the settled disciplinary norms including anthropocentrism (Bang et al., 2012). In this context, we pose a question: How can we as human researchers orient ourselves to the agency, voice, and teachings of the Land (with capital L) (Styres, 2011)?

## Theoretical framework

We see transdisciplinarity as commitment to questions greater than the discipline itself, aiming for the emergence of new epistemological and ontological realities by embracing historically silenced voices in the colonial 2279 knowledge production (Gordon, 2006; Takeuchi & Marin, 2022). In the presence of the Land, which is larger than us and continually showing us layers of their portraits, we came to embrace epistemic humility to listen carefully to the voice of more-than-humans (Kimmerer, 2013).

## Data and methodology

We draw from social design experiments that center historicity, equity, and ecological resilience as design principles (Gutiérrez & Jurow, 2016), and from participatory design research that aims to co-design just practices in partnership with communities (Bang & Vossoughi, 2016). We, as co-authors and a part of the core design team, bring diverse disciplinary expertise (Learning Sciences, Soil Sciences, Sustainability Studies, and Ancestral/Indigenous Sciences/Mathematics). We illustrate our process of co-design rooted in our research and design partnership, Soil Camp (www.soilcamp.ca). The project sprouted as a summer camp research– design partnership on a community urban farm during the COVID-19 pandemic, based on an invitation from our community partner, a local refugee resettlement agency. In the past five years, a total of 135 children (aged 5– 15 years) and 39 facilitators (21 teachers) joined our program. Many of the children used to be intimately connected to the land in intergenerational and embodied ways prior to their forced displacement. All the sessions and fieldtrips were video-recorded with multiple cameras including child-worn Go Pros. For this paper, we engaged in reflective video-mediated interviews with the core design team, focusing on the vi deo-recorded episodes on land-based activities co-designed and facilitated by them. Co-design with the Land meant deepening our understanding of the settler-colonial histories embedded in the earth underneath us. The process of co-design was entangled with our co-learning as researchers and our disposition to listening. One of the key layers of co-design with the Land was our engagement with temporality. For example, in the design of pedagogical activities using a number line, our facilitation initially highlighted 12,000 years of history of land stewardship by local Indigenous communities. However, this timemaker is derived from the commonly accepted “Colvis First” Theory, which does not fully acknowledge oral histories of many Indigenous communities, telling the “time immemorial” of their presence (Steeves, 2021). Over time, in collaboration and conversation with Blackfoot Elders and Knowledge Keepers and through our interactions on, and with the Land, the design team critically reflected on what was enumerated and how. In our re-design under the guidance of Elder, we experienced the magni tude of land stewardship that preserved native wild plants, animals, and the bones of bison through a field trip to ancient hunting grounds of bison. Co-design with the Land also meant to us, submitting ourselves to the mysteries embedded in the soil and honoring the worldviews and stories embedded in the Land in their own light. For instance, our assumptions about the land we were working on to be a typical grassland changed drastically when a soil scientist in our team (Author 5) found a white layer of volcanic ash. This encounter with the unknown left a strong impression on the children, and many shared their awe, surprise, and wonder with us. Children were together with us in joy and grace to witness this (hi)story buried under the top horizon of the soil. Reflecting on our own process of co-learning and design/redesign, we highlight the significance of attending to the voice and agency of the Land as a co-design partner. In the process of co-design, we strive to bring forth ways of knowing and being which are relational, reciprocal, humble, and non-anthropocentric. Examining decision making in a research-practice partnership Maria Romero, Victor R. Lee Statement of purpose Multi-stakeholder decision-making is an essential process of participatory and design based studies as the goal is oriented towards generating a shared product (Bratteteig and Wagner, 2012). Decision-making can be especially complex to understand due to the extensive amount of decisions occurring between participants such as designers, researchers, and practitioners (Design-Based Research Collective, 2003). In this work, we examine decision-making within a multi-year research-practice partnership between a rural elementary school district and a university researcher. The goal of this partnership was to co-design cross-setting curriculum materials integrating mathematics and computer science across the classroom and computer lab. Within this partnership, a design team was identified to include fifth grade math teachers and paraprofessionals who led the computer lab and taught computer science. Our focus is on the exponents lessons, the first math topic chosen for lesson development. 2280 The purpose of this paper is to understand how decisions are navigated within the design of the curriculum and how co-designers reflect on their own contributions and participation. Bogdanov (2022) characterizes “design dilemmas” in co-design as moments “when team members were at a crossroads in the curriculum or were faced with problems that had multiple possible directions”. We build upon this framing to connect decision and curricular design to broadly focus on decision moments or instances where a change in curriculum framing, content, or artifacts is presented. We explore the following questions: (1) How are decisions about curricular design introduced, explored, and ultimately accepted? (2) How do co-designers perceive their contribution and roles?

Data sources The data sources include district and research team meeting notes, recordings and transcripts of the design team meetings, interviews with design team members, and the designed curriculum. For the first design iteration of the lesson, we identified decisio n moments over time to understand how the initial lesson concept was iterated on. We analyzed new iterations of lesson activities created in the programming platform Scratch to document and map changes to conversations taking place in recorded subgroup meetings. Finally, we coded participant interviews to understand their perspectives on their experience as co-designers. Our analysis showed cycles of co-design and contributions were distributed through the development of the exponents lesson across time and partners. While the project included a formal design subgroup, there were additional cycles of co-design that operated between  researchers and district leadership beyond that design subgroup. We also found that some key participants-the paraprofessionals-perceived their participation differently across roles with some feeling less comfortable highlighting their valuable contributions to the final products. As co-design is intended to reduce barriers and support balanced partnership, this raises questions for the field regarding possible disconnects that some may feel regarding how critical their contributions are. Moreover, the extended and distributed process of decision-making and modification of materials across time and individuals invites future questions of how co-design can optimally unfold given real-world time and space constraints.

Between the multiple rhythms of research and practice: exploring the possibility for a non-interventionist first year in a research-practice partnership Nadav Ehrenfeld, Soryna Sabbah, Rima Zgair-Araidy Statement of purpose As part of this symposium’s shared effort to conceptualize key challenges and possibilities in co-design spaces, our goal in this session is, first, to articulate the mismatch between the rhythms of research and practice. We do that within the context of a  partnership with school-based learning communities of mathematics teachers. Then, we suggest and explore one possibility to balance that problem in the form of a first non-interventionist year, in which we are building relationships with the participating teachers and getting to know the local teams, including their main concerns, resources, and ways of being together in a community. By the end of that first year, we codesigned “key ideas to experiment with” for each team, towards the second year. Finally, we suggest some affordances as well as dilemmas that emerge from that process. Working in partnership with teachers requires attentiveness to the multiple rhythms of teacher learning ecologies (Alhadeff-Jones, 2016; Ehrenfeld & Stengel, 2025). It also requires being responsive to the mismatch between the long and slow cycles of research and the quick and fast cycles of school life. One of the consequences of this mismatch is that “the right moment” to co-design over data-driven insights, might not converge. The context for this study is a broader research project that explores and supports teacher learning ecologies (Ehrenfeld, 2022) in partnership with a network of school-based Professional Learning Communities (PLC Network). One aspect of this partnership is that analysis from a non-interventionist first year in two schools is used to co-design recommendations (or “key ideas to experiment with”) in three levels: with PLC leaders;PLC teachers; and PLC Network, towards the following year of a more robust partnership around these key ideas. 2281

## Data/Methodology

Data come from a non-interventionist first year with two school-based PLCs of mathematics teachers. The first is a Hebrew-speaking elementary-school, and the second is an Arabic-speaking middle-school. We attended 30 yearly hours of meetings with each team  and interviewed all teachers once. The middle-school team also visited our institution for their final meeting of the year. All meetings were recorded. During the year, we watched the videos with questions about the teachers’ interests and concerns, considering the productive “key ideas to experiment with” to brainstorm with the teams. Then, articulating the preliminary five key ideas, we first brainstormed the ideas with the team leaders, then with the whole team, and finally, we explored them with the PLC Network leadership team.

## Preliminary findings

The findings present the five key ideas we explored with both schools. We then briefly report on co-design meetings with local team leaders to consider these ideas for the following year. We articulated five initial ideas:

(1) different participation structures in their communities; (2) problems of practice introduced by the leader vs in

a “bottom-up” fashion by the teachers; (3) the connections between the mathematical and pedagogical discourses;

(4) an ecological perspective on teachers’ identities and expertise our within the PLC; (5) and relatedly, the extent

to which conversations and ideas echo onto following meetings or to other contexts. The local team leaders responded with a mix of agreement on several points and professional negotiation around the rationales that shaped choices made during the meeting. This became a site of mutual learning, which at times involved frustration about the timing of the feedback, as well as a sense of expectations by the leaders to inform next-year planning.

## Implications

We explore the possibility that analysis during a “non-interventionist first year” would produce “key ideas to experiment with” towards co-design. On the one hand, the delay in intervention allows relationship building and provides a balancing effect to the mismatch between the rhythms of research and practice. On the other hand, this type of partnership introduces new challenges and dilemmas. For example, is it ethical for researchers to notice ways to improve teacher learning and only share them at the end of the year? Keeping these “backstage” dilemmas in mind, a non-interventionist year is one way to balance the mismatch between the rhythms of research and practice, potentially towards a deep collaborative design, even if late.
