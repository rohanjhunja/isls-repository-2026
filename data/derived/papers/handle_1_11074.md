# Prospective Mathematics Teachers' Perceptions of Mathematics as a Disconnected and Dehumanizing Subject

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study explores prospective mathematics teachers’ perceptions of mathematics based on their experiences of learning and teaching mathematics in relation to prevailing social norms. It seeks to uncover whether they view mathematics as a human endeavor or exhibit (de)humanizing characteristic. Findings suggest participants perceive mathematics as disconnected, with collegiate experiences being dehumanizing, lacking a strong mathematics identity. Future research should investigate the link between these encounters and the development of prospective teachers’ mathematical identities.

## Background

Mathematics has socially formatting power, gatekeeping and determin ing access and success in STEM fields (Martin et al., 2010). Therefore, designing equitable mathematics learni ng experiences is both a matter of equity and politics (Gutierrez, 2018). Understanding what mathematics entails, who excels in it, who creates it, who accesses it, and what constitutes quality teaching is crucial (Boaler & Greeno, 2000; Gutierrez, 2018). He nce, it is recommended to meaningfully center identity and power in mathematical learning (Gutierrez, 2018). Learning is a political process characterized by ev olving relationships through participation with communities and practices (Lave & Wenger, 1991). Pr ocesses of learning and identity are simultaneous, reciprocal, and situated within cultural practices (Nasir, 2011). Identities develop in and through social  practice (Holland et al., 1998) and people’s participation i n community activities (Lave &Wagner, 1991). Research suggests that learners' identities are closely link ed to their learning experiences (Boaler & Greeno, 2000; Nasir, 2011). Integrating identity development in mathemat ics methods courses can better prepare teachers for  diverse teaching settings (de Freitas, 2008). Learners may excel in math practices without fully embracing a mathematical thinker identity (Nasir, 2011).

The study aims to explore prospective teachers’ perceptions of mathematics, whether their math practices are (de)humanizing, investigating the research question: “What are prospective mathematics teachers’ perceptions of current mathematics practices within their learning and teaching environments?”

## Methodology

This interpretive qualitative study aimed to explor e prospective mathematics teachers' perceptions and experiences regarding mathematical practices within  a prestigious university in New York, USA. Three prospective secondary school mathematics teachers (PTs), identified as Karen (she/her), Sera (she/her), and Rita (he/him), were purposively sampled from senior unde rgraduate honors students, representing diverse rac ial and social backgrounds, includi ng Chinese American, Chinese international, and Whi te American. Sampling senior students ensured active engagement in teaching experiences within public or private schools in grades 9-12. Being honor students implied exceptional academic perform ance, evidenced by high GPAs and participation in educational research seminars. PTs were required to  complete 128 credits, including specific program requirements in core content courses offered by the College of Arts and Sciences (CAS) and School of Education (SE). Semi-structured interviews lasting 45-60 minutes were conducted during Fall 2022 and Spri ng 2023. Interviews were transcribed and analyzed using open  coding, with the codebook refined through peer rev iew to ensure validity and reliability. Findings were further validated within the scholarly community.

## Findings

The findings revealed that PTs perceive mathematics  as disconnected and dehumanizing based on their experiences in the teacher education program, and t hey lack a strong mathematics identity. When asked about their understanding of mathematical practices, Sera said “It depends on which level math.” She continued “When I was in middle school or high school, math is my strong subjects. And like I feel I can just understand what math is” and expressed the disconnect between K-12 and college math, saying, “But when I go to [Institution Name], I feel like college level math is another thing, like you really need to be i nterested in math. And you really need to like learn how to prove everything.” While she excelled in K-12 math, she finds college-level math to be different, such that “you need to be a mathematician to do all  the math at the college level”. Similarly, Rita sh ared the difference between mathematical practices in math courses offered by CAS and SE. Rita:... The divide between there ’s, the way [Institution Name] works is that math ed  students take CAS math courses, and then they also take SE m ath courses, which are usually methods courses for teaching. I would say that my practices are very different between those two. At SE, at my te aching classes, there really is the emphasis on the  communication aspect, on the persistence, on the problem-solving and exploration. I would say that those are  the practices that I see. That’s why I enjoy mathematics in those classes. Honestly, I’ve had pretty rough time at the CAS and higher-level mathematics.

He further explained later in the interview “I think the only time that I’ve been able to engage with math in a fun way like that I’ve enjoyed in college has been SE classes.”  Conversely, Rita found CAS courses challenging and less fulfilling, mentioning a loss of connection to human existence and a lack of engagement in pure math as “…you kind of got into the abstract at some point a nd the connection to human existence is loss, which is really sad.” Similarly, Sera expressed a preference for SE courses, finding them “definitely more useful” since they are conducive to exploring math in various ways. The loss of connection to human existence in CAS courses felt unfamiliar and disconnected, thus dehumanizing. Moreover, despite finding SE practices enjoyable and successful, PTs didn't identify themselves as “math persons” or “mathematical thinkers,” indicating a disconnect between their experiences a nd their mathematical identity. Sera admitted, “I just feel like I’m not that good at doing college-level math…I would never call myself a mathematician.” Similarly, Rita shared, “I was actually really bad at math…I am kinda still bad at math…To be honest, it’s kind of like I personally will probably never see  myself as a mathematician. ” Additionally, Karen envisioned mathematicians’ practices as “working on math alone”, contrasting sharply with th e collaborative nature of SE courses. This suggests that the program’s mathematical practices and curriculum may not be fostering a strong mathematics identity among PTs.

## Discussion and conclusion

The study revealed that PTs perceive mathematics as  disconnected and dehumanizing, lacking a strong ma th identity. While the mathematics education program aims for equitable and socially just education and prevent the perpetuation of unjust teaching practices,  PTs identified CAS math practices as perpetuating h istorical dehumanization. The PTs’ mathematical skills and identity are sha ped more by experiences with dehumanizing practices, overshadowing SE courses’ efforts. CAS classes aim to enhance content knowledge but la ck a dialectical problem-solving process (Lave, 1988), communication, exploration, and joy.  Gutiérrez (2018) stresses the need for rehumanizing practices, requiring evid ence from those affected. The teacher education program should model rehumanizing mathematical practices by  incorporating various dimensions such as participation/positioning, cultures/histories, windows/mirrors, living practice, creation, broadening mathematics, body/emotions, and ownership (Gutiérrez, 2018). Future studies can explore this phenomenon in diffe rent institutions to transform mathematics education and prioritize marginalized voices (Gutierrez, 2022).
