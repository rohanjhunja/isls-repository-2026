### Abstract

Classroom discussions enrich science learning by enabling students to co-construct scientific knowledge, strengthen critical thinking, and achieve deeper understanding (Kelly, 2013; Murphy et al., 2020; Sun et al., 2015). Furthermore, students bring diverse cultural, linguistic, and experiential perspectives into classroom discussions, which are valuable resources that can enrich the collective learning experience (Gay, 2018; Gee, 2017; GonzálezHoward et al., 2021; Zwiers & Crawford, 2011). However, in many classrooms, students may not learn  the skills to engage effectively in classroom discussions, and teachers might be underprepared to facilitate equitable talk or to notice and disrupt inequitable participation patterns (Yan et al., 2023; Gotwalt, 2023; Patterson, 2019). Students from culturally and linguistically diverse backgrounds may experience additional challenges due to inaccessible pedagogies and social exclusion (Mack, 2012). These challenges can lead to inequitable discussions and correspondingly disparate learning outcomes.

Theoretical framework: the equitable classroom discussion observation protocol To address these issues, I previously developed a novel tool, the Equitable Classroom Discussion Observation Protocol (ECDOP), to help science teachers create inclusive and equitable science learning environments that promote the engagement and participation of culturally and linguistically diverse students in scientific discussions (Yan et al., 2024, 2025). The ECDOP addresses student engagement across five dimensions  (learning materials and resources, behavioral, cognitive, cultural-and-linguistic, and social‑emotional) and features 25 specific, observable teacher practices for facilitating discussion. The implementation gaps Equitable discussion practices depend on teacher enactment, yet written materials alone seldom yield high‑quality implementation. Classroom implementation frequently struggles with fidelity because of contextual constraints, variable teacher motivation, and limited ongoing support (Reinke et al., 2014). While interactive, sustained professional learning with opportunities for reflection improves uptake, traditional professional development and coaching remain constrained by time, location, and cost. As a re sult, many teachers lack accessible, just‑in‑time guidance for enacting equity‑focused talk moves during planning and teaching. Designing the Equi-Talk AI assistant To address the need for accessible, ongoing teacher support, I designed and developed Equi-Talk, a teacher-facing AI Assistant prototype. Equi-Talk translates the ECDOP into an interactive environment for planning, reflection, and just-in-time guidance. As  outlined in this paper, the platform guides educators through a structured, user-friendly workflow. Upon entering the platform, teachers are welcomed on the Home tab with brief instructions. Before diving into lesson design, users are encouraged to review  the "Equitable Classroom Discussion" tab to familiarize themselves with the framework’s core concepts, its five dimensions, and the associated pedagogical practices.

Once familiar with the framework, teachers can navigate between three core interactive modules tailored to their immediate needs (Figure 1):

• Lesson planning: Teachers input contextual details (e.g., topic, grade, goals) to co-create standards-aligned lesson plans with the AI, or upload existing plans for targeted refinement. 2889

• Scenario support: Delivers actionable solutions for real-time classroom challenges through quick-access buttons or open chat with the AI Assistant.

• Post-lesson reflection: Facilitates structured self-evaluation using guided prompts and interactive chat

Lesson Reflection Contributions and future work This work operationalizes the five-dimensional ECDOP framework into an AI-enabled planning and reflection workflow. By extending ECDOP beyond human-facilitated workshops, Equi-Talk embeds equitable discussion principles directly into daily practice. Next steps include refining the user interface, conducting beta testing in elementary science classrooms to evaluate core usability, and iterating based on teacher feedback. Ultimately, this work expands teachers’ capacity to lead equitable science discussions and promotes more just participation in STEM learning.
