# Navigating Boundaries in Research–Practice Partnerships: Integrating Computing in Elementary Education

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Integrating computer science (CS) at the elementary level is critical for broadening participation in STEM, yet these efforts face barriers such as limited teacher expertise, curricular misalignment, and institutional turnover. Research –practice partnerships (RPPs) offer a promising model for addressing these challenges, but little is known about how they evolve in practice. This study examines a five-year RPP in a mid-Atlantic state in the USA focused on implementing the CSTA K –12 Computer Science Standards in grades 3– 5. Using mixed methods—surveys (n=53), interviews (n=15), and document analysis —we examine both the partnership’s planned design and its enacted evolution through Farrell et al.’s (2022) Learning at the Boundaries framework. Findings show that while institutional pillars weakened, teacher leaders and flexible boundary practices sustained progress, generating teacher growth, student engagement, and a public repository of lessons. The study extends the boundary framework by highlighting the fragility of institutional spanners and the importance of adaptability in sustaining and scaling outcomes.

Integrating computer science (CS) into formal education has become a priority for researchers and policy makers (Boulden et al., 2018), yet it continues to present complex challenges.  Effective implementation requires alignment across systems, sustained professional development (PD) for teachers, and approaches that foster teacher ownership rather than top-down reforms (El-Hamamsy et al., 2023). Teachers need sustained support to adapt instructional practices to their specific classroom contexts and to develop the  CS-specific knowledge necessary for effective implementation  (El-Hamamsy et al., 2023).  Early introduction to CS is particularly important, as exposure in elementary school can shape students’ interest and persistence in STEM fields (Novak & Khan, 2022). However, integration efforts are often hindered by curricular misalignment, high-stakes testing pressures, and limited teacher expertise (Boulden et al., 2018). To address these challenges, research –practice partnerships (RPPs) offer a promising approach by fostering long-term, mutualistic collaboration between researchers and practitioners to address problems of practice (Coburn & Penuel, 2016; Herro et al., 2022; McGill & Reinking, 2022; Partee et al, 2023; Sjölund & Lindvall, 2024). Several case studies highlight the potential of RPPs in CS education. Adrion et al. (2022) described teacher-led design teams collaborating with researchers to co-develop equity-centered curricula, supported by professional learning communities and district-wide initiatives. Similarly, Novak and Khan (2022) demonstrated how a RPP engaged teachers and students as co-constructors of a CS curriculum for elementary students. In their work with middle grades teachers, Boulden et al. (2018) found that RPPs helped integrate computational thinking (CT) into science curricula by fostering teacher buy-in and embedding research in classroom realities. While RPPs have the potential to improve teaching quality, student learning, and equity, they are also vulnerable to communication difficulties, conflicting priorities, and organizational turnover (Denner et al., 2019; Farrell et al., 2022; Goldman et al., 2023). Despite their promise, RPPs in education remain under-examined relative to other fields. Much of the existing literature focuses on challenges rather than solutions, and there is limited evidence on long-term impacts beyond the lifespan of individual partnerships (Coburn & Penuel, 2016). Additionally, few studies provide detailed accounts of how RPPs are operationalized in educational contexts (Cooper et al., 2021; Novak & Khan, 2022). In the context of elementary CS education in particular, there are still relatively few documented examples, leaving important questions about sustainability, equity, and large-scale implementation largely unanswered (Novak & Khan, 2022; Adrion et al., 2022). This study addresses these gaps by applying the “Learning at the boundaries” framework (Farrell et al.,

2022) to examine a five-year RPP in a mid-Atlantic state in the U nited States focused on integrating the CSTA

K–12 Computer Science Standards (CSTA, 2025) into grades 3– 5. The partnership prioritized broadening participation in CS through culturally responsive practices, strengthening teacher readiness, and developing modules (i.e., series of lessons)  that integrate CT into core subjects such as math and literacy. Drawing on RPP 320 documents, interviews, and surveys, the study explores two research questions: RQ1: What was the planned design of the RPP, including the intended roles of partners, activities, and resources to support collaboration and CS/CT integration in elementary education? RQ2: How did the RPP evolve over time to overcome challenges in integrating CS/CT into elementary education?

This paper makes three key contributions. First, it provides a unique comparison between the proposed initial design and the enacted evolution of an RPP, illustrating how the infrastructure envisioned at the beginning shifted over time in practice. Second, it provides longitudinal, mixed-methods evidence of how an RPP adapted to challenges while sustaining its stated original goals. Third, it extends the application of Farrell et al.’s (2022) framework to the context of elementary CS education, generating insights into sustainability, equity, and scale-up in a field where documented RPP cases remain scarce.

## Literature review

Research–Practice Partnerships (RPPs) Research–Practice Partnerships (RPPs) in education are collaborative relationships between researchers and practitioners aimed at addressing problems of practice and improving educational systems (Sjölund & Lindvall, 2024). In recent years, RPPs have grown as a means of fostering collaboration among stakeholders and promoting research-based decision making in educational practice and policy (Cooper et al., 2021). At their core, RPPs are distinguished by several key features: they are long-term collaborations, they are mutually beneficial (“mutualistic”), they are intentionally organized around clear structures, they bring together a diverse range of expertise, and they are focused on solving pressing problems of practice (Coburn et al., 2013; Sjölund & Lindvall, 2024). Partnerships typically involve processes of co-design, shared decision-making, and cycles of continuous learning that balance research goals with the  needs of schools and communities (Cooper et al., 2021; Lawrence et al., 2024). These characteristics make RPPs a promising model for ensuring that research informs practice, particularly to address issues of equity and systemic improvement (Denner et al.,  2019). Participation in RPPs benefits both sides: practitioners gain greater access to research evidence while researchers can  conduct studies that directly inform practice (Farrell et al., 2018; Sjölund & Lindvall, 2024). Although still emerging, the evidence suggests that RPPs can improve student outcomes, enhance teaching quality, and promote equity in classrooms (Farrell et al., 2022). They can also build capacity by strengthening practitioners’ ability to use research and researchers’ ability to engage with r eal-world educational contexts (Farrell et al., 2018).

Despite their promise, RPPs face persistent challenges. Partners may struggle to engage consistently (Sjölund et al., 2022) and communicate effectively due to different priorities, languages, and timelines (Denner et al., 2019). Specifically, taking on new or unfamiliar roles can create uncertainty and conflict (Farrell et al., 2022; Vedder-Weiss et al., 2020). In addition, organizational complexity and leadership turnover may disrupt continuity (Coburn and Penuel, 2016; Goldman et al., 2023). Finally, d efining and sustaining shared agendas requires ongoing collaboration (Lawrence et al., 2024), while power dynamics and cultural differences underscore the need for explicit attention to equity (Denner et al., 2019; Sjölund & Lindvall, 2024).

## Theoretical framework

The original design of the RPP that served as the foundation for this study was informed by Design-Based Implementation Research (DBIR), a model that emphasizes iterative co-design among diverse stakeholders, systemic capacity building, and the integration of research and practice for mutual benefit (Fishman et al., 2013). Several scholars who have been central to advanc ing DBIR and early work on RPPs — including contributors to foundational DBIR publications (e.g., Fishman et al., 2013), and subsequent RPP scholarship (e.g., Coburn et al., 2013; Coburn & Penuel, 2016)—have also played a key role in the conceptual development of the “Learning at the Boundaries”  framework (Farrell et al., 2022). This framework builds on and extends the DBIR and RPP traditions by examining how collaboration and mutual learning emerge within partnerships. According to Farrell et al. (2022), partners from diverse backgrounds come together and experience encounters known as boundaries, where differences in norms, roles, contexts, or expectations must be negotiated. Although such scenarios  can impede collaboration, they also create opportunities for learning when effectively addressed (Akkerman & Bakker, 2011). To navigate these differences, an RPP establishes a boundary infrastructure that supports partners in learning collaboratively and overcoming challenges. This infrastructure consists of three key elements: (a) boundary spanners —individuals who bridge organizational or role-based divides; (b) boundary practices, which include activities such as co-design routines; and (c) boundary objects, such as shared tools or rubrics (Farrell et al., 2022). Through engagement with this infrastructure, the partnership generates intermediary outcomes, such as organizational learning, and longer-term outcomes, including 321 educational improvement and the production of usable knowledge. In this framework, boundaries emerge through interactions among participants from different organizational and professional contexts (e.g., researchers, educators, and policymakers), though these actors are not explicitly represented as distinct components  in the framework. Instead, the framework focuses on how encounters of difference give rise to boundary infrastructure that supports collaboration and learning. Figure 1 presents a visual adaptation of Farrell et al.’s (2022) framework. This framework is particularly well-suited for analyzing RPPs in CS  education, where collaborations often span multiple organizations and involve evolving roles and practices. Applying this framework allows for a systematic examination of how the RPP’s originally envisioned structures evolved over time, how boundary infrastructures facilitated adaptation, and how outcomes emerged through these processes. This study employed a concurrent mixed-methods design combining quantitative and qualitative data. Sources included survey responses, semi-structured interviews, and RPP documents such as the original proposal and annual evaluation reports. Together, these data sources provided complementary perspectives on the partnership’s planned design and enacted evolution.

Survey participants were drawn from educators who engaged in PD  or related activities through the RPP. The survey items included both closed-and open-ended questions  capturing years of participation, use of RPP resources, experiences with CS/CT instructional integration, factors imped ing CS/CT integration progress, and the partnership’s influence on practice. A total of 53 educators completed the survey. Interviews targeted individuals whose involvement extended beyond their own classrooms to influence school, district, or state-level change. Nineteen participants were invited, 17 consented, and 15 completed the interviews. Interviews lasted 20 –30 minutes and were conducted over Zoom, following a semi-structured protocol. Interview questions addressed membership in the RPP, assessments of progress, professional impact, collaboration processes, and perspectives on RPP sustainability. In addition, partnership documents—including the RPP proposal and evaluation reports—were reviewed to provide contextual data and trace shifts between the planned design and actual implementation.

## Participants

Survey respondents provided a school or district affiliation. Eleven public school districts in the state were represented. They also reported a range of professional roles. The largest group were all-subjects teachers (25.9%), followed by STEM teachers (22.2%), arts and humanities teachers (18.5%), other school-based professionals such as principals, social workers, IT specialist or enrichment specialist (20.4%), and librarians (13.0%). Most taught in traditional public schools (73.6%), with smaller representation from charter (18.9%) and private schools (5.7%). Only one respondent (1.9%) was outside the elementary context due to a shift in their career path. Of the 15 participants interviewed,  three (20%) were State Department of Education staff, ten (66.7%) were current educators (six from public, two from charter, two from independent schools), and two (13.3%) were retired public school educators. All had at least two years of project experience, with several reporting more years through early RPP planning or involvement. Of those interviewed, many held  overlapping roles in the RPP: 322 summer PD participants (n=7), facilitators (n=8), research collaborators on module (i.e. sequence of lessons) implementation (n=4), instructors who worked with undergraduate students supporting project activities (n=9), project staff (n=1), and state department of education representatives (n=3). With the exception of the state department staff, all engaged in more than one role.

## Data analysis

Survey data were analyzed using descriptive statistics to identify trends across participants. Qualitative data — including interview transcripts, evaluation reports, open-ended survey responses, and RPP documents —were analyzed using grounded theory procedures (Strauss & Corbin, 1990), including open, axial, and selective coding. Data were initially open coded independently by two researchers to identify emergent categories related to the research questions. The researchers then met to compare codes, resolve discrepancies, and develop a shared coding scheme.

Following this inductive phase, the analysis incorporated Farrell et al.’s (2022) framework of learning at the boundaries in an abductive manner, using codes aligned with its core components (e.g., boundary spanners, boundary practices, boundary objects) t o further interpret and organize the data. This iterative process involved multiple rounds of discussion to refine code definitions and ensure consistency in interpretation, enabling the team to examine how boundaries were experienced, how boundary infrast ructure facilitated or impeded collaboration, and what outcomes emerged from these dynamics.

The RPP examined in this study spanned five years and involved collaboration among a research university, a state Department of Education, and educators across multiple districts, focused on integrating CS/CT into elementary education through PD  and module  co-design. Further details on how the partnership evolved are presented in RQ1.

The analysis is organized using Farrell et al.’s (2022) Learning at the Boundaries  framework, which emphasizes partner roles, boundary infrastructure, and outcomes. This framework guided the comparison between the planned design of the RPP (RQ1) and its enacted evolution (RQ2), highlighting how challenges were addressed, how infrastructure supported adaptation, and the outcomes that emerged for teachers, students, and the broader field.

RQ1: What was the planned design of the RPP, including the intended roles of partners, activities, and resources to support collaboration and CS/CT integration in elementary education? The RPP was conceived as an extension of an existing collaboration between a research university and a state education agency, with the adoption of new K –12 CS standards prompting a n increased  focus on elementary education. Four central goals were established: (1) to adapt an existing PD program for teachers in grades 3 –5;

(2) to integrate equity into the program through culturally relevant pedagogy; (3) to strengthen teacher capacity

in designing and implementing interdisciplinary CS/CT-integrated literacy and math modules; and (4) to establish a free public repository of modules and resources for statewide use. These goals reflect the project’s ambition to strengthen teacher practice while simultaneously building systemic infrastructure for sustainable CS/CT education. In terms of the RPP structure, Figure 2 presents a general overview of the proposed model based on Farrell et al.’s (2022) framework. Figure 2 Boundary Infrastructure and Expected Outcomes of the RPP 323 Proposed partners acting as boundary spanners The original plan envisioned a partnership that would bring together researchers, education agency staff, and practitioners with complementary expertise. While these actors were expected to assume distinct roles within the partnership, the design also expected that some would take on boundary-spanning roles by facilitating connections across organizational and professional contexts: (1) University-based researchers would contribute expertise in CS education, teacher learning, and equity-focused pedagogy; (2) State Department of Education (DOE) staff would ensure align ment of  the work with state-level standards and curricular initiatives; (3) Teacher leaders, drawn from existing professional communities, would mentor peers and help establish a supportive network of practitioners; and (4) Evaluators and external advisors would provide independent guidance and methodological oversight. The proposal emphasized a shared commitment to collaboration, especially between the university researchers and the Department o f Education: “Both organizations are enthusiastic about partnering to improve state capacity for CS education”.

Proposed boundary practices The design also specified recurring boundary practices to support collaboration, including: (1) A summer PD institute, intended as a co-design space where teachers would create CS/CT-integrated modules (lesson plans) using digital tools and culturally relevant pedagogy; (2) Iterative design cycles, in which teachers would design, pilot, and reflect on aspects of their CS/CT-integrated modules. This process involves using modules developed during the PD, refining them as needed and integrating them into their classroom practice with support from partnership members; (3) Monthly follow-up meetings, proposed as structured opportunities for module refinement and feedback, as well as spaces for joint decision making with all the partners; and (4) Evaluation, in which an external evaluation team assesses the progress and outcomes of the partnership by collecting insights from the partners and individuals impacted by the activities. Proposed boundary objects Several boundary objects were described as supporters of joint activity: (1) CS/CT-integrated modules, defined as “a sequence of activities focused on learning specific CT concepts integrated with literacy/math units and may include kinesthetic activities, videos and creative projects”; (2) A public repository of CS/CT-integrated materials, envisioned as a free and accessible platform for disseminating modules and resources statewide; and (3) Evaluation tools and reports, intended to provide evidence for iterative refinement. Anticipated outcomes The original plan anticipated both organizational and long-term outcomes. At the organizational level, the aim was “to build local district capacity for the implementation of the CSTA standards in grades 3-5 by engaging teachers directly in co-design and implementation work ”. In the longer term, the design anticipated that “ by the end of Year 3 the process will be institutionalized and teachers…will be able to implement all modules developed by teacher participants”.

RQ2: How did the research–practice partnership (RPP) evolve over time to navigate and overcome challenges in integrating CS/CT into elementary education?

Figure 3 is an updated version of the figure synthesizing the boundaries, boundary infrastructure, and outcomes of the RPP as it was implemented. An explanation of each component is provided in subsequent sections. Boundaries The RPP faced persistent challenges. Staff turnover at the Department of Education limited its role: “ massive turnover has been a problem for us… and that’s been detrimental. ” as reported by one of the DOE interviewees. Another agency colleague described being “ more of a thought partner than a hands-on actor.” Turnover also affected the research team, with retirements, leadership changes, and the graduation of research assistants. As one interviewee summarized: “This was interrupted by a lot of things like [PI] left and went somewhere else, and people retired, and Covid happened, and there was a lot of teacher turnover.”  Due to Covid-19 disruptions, Cohort 1 (2020–21) had only nine teachers, four of whom piloted lessons, while Cohort 2 remained small (five teachers, three pilot implementations). At the school level, teachers described barriers to integrating CS/CT  into their classrooms. According to responses in the survey, these included competing curriculum pressures, time constraints, and self-confidence (See Table 1). Even when motivated, teachers found it difficult to add CS/CT on top of existing responsibilities.

Teacher leaders became especially important spanners. As  the RPP struggled to leverage its partnership with the Department of Education, it followed a more bottom-up strategy by mobilizing teacher leadership. Some teachers were invited to have a more prominent role in the planning of the PD and took part as instructors in the summer PD. Invited teachers were PD participants in prior years and had developed close and strong relationships with the research team. They brought teachers’ ideas and perspectives w ithin the spaces of design and decision making and were also instructors during some of the sessions during the summer PD institute, which helped make activities more relevant for attending educators. After the conclusion of the last summer institute,  a group of teachers met with a DOE representative to discuss how the work could continue beyond the duration of the funding period. One teacher recalled, “we all kind of agreed that it was really sad that it was coming to an end, and… we still have this passion that we would like to continue to explore.” This illustrates how teachers assumed ownership of sustaining the partnership’s work.

Members of the research team, including graduate research assistants and  project coordinators, also played spanner roles. They regularly translated teacher feedback into PD design. One interviewee noted how a graduate research assistant “ would say, you know, ‘The teachers said that they want more examples of how to integrate computer science into language arts, so we have to make sure that one of the components of what we do is that.’” Another educator reported in the interview how the research team was focused on developing PD that was relevant, useful and used “I can remember [the PIs] saying like, ‘Hey, do you think this is a good idea? Do 325 you think this is practical?’ Or they would ask an opinion like, ‘Would you use this in the classroom?’…I definitely think that was valued.” The evaluation team also served as a spanner by systematically gathering teacher feedback, analyzing it, and sharing insights with the research team to improve program design. Their role ensured that teacher voices were consistently included in shaping activities.

Boundary practices Three key boundary practices sustained the RPP. First, summer PD institutes and follow up meetings were central activities. Across five cohorts (2020–24), 88 unique educators from 54 schools and 12 districts participated. While the early years were constrained by the pandemic, later cohorts grew substantially, culminating in 40 t eachers in Cohort 5. PD was designed to be iterative and responsive, with teachers completing pre-institute needs assessments and daily exit tickets. One participant explained, “It was clear… that the research team wanted the PD to be relevant, useful. ”. The PD was the space in which the partners came together, discussed, learned from each other and built stronger relationships.

Second, evaluation itself was a recurring practice. Teachers completed surveys, interviews, and feedback forms that not only informed adjustments to the design of the PD but also served as a continuous process of reflection and documentation for the partnership.

Third, the iterative design cycles, in which teachers implement their  modules with classroom-based support provided by undergraduate  students with a background in CS, created opportunities for teachers to experiment with CS/CT instruction directly in their classrooms. Over the course of the project, 37 undergraduates from the University served as classroom facilitators. Based on evaluation data, educators indicated that having undergraduate students come to their classrooms was “amazing, ” “so much fun” and “really helpful in a number of ways, ” (quotes from three different teachers/educators). A fourth called it “my favorite part of the whole experience.” Teachers themselves benefited in their content knowledge from working with undergraduates with more CS content expertise. The undergraduate students came prepared with lessons, activities and materials which were also helpful.

Boundary objects Several shared boundary objects helped to mediate collaboration across groups. Modules were developed, piloted, and shared widely. Teachers not only used them in their classrooms but also shared them with colleagues, extending the project’s reach. These modules  were later compiled into a public repository, ensuring their accessibility beyond the life of the grant. Evaluation reports and workshop surveys also functioned as boundary objects. Teachers used them to express needs and provide feedback, researcher s used them to adapt PD, and administrators used them to monitor progress. Finally, digital tools and resources such as Scratch, Code.org, CSFirst, and robots (e.g., BeeBots and Finches) were recurring objects around which learning and collaboration took place. Surveys revealed that 35% of teachers utilized Code.org, 27.50% used Scratch, and 17.50% integrated robots.

Intermediary outcomes Across five years, 88 educators from 54 schools participated in the summer PD institutes. Interview data revealed that teachers reported gains in confidence and knowledge, with one noting the program “ gave me the confidence to step out of my comfort zone and try something new, ” and another crediting it with preparing them to pursue a STEM position. Some also reported shifting perceptions about topics like artificial intelligence and a greater willingness to teach it (Ospina-Tabares et al., 2025). The RPP also fostered strong networks of collaboration. Teachers described learning from one another during the summer institute s, building relationships that carried into their schools, and in some cases co-developing and co-teaching lessons. As one participant put it, “ we were learning from each other, and that made me feel more confident to try computer science.” This collaborative spirit extended beyond the partnership: more than half of participants (55.6%) reported sharing lessons and resources with colleagues, indicating a multiplier effect.

Long-term outcomes The RPP generated several long-term outcomes in teaching, student learning, and research. A large majority of teachers (73.9%) reported teaching at least part of the lessons they created during the PD, with over half (52.9%) implementing them with multiple groups and 14.7% sustaining them across multiple years. Most teachers (76.5%) modified their original lessons, while 20.6% used them as written. These examples demonstrate increased teacher confidence and ability to blend CS/CT with other subjects. As one teacher noted, “This year, I used Scratch to practice Spanish… reinforcing everyday vocabulary 326 and oral expression.”  In the survey, teachers also reported substantial professional growth, with 67.31% indicating the program influenced their practice “a lot” or “a great deal.”, and 25% reporting a moderate amount. The project also had measurable impacts on students. Research documented positive shifts in attitudes toward computing, with students reporting enjoyment and interest in engaging further in CS (Mathayas et al., 2025). Direct implementation reached 666 students, while the broader indirect reach, calculated by the evaluation team, extended tonearly 14,000 students by May 2024. Finally, the partnership contributed to the research community through 15 research products and created a lasting public repository of CS/CT-integrated module plans for broader use.

The comparison between the proposed design (RQ1) and the evolution of the RPP in the implementation (RQ2) reveals important insights about the dynamics of partnership work. The original design anticipated a stable and formal collaboration anchored by the DOE and university researchers, with structures such as monthly meetings. In practice, however, staff turnover, leadership changes, and the COVID-19 pandemic disrupted these institutional anchors. Instead, the partnership leaned on a more bottom-up dynamic with teacher leaders, graduate research assistants, strong relationships between teachers and university researchers, and evaluation feedback as boundary spanners that maintained continuity. Similarly, while the proposal envisioned a more structured dynamic, the most resilient practices included the  annual summer PD institute and module  implementations, which functioned as flexible spaces for collaboration and adaptation. The boundary objects that seemed more useful were the modules and CS/CT tools, which were the objects that teachers found most useful in practice. These findings have implications for Farrell et al. (2022) learning-at-the-boundaries framework. The framework is useful in explaining the main dynamics of collaboration in RPPs, but our study reveals that  these elements can substantially change over time, and they evolve in ways that require theoretical attention. Specifically, we argue that the framework should account for (1) the fragility of institutional spanners considering that the framework highly emphasizes organizations within RPPs, and the compensatory role of emergent spanners, which can become relevant in those moments of fragility. In this case, teachers played the role of emergent spanners; (2) the fact that boundary practices must be adaptive, with flexible and more horizontal practices, like PD, proving more resilient than formalized structures; and (3) the emergence of participant-valued boundary objects that may differ from those initially proposed. Attending to these dynamics would strengthen the framework by recognizing the need for adaptability, flexibility, and openness as core design principles for sustaining RPPs in complex educational contexts.

Regarding RPPs for CS education at the elementary level, this partnership demonstrated that, even amid institutional turnover and external disruptions such as the COVID-19 pandemic, RPPs can generate meaningful outcomes for teachers and students when they embed responsive structures and prioritize teacher leadership. Teachers emerged as critical boundary spanners who not only bridged research and practice but also sustained momentum when institutional engagement was weak. This underscores the importance of positioning teachers as co-designers and leaders, promoting a more bottom-up structure, aligning with prior findings by Adrion et al.

(2022) and Novak and Khan (2022).

Professional learning was most impactful when iterative and grounded in authentic classroom practice. The combination of responsive PD,  opportunities to test lessons with undergraduates, and avenues for ongoing collaboration between participants fostered teacher confidence and agency. These findings suggest that RPPs should move beyond exclusive PD models to ongoing cycles of co-design, f eedback, and adaptation that strengthen teachers’ capacity to integrate CS /CT meaningfully across content areas. At the classroom level, integration of CS/CT within literacy, math, and other subjects provided a feasible pathway to overcoming implementation barriers such as time and curriculum constraints. Open, modifiable lesson resources proved essential for supporting adaptation across contexts, while interdisciplinary framing helped normalize CS/CT as part of everyday teaching.

Finally, the outcomes of this experience suggest that RPPs can play a pivotal role in expanding CS access by cultivating strong partnership structures, empowering teacher leadership, and embedding CS/CT in interdisciplinary and community-relevant ways, which may lead to a multiplier effect in which the reach of these initiatives may be scalable (in this case, 88 teachers and 13,882 students). Therefore, for sustainability and scale, future RPPs should build an infrastructure that challenges turnover and amplifies the multiplier effects of teacher networks and open resources.
