# Agentic Collaboration with GenAI: A Cautionary but Optimistic Tale

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) extends human performance but may diminish users’ sense of ownership. Drawing on research in CSCL and sociocultural theories of agency, we analyzed 559 naturalistic conversations from the LMSYS-Chat1M dataset, and coded for collaboration (e.g., Externalization, Co-construction) and agency (e.g., Critical Vigilance). Deep collaboration and high agency were infrequent but related. This suggests that CSCL research can inform the design of agency-supportive human-GenAI interactions.

Generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) can extend people’s capabilities to accomplish previously unattainable tasks, such as producing professional-quality videos or enabling non-programmers to write scripts. Yet, there is growing concern over diminished human agency (Favero, Pérez-Ortiz, Käser, & Oliver, 2025; Mei et al., 2025; Yepiskoposyan, 2025). Agency is the capacity to act to achieve one’s goals through volition, control, ownership, and engagement. But i f activity is increasingly offloaded to GenAI, then people may lose this sense of agency over the processes and products of user-GenAI interactions. We share this concern and aim to understand how we can maintain human ag ency. To this end, we analyzed conversations with  GenAI chatbots. We draw on sociocultural approaches (Ahearn, 1999; Bakhtin, 1981; Wertsch, Tulviste, & Hagstrom, 1996)  that consider agency as context-dependent and accomplished through mediational means. This implies that agency does not reside in the AI or the individual, but emerges from interactions among the setting, task, available resources, user, and GenAI. Our grounding assumption is that if interactions between humans and AI reflect deep collaborat ion akin to that experienced among humans (Roschelle & Teasley, 1995; Vogel, Weinberger, & Fischer, 2021), then such interactions may support agency. We asked: (1) Do conversations between users and GenAI chatbots reflect collaborative moves as previously identified in human-human collaboration? (2) Are there markers of user agency in user-GenAI conversations? (3) Is there a relationship between deep collaboration and high user agency? (4) Can we identify features of conversation that advance or impede user agency? The data were drawn  from an openly available corpus of naturalistic conversations with GenAI chatbots (“assistant”) collected from April to August 2023 [https://huggingface.co/datasets/lmsys/lmsys-chat-1m] (Zheng et al., 2023). Anyone (“user”) could access these chatbots freely and  anonymously after consenting to release conversation data.  To maximize the likelihood of observing collaboration, we included only problem-solving conversations (559 total). Table 1 shows a breakdown of turns and words. We adapted a collaboration coding scheme from CSCL research (Buseyne et al., 2024; Rosé et al., 2008; Weinberger & Fischer, 2006), (Table 2), and developed an agency scheme (Table 3) based on theoretical conceptualizations (Ahearn, 2001; Bakhtin, 1981; Wertsch et al., 1996). We expect that interactions that go beyond transactional to engage with or take up the other’s contributions (Roschelle & Teasley, 1995) are more likely to be associated with agency. Therefore, we derived twonew composite variables: deep collaboration and high agency. We used ChatGPT (OpenAI, 2025) to classify and filter the dataset and to code the conversation turns. If, as we posit, agency is a function of available resources, settings, and social positioning, then the qualities of the interaction could play a role in sustaining or dampening agency. Therefore, we examined whether human-GenAI conversations reflect agentive and collaborative moves. Deep collaboration and high agency were rare but correlated, motivating further research. We need to uncover the prevalence, strength, and direction of this relationship. We alsoneed to identify whether individual practices and dispositions, such as intellectual perseverance, can counter the tendency to defer intellectual engagement to machines (Turner, 2022). Consistent with our findings, recent work calls for the design of shared human-machine agency (Mathewson et al., 2023). Prior research in CSCL and the Learning Sciences, such as research on how to design for a balance between authoritative and internally persuasive voice (Tabak, 2020; Tabak & Baumgartner, 2004), and on how to guide and structure deep collaboration (Ludvigsen, Cress, Law, Rosé, & Stahl, 2016; Rummel, Spada, & Hauser, 2009; Vogel, Weinberger, & Fischer, 2021), can inform these efforts.

One can hardly go through a day without encountering some mention of GenAI. Much of the discourse  revolves around risks, such as the displacement of learning  and the dissemination of inaccurate information. This study, alongside others (Favero et al., 2025; Mei et al., 2025; Yepiskoposyan, 2025), extends the conversation to issues of diminishing agency. Our findings tell a cautionary but optimistic tale. Sustaining human agency is paramount because even if technological advances eliminate GenAI errors, the need for agency remains.
