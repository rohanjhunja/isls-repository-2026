# Designing Digital Enhancements for Social Emotional Skill in a Collaborative Learning Environment

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study presents the design and implementation of a fifth-grade Social and Emotional Learning (SEL) curriculum within a hybrid digital collaborative environment. Using a user-centered design approach, we engaged teachers and a school administrator as co-designers over a four-month period to iteratively refine the features and tools from a collaborative learning and assessment system (EPCAL). Key design decisions, such as tailoring class analytics to SEL and integrating feedback and rewards systems, were informed by teacher input. A semester-long pilot study involving two teachers and 22 students demonstrated the feasibility of delivering SEL through a hybrid model using EPCAL. Findings highlight strong teacher engagement and meaningful student participation. This research contributes a scalable framework for hybrid SEL delivery, demonstrating how a participatory design approach can be effectively applied to curriculum and technology enhancements-an approach that can be generalized to other hybrid learning contexts.

Social and Emotional Learning (SEL) is a foundational component of education, equipping students with essential skills such as emotional regulation, empathy, relationship-building, and responsible decision-making. These competencies support healthy identity development and help students navigate complex social environments (CASEL, 2021). However, teaching and assessing SEL remains challenging due to its reliance on interpersonal interaction and collaboration-elements that traditional classroom methods often struggle to capture effectively. Hybrid or blended learning environments, where students engage both in-person and through digital platforms, offer new opportunities to support SEL instruction. This format allows for flexible lesson delivery and classroom management, while enabling students to connect through multiple modalities. These settings are ideal for SEL’s social and experiential nature, while also enabling data collection to benefit teachers, students, and researchers. To support SEL in this context, we adapted the Social Skills I mprovement System Classwide Intervention Program (SSIS-CIP; Elliot & Gresham, 2020), grounded in the CASEL framework (Borowski, 2019; Jagers et al., 2019). This program outlines five core competencies: self-awareness, self-management, social awareness, relationship skills, and responsible decision-making. SSIS-CIP is traditionally delivered through a teacher-led slide deck with scripted activities, where teachers cannot get the detailed interactions of students in each team. To address this limitation, we introduced a computer-supported collaborative learning (CSCL) solution designed to enhance both instructional delivery and assessment, where students still participate in physical activities like role plays and discussions, but also engage in small-group chats via an on-screen interface (Hao et al., in press). This CSCL solution is facilitated by the ETS Platform for Collaborative Assessment and Learning (EPCAL; Hao et al., 2017) comprises two core components: a learner portal that facilitates collaborative tasks, displays performance data, and integrates teacher feedback; and a teacher portal that enables team management, real-time monitoring of student interactions, and post-lesson review of behavioral and performance indicators. Adapting EPCAL for SEL instruction in hybrid classrooms presented four key design challenges:

• First, the teacher portal, originally developed for remote collaboration, required greater flexibility to

accommodate in-person classroom dynamics, such as late arrivals, early departures, and weak internet connectivity issues.

• Second, the existing analytics dashboard lacked SEL-specific metrics and feedback mechanisms, limiting

its utility for formative assessment.

• Third, feedback from prior studies indicated a need for a more engaging and user-friendly interface to

better support student and teacher interaction.

• Finally, the curriculum itself required adaptation to fit the hybrid format, including the integration of

group discussions, self-reflection prompts, and generalization questions into the digital system, along with revised teacher scripts to guide their implementation.

379 Design process A Design-Thinking approach Designing for hybrid classrooms requires  a multi-faceted strategy that integrated human-centered design and UI/UX principles to better understand user needs and improve system usability. We adopted the five-stage design thinking framework-Empathize, Define, Ideate, Prototype and Test (Hasso Plattner Institute of Design at Stanford University, n.d.), to guide our process. During the Empathize and Define stages, we conducted contextual research and cognitive lab studies with teachers and students in a summer program, gathering initial feedback on the existing system. These insights informed the development of user personas, journey maps, and prioritized feature sets. In the Ideate  phase, we generated user stories, explored classroom scenarios, and defined user flows to address key challeng es. The Prototype and Test phases involved iterative co-design sessions with teachers, allowing us to refine the system collaboratively. A particularly impactful method was mapping the teacher journey within EPCAL (see Figure 1). Using a collaborative visualization board to capture  key steps, emotions, and pain points significantly improved design efficiency and fostered a shared understanding of the features needed to support collaborative learning.

Figure 1 A snippet of the Teacher Journey Map The teacher  journey map highlighted the main steps, goals and actions in delivering a SEL lesson, as well as teacher’s feelings and thoughts, pain points and opportunities to mitigate them. Before teaching a lesson, the teacher configures system settings, selects a profile image, creates a class, adds student accounts, chooses a lesson, and assigns students to teams of 2–4. During the lesson, they use the presentation tool to guide slides and manage classroom dynamics, such as removing offline students, restarting tea ms, or freezing students’ screens. Teachers can monitor real-time student conversations and review class performance afterward. Feedback can be given to the whole class or individual students.

Co-Design During Prototype and Test phase, we adopted a co-design approach (Dollinger et al., 2019; Dollinger & Lodge, 2018; Sanders & Stappers, 2008)  by inviting two teachers from our summer cognitive lab study and a district-level school administrator to join our research team as co-designers. This collaboration began in Fall 2024 and continued through monthly sessions until the end of the year.  Under the co-design model, researchers and designers work closely with end users to iteratively shape the final product. Each session included one hour of synchronous discussion and approximately 30 minutes of asynchronous tasks for participants, such as surveys or preparatory work for the next meeting. Developers, UI/UX designers, and content experts contributed additional time between sessions to refine prototypes and incorporate feedback. Teachers were encouraged to envision ideal features and workflows, which were then prototyped, tested, and reimagined collaboratively. This iterative process not only accelerated development and reduced redundancy but also deepened our understanding of how the system would be used in practice. Below we highlight two main design decisions derived from co-design with teachers. Class analytics dashboard One of the primary challenges within the teacher portal was the need to tailor the class analytics dashboard to the SEL domain. To address this, we presented teachers with low-fidelity wireframes and mock-up charts, prompting them to reflect on the types o f insights they would expect from such a dashboard. Teachers emphasized the importance of conversation-based analytics, such as the frequency of specific keywords, indicators of productive versus non-productive chat, and individual contributions to group activities. They also noted that certain metrics, like participation rates or lesson timing, offered limited value in assessing SEL development.  Three key 380 components: class chat analysis, student skill performance ratings, and lesson ratings, were derived through codesign. The presentation format and level of detail for each component underwent multiple design iterations. (Figure 2).

Figure 2 Design Mockups of Class Analytics Dashboard in Teacher Portal. From left to right, this figure illustrates the evolution of the class analytics page within the teacher portal. All three designs feature a class chat meter-a vertical bar graph showing on-topic (green) and off-topic (orange) chats. Lesson ratings have shifted from a pie chart to a star-bar chart, aligning with familiar rating conventions. Feedback and rewards system In a separate co-design session, we focused on the teacher feedback loop and the integration of a rewards system.

Using a Think-Aloud protocol, teachers walked through the process of completing a lesson, reviewing student responses and chat interactions. They also went through the feedback page where they can provide class-level or individual feedback. To support student engagement, we proposed a set of digital stickers as age-appropriate rewards. Teachers provided input on the relevance and visual design of the stickers, as well as their placement within the system. We discussed two integration options  (see Figure 3).  (1) Awarding stickers during real-time chat interactions, and (2)  embedding stickers within the feedback workflow. Teachers favored the latter, noting that it aligned more intuitively with their existing feedback practices and avoided overwhelming the chat interface. Pilot study In Spring 2025, we conducted a four-month pilot study in Northern Michigan (U.S.) involving two teachers and 22 students  in 5th grade. Across this period, teachers delivered 36 lessons covering 12 SEL skill units (e.g., “Listening to others,” “Taking turns when you talk”). Each lesson followed six  instructional events: Tell, Show, Do, Practice, Monitor, and Generalize. Students worked in small groups (2-4 members) to engage in collaborative SEL activities such as group chats, role plays, reflection, and generalization exercises. All student interactions, responses, and activities were recorded through EPCAL, enabling teachers to monitor engagement in real  time and review behavioral and performance data after the lesson. We partnered with a local school administrator to observe classroom implementation and collect teacher and student feedback through observation notes, while also analyzing system log data to track usage patterns. The pilot study focused on five key dimensions of technologyenhanced curriculum delivery: (1) classroom setup and technology integration, (2) classroom management and teacher engagement, (3) student engagement, (4) role play implementation, and (5) lesson  disruptions. and circles, to support interaction and role play. Teachers consistently demonstrated strong engagement, using the system to manage classroom dynamics including late arrivals and early departures. Larger classes experienced more frequent technical issues, such as logouts and chat failures, though overall system reliability improved over time. Despite these challenges, students were generally thoughtful and engaged, showing increasing comfort in expressing ideas and participating in discussions. Role play remained a central instructional strategy, though its effectiveness varied depending on clarity of instructions and transition management. Feedback on curriculum modifications highlighted concerns about the chat feature, which some teachers found disruptive to pacing. Students occasionally misused the chat, e.g., excessive emojis or switching keyboard languages, leading to delays and peer frustration. While students generally appreciated the chat function, its misuse prompted targeted design changes to improve system messaging, streamline teacher tools, and promote appropriate communication within the student portal.  Both teachers found the sticker feature easy to use and effective for providing individual feedback. However, they expressed differing views on its usefulness when issuing stickers to the entire class. Although they used the feature occasionally, they mentioned that it effectively supported student engagement and reinforced positive behaviors. Overall, the design practices implemented in this project, specifically co-design and teacher partnerships, significantly advanced the development of a hybrid classroom model that integrates curriculum and technology enhancements to support SEL. Looking ahead, incorporating role plays and other interactive modalities into the digital platform holds promise for creating more immersive learning experiences and enabling more authentic assessments of student performance. These efforts lay the groundwork for scalable, data-informed SEL instruction that is both engaging and pedagogically sound.
