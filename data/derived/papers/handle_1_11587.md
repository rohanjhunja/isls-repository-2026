# The Interrelated Nature of Belonging and Self-Assessments in Computing Students’ Decisions to Persist

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Understanding small moments that influence students’ decisions to leave CS is crucial for supporting diverse participation. Research has found that students’ sense of belonging and perceptions of their ability affect persistence, but how students develop these perceptions in specific moments is less clear. We analyzed how eight introductory CS students discussed their belonging and self-assessments during common programming moments, showing how these factors are intertwined and arise from shared underlying experiences.

## Introduction

Women, Black, Indigenous, and Hispanic/Latine stude nts disproportionately leave undergraduate majors i n CS (Persistence & Retention, 2017). Their choices are not made in a vacuum; rather, decisions to enter and remain in CS are shaped by social and cultura l factors, such as stereotypes of computer scientis ts as masculine and solely focused on technology (Cheryan et al., 2015). Moreover, these forces are constantly being reproduced by the small moments that comprise a student's life. Taking a fr actal approach (Rogoff, 2023), we see that small moments in CS form and are formed by larger cultural trends. T hus, it is crucial to understand the moments that i nfluence students’ decisions to continue in or leave CS, as these decisions shape the broader CS culture. To investigate the moments that influence student decision making in CS, we take a qualitative approach to understand the constellation of experiences and understandings that a diverse group of introductory  CS (CS1) students called upon when (1) evaluating their sense of belonging in CS and (2) self-assessing their programming ability. We focus on these moments because students’ beliefs that they both belong and can succeed academically influence their decisions to continue studying CS (Lewis et al., 2011; Biggers et al., 2008), but previous research has not yet investigated the relationship between t hese factors during everyday programming moments. B y analyzing in-depth interviews with eight CS1 students, we found that certain experiences, identities, and understandings inform both students’ perceptions of  their belonging and self-assessments of their ability. Our primary contribution is empirical evidence of the c oncepts students draw on across belonging evaluatio ns and self-assessments, showing how these factors are interrelated.

## Theoretical framework

In this work, we build upon a large body of researc h on students’ sense of belonging, which has found that low belonging is linked to students’ decisions to leave  the CS major (e.g. Blaney & Stout, 2017; Höhne & Z ander, 2019; Master et al., 2016). One common definition o f belonging is “the extent to which students feel p ersonally accepted, respected, included, and supported by oth ers in the school social environment” (Goodenow & G rady, 1993, p. 80).  Much of the work on belonging in CS1  has been conducted through surveys that measure ov erall feelings of belonging (e.g. Mooney & Becker, 2020),  but these surveys may obscure how a student’s perc eption of their belonging can change rapidly depending on the situations they are encountering (Allen et al., 2021). A second construct linked to attrition from CS is s tudents’ self-perceived academic ability (Biggers et al., 2008; Tamer & Stout, 2016). Students’ self-assessments of ability correlate with self-efficacy, (Gorson & O’Rourke, 2020), predict interest (Lishinski & Yada v, 2021), and predict persistence in computing (Hun t et al., 2022; Lewis et al., 2011). Research has identified 14 common moments in the programming process when students often self-assess their programming abilities overly negatively (Gorson & O’Rourke, 2020). These moments include stopping to plan and getting help f rom an instructor. Furthermore, women tend to make more critical self-assessments than men (Gorson & O’Rourke, 2021; Hunt et al., 2022) due tonegative interactions with instructors and peers (Hunt et al., 2022). We suspect that belon ging may shape these interactions, and thus the expectations hold in their self-assessments, but this interaction has not been studied yet.

## Methods

We conducted eight semi-structured interviews with CS1 students at a mid-size midwestern university. We began these interviews by asking participants to talk abo ut how they came to take their CS class and about t heir experiences with CS during and before their undergr aduate studies. The remainder of the interview inco rporated survey questions on sense of belonging and questions designed to prompt self-assessments in response to specific programming moments, adapted from Gorson & O’Rourke (2020). We followed up and asked students to explain the reasoning behind their responses to gain more richness. All names are pseudonyms. We used grounded theory (Strauss & Corbin, 1998) to analyze the interviews. The first two authors coded all interviews together, discussing disagreements until we reached consensus. In this poster, we focus on instances when participants drew on the same c oncept when describing their belonging and self-assessments. To further understand this, we also applied techniques from na rrative analysis to explore the small stories (Wats on, 2012) participants told in their responses in relation to their broader stories of continuing in or leaving CS.

## Discussion

We showed that CS students use similar experiences and understandings to evaluate their ability and be longing, providing initial evidence that sense of belonging and self-assessments of ability are interrelated. Future wor k could build on our findings by designing interviews to explicitly focus on stories of how small moments influence student persistence, and investigate these moments in naturalistic settings and over time. Our findings build upon Seymour’s (2019) idea that “leavers and persisters alike experience challenges; the differences in outcome emerge from how they react, respond, and/or adapt to these ” (p. vi). By presenting a set of programming moments tha t present small-scale challenges to our participants, we were able to see how they came to different reactions. Three concepts that we saw across multiple students’ disc ussions of their belonging and self-assessments may help us understand these differing responses: preparedness for CS due to prior experience, being on-track in comparison to peers, and community membership. Our findings ca n help set the foundations for a theoretical framew ork grounded in these concepts, from which we can generate concrete recommendations for supporting persistence in CS.
