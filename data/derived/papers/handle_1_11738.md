# Held Between Two Fingers: Exploring the Bounds of Storying Land

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Contributing to scholarship attending to the uprooting of settled threads of coloniality in informal science learning environments, this paper aims to explore the ways youth workers dissuade and/or invite relational practices between and among humans and the more-than-human, and how these moments shape the resources young people take up to story land. Preliminary findings demonstrate that despite physical, programmatic, and institutional constraints, young people, alongside adult allies, still find ways to resist nature-culture divides.

## Introduction

Palestinian poet, Refaat Alareer writes, “If I must  die / you must live / to tell my story.” His poem continues by illustrating how, after his death, his possessions must be used to inspire love and hope; an invitation of reciprocity and  the carrying on of memory. To build towards ju st and sustainable futures must include reckoning w ith and working towards dismantling all prongs of settler colonialism, and inviting future visions that prioritize life-giving ways of being (Kelley, 2022; Vossoughi et al., 2023). To speak, here, of settler colonialism thus dema nds the acknowledgment of the ongoing genocide of the Pales tinian people and the occupation of Palestine by th e state of Israel. With a focus on the experiences of Black  and brown young people within an informal science learning environment, specifically within an activity dedicated to plant identification, this paper begins to explore the ways youth story land despite the constraints of nature-culture divides present within program facilitation.

## Theoretical framework

This work remains firm in its understanding that settler colonialism is a project specific to land, and a continuous process aimed to eliminate Indigenous communities o r privilege their assimilation to build and justify  a settlercolonial state (Wolfe, 2006). Through settler colonialism, Indigenous communities have been constructed within the past tense, instead of being presenced now and in the future. Further, land itself has been made u p to be uninhabited, making way for settlers to claim land and entitle a sense of discovery (Bang et al., 2016; Césaire,

2000) fueling nature-culture divides, which point to the severance betwe en humans and nature, and a sense of

human exceptionalism (Bang et al., 2016; Bang et al., 2012). In science education, these divides in nature-culture relations may look like a prioritization of codific ation, where relationships between beings are fashi oned in a static, hierarchical manner. These understandings r esult in a limited scope of valued science knowledg e and obstruct more expansive ways that learners may engage with science, disproportionately affecting the experiences of young people of color (Bang et al., 2012).

Yet, it would be remiss to ignore the lineage and i nnumerable ways marginalized communities have resisted oppressive systems, including within scien ce learning spaces and pertaining to nature-culture relations (Davis et al., 2020). Previous literature shares ho w young people have enacted the methodology of walk ing, reading, and storying land (WRSL) that presences In digenous communities and encourages the sharing of ways of thinking among learners (Marin, 2020); desettled  settled expectations through problem atizing the living/nonliving binary frame, and recontextualizin g curricula beyond the classroom space and within l arger systems of inequity (Bang et al., 2012); and engage d microacts of self-determination in an afterschool program through their contestations and movements to elsewhere (Davis et al., 2020).

## Data and methods

Preliminary findings were gathered from EcoExplorers, a program partnership between an informal science learning space based in a private university lab space, and a community-based organization (CBO) offering youth and adult programming to marginalized communities in a large Northeastern. During weekly, 90 minute sessions over the course of 12 weeks and geared towards midd le-school-aged youth, EcoExplorers emphasizes inquirybased, hands-on lab sessions, facilitated by community scientist s and focused on engaging with key ecological concepts.

Situated within the 2nd year of a larger, participa tory-design research project, this piece pulls from a compilation of participant observations and ethnogr aphic field notes, 5 hours of video data, and inter views from 5 community scientists. Clips of video  data were shared within 2 sessions of an interactio n analysis working group, building to 2 collectively-written, and then a thread of individual, culminati ng research memos. Through an interaction analysis (Jordan & Henderson, 1995) of a dense 3 minute clip, this work aims to explore the extent to which young people and adult allies reinforce, r esist, and recontextualize nature-culture relations in science learning spaces while sitting on unceded land. This piece asks (1) How is land being storied within EcoExplorers and (2) What resources are used to construct these stories? Analysis Each session of EcoExplorers roughly follows the same 4-part sequence: an opening question and brief, informal check-ins; a full group activity; station activities; and an open lab component. The activity that follows showcases two threads of facilitation, articulated through the movements and dialogue of Will, a senior community scientist, and Diana, an undergraduate intern. Will increasingly moves both himself and a plant identif ication book closer to Mia and Natalie, sitting on the opposite side of the table. He presents questions such as, “How big are the leaves on that one?” “Those are big, are there any smaller leaves?” and “Which one’s that?” Each question encourages the youth participants to use the book as reference  to engage with the plants on the table. Will also refers to the plants as “pretty beat up”  eliciting shared smiles and eye contact with the ot her adults at the table, and before backing away from the table, he shares, “So using the system, we can figure out one of them.” Accompanying Will, Diana, shares questions such as “You think it’s that one?”  and “[Is this] the name?”  Offering Diana and Natalie the space to share their own thinking. In c ontrast to Will, Diana stands away from the table d uring the activity, until a moment where Will offers a torn l eaf from one of the clippings. After Will and Diana  pose their questions, Mia and Natalie pause and then alternate between looking towards or  pointing to a place in the book, holding the plants between their fingers, and sweep ing up the soil o n the table into their palms into small piles before scattering the soil once more across the tab le. When Will asks them to tear off a leaf, neither  moves to do so, but later Mia picks a whole clipping up using t wo fingers. After Will moves away from the t able, both Mia and Natalie move closer, shoulder-to-shoulder to Diana.

## Discussion

Through tracing the facilitation decisions of both Will and Diana, it may be possible to see the emerg ing ways that youth participants are invited to engage and build relationships with both one another and the plants present: (No) Walking, reading, storying land In inviting storying land, it is necessary to attend to the entire methodology of WRSL (Marin, 2020). With this in mind, it becomes obvious that within this station activity, there is seldom to no walking present. Yet, if this piece aims to take up a stanc e of relationality (Vossoughi et al., 2023), where relations to land and waters are always present and may be attended to, perhaps attention m ay be directed instead towards the mechanisms that hinder walking and the movements of youth participants that are present, holding Natalie and Mia at the table. Surfacing the settled Applying the understanding of settled expectations as implicit and associated with an unawareness of institutionalized privilege (Bang et al., 2012), ma y be helpful in situating (1) the plant identificat ion book, advantaging unidimensional visual learning;  (2) Will, a cis-white man, positioning himself as the station and taking up increasing while working with youth of co lor; and (3) the lab space, hosted within a private  university lab space on unceded land together to compound and contribute to the restraint of physical movement by  youth participants at the station activity.

Limited movements to elsewhere To explore their moves to elsewhere (Davis et al., 2020, p. 6) attention is turned to Natalie and Mia’s interactions with Diana: Diana invites Natalie and Mia to make v isible their thought process; holds the leaf offere d by Will between her hands; and makes physical space for Nat alie and Mia by moving off the table, as Will encro aches closer. The way Natalie and Mia mirror Diana, and move towards her after Will moves away from the table, feels indicative of their acceptance of her invitations t o engage with her, with one another, and with the m ore-thanhuman beings present.

## Significance

Though there is much to be added on to this work, may this piece contribute to highlighting the many ways young people consistently resist systems of oppression, p articularly around coloniality, and how marginalize d communities persistently shape worlds to be more just.
