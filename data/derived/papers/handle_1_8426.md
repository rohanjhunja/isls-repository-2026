# AI-Supported Scaffolding for Writing Academic Arguments

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Despite the importance of writing academic arguments, instructors often lack enough time and knowledge to provide prompt support adaptive to individual students' disciplinespecific arguments. AI techniques can enable automated and adaptive educational scaffolding. In this study, we created a test version of the Artificial Intelligent-Supported Scaffolding (AISS) system that provides scaffolds in the form of alternative writing examples that human experts would write. Our mix-methods data gathered from 14 students enrolled in two sections of the same graduate-level online course revealed that the students leveraged AI-generated scaffolds to build a more substantial claim with elaborated ideas in a cohesive text structure. The current study's findings demonstrated the potential of AI to provide personalized scaffolding for writing academic arguments.

## Introduction

Competent writing capabilities are required for tertiary students because they are often tasked with communicating in a scientific manner through written arguments (P elger & Nilsson, 2016). Students must utilize disci plinespecific knowledge, integrate various information f rom multiple sources, and construct compelling argu ments (Goldman et al., 2019). Despite the importance of a cademic argumentation, due to the complexity of wri ting arguments, writing arguments often remains as a demanding task even for proficient learners. However, students rarely receive formative and prompt support due to instructors’ lack of time and skills to teach indiv iduals’ discipline-specific argumentation topics (McNamara et al., 2019; Passonneau et al., 2017). Scholars have explored the use of AI that may assi st or substitute for expert human tutors (Puri & Mishra, 2020; Yang, 2021). For example, Yang (2021) demonstrated that an AI system as a teaching agent guided students to deploy good processes and strategies in writing.  However, the current AI's ability tends to focus o n wellstructured domains (e.g., algebra) and therefore is  not enough to understand ill-defined and complex p roblems such as individual students' domain-specific written arguments (Koedinger & Corbett, 2006; Goel & Joyner, 2016). Admitting that the use of AI in education is in it s infancy, we attempted to leverage AI techniques t o help students to write academic arguments. To this end, we developed a pilot application named the Art ificial Intelligent-Supported Scaffolding (AISS) system. In  this initial investigation, we deployed a mixed-me thods approach to examine how AI-provided scaffolding dro ve students to revise their arguments. Specifically, the following questions guided this study.

¥ To what extent did the AI-enhanced scaffolding help students to improve the quality of their arguments? ¥ How did students draw on the AI-based scaffolding to revise their arguments?

## Background

Academic argumentation and AI-Enabled Scaffolding A skilled writer leverages domain knowledge and the  source of ideas they have learned to support a cla im and articulate a process of argumentation in cohesive a nd coherent ways to persuade others. One effective way that helps students improve argumentation skills may pro vide them with good examples pertaining to individu als’ argumentation topics and use examples as scaffolds to articulate their arguments (Westby et al., 2010). Given scaffolds, students may learn about more appropriat e languages, ideas, and professional tones and mann ers to write a strong argument.

We focused on the role of AI to provide automated scaffolds adaptive to individuals’ arguments. Scaffolds– is a broad range of instructional support that enables students to perform beyond their current capacity can take various formats, including prompts, feedback, guidance, hints, and expert models (Wood et al., 1976). In this study, we regarded expert modeling as the most effective way to support students’ argument development because expert models can serve as a conduit throug h which students consider better ideas and sound ev idence (i.e., conceptual scaffolding), learn about writing strategies (i.e., strategic scaffolding), and eval uate the quality of their arguments against given experts’ examples (i.e., metacognitive scaffolding (Anderson et al., 1997). We drew on the current Natural Language Processing (NLP) AI techniques that can make sense of the complex disciplinary textual data (Goel, 2019) and provides individualized and prompt scaffolds in the forms of computer-generated written examples that human experts would write. Therefore, augmented by AI, personalized ICLS2022 Proceedings 1129 © ISLS support for individuals' argument development can b e scalable to many students from diverse discipline s and backgrounds.

Lexical features, text cohesion, and argument quality Academic writing consists of developments at variou s interconnected competencies across different leve ls of words, phrases, clauses, intra-sentential, inter-sentential, paragraph, and textual levels of analysis. Accordingly, an argument is structured with lexical items—typica lly nouns, verbs, adjectives, adverbs, etc.—that bu ild their syntactic expressions and semantic relations together (Williams, 2015). Therefore, understanding an argument's lexical elements and syntactic structure is critical to determining the quality of arguments.  Scholars have agreed that lexical features contribute to both construction and comprehension of semantics of a text and therefore relate to the quality of arguments (Morris & Cobb, 2004; Vogelin et al., 2019). In this study, we focus on four aspects of text cohesion, as follows: ¥ Lexical diversity indicates the extent to which unique words are used in the text. ¥ Lexical density describes the extent of using content words (i.e., nouns, verbs, adjectives, and adverbs) in text.

¥ Sentence overlap indicates the number of text items repeated across sentences. ¥ Semantic overlap indicates the extent of underlying semantic relations in the text. ## Research context and participants

Participants were 14 students enrolled in two sections of the same graduate-level online course, which allowed us to have more cases and compare two cohorts. Cohort 1 included 6 students who consented to the study participation out of 13 students enrolled in a spring semester section. Cohort 2 consisted of 8 students out of 12 enrolled in the following summer semester. During W eek 9 of the course, as part of the assignment, on AISS, students developed their arguments for the question: "Is the constructivist learning approach better? Choose your position. If YES, what is a constructivist learning  environment, and what effects are intended? If NO,  are there downfalls in using the constructivist teaching approach?"

## Data collection and analysis

Scholars have used TAA CO to evaluate discourse-level cohesion–the degree to which information is connected across the text (Skalicky, 2019). In this study, we selected 17 indices corresponding  to the four text cohesion categories (see Table 1). Also, we conducted visual inspections of the written arguments to examine how students used the feedback to revise their arguments.

## Findings and Discussion

As described in Table 1, in both cohorts, final versions showed a decrease in the values of lexical diversity indices. Results indicate that the students tended to use se lected words (i.e., concepts), including their synonyms, rather than bringing in more new languages in their revisi ons. It seems that students became aware of and foc used on key ideas supported by the AI scaffolding. Contrari ly, indices of lexical density increased from draft s to final versions, meaning that the students more frequently used content words in their final arguments that contribute to the meaning of the text. Taken together with lexical diversity, it appears that students developed their arguments containing more words possessing semantic content while centering on key ideas. As to sentence overlap, both one sentence and two sentences adjacent overlap indices for all lemma types and content words lemma types tended to decrease while their overlap binary values increased, indicating that the students attempted to avoid a redundant use of the same languages (overlap indices) but tried to properly use main ideas across adjacent sentences (overlap binary indices), resulting in a more cohesive argument. In the same vein, semantic overlap indices showed an increased overlap in the students' arguments, indicating that the students used languages within similar topics (i.e., LDA indices) and contexts (i.e., Word2vec indices). Besides, visual inspection of students' arguments showed that students used examples to add new ideas and paraphrase AI-generated texts. Such additional activities entailed restructuring the logical flow of the arguments (examples of visual inspections will be presented at the conference). The findings of this exploratory study demonstrated  the potential of AI to provide personalized scaffolding for writing academic arguments. The AIS S scaffolding appeared to help students to locate r elevant and most current literature. Students leveraged AI-generated scaffolds to build a stronger claim with elaborated ideas in a cohesive text structure. However, the cu rrent study revealed several limitations. NLP AI te chniques need be fine-tuned to are still at the infancy stag e, requiring further articulation.  The original AI  algorithms in AISS have not been tuned to educational use, especi ally for scientific argumentation. For example, the  AI sometimes generated thematically irrelevant suggestions. Also, we need to detect whether students just copy and paste the suggestions without learning how to formulate their own responses to improve text.
