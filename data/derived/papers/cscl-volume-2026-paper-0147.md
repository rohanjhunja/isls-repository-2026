# Reflective Assessment for Authentic Research among High School Students: Enhancing Epistemic Agency for Knowledge Building

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study investigates how high school students engage with reflective assessment during their authentic research experience. An individual interview was conducted to explore students’ reflections on their use of a reflective assessment document. Grounded theory analysis of the interview data and their reflective assessment document revealed key engagement. As a compelling theme, this document guide s students to interpret research evidence and identify progressive questions. The findings inform educational designs to cultivate epistemic agency in a more student-centered, open-ended, collaborative inquiry context.

To prepare students for college, careers, and citizenship in the rapidly changing world, education needs to cultivate students’ abilities to solve complex problems. In this context, recent educational reform highlights the need to engage students in authentic knowledge creation practices, similar to those used by real-world researchers to explore and address real-life problems (OECD, 2018).  The work reported herein explores how such authentic research experiences can be assessed in a way that engages students’ agency for collaborative knowledge building. Our design of authentic research for high school students draws upon frameworks of community-based learning (Rogoff, 2014) and Knowledge Building (Scardamalia, 2002). Students enact epistemic agency for charting their research process in collaboration with their teachers and community partners. Epistemic agency refers to “a capacity that enables groups to deliberately carry out collaborative, knowledge-driven activities to create shared knowledge objects” (Damşa et al., 2010).  Thus, students intentionally take epistemic agency and collective responsibility for collective idea improvement while navigating the process of collective inquiry to work towards a shared epistemic artifact.

Implementing such authentic research experience among high school students requires new ways to assess student engagement and progress. Practices of authentic research experience in K-12 engage in predefined courses and tasks (Aydeniz et al., 2011; Melton et al., 2022), limiting student epistemic agency to engage in openended inquiry and do the knowledge building. Assessment in these practices usually emphasizes the acqui sition of knowledge and skills in individual students, blocking students from working with their peers to co-construct new knowledge and develop competencies. This paper addresses this gap by designing reflective assessments and investigating student engagement with one document of reflective assessment. Reflective assessment is a type of formative assessment that guides students to enact metacognitive processes for charting and reflecting on collaborative inquiry. More research should be done to investigate favorable circumstances  in which reflective assessment could enhance students’ high-level epistemic engagement in real-world knowledge  building wor k (Scardamalia, 2002; van Aalst & Chan, 2007; Dan & Zhang, 2018). Based on this, our research question investigates how students engage in reflective assessment to address authentic research problems.

## Method

Classroom contexts and reflective assessment design This study is part of an ongoing design-based research project. For the past three years, our research team has been collaborating with a team of teachers and community partners (CPs) to develop a collaborative environment for high school students to engage in authentic research experiences (Zhang et al., 2024, 2025; Ding et al., 2025). At the beginning of the project, our research team shared a few knowledge building principles with students and teachers, including continual idea improvement, collaborative build-on and rise-above reflection (Scardamalia, 2002). Thus, students get an understanding of research. Research is an ever-deepening process, they can build on their peers’ contributions to advance the community’s knowledge, and they need to chart, reflect on the paths of inquiry together, and search for new goals during this process. As a part of building this collaborative environment, our research team worked with three teachers to develop the first iteration of reflective assessment design to 761 enhance students ’ epistemic agency. To support students’ idea improvement, metacognitive engagement, and ongoing reflection, the design mainly includes a reflective assessment document for  guiding students over the cycle of setting the shared goal for their research, documenting the individual research activities for solving their research question, team discussion for monitoring and reflecting on their research findings, and the team’s next steps. What is more, three teachers were present in the classroom to give instructions about the use of this document. They also monitored the progress of students’ teams through the  document and provided guidance to address students’ needs. Students received feedback from their peers, teachers, and partners during the project. Data sources and analysis The data sources are mainly  each team’s reflective assessment document, known as research tracking document (RTD) by students, and student interviews. A total of 17 students volunteered to participate in an interview about their research experience. The current study zoomed in one interview question focused on students’ reflection on RTD: I remember your teachers introduced the Research Tracking Document. Here is your group’s doc, you could take a look at it. Did you find it useful for your research? If yes, in what ways? In total, 6 out of 17 students ’ interviews were excluded because they did not provide sufficient clarity or details to support meaningful sense-making. The interviews were transcribed and analyzed using grounded theory approach (Strauss & Corbin, 1998). Before coding, the first author read and re-read transcriptions of interviews and team’s RTD, creating raw codes to capture students’ engagement with reflective assessment. Then, for axial coding, the first author reviewed the raw codes and examples to identify thematic clusters. The second coder selected 20% of students’ interviews and repeated the process of grounded theory coding. The interrater reliability between two researchers was 95%. Theme 1: Eliciting research evidence in an organized way This theme focused on processing information using RTD. Students emphasized that they use RTD  to organize components of research, such as research topics, questions, and sources, more clearly and systematically. Theme 2: Guiding students to interpret research evidence and identify progressive questions The second theme focused on the epistemic function in the process of research. Students highlighted that reflective assessment helped them answer the existing research question and uncover emerging questions  in their research progress. David (pseudonym) told us: “You do it in a linear thinking process from one to another. So our first thing was like ‘W’ Community Board. We found that, and then we summarized it, and then what are our next steps, and what did we take away from this? And we just kept doing that, did that like 10 or 15 times. ” Theme 3: Developing a coherent understanding of research thinking The third theme conveyed the epistemic results of using RTD, students developed  a coherent understanding of research thinking by using a linear thinking process manifested in RTD.  Kelly mentioned that “ The document [Research Tracking Document] really helped organize my thoughts, even, like some of it wasn't just research. And I still put my main points of our meetings, like meeting with our CP and other stuff. ”

## Theme 4: Finding the ways to solve their challenges

The fourth theme focused on epistemic results of RTD. In the project, students needed to solve the challenges raised by the CPs. Students noticed that using RTD to do research would help them find their way to solve their challenges. As we heard from Leo  (pseudonym), “We go down the line of questions until we a re good on that, keep going until you find the answer you need. All of the answers work together [to solve our challenge].” Theme 5: Monitoring and reflecting on research to have a clear sense of progress The fifth theme focused on the regulative function of RTD. Students highlighted the function of monitoring, reflecting their research activities, thus having a clear sense of research progress.  As Max (pseudonym) told us, “This [RTD] helped pace our research. This helped us know where we were at and when we were done.” Essential to  students’ epistemic agency, the findings shed light on students’ engagement with reflective assessment. The study is among the first to design and implement the reflective assessment for high school students in authentic research experience, integral to open-ended collaborative inquiry. The findings indicate that reflective assessment is conducive to the development of epistemic and regulative dimensions of episte mic agency.
