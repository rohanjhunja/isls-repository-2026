# Design as Pedagogy: Employing Design Problem Solving to Develop Critical AI Literacy in Teacher Education

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study explores design problem solving as a framework for developing critical AI literacy in teacher education. Twenty-five in-service teachers in a graduate-level course designed “AI Information Sheets,” modeled after nutrition labels, to make AI sy stems more transparent and accessible. Data sources included the fact sheets, peer feedback, and reflections. Thematic analysis showed that teachers emphasized accessibility, privacy, transparency, and civic responsibility, linking technical understanding with ethical concerns. The findings suggest that design-based pedagogies can scaffold critical AI literacy by fostering critical thinking, navigating constraints, and engaging teachers in authentic reflection on the societal dimensions of AI.

Artificial intelligence (AI) is socially constructed and both shapes and is shaped by human behavior and relationships (Buccella, 2023). By shaping individual action and reconfiguring power dynamics, AI technologies influence social practices, including teaching and learning. As generative AI (GenAI) enters domains such as writing, reasoning, and teaching, it mediates how learners and teachers engage in sensemaking, information processing, and problem solving. These shifts highlight the need to prepare students not only with technical competencies, but with the epistemic and critical capacities required to participate in an AI-facing society (Bae et al., 2026; Ng et al., 2021; Miao & Holmes, 2023; U.S. Department of Education, 2023; World Economic Forum, 2026). Accordingly, AI literacy needs to extend beyond technical skills. AI literacy has been broadly defined as the ability to understand, use, and evaluate artificial intelligence technologies (Long & Magerko, 2020; Mills et al., 2024; Sperling et al., 2024). Critical AI literacy combines technical knowledge with reflective analysis of personal and social, cultural, and ethical implications (Adams et al., 2023; Whittaker, 2021). This perspective emphasizes user agency (i.e., teachers and students): they should be empowered to decide when to use them, how to use them responsibly, or whether to resist  their use. Critical AI literacy therefore invites users/learners  to interrogate whose interests AI serves, how it redistributes power, and what consequences it carries for equity, the environment, and society more broadly (Guest et al., 2025). In educational contexts, raises important pedagogical questions about “which skill sets or competencies can be ethically or pedagogically allowed to become deskilled through automation or be better supported by AI,” and “which forms of thinking and learning should we preserve as uniquely human?” thus this orientation reframes AI literacy as a form of civic empowerment, enabling teachers and learners to make informed, value-driven choices about technology. Motivations for developing critical AI literacy arise from the complexities and tensions surrounding AI development and use. Scholars have observed that industry narratives and technical language can make it difficult to recognize or fully understand potential limitations and unintended consequences of AI systems. (Guest, 2025). Moreover, discrepancies between what AI technologies are marketed to do and what they actually deliver are well documented (Raji et al., 2022), prompting strong critiques from some authors  (e.g., Bender & Hanna, 2025; Narayanan & Kapoor, 2024). Research demonstrates that they reproduce bias, reinforce discriminatory hierarchies, and normalize dominant perspectives regarding culture, race, and gender, even while presenting themselves as neutral (Almeida et al., 2022; Avraamidou, 2024). In educational contexts, these gaps between the promises of AI and its actual outputs can narrow pedagogy to prompt-and-paste workflows, reducing opportunities for reflection, critique, and creativity (Previtali & Fagiani, 2015; Watters, 2023). Thus, the question is how to prepare learners and educators to engage with AI in a critical and responsible manner. Critical AI literacy frames literacy as a socio-cultural and ethical practice, not merely as tool proficiency. It emphasizes the right and responsibility of learners to discern when and how AI should be used, situating these choices  within broader issues such as mis/disinformation, bias, corporate influence, and the consequences of AI for democracy and justice, while also affirming their agency to make informed decisions about adoption, adaptation, or resistance (McQuillan, 2022). Accordingly, learners are positioned as agents who question systems rather than consume outputs.

Ethical principles and guidelines for critical AI literacy in education Recent reviews of AI ethics guidance reveal broad consensus around core principles such as transparency, privacy, fairness, and accountability, yet they also highlight the need to adapt these concepts to the specific developmental and pedagogical contexts of schooling (Adams et al., 2023). The studies add concerns such as pedagogical appropriateness, children’s rights, AI literacy, and teacher well-being, reflecting recognition that high-level ethical principles might be  insufficient without translation int o actionable classroom practices (Adams et al., 2023; Holmes et al., 2022). However, existing guidelines often remain abstract, leaving educators with little concrete support for evaluating and understanding AI tools. In this context, it is critical to offer a way to operationalize these principles  and address a key gap between principle-driven ethics discourse and classroom practice.  By making issues of data use, privacy, limitations, and risks visible in accessible formats, intentionally designed information sheets can scaffold transparency, promote informed decision-making, and model civic responsibility for students, thereby promoting critical AI literacy.

Design problem-solving to support critical AI literacy Design problem solving engages ill-structured problems through iterative generation, evaluation, and refinement under multiple constraints (Jonassen, 1997). Unlike well-structured problems with clear solutions, design problems require co-constructing problem and solution spaces (Goel & Pirolli, 1992). Design demands decisions that balance functionality, feasibility, and contextual appropriateness. These practices mirror professional work that negotiates trade-offs, integrates diverse perspectives, and justifies choices under competing constraints (Hong & Choi, 2011). Decision-making in ill-structured contexts aligns with learning science perspectives on authenticity as a driver of conceptual understanding, such as problem-based learning (Bae et al., 2023; Hmelo-Silver et al., 2018). Jonassen (1997) highlight ed that ill-structured problems foster higher-order thinking by requiring learners to engage in sustained inquiry, interpret information from multiple sources, and reconcile ambiguity. Similarly, Lombardi (2008)  argues that authenticity in learning tasks connects classroom activity to the complexities of the real world, thereby promoting critical thinking, transfer, and student engagement. In this study, students designed an “AI info sheet” modeled on a food nutrition label (see Figure 1). This task uses a familiar analogy and requires design decisions about representation, clarity, accessibility, and ethics when communicating about AI systems. Through the iterative process of designing the label, students encounter and reason about technical, social, and ethical constraints, such as transparency, bias, equity, and the accessibility of information. Thus, design problem solving can serve as an instructional strategy to support the development of critical AI literacy by scaffolding authentic engagement with technology, encouraging reflective judgment, and cultivating the skills necessary to interrogate AI’s role in society rather than accepting it as a neutral tool.

## Participants and research context

This study involved 25 in-service teachers enrolled in a graduate-level course in the Northeast USA. The course provided an overview of how the AI system works, the technological, social, and ethical aspects of AI, emphasizing how AI could be critically evaluated and integrated into K-12 classrooms. For example, participants explored educational GenAI applications and engaged in group discussions about the societal and ethical dimensions of AI, such as detecting biases, machine errors in AI tools, and writi ng “my AI classroom policy. ” This study is part of a bigger project and focuses on one project called "Designing AI Tool Info Sheet.” They were asked to design an AI Tool Information sheet similar to a food nutrition label but for websites or tools that use AI. The goal was to creatively and critically think about how transparency in AI usage can be communicated to the public in an accessible and meaningful way, as well as how the teachers candevelop their AI literacy skills through the activity. They were asked to design the sheet or infographics  and provide the rationale with their design. Then they had to share their design and provide feedback to each other. Later, they were also asked to submit a reflection paper. their written rationales, peer feedback, and individual reflection papers. To analyze these materials, we employed 1528 a qualitative thematic analysis approach (Braun & Clarke, 2006), which enabled us to identify patterns across participants’ reasoning, design decisions, and reflections related to critical AI literacy. The analysis began with open coding of both textual and visual data, followed by iterative clustering of codes into t hemes that captured how participants conceptualized issues such as transparency, bias, and equity in AI systems. Given the designoriented nature of the task, we also examined the artifacts themselves through a design-based lens (Goel & Pirolli, 1992), attending to how participants navigated constraints, made representational choices, and envisioned their intended audiences. This dual focus on thematic and design analysis provided a robust account of how design problem solving served as a scaffold for developing critical AI literacy. revealed several interconnected themes regarding how these tools can foster critical AI literacy in educational contexts.

Nutrition labels format as boundary objects in design problem solving to support transparency and accessibility It was emphasized that AI information is often buried in lengthy “Terms of Service” documents or scattered across corporate websites, making it inaccessible to educators and students. Designing information sheets in a visual, “nutrition label” format was perceived as a way to foreground key details such as privacy, data collection, risks, and limitations without overwhelming users. Th e format functioned as a boundary object, enabling teachers to translate complex, distributed information about AI systems into a shared, accessible representation that supported coordination and critical engagement across stakeholders.  As one participant explained, “I designed the sheet to be visual and straightforward since most people do not have time to click through all the different website links.” This design principle was not only about clarity, but also about lowering barriers for busy teachers, families, and students to engage critically with AI tools.

Figure 1 One Sample Design of AI Information Sheet Supporting critical AI literacy through ethical and civic responsibility in education Participants consistently described information sheets as scaffolds for critical thinking and authentic learning about AI. By naming the type of AI (e.g., natural language processing, machine learning), highlighting risks and limitations, and clarifying data practices, the sheets acted as a way to help end users “understand what’s happening behind the scenes” and challenge the assumption that AI tools are “always accurate or neutral.” One participant observed that many students “just use it without even thinking about it,” emphasizing the need for educational tools that prompt reflection and responsible AI use. In particular, information sheets were also framed as tools for modeling civic responsibility, particularly within classroom contexts where students are taught to make informed decisions. One participant described, “In history class, we focus a lot on civic responsibility, so modeling informed choices for students is essential.” Information sheets thus served a dual function: providing technical information about AI systems and supporting educators in advancing ethical AI use as part of broader civic education. In addition, participants highlighted various social issues such as privacy protections, Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act (FERPA) and Children's Online Privacy Protection Rule (COPPA) compliance, carbon footprint, known biases, and misleading marketing.

1529 Practical challenges of implementation: Tension between marketing and transparency Finally, participants identified challenges to widespread adoption, including difficulties in obtaining accurate information from companies, the risk of either oversimplifying or overwhelming users, and the possibility of information sheets being ignored like Terms of Service a greements. To address these issues, participants suggested that legislation or standardized guidelines may be necessary to require AI companies to provide the information in transparent, usable formats. At the same time, several participants noted the inherent difficulty of convincing companies to disclose limitations, biases, or ethical concerns. As one reflected, “Transparency often conflicts with marketing goals, and unless required by law or pressured by public demand, I don’t see many companies volunteering to highlight their limitations or ethical concerns.” This tension positions information sheets not only as educational artifacts, but also as interventions in the broader sociotechnical ecosystem, potentially by requiring regulation to ensure meaningful transparency. The findings illustrate how the activity of designing  AI Information Sheets supported teachers in engaging with AI systems through practices that align with the broader goals of critical AI literacy. Rather than treating AI as a purely technical object, teachers foregrounded questions of accessibility, transparency, privacy, and civic responsibility. These orientations reflect key dimensions of critical literacy in education: fostering awareness of power and bias, promoting informed decision-making, and situating learning in authentic, socially relevant contexts. By working through the ambiguity and constraints of a design problem, teachers articulated how such knowledge should be communicated to students and the public and deepened their understanding of how AI systems function. This suggests that design-based activities can serve as pedagogical scaffolds for critical AI literacy, linking technical comprehension with ethical reflection and civic responsibility.

## Implications and conclusion

This study manifested the potential of design problem solving as a pedagogical approach for fostering critical AI literacy in teacher education. By engaging teachers in the creation of AI Information Sheets, the activity moved beyond theoretical and technical discussions of AI ethics to an applied, reflective practice in which educators navigated real tensions between transparency, accessibility, marketing discourse, and their roles as teachers. The critically understand and evaluate AI systems, communicate their implications to students, and model responsible technology choice. In doing so, teachers practiced essential elements of critical AI literacy from analyzing risks, questioning assumptions, and to considering how AI affects their teaching and civic responsibility. As this short paper primarily emphasizes design outcomes, future work will extend the analysis to more closely trace the development of teachers’ thinking throughout the problem-solving process. In particular, we will draw more systematically on reflective  essays and peer feedback to examine how teachers’ reasoning evolved during the design activity. Building on this, we plan to develop a set of process-oriented case study that illustrate how teachers navigated design challenges, what motivated their engagement, and how participation reshaped their perceptions of AI. We will also more closely examine how structured reflection supported the development of critical understanding and making sense of their teaching practices. Together, this line of work can contribute  to explaining the relationship between design-based engagement, teacher participation, and the development of critical AI literacy.

Pedagogically, these findings highlight the value of authentic, design-oriented tasks that allow educators to grapple with both the technical and ethical dimensions of AI. Integrating similar activities into teacher preparation and professional learning can help bridge the gap between high-level ethical principles and classroom implementation, equipping educators to guide students in discerning when and how to choose to use AI technologies responsibly. More broadly, this study underscores the need for educa tional practices that empower teachers and learners as informed agents who are capable of interpreting, questioning, and shaping the role of AI in their professional and civic lives.
