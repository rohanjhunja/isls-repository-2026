# Domain-Inherent Metaphors in Educational Practice: A Single Case Study

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Scientists as well as students make sense of n ew information by relating it to prior experiences. For imperceptible concepts, doing so often involves constructing metaphors. As a result, disciplinary discourse contains a wide range of domain-inherent metaphors that date back to their initial discovery. Therefore, students need to learn these domain-inherent metaphors on their way to becoming scientists. However, it is unclear what characterizes these domain-inherent metaphors when teachers use them during instruction. Using chemistry as an example, we offer a detailed analysis of the domain-inherent metaphors that a high school teacher employed during his lessons both in speech and gesture. To guide future research, we theorize on potential positive or negative effects on students’ learning.

Philosophers of science theorize that metaphors are an important aspect of scientific discourse and theory building (Hesse, 1965; Mahootian, 2015). Metaphors allow scientists to relate new discoveries to prior, often bodily experiences and hence support sense makin g. Therefore, these metaphors make their way into expert discourse, such as in nomenclature and terminology, but also gesture  (Hesse, 1965; Jäkel, 2003; Mahootian, 2015). For example, the activation “barrier” of a chemical “ reaction” denotes the “increase” in energy when chemical “bonds” “break.” All terms in quotation marks constitute so called domain-inherent metaphors, meaning that they

(i) are inherent to expert discourse (Müller & Rau, 2025)  and (ii) possess a second  meaning that describes a

sensorimotor or social experience  (Steen et al., 2011). This metaphor use is often implicit as the metaphorical term has historical roots that became conventionalized. For example, even experts may be unaware of how Edward Frankland’s bodily experience (who coined the term chemical “bond,” Frankland (1866)) shaped today’s scientific language.

Domain-inherent metaphors yield both learning opportunities and potential ground for misconceptions. Even though these metaphors are highly conventionalized, the source domains that once helped the scientist make sense of the concept might help students to learn about the concept. For example, potentially it helps a student to think about the chemical “bond ” in terms of a physical connection between atoms  to make sense of molecular structure. Simultaneously, over-reliance on these metaphors may lead to misconceptions, for example when students believe chemical bonds to be rigid and physical (Boo, 1998). In addition, like scientists, students make sense of new information by grounding it in prior bodily experiences. When new information is highly abstract, imperceptible, or potentially even non-physical, students tend to struggle more to find something that they can relate the information to (Johnstone, 1991). Therefore, they may aim to grasp at any grounding opportunity that comes their way, including domain-inherent metaphors. In a recent systematic review, we examined research on metaphorical mappings in chemistry specifically (Müller & Rau, 2025). We found that research so far has focused on instructional analogies; that is, metaphorical mappings designed for instructional purposes. However, domain-inherent metaphors used in chemistry classrooms remain underexplored, even though they are a central component  of expert discourse. We consider investigation of domain-inherent metaphors a first step towards understanding their effects on student  learning. To start filling  this gap, we present  a single case study of one teacher. Specifically, we analyzed  all metaphorical mappings describing imperceptible concepts that the teacher expressed in language and gesture.

## Conceptual framework

Our work takes an embodied, constructivist stance towards cognition and specifically, we  discuss our findings through the lens of conceptual  metaphor theory (CMT, Jäkel, 2003; Kövecses, 2020; Lakoff & Johnson, 1980). In the following, we first elaborate on this theoretical stance. Second, we situate the study in existing science education research on conceptual metaphors and gesture. Embodied cognition theories assume that cognition arises from human existence in and interaction with the w orld (Shapiro, 2019). For science learning, this means that children first make sense of the world by observing and interacting. For example, they may develop the theory that the earth must be flat because whenever they look outside, the earth seems quite flat (Vosniadou & Brewer, 1992). They then bring these initial embodied 1367 conceptions to the science classroom, where they sometimes conflict with the scientific conceptions that the students are supposed to adopt, for example, that the world is round. One mechanism relevant to this context  is conceptual metaphor construction.  We define conceptual metaphors as a set of cross-domain mappings between a more familiar source domain and a less familiar target domain (Jäkel, 2003). Importantly, this mapping is not only relevant for students who learn new information, but also for scientists who make sense of yet unexplained phenomena. For the latter, the metaphorical mapping serves an explanatory as well as sense making function. For example, Aristotle already found that sound propagates like (water) waves. This metaphor enabled scientists to explore when the wave metaphor works and when it fails. The metaphor stayed in scientific terminology (e.g., “sound waves,” Hesse, 1965). Finally, learners may express these mappings not only in language but also in gesture as simulated (metaphorical) action (Cienki, 2016; Hostetter & Alibali, 2019).

In chemistry education specifically, research has mainly focused on instructional analogies and has to a large extent  neglected domain-inherent metaphors  (Müller & Rau, 2025). The limited extant research  on chemistry-inherent metaphors found that experts use more conceptual metaphors in general and more empathetic metaphors in particular than novices when solving problems related to entropy (Jeppsson et al., 2013, 2015). Therefore, this research indicated that indeed, metaphors are a cognitive resource for experts. Similarly, Dreyfus and colleagues analyzed one professor and one student discussing negative energy (Dreyfus et al., 2015). They found that energy was often conceptualized as location or substance, often within the same utterance.  Thus, research on chemistry-inherent metaphor s is limited to two concepts, energy and entropy, and to the higher education context.

We aim to understand the role of implicit, often unacknowledged domain-inherent metaphorical discourse in education, using chemistry as an example domain.  This work will serve as basis for future research to understand how instructors may support students in learning of and with domain-inherent metaphors. We ask:

1. What metaphorical language and gesture does a chemistry teacher use when talking about imperceptible

chemical concepts?

2. What role does this teacher attribute to certain metaphors?

While RQ1 aims at collecting metaphorical language and gesture generally, RQ2 regards the role that these metaphors played in the discourse and whether the teacher  used the metaphors intentionally; that is, whether he was aware of the metaphorical language and accompanied gesture production. Furthermore, we aimed to understand whether he was surprised by our findings of RQ1, or whether he was fully aware of his metaphor use. Here, we present a single case of one teacher. This case study is part of a bigger analysis of multiple high school teachers. With this contribution, we present an initial fine-grained analysis.

## Participant

We describe the case of Daniel (pseudonym), who we observed during a total of 7 lessons (307 minutes)  in three different classes spanning different high school levels. Daniel is a 40-year-old man who has been teaching chemistry for over 10 years at a Swiss high school, after having earned a master’s degree in chemistry. He never pursued profession other than teaching. Therefore, we can confidently consider him a chemistry expert as well as a teaching expert in the sense that he actively participates in both discourses. During these lessons, Daniel focused on the concepts of energy, reaction velocity, and dye chemistry. He taught in his native language German.

## Procedure

The study consisted of three parts. In the first  meeting, we  explained the study procedure and answered  his questions. Further, he completed a demographics questionnaire and consented to the study procedures. Second, we observed seven 45-minute lessons on three non-consecutive days. When videotaping, we recorded only Daniel’s and not his students’ voices. We edited the videos so that no students were visible. Finally, after completing the analyses, we conducted a follow-up interview with  Daniel. Here, we first asked him to explain a specific concept to see whether the metaphors in speech and gesture were robust over time. Second, we presented him with illustrative segments from the recordings and asked him to describe the metaphors that he used. Third, we described our own interpretation and discussed with him whether he agreed or not. We analyzed metaphorical language and gesture in the video material in three main steps: data preparation, the 1368 research assistant studying comparative linguistics and biochemistry. Therefore, both coders had expertise in highschool level chemistry. We discussed and resolved disagreements continuously for both the speech and gesture analyses. We first isolated sentences in the transcripts that mentioned an imperceptible chemical concept. In these sentences, we coded all words or phrases that related to the imperceptible concept. This included terminology, but also verbs associated with the concept as well as adjectives and pronouns. To answer RQ1, we used the metaphor identification procedure (MIPVU, Steen et al., 2011) to identify words or phrases related to metaphors. Importantly, the MIPVU does not claim to identify the role of these metaphors or whether they are a cognitive resource or a stylistic choice. Instead, it is a linguistic procedure that allowed us to identify metaphors systematically and largely objectively. In the following, we will use the notation “ TARGET CONCEPT is SOURCE DOMAIN” to indicate metaphors that map the source domain to the target concept. To identify the source domain, w e gathered  the basic (or most sensorimotor or emotional) meaning from the Germandictionary “Digitales Wörterbuch der deutschen Sprache” (DWDS). For every metaphorical word, we identified accompanying metaphorical gestures based on the metaphor identification guidelines for gesture (MIG-G, Cienki, 2016). Therefore, we defined a gesture as the dynamic phase of movement and tension in the hand occurring after either a relaxed position of the hands or from a post-gesture hold. Without sound, we first described the movement of the hands (e.g. hands positioned on chest-height, slightly cupped, as if loosely holding something). With sound, we then analyzed  whether the gesture was referential. For referential gestures, we differentiated between enacting  (e.g., throwing an imaginary ball), embodying (e.g., the fists representing balls), holding/touching (e.g., the hands holding an imaginary ball), and tracing gestures  (i.e., the index finger tracing an imaginary trajectory). This allowed us to identify the physical referent of that gesture (e.g., in the examples above, a ball). If the physical referent differed from the contextual topic (e.g., an atom), we coded the gesture as metaphorical (e.g. “ATOM is BALL”). We grouped the resulting set of individual metaphors to metaphor concepts according to systematic metaphor analysis (Schmitt, 2015). Two metaphors belonged to the same metaphor concept if they drew on the same source domain to explain a certain target concept. For example, the metaphor concept “ENERGY is (SPECIFIC) LOCATION” contained both the metaphor “ CHANGE IN ENERGY is LOCATION CHANGE” and “SPECIFIC ENERGY is SPECIFIC HEIGHT” as they both made use of the source domain LOCATION. This allowed us to understand metaphor use beyond individual metaphors. A metaphor concept that consist ed of many metaphors point ed to a source domain that served to describe a certain target concept frequently. Finally, to address RQ2, we analyzed the follow-up interview. Specifically, we noted which metaphors from the classroom data  the teacher employed again in the interview, potential disagreements with our interpretation, as well as whether he was aware of the metaphors that he used. This allowed us to validate our as perceived by the teacher.

We coded a total of 557 words  or gestures as metaphorical, corresponding to an average of 1.8 metaphors per minute of data. We found 120 different metaphors across speech and gesture  corresponding to 37 metaphor concepts. However, many of the metaphor concepts only contained a single metaphor. Therefore, they are rath er weak compared to other metaphor concepts. Table 1 summarizes the metaphor concepts that encompassed at least three metaphors.

This finding is consistent with CMT, which proposes that metaphorical language is rather the norm than the exception, especially 1369 in language describing imperceptible concepts (Jäkel, 2003). Further, two metaphor concepts consisting of the most metaphors were “ENERGY is SUBSTANCE” and “ENERGY is LOCATION.” These metaphor concepts have been comprehensively discussed in physics education (see Amin (2020) for an overview). They are further consistent with CMT in that they draw on image schemas (Lakoff & Johnson, 1999). Image schemas are “recurring, dynamic pattern[s] of our perceptual interactions and motor programs that give coherence and structure to our experience” (Lakoff & Johnson, 1980, p. xiv). In this case, Daniel employed the image schemas LOCATION and SUBSTANCE. In short, we found that Daniel drew on common sensorimotor experiences when discussing energy. These findings reproduce prior research in the context of a Swiss chemistry classroom  and support the claims of CMT that metaphor use is extensive when discussing imperceptible concepts. In the following, we highlight two representative reflections by Daniel on his use of metaphor. One metaphor that Daniel introduced intentionally was the “ ENERGY is LIQUID” (as opposed to the commonly used SUBSTANCE) metaphor. In the interview, he explained that he wanted to move away from the idea that ENERGY needed to be “added” to a chemical system to start a reaction like a SUBSTANCE would be “added”. He thought this would falsely suggest that there must always be a person present who manually does this adding of energy. Therefore, he changed his language intentionally to ENERGY “flowing” in and out of the chemical system. However, when confronted with our findings that he still used several metaphors conceptualizing ENERGY as a SUBSTANCE in his teaching, he voiced surprise and the goal to be more careful in the future. However, based on CMT, one might argue that refraining from using the “ENERGY is SUBSTANCE” metaphor is (i) likely not possible because it is highly engrained in expert discourse  and consequently likely also in his own thinking, and (ii) potentially not beneficial to students because they need to learn about and adopt expert discourse sooner or later.

The second reflection  that we want to highlight is related to the metaphor “ REACTION is LOCATION CHANGE.” This metaphor belongs to the metaphor concept “ENERGY is LOCATION” and is employed, for example, when chemists talk about the change in energy throughout a reaction in terms of a movement across a landscape. Daniel was not surprised that he used this metaphor, but he was not aware of a set of gestures that he used alongside the metaphor. For example, he often move d his hands along a path in the air when describing the progress of a reaction. When reflecting on these gestures during the follow-up interview, he connected the metaphor to teaching materials in the classroom. His reflection thereby complemented our analysis, as we only focused on language and gesture but not materials. He explained that as chemical reactions are traditionally written from left to right, he would further be careful to arrange substances that he would use during a lesson from left to right. He therefore pointed towards how the writing conventions of reactions from left to right, from reactant to product, constituted a metaphor. Previous work has similarly identified metaphors in visual representations, for example  in diagrams (e.g., an energy level diagram reproducing the ENERGY is LOCATION metaphor, Amin, 2020). We found that he was aware of many metaphors in speech although he was not aware that he used some of them. However, he was not aware of the metaphorical gestures he produced along with the metaphors in speech. We found that indeed, the language and gesture high school teacher Daniel used during teaching was rich in metaphors. We further found that he was aware and careful about some of these metaphors, and unaware of others. For the most part, he was not aware that he produced gestures accompanying these metaphors. We theorize that the use of 1.8 metaphors per minute of teaching will impact student understanding. For example, these metaphors may ground students ’ sense-making processes as they try to understand new information. However, it is possible that without additional scaffolding, they may over-rely on the metaphors and generate misconceptions. Examining differences between metaphors used by teacher s and students may  point towards a knowledge gap that teachers must address (Niebert & Gropengiesser, 2015). Here, we offer ed a first step towards a comprehensive collection of metaphors in educational discourse that the students need to understand.

While we were able to describe the role of these metaphors as perceived by Daniel, our analysis does not provide further information of what cognitive mechanism s led to his use of metaphors.  F uture work should investigate to what extent students adopt these metaphors easily and whether they truly think about the target concepts in terms of the source domains. If they do, teachers should introduce these domain-inherent metaphors more explicitly,  including their limitations. Furthermore, this would point towards designing instructional analogies aligned with these domain-inherent metaphors (Amin et al., 2012). Finally, we did not discuss speech and gesture separately in this short paper. Further disentanglement of which metaphors occur in speech only and which are supported by gesture may yield in further insights.
