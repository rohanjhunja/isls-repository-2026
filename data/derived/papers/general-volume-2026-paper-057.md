# Listening to the Land and Sea: Community Learning at Crystal Cove Conservancy

**Conference:** ISLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This community engagement workshop explores how environmental learning can be designed, enacted, and studied across interconnected land and sea systems. Centered at Crystal Cove State Park, participants engage in an immersive field-based experience that illustrates how public landscapes support connections among schools, community organizations, and professional science and land-management communities. Drawing on learning sciences perspectives, the workshop integrates guided exploration of inland and coastal ecosystems with structured reflection and collaborative sensemaking. Participants examine how learning designs incorporate scientific practices, local ecological phenomena, and community relevance, and how participation structures influence inquiry, equity, and civic reasoning. The workshop also considers how Crystal Cove’s existing STEM programs provide a meaningful context for examining field-based learning in environmental and climate education. Participants collaboratively generate design principles and research questions to inform future educational efforts and study. Overview and rationale for the community engagement workshop Across the learning sciences, there is growing recognition that consequential learning is not confined to classrooms but emerges through participation in community life and sustained engagement with meaningful problems that matter to people, institutions, and shared environments. Community-based learning frameworks emphasize learning as a process of developing knowledge, identity, and agency through participation in culturally and socially organized practices that are embedded in everyday activity systems (Lave & Wenger, 1991; Rogoff, 2003). This perspective is particularly salient for climate and environmental education, where learning is inseparable from community histories, public decision-making, and collective stewardship of ecological systems that learners candirectly observe, measure, and revisit over time (Bang et al., 2012; Fenwick et al., 2015). Despite increased attention to community-based approaches, the field continues to grapple with how to design and study learning environments that meaningfully connect classrooms, communities, and public institutions at scale, while also supporting equity, civic participation, and rigorous disciplinary learning. Learning scientists face persistent questions about how learners move across settings, how participation structures shape belonging and legitimacy, and how public institutions and shared landscapes function as infrastructures for learning that bridge formal and informal education. Initiatives such as the Environmental and Climate Change Literacy Projects (ECCLPs), a multi-institutional partnership linking universities, school districts, informal learning organizations, and community partners, have emerged in response to these challenges by supporting coherence, relationship building, and mutual learning across educational sectors. This community engagement workshop centers Crystal Cove State Park and the education programs of Crystal Cove Conservancy as an exemplary community-based learning ecology situated within this broader ECCLPs context that connects schools, informal learning environments, and professional science and land-management communities. Crystal Cove provides a distinctive opportunity to examine how learners engage in authentic scientific practices across interconnected land and sea systems, how they develop relationships to public natural resources, and how educators design and facilitate learning experiences that integrate disciplinary inquiry, civic reasoning, and cultural responsiveness. As a public landscape where scientific inquiry is deeply intertwined with conservationand coastal change, Crystal Cove offers a compelling site for examining learning at the intersection of science, community participation, and civic life (Ardoin & Heimlich, 2021).; Sezen-Barrie, et al., 2023). Theoretical background: Community-based learning Learning as situated participation Community-based learning builds from the premise that learning is not simply the accumulation of individual knowledge but a process of participation in social practices and the development of competence in activities that are valued within communities (Lave & Wenger, 1991). From this perspective, learning is inseparable from the s ocial relations, cultural norms, tools, and material conditions within which activity unfolds. Context is not a backdrop for learning; rather, it is constitutive of what is learned and who learners become through participation. Complementary sociocultural perspectives emphasize that learning involves the co-construction of meaning through interaction, dialogue, and joint activity within historically developed practices (Rogoff, 2003). These commitments have shaped learning-sciences research on apprenticeship, collaborative inquiry, and learning across settings, and they provide a strong conceptual foundation for understanding community-based learning environments that span schools, informal programs, and public institutions. Environmental and land-based contexts heighten the importance of this framing because ecological systems are material, dynamic, and consequential for communities. Learners encounter tangible evidence of change, such as shoreline shifts, erosion patterns, biodiversity indicators, and species distribution, and these observations are often connected to public concerns and local decision-making. These conditions create opportunities for learning that link scientific practice with lived experience and collective responsibility, enabling learners to interpret evidence in relation to community histories and future possibilities (Bang et al., 2012; Fenwick et al., 2015). Community histories, relations, and learning infrastructures From a community-based learning perspective, public landscapes and institutions are not neutral spaces but are shaped by layered social, political, and ecological histories that are unevenly experienced across communities. These histories matter for learning because they influence whose knowledge is recognized, how participation is organized, and what forms of engagement are legitimized. In learning-sciences research, shared environments can function as epistemic resources that support modeling, explanation, and argumentation, while also foregrounding questions of access, representation, and civic responsibility. Research suggests that when learners engage with environmental issues that are embedded in community contexts they can revisit over time, they are more likely to sustain inquiry, develop systems-level reasoning, and coordinate evidence with explanation (Hmelo-Silver et al., 2007; Zimmerman & Weible, 2017). Repeated engagement across settings supports iterative cycles of data collection, interpretation, and revision, aligning with learning-sciences work on inquiry, modeling, and the development of scientific argumentation. At the same time, extended participation in community contexts can support affective and relational dimensions of learning, fostering motivation, care, and persistence with complex environmental problems. Crystal Cove as a community-based learning ecology Crystal Cove State Park is one of California’s ecologically and culturally significant coastal landscapes. Established in 1979, the park encompasses approximately 3,936 acres along 3.2 miles of Pacific coastline and extends inland through coastal bluffs, chaparral canyons, and undeveloped backcountry within the South Coast Wilderness area (California State Parks, 2010). Located between Newport Beach and Laguna Beach in Orange County, Crystal Cove includes extensive beaches, tide pools, inland canyons, and a large marine conservation area and underwater park, creating continuity between terrestrial and marine ecosystems (California State Parks, 2011). Ecologically, Crystal Cove is a biodiversity-rich environment supporting diverse bird species, reptiles, amphibians, and rare coastal plants and animals found in Southern California (National Audubon Society, 2012; Californian Wildlife Region, 2013). The park supports a wide range of recreational and educational activities, including hiking, tide-pool exploration, and marine studies, which shape who encounters the park and how learning opportunities can be designed to be accessible and inclusive. In partnership with California State Parks, Crystal Cove Conservancy plays a critical role in expanding public learning opportunities through structured STEM programs. The Conservancy brings more than 7,000 low-income students to Crystal Cove each year, many for their first visit to the ocean, and provides programs at no cost to schools to reduce economic barriers. These programs exemplify community-based learning through sustained engagement, collaboration with scientists and resource managers, extended curricular sequences, and opportunities for youth to contribute data and interpretations that matter to real conservation and management questions. More recently, Crystal Cove Conservancy has attracted interest from university-based researchers studying environmental and climate literacy. The Conservancy’s STEM programs offer a valuable educational setting for examining how students engage with ecological phenomena, scientific practices, and conservation questions in field-based contexts. At this stage, engagement is exploratory and focused on understanding how existing programs contribute to student learning and teacher development. Any research activity is conducted in alignment with Crystal Cove’s established educational mission and supports ongoing efforts to enhance public learning experiences within the park.
