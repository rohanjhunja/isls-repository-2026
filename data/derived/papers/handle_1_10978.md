# The Development of Indigenous Allyship Among White Settlers

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
The promotion of Indigenous sovereignty is approached as a coalitional justice project involving decolonial and anti-colonial efforts. This necessitates that non-Indigenous people engage in developmental processes focused on recognizing and unlearning coloniality, disrupting white colonial structures, and taking direction from Indigenous Peoples through trusting relationships. I present a social practice framework to guide development of Indigenous allyship among white settlers based on case studies and interviews from Living in Indigenous Sovereignty (Carlson-Manathara & Rowe, 2021).

## Background: Approaching decolonizing as a coalitional justice project

The securing of Indigenous rights, self-determination, and the rematriation of Indigenous lands and lifeways—in the face of historical and ongoing colonial oppression—is a fundamental social justice issue (Tuck & Yang, 2012). Education—broadly conceived  to span settings and degrees of formali sm—is key to making progress on decolonizing societ y in ways that support Indigenous thrivance  (Brayboy & Bang, 2019; Yan et al., 2023). Learning scientists have made significant progress on designing and studying learning environments for Indigenous resurgence (e.g., Bang & Marin, 2015; Tzou, et al., 2019). Given the systemic and structural nature of how colonial oppression operates in society, it is vital to advance educational work through coalitional approaches where non-Indigenous people work to educate themselves and ot her non-Indigenous people to work in allyship around the decolonizing, anti-colonial, and resurgent efforts of Indigenous Nations and communities (CarlsonManathara & Rowe, 2021). We lack comprehensive frameworks for understanding how Indigenous allyshi p among non-Indigenous people —especially white settlers or occupiers —develops as they engage in learning experiences across years and decades through participation in various social arrangements and contexts.

## Methods: Conceptual analysis of adult case studies and expert interviews

To theorize the development of Indigenous allyship, I conceptually analyze d individual case studies o f nonIndigenous people learning to work in solidarity wi th Indigenous communities. A fabulous book  by CarlsonManathara and Rowe (2021), Living in Indigenous Allyship, provided the relevant case studies, interviews, and theoretical insights. First, I identified instances of learning across the 13 adult case studies of non-Indigenous people and interviews with numerous Indigenous scholars and activists in the text. Working from a social practice perspective (Holland  & Lave, 2009), I then engaged in thematic analysis of these learning actions to identify coherent sociomaterial practices used by multiple individuals—resulting in the framework shown in Figure 1. How non-indigenous people learn to live ethically in indigenous sovereignty According to Carlson-Manathara and Rowe (2021), the developmental accomplishment of non-Indigenous people learning to live in Indigenous sovereignty can be u nderstood as people “being in relationship with one  another and the land in ways that centre Indigenous knowled ge systems, governance structures, and prioriti es” (p. 30) which involves “ reorienting [settler] lives toward Indigenous Lands, stories, relationshi ps, laws, governance, resurgence, Treaties, prophecies, and Protocols ” (p. 114-115). With this overarching focus, m y theoretical synthesis resulted in nine social practices and six  underlying commitments documenting how settlers engage in such learning and development (see Figure 1). Each practice—which is a bundle of multifaceted actions guided by specific values—represents significant complexity when considered in social context. For example, the practice used to “d ecolonize [settler] ways and relationships with Indigenous nations, peo ple, and lands” involves: (a) gaining awareness of current structures and dynamic s of settler-colonialism, (b) developing a critical awareness about messages about indigeneity from white society, (c) confronting the ‘colonizing cage’ they are in by refusing their colonizing situation, and (d) learning to not reinforce colonial oppression in all of its forms. This practice accomplished across contexts relates to all of the enacted commitments and intertwines with the other practices. A leading  goal is to use this theoretical framework to inform the design and stud y various learning environments and infrastructures that can support the extended cultural pathways of becoming for non-Indigenous people (Bell, et al., 2012). For example, developing a critical awareness of mes sages about indigeneity in white society involves debunking adverse stereotypes  and presencing Indigenous histories. The case studies of individuals involved significant periods of develop ment over years and even decades and across various  social arrangements, relationships, and practices (e.g., activism, teaching, ministering, sales, pare nting). I imagine this framework could evolve into a learning design framework for cultivating Indigenous allyship across scales. ## Conclusions and implications

This work is motivated by an ethical imperative to respond to colonial structures of injustice and oppression and a commitment to make every effort to dismantle them in support of Indigenous rights and self-determination. “Colonialism is historical, colonialism is present, and colonialism is daily; not only for Indigenous P eoples, but also for settlers” (Carlson-Manathara and Rowe, 2021, p. 46). I consider this work to be a specific form of antioppressive and culturally resurgent theorizing with  broad educational implications for non-Indigenous people. Also, at a time of escalating climate catastrophe and ecocide, a just societal response is best accomplished through societal support of Indigenous leadership, rights, and knowledge (Mitch ell, 2020). We should work to promote critical consciousness and widespread action to accomplish these social transformation s. I look forward to discussions and collaborations to relate and refine this theorizing to specific contexts, histories, and possibilities.
