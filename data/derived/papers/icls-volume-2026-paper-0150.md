# Infrastructuring for Climate Justice in STEM-Rich Making

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study employs critical participatory design-based research to investigate how integrating infrastructuring practices into a community-based STEM-rich maker program supports youth in responding to urgent, felt challenges of climate change through their making, while also promoting sustainable communities. Grounded in the experiences of middle school youth participating in a year-long, community-centered program, our findings highlight how infrastructural resources (material, relational, social /emotional, and intellectual) enabled youth to engage in meaningful, climate-focused making. These resources became a site for incubating ways of knowing and being, as they emerged as connective tissue linking people, practices, and contexts through acts of making for change. They fostered solidarity and shared responsibility by positioning youth as active agents in generating educative, solution-oriented artifacts for climate justice and reorganizing physical spaces using maker materials. We argue that int egrating infrastructural resources into community-based STEM-rich making supports youth-led responses to climate change and advances justice-oriented outcomes.

STEM-Rich Making has sparked interest due to its potential to break down barriers, fostering justice-oriented STEM education for many minoritized youth (Martin, 2015). Making involves designing, tinkering, modeling, solving problems, developing multilitera cies, and producing outcomes. However, little empirical evidence describes how youth are supported in working toward robust STEM-rich making projects, especially in ways that respond to their lived experiences and evolving needs in a rapidly changing world, where the impacts of climate change add another layer of complexity to their lives.

Climate change education frameworks, shaped by technocratic, neoliberal, and settler colonial logics, often perpetuate colonialist and racist discourses. These discourses are embedded in science and engineering education standards and do not account for the multifaceted nature of climate change, and have not succeeded in preparing society to respond to it (Gunckel, 2023). Climate justice education, which addresses the multifaceted nature of climate issues, requires infrastructuring resources and practices t hat enable youth to grasp its complexity while empowering them to take meaningful action (Lee & Grapin, 2022). This study  draws upon critical, participatory design-based research to investigate how integrating infrastructuring practices into a community-based STEM-rich maker program supports youth in responding to urgent, felt challenges of climate change through their making, while also promoting sustainable communities. We refer to infrastructuring as the actionable components that are the building blocks of connecting pathways between community-based making and STEM-rich making programs. We view infrastructuring as youth’s intentions to assemble resources embedded in social activity systems (family, makerspace community, society) in systematic and sustainable ways. We ask: What infrastructural resources are mobilized, and in what ways, position minoritized youth as agents of change in STEM-rich climate making?

## Theoretical framework

Infrastructuring advances understanding of educational innovations by foregrounding the critical design involved in “redesign components, relations, and routines” (Penuel, 2019, p. 659). Karasti and Syrjänen (2004) use infrastructuring to describe the ongoing, dynamic renegotiation and reconstruction of systems, moving beyond seeing infrastructures as static. Justice-oriented infrastructuring faces the challenge that educational research and reform are built on infrastructures historically shaped by White supremacy and heteropatriarchy, perpetuating systemic inequities. Disrupting these systems requires intentional efforts to create new conditions, resources, and practices that enable political and systemic transformation (Bang, 2020). By expanding understandings of infrastructural resources beyond technical components to include new forms of knowledge, and alternative practices, this perspective challenges the authority of those who originally constructed these exclusionary structures of practice and interac tion (Dantec & DiSalvo, 2013). Questions of when, what, and to whom infrastructures are extended shift attention beyond materials, 1232 tools, and ideas toward the histories of practice and lived experiences of those who inhabit, sustain, benefit from, or are harmed by them (Calabrese Barton et al.,2022).

Infrastructuring educational innovation involves creating new relationalities that challenge oppressive systems from within. This requires deep ethical and political commitments, prompting us to interrogate not only what we do, but how we understand ourselves, others, and structures in relation to one another and to context, both present and historical. Understanding these relationships helps answer how and why we can redesign infrastructures to support equitable informal STEM education. Central to this pro cess is the empowerment of agency, the capacity to mobilize and integrate diverse forms of knowledge in pursuit of collaborative problem-solving (Herrenkohl et al., 2020). In our study, we investigate the infrastructural resources and how they can be leveraged to cultivate new relationalities that enable transformative opportunities for youth to engage in STEM-rich making. Drawing upon participatory co-design ethnographic methods, we worked with middle school youth in a community-centered making space program housed in a community center over the course of one year. These were not drop-in programs, but sustained after-school initiatives. The club primarily serves low-income youth, 95% from multigenerational African American communities, alongside smaller percentages of white and Latinx youth. We have partnered with the club for two decades, collaborating on weekly STEM-based programs that are youth-and community-centered, and allowing us to gain legitimacy as stakeholders in the community, taking on additional roles as mentors and participating in club activities, including events and tutoring sessions. During this year, 14 youth, 4 university student mentors, and 3 youth mentors-in-training participated in the program. The mentors-in-training were high school youth from African American communities who had previously participated in the makerspace program during their middle school years and were now returning to support younger participants from their own community. Data were co-generated with the youth, including makerspace artifacts, images captured by youth, mentors-in-training and adult members, interviews and surveys conducted within the community, conversations, and fieldnotes. The analysis followed multiple stages/levels of coding based on grounded theory and constant comparison (Straus & Corbin, 1998). We began with open coding to identify infrastructural resources that support emerging climate justice learning. In the next phase, we employed a relationality lens to explore how these infrastructural resources were leveraged to cultivate relational connections among youth, adults, the broader community toward climate justice. This includes coding of connections to climate engagement/ experiences and the development of youth agency.

Our findings reveal two interconnected ways in which infrastructural resources supported youth climate investigations and STEM-rich making practices. First, youth infrastructured material, intellectual, social, emotional and relational resources as they designed and led climate change investigations and maker solutions. These resources opened up opportunities for storytelling, intergenerational dialogue, and collaborative interviews, allowed youth to process emotions linked to climate events, strengthen relationships, and build a richer, locally grounded understanding of climate mechanisms, impacts, and mitigation strategies.  Second, these resources served as incubators for just ways of knowing and being, acting as connective tissue that linked people, prac tices, and contexts. This connective tissue emerged through youth enacting their agency, leveraging  these resources in acts of making for change. Infrastructural resources to investigate climate change: A diverse range of infrastructural resources supported youth in navigating the urgency of climate change, encompassing material, intellectual, social-emotional, and relational dimensions. Material resources such as temperature maps, graphs, images of visible signs, and air quality tracking tools were used to help youth understand the local climate change impacts (Figure 1). In investigating greenhouse gas emissions, the youth focused on CO 2 as a primary greenhouse gas. They identified energy sources within the club and the surrounding community that contribute to carbon emissions —including electricity use (computers, lights), furniture, food, and human activity. They paid close attention to the different energy sources used in various rooms of the club, with special emphasis on calculating and graphing the makerspace’s carbon footprint. To deepen their investigation, youth used sensors to collect CO₂ data inside the club and around the neighborhood. The y made predictions and explored factors contributing to increased CO₂ levels, modeling urban emissions in their city and in other cities across the country where they have family and friends. Online GIS tools supported their exploration and modeling of urban emissions across these geographic contexts. As youth deepened their understanding of carbon emissions, they explored mitigation strategies, focusing on trees’ role in climate resilience. They used GIS data to assess tree equity scores in their neighborhoods and in those of loved ones nationally, building evidence-based understandings of environmental inequities.

Fostering Youth as Agents of Change in STEM-Rich Making: Within the makerspace, infrastructural resources interacted to create conditions for incubating ways of knowing and being, as these resources emerged as connective tissue linking people, practices, and contexts through acts of making for change that were attuned to solidarity and shared responsibility. Youth leveraged this connective tissue across multiple dimensions, such as between generations, knowledge systems (scientific data with community exper ience), spaces and actions. For example, Ana explained what it meant to her to persist in collective, justice-oriented action by saying: We cando anything for little kids [...] [our makerspace] proves to the outside world that even though we have a small appearance, we cando big things and it teaches us the value of you just keep working and no matter how mistakes you make, you still go because [our makerspace] is here for [you], you guys [mentors] here for all of us.

Her quote highlights how this connective tissue fostered Ana’s agency to contribute through making for younger children (“we cando anything for little kids”), her connections with adult mentors who support her (“you guys here 1234 for all of us”), and the ways these relations enable collective action to “do big things.” The material and social infrastructures create learning opportunities within supportive relationships (“no matter how many mistakes you make”).

Youth act as active agents who facilitate community dialogue and create educational artifacts, bringing together material, social, emotional and relational elements. Here, we see youths’ agency not as individualistic but as a strand of the makerspace’s connective tissue, developed as youth collaborate across generations, blend scientific and community wisdom, and create artifacts for climate justice. For example, youth designed puppet trees using recyclable materials, fabric, and sewing supplies, using the makerspace filled with diverse materials to craft artifacts that conveyed climate change messages in engaging ways. Through these puppets, they created imaginative d ialogues between different entities (tree-tree, tree-human, tree-car) exploring relationality across human and more-than-human relations. These imaginative dialogues made visible the interconnected relationships that often remain invisible in climate disco urse, serving as solution-oriented artifacts for climate justice and making these ideas accessible to diverse audiences (see Figure 2).

Youth also designed succulent hanging planters to improve indoor air quality by filtering pollutants from the air. They reorganized the physical makerspace and utilized maker materials such as wood, wire, soil, and living plants for their designs. Beyond redesigning their physical space, the youth developed creative ways to break boundaries and share ideas with the broader community for climate justice. For example, Kylo designed his planter in the shape of a house, placing a succulent on the roof to symbol ize how homes could integrate more greenery. Ana created a hexagonal planter with a tube for efficient watering (see Figure 3). After presenting her design to her community, Ana shared: “They didn't know what a succulent is. The succulent is a plant that doesn’t really need water that much. Yeah, so it's very efficient, they require less water so it means they can survive and they are very good at absorbing CO₂.” Ana’s explanation to her community members demonstrates how infrastructural resources support developing agency and extend climate justice work beyond the makerspace. Figure 3 Youth STEM-Rich Making Artifacts Our findings demonstrate that integrating infrastructuring practices into community-based STEM-rich maker programs creates powerful pathways for youth to respond to urgent climate challenges while advancing justice-oriented outcomes. The infrastructural resources encompassing material, relational, social/emotional, and intellectual components embedded within social activities enabled youth to engage with the multifaceted nature of climate change as situated within their local contexts. These resources supported youth making in two critical ways. First, they created conditions for incubating onto-epistemologies, ways of knowing and being, while affirming axiologies that reflect just and ethical forms of knowing. Second, they served as connective tissue, bringing youth together with community members and practices in ways that fostered solidarity and shared responsibility for action-taking. By enacting these resources and redistributing power in learning and design, youth were legitimized as agents of change. T hey enacted this agency through collaborative making, dialogue, and artifact creation, ultimately expanding STEM culture to include socially and spatially just futures. This work offers a generative framework for designing inclusive and transformative STEM  learning environments, empowering minoritized youth and cultivating community resilience and thriving.
