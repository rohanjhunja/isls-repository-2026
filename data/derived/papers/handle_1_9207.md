# Empowering Students in a Datafied World: Adult Stakeholders’ Perspectives on Digital Self-Determination in the U.S. K-12 Environment

**Conference:** ISLS 2023

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
During the COVID-19 pandemic, when educational systems underwent a massive shift to online learning, stakeholders had limited time and resources to address the impact of increased datafication on their students. This use case describes adult U.S. education stakeholders’ perceptions of that shift and the path forward. It does so by applying a new, emerging principle, “Digital Self-Determination” (DSD), in which students’ agency and rights are respected throughout data use. We found that infrastructure, capacity, and knowledge barriers have kept schools from safely and effectively using data to improve student learning; that adult stakeholders’ needs and incentives as agents of DSD are, in some cases, poorly aligned with one another; and that while educators do have some concerns about the privacy of student data, they are poorly equipped to prioritize student data privacy.

## Introduction

Before the COVID-19 pandemic forced educational systems in the United State s to engage in a massive shift to online learning, the design and implementation of technologically-supported learning was already a key concern for educators. Amid the crisis, decision-makers had limited time and resources to consider whether or how these changes would facilitate trustworthy and empowering data spaces for young people. Educati onal technology, or edtech, can be a powerful tool to promote self-directed and active learning, critical thinking, and collaboration among students (Gray & Lewis, 2021). Technological tools can also streamline assessment for educators, allowing them more time for their students (McKnight et al., 2016). However, edtech c an also present substantial risks to students’ privacy, autonomy, and human rights. Given that young people have limited agency over the data collected, analyzed, and stored about them (Plunkett et al., 2019; Young & Verhulst, 2020), the stakeholders that represent them need to enable sociotechnical systems that empower childr en to develop into fully actualized people. In this paper, we investigate adult educational stakeholders’ perspectives on three areas of pandemic-era data use: the unfulfilled promise of the use of student data, the misalignment of stakeholders’ incentives with one another, and stakeholders’ data privacy concerns.

The problem of datafication and the principle of Digital Self-Determination The psychological self-determination theory first conceptualized by Deci and Rya n (1985; Ryan & Deci, 2000) describes the factors that motivate learners – in particular, autonomy, competence, and relatedness. When learners’ environments support them to fee l autonomously able, competent, and related or connected to others, they will be most intrinsically motivated to commit themselves to their educati on. With the wealth of education data collected about students –including  formative assessment results, standardized test scores, a ttendance data, behavioral data, developmental or growth reports, and demographic information (Sun, Prz ybylski & Johnson, 2016), as well as information generated by student interactions with technology platf orms, such as real-time learning data, user preferences, and engagement data (Livingstone et al., 2021) –educational systems are wellpositioned to understand what motivates students and to support their autonomy, competence,  and relatedness broadly.

Although conversations about the implications of an increasingly digitized and datafie d society have yet to generate approaches that fully empower data subjects. To this end, a new prin ciple of Digital SelfDetermination (DSD) has emerged. Drawing upon the Kantian conception of self-determina tion, DSD provides a framework for re-imagining the self in digital contexts: how people manag e the digital environments they encounter, how they access and operate personal data, and how they exercis e autonomy over the data-driven decisions that affect their lives (Remolina & Findlay, 2021). DSD is, as Ve rhulst puts it, “the principle of respecting, embedding, and enforcing people's agency, rights, interests, preferences, and expectations throughout the digital data life cycle in a mutually beneficial manner for all parties involved” (Verhulst, 2022, p. 10). The promise of student data is complicated by the diverse nexus of stakeholders, practices, literacies, priorities, and policies that impact the agency that students have over the data that is collected about them, as well CSCL 2023 Proceedings  © ISLS 258 as the efficiency with which that data is used to improve learning outcomes. For instance, the beliefs and motivations that stakeholders have concerning data use –and the school, political, and national systems in which they operate–are highly variable. Students have varying levels of access, control, and understanding of their DSD. The families representing their needs will do so with varying levels of capacit y, understanding, and interest. Likewise, school districts and administrators may make decisions about tec hnology procurement and data use with limited finances and based on varying obligations to the law, as well as distinct societal and cultural pressures. This study seeks to establish a foundational understanding of the infrastructures, attitudes, and visions of adult stakeholders in the US K-12 education system to identify the barriers to and opportunities of institutionalizing student DSD.

## Methods

In the spring of 2022, we conducted six online focus groups with 18 people distributed across four US states and the District of Columbia. These included two edtech providers, four teachers, two s chool administrators, and 10 district administrators. Both edtech providers and most of the school and district a dministrators were former teachers. Those employed by school systems worked for districts ranging from 1,000 to over one million students. The one-hour conversations were guided by a semi-structured interview format that briefly introduced participants to a foundational definition of DSD before exploring a.) their understanding of, concern s for, and hopes for the edtech data ecosystem, b.) how they use edtech data currently in classroom s, schools, and districts, c.) their concerns about data ownership, access, persistence, and use for students, teachers, and districts, and d) their vision for how student data could better serve students, teachers, families, or districts to support student DSD. The focus group method was selected because group interviews tend to reveal participants’  differences of opinion and personal preferences (Cerón & Cerâon, 2006). Additionally, the methodology lends itself to this research because while behaviors such as a decision-making process are inherently unobservable in a research setting, focus groups may prompt participants to more fully articulate their thoughts, as well as allow researchers the opportunity to observe collective sensemaking (Wilkinson, 1998). The research team took notes on each session conducte d and iterative readings of the interview transcripts.

## Findings and Discussion

The unfulfilled promise of student data Particularly since the passing of the No Child Left Behind Act (NCLB, 2002), American educators have become accustomed to using testing data to assess students progress (Datnow & Hubbard, 2015; Mertler, 2011; Young & Kim, 2010). Meanwhile, the explosion of digital learning environments has opened additional a venues for analyzing, modeling, and predicting optimal learning experiences based on ever-more granul ar student data (Wagner & Ice, 2012). Together, these data streams can help teachers to understand students’ progress, craft personalized learning plans, and streamline schools’ administrative processes, am ong other uses (Livingstone et al., 2021). Our participants believed in the promise of edtech and governmental data systems to support student DSD but wished that the promise was more fully realized. Even though students relinquish enormous amounts of data, their schools play victim to both data and information asymmetries; they la ck not only access to data but also the resources, infrastructure, and capacity to turn the data into meaningful information that would safely and effectively support students’ growth as learners. As one administrator described: We collect ridiculous amounts of data through these digital tools and, most of it, without …data experts, goes to waste and it's never seen or used by anyone…all of the diff erent silos for the data …none of it is really connected… I haven't really seen com prehensive solutions that take into account all of those pieces together and produce information for teachers that they can ac t on in near real-time as much as possible.

This leaves the arduous task of cleaning and synthesizing data to teachers, on top of their many other responsibilities. The ultimate promise of individualized and customizable instruction will not be realized if educators cannot easily integrate data with practice. Edtech developers, s chool districts, school administrations, teachers, and policymakers all have a role to play in facilitating efficient data systems, but a barrier exists to address this concern: stakeholders’ competing needs and priorities. The misalignment of stakeholders’ incentives Each type of stakeholder we studied is incentivized differently in the U.S. student data ecosystem, but no single stakeholder is perfectly positioned to support students' authority over their data, their ability to understand it and CSCL 2023 Proceedings  © ISLS 259 use it to suit their needs, or their development of a strong sense of their  own DSD. Administrators and teachers face different constraints and offer different insights, which according to some participants can result in poor communication and the ineffective roll-out of technological systems. These disconnects happen not only between teachers and administrators but also between levels of leadership; for exa mple, on-the-ground leaders like principals may align with their teachers, while senior administrators may take a different view. Moreover, school and district leaders face a complexity of competing goals with limited resource s. Integrating needs assessments into technology procurement protocols at the state, district, and school levels was cit ed as one potential solution to this misalignment, as one participant suggested: People [in authority] just pull stuff out … and they think it's cool and they brin g it to their district, but how much money is actually placed on researching what the needs ar e? I feel like that's one of the steps that needs to happen first.

Needs assessments are also relevant to the design of technology. Edtec h companies, which have the greatest access to student data, have a limited obligation to work in collabora tion with governmental, parental, and student stakeholders. Many of the participants we spoke with envisioned educa tional applications for commercial innovations; for example, one cited the potential for predictive modeli ng to support individualized instruction. Yet they also recognized the limitations of applying commercial models developed without educator input to educational contexts. The top-down structure of product development was a com mon refrain. Educators and providers alike acknowledged a need to shift toward a human-centered des ign approach grounded in the experiences of teachers and schools. Human-centered design is an iterative process that requires a constant cycle of investigation, ideation, and implementation in conjunction with users (Hartung & Rotte nberg, 2019). As technology remains an integral component of education, it remains worthwhile to r eflect on how collaborative and participatory approaches to both technology design and procurement may make educati on systems more resilient to future crises.

Stakeholders’ data privacy concerns were not our own While our participants expressed great concern about the power that edtech com panies have over how student data is used, they were far less focused on student data privacy than we a nticipated. Given that regulatory frameworks are in place to safeguard student privacy, some educators assumed that students were protected as long as the school complied with the law. Others feared what would happen to the m if student data was compromised, given that, though insecure software is ultimately the cause of data breaches, administrator error can play a role as well. Many of our participants did not want protections to be so broad as to become barriers to innovation, like this former district administrator from Texas: We over-index on the privacy question and don't use data for good in schools as a re sult. And the law is there. FERPA [the 1974 Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act] exists… A lot of good data initiatives have been derailed because of sort of an irrational concer n over, like, what might happen to this third-grade math score.

On the whole, participants tended to see student data privacy as one of count less responsibilities assigned to educator, one often navigated with limited guidance from school administrations. When gui dance was given to them, it was informed not by a values-based accounting of privacy as a critical issue but was i nstead more likely to be a response to system failures, including Zoom-bombing and breaches of student data. Thus, an amalgam of factors, including the limited wcapacity of teachers, an inadequate sense of agency on the part of educators, a lack of clear consensus on how best to protect student privacy, and a constant ly shifting technology landscape all contribute to an education system in which educators are poorly equipped to prioritize student data privacy.

## Conclusions

Though the pandemic has disrupted K-12 education, we have an opportunity  to reflect upon the rapid technological shifts it necessitated and whether they support student DSD. When th rust intonew edtech ecosystems, teachers adapted as they could but needed additional support with decis ion-making and implementation. Going forward, teachers need clear, consistent communication about which tools and approaches they should adopt and what level of control and support they will have. Policy-makers and districts should consider usability when selecting technologies and accompany any new product with adequate training. Both processes would be more effective with educators’ input. Districts might also consider choosing edtech products that are beneficial to broader educational systems. They might select technologies that are  interoperable and have interoperable data to allow for secure data storage. Policymakers may ince ntivize vendors to support CSCL 2023 Proceedings  © ISLS 260 interoperability and analytics that support best teaching practices and student portfol io and skill documentation. Districts or states might create a dedicated role for data analytics or work with external research institutions, either of which could work with teachers on best practices for the data use. Finally, an effective strategy might involve working with edtech companies themselves, perhaps with nonprofits or foundations supporting the collaboration and incentivizing the companies’ involvement.

Though our participants did not believe that the promise of student data had been fully  realized, they remained passionate about its potential. In the words of a technology professional from a Washington DC private school who wished  for “a massive coalition of people, both students and adults”: If we could all team up and own this data, to help to shape the laws, help to make things public[ly regulated]. Internet service perhaps should be treated as a utility, regulated as such. The only thing that can stop [it] is really teaming up together to take some of that power back.
