# Insights About Helpful Covid-19 Learning Resources From Caregivers of Elementary School Children

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Media resources are a central component of children’s learning ecologies in the U.S. However, much of the coverage of the Covid-19 pandemic came through channels for adult audiences, and the “infodemic” added to the difficulty of interpreting information from media sources. This paper analyzes learning resources collected in a diary study conducted with 109 families with elementary school aged children at the beginning of the Covid-19 pandemic. Findings show that the resources caregivers found useful in communicating with their children about the pandemic are intertwined with their life experiences and interpretation of their children’s information needs.

## Introduction

When COVID first started we were listening to the news on the television nonstop. So my girls kept seeing the news and what was going on. It was challenging for them because I would give them an answer to a question and literally rules and guidelines changed within an hour on certain days. Layla, mother of 5-year-old Maia and 8-year-old Rachel When Covid-19 arrived and sparked a wave of lockdowns, parents and other caregivers (e.g., grandparents, adult siblings) had to decide what they were going to tel l their children, how they were going to respond to  their questions about the changes in their routines, and how much to shelter their children. Media resources  such as national and local news outlets, government press conferences, and social media were flooded with coverage of the pandemic. In addition, an “infodemic” of conspi racy theories, misinformation, and fake news about the coronavirus and Covid-19 erroded public trust in he alth officials and spread fear and feelings of hope lessness around the world (The Lancet, 2020). Access to reli able health information and the ability to apply th at information is a critical component of health equity (Gonzalez et al., 2020). Since families are mediators of both children’s health learning (e.g., Squires et al., 2012) and interpretation of media (e.g., Takeuchi & Stevens, 2011), unpacking how families were using media resources t o learn about Covid-19 is critical to understanding  how families adapted to the pandemic.

Using digital media with young children is a conten tious topic, with debates about the benefits and dangers of screen time cast in moralizing language.  For years experts in child health, such as the Ame rican Pediatrics Association, have encouraged caregivers to limit their children’s interactions with digital  media (Lemish, 2021). Yet years of research on children and media demonstrate that well-designed media can promote learning (e.g., Sesame Street) and social interacti on (e.g., cooperative multiplayer online games and virtual communities), and that joint engagement with media is a productive form of collaborative learning that can arise between caregivers and children (Takeuchi & Stevens, 2011). To understand how caregivers approached learning a bout the pandemic with their families, this paper examines how 109 families of elementary school children were exploring pandemic related topics and concepts through the use of social and media learning resources. In this context, “media resources” include both information media designed for adults as well as educational pr ogramming for children. Given the dynamic nature of  the infosphere and the limitations of our static data collection method, this paper does not aim to analyze the content of the media learning resources, but rather documen t caregiver perspectives and reflections on using m edia to learn about the pandemic with their families.

## Methods

The analysis presented in this paper considers the research question: What resources were caregivers u sing to learn about the pandemic, and which ones were most helpful for talking to their children about the pandemic? The data come from a larger diary study conducted with 109 families from 28 states across the U.S. over th e course of two weeks in May 2020 using dscout, a mobile qualitative research platform (Barron et al, 2021; Pozos et al., 2022). The participants were recruited from dscout’ s panel. The caregivers were mostly women (67%) wit h ICLS2022 Proceedings 1545 © ISLS children in public elementary schools (84%). Racially, 55% self-identified as white, 16% as Black, and  15% as Latinx, 9% as Asian, and 4% as Middle Eastern or North African. This paper considers data from one portion of the f ive-part study where participants shared how they were learning about the pandemic with their children. Participants provided a snapshot of their Covid-19 learning ecologies (e.g., Barron, 2006) by responding to checklists of pandemic learning topics and sources, and providing examples of learning resources they found most help ful. The checklists were analyzed with statistics b y family demographics. Given the lack of a unified response to Covid-19 and the speed at which the news cycles were occurring at the time of the study, we felt it was important to differentiate between the resources caregivers were generally accessing compared to the ones that they found to be most helpful for talking to their children about the pandemic to give us insights that could help with the design of future crisis learning materials. The examples of learning resources were cataloged, specifying the name of the source, type of source (e.g., YouTube video, CDC website, PDF, family member), the caregiver’s descr iption of the resource, and why (if explicitly stat ed) they chose to use that resource in particular. Types of resources were standardized and one researcher independently applied the codes to the log of resources in order to understand the frequency with which the types of resources appeared in the sample. In total, 272 examples of learning resources were given by the caregivers.

## Findings

While all caregivers indicated using at least one resource, there was a lot of variation in response to the prompts with a few caregivers explaining how they try to av oid Covid-19 being the focus of their day, others d escribing how their children bring sources to them to talk about, and yet others who wanted to help their children understand what was happening, but were not sure how to go about doing so. Topics and sources families used to learn about Covid-19 Overall, the topics and resources that caregivers r eported using with their children to learn about Co vid-19 revolved around accessing information that they nee ded to navigate the “new normal” of life in the pandemic. The topics that the caregivers most frequently repo rted their families learning about were how to prot ect themselves (91.7%), public health orders (88.1%), what Covid-19 is (73.4%)  and how Covid-19 spreads (71.6%). About half of the caregivers indicated that they we re learning about the symptoms of Covid-19 (55%), t he economic impacts of the pandemic (43.1%), and the g lobal outbreak (40%). No meaningful differences wer e found by family demographics.

The most frequent types of sources caregivers used to learn about the pandemic were family discussions (85.3%), television (68.8%), social media (57.8%), and the CDC website (38.5%). Approximately one-thir d of caregivers were consulting local government websites (35.8%), online searches (32.1%), friends (32.1%), family members (31.2%), or  teachers (29.4%). Again, no meaningful differences were found by family demographics. Media resources caregivers found most helpful Overall, caregivers found resources that helped them keep up to date and find explanations for questions that they and their children had to be the most helpful (see Table 1). TV news providers, websites with articles about Covid19, the CDC communications channels, popular cartoons, and family/friends were the resources most frequently listed by caregivers to meet this need. The types a nd topics of the questions the caregivers noted the ir children asking and the conversations they sparked are the s ubject of a separate paper (Pozos, 2022). However f or the purposes of understanding this analysis, it is help ful to know that 75.2% of caregivers reported their  children asking them about daily pandemic disruptions, such as why schools were closed, when they would be able to visit family and friends, how long the pandemic would last, and why the families made changes to their routines. Many caregivers were challenged to provide satisfying answers to these questions. ## Discussion & conclusion

Analysis of the learning resources that caregivers reported using to learn about the pandemic with their families showed caregivers drawing on a range of national media, institutional, educational, and social resources to make sense of the rapidly changing landscape of the early pandemic. The resources that ended up being the most helpful for them to use when discussing the pandemic with t heir children varied by the age of the children and  the caregivers’ perceptions of what information was necessary and appropriate to share with the children. Unpacking how caregivers interpret their children’s information  needs and what role media play in them also helps us think about what caregivers really mean when they say the y are taking a specific approach, such as shelterin g their children. There is also more to learn about how caregivers manage their children’s access to information at home, beyond just screen time, building on this and brokering research by Gonzalez et al. (2020). How and why do they mediate content? How are their perspectives manifested in their goals for their children’s engagement with media? ICLS2022 Proceedings 1547 © ISLS In what ways might they be learning through negotiating access to information with their children? Finding and interpreting information about the Covid-19 pandemic became a particularly fraught topic when this study was conducted in May 2020. Since then, more educational programming about the pandemic for children and adults has been released, while partisandivides about everything from the nature of the virus to the vaccines have grown simultaneously. The Covid-19 le arning ecology continues to be complex and families ’ decisions about how to engage with learning resources models for their children knowledge systems and values that are embedded in community practice. Thus, the findings from this study contribute to an understanding of how families assemble learning resources in times o f crisis  to fulfill the information needs of both adults and children.
