# Coordinating Multiple Equations that Model Complex Systems

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Complex systems demand multiple representations to characterize changes to their physical and quantitative properties. We sought to determine how undergraduate students coordinated multiple equations that modeled a system in two distinct situations. Results revealed that learning experiences with a multi-representational computational environment prompted students to shift from describing calculations and quantitative dependencies to coordinating the conceptual meanings between equations. We describe how this proof of existence informs theory and the design of future learning technologies.

## Introduction

Representing complex systems demands multiple representations to capture their multiple levels of organization (Wilensky & Resnick, 1999; Wilensky & Reisman, 2006). Students, however, often fail to coordinate conceptual meaning across multiple representations (Ainsworth, 2006). Despite this learning challenge, Knowledge-in-Pieces (KiP) theories suggest that students construct conceptual schemata (e.g., symbolic forms) derived from formal representations such as equations (Sherin, 2001). We build upon these past and recent KiP approaches that have illustrated how students blend conceptual and forma l reasoning when learning with equations (Kuo et al., 2013). We add to th e KiP tradition by determining how students coordinate the conceptual meanings of multiple equations that model two distinct but related situations that occur in biological cells. NetLogo offers a  useful approach to support students in  understanding how multiple equations model quantitative changes in complex biological systems. Such agent-based environments ground  complex mathematics by visualizing for learners the dynamic changes that manifest in a complex system’s physical and numerical quantities (Sengupta & Wilensky, 2009; Wilkerson-Jerde & Wilkensky, 2015). We, therefore, sought to assess how students’ understanding of a complex biological system —the Resting Membrane Potential — changed after learning with a NetLogo model that depicted two distinct biological and quantitative situations: (1) when a cell is idealized as perfectly permeable to one ion and (2) when a cell is idealized as perfectly impermeable to the same ion due to state changes in cell membrane structure. We therefore ask: How do students coordinate the conceptual meanings of multiple equations that model changes in a complex system?

## Participants, research design and analysis

31 participants were recruited from a biology course (200-level on Homeostasis) at a Midwestern R1 university. The students were recruited via mass email and in-class announcements and received $20 for participation.

The study employed a pretest /posttest design. First, students completed  a written assessment with two equations printed and a scientific calculator provided. Next, students completed a think-aloud protocol that guided their interpretations of the NetLogo simulation that modeled (1) a cell completely impermeable to all ions and then (2) a cell completely permeable to only one ionic species. Students then completed the written assessment once more. W e employed a constant-comparative approach to analyze students’ written responses to an assessment item that tasks students to coordinate the meaning of two equations under ideal conditions (Lira, 2020): the ideal equation —Nernst equation (permeability to only one ion) and the  non-ideal equation —Goldman equation (permeability to multiple ions). Using a constant comparative method, we identified three interpretation levels: Calculation Description,  Mathematical Dependency, and Equation Coordination; due to the pre -/postdesign, each student had two responses recorded for both pre-and post-tests.

## Results

Examining students’ written responses before and after the think-aloud protocol revealed a notable shift in their conceptual understanding of multiple equations  (s ee Table 1). At pre-test, students predominantly presented Calculation Description —this code  describes quantitative operations or variables (e.g., “[…] the Goldman equation because it can calculate the membrane potential”), and Mathematical Dependency—this code identifies quantitative dependencies within the equation (e.g., “[...] the Goldman equation because it takes into account the permeability coefficient”). At the post-test, however, a shift was evidenced by Equation Coordination—this code characterizes mathematical equivalence under ideal conditions (e.g., “The Nernst equation would be the same as Goldman equation to calculate the membrane potentia l in this case”) with frequencies increasing to 14 (2 at the pre-test). Students’ shift suggests that they were  becoming sensitive to the key variable permeability (P) that coordinated the conceptual relations between the two equations under ideal or non-ideal conditions. ## Discussion and implications

We propose th e theoretical possibility that the conceptual schemata that students construct fro m equations can coordinate across multiple equations with distinct symbolic template s (e.g., the Nernst & Goldman equations). The shift that some students made illustrates how conceptual knowledge of multiple equations reorganizes around computational experiences that visualize quantitative and biological changes. Students were not instructed with either equation during the think-aloud protocol. Nevertheless, some students were able to coordinate  the key variable. This finding builds upon recent KiP (Kuo et al., 2013) and computational investigations (Sengupta & Wilensky, 2009) by extending the theoretical foundation of symbolic forms that Sherin (2001) first established. It provides proof of existence that biology students do coordinate multiple distinct equations meaningfully. These results, however, must be tempered regarding instructional implications. Few students shifted to coordinating multiple equations. We reported previously that only half o f these students demonstrated shifts in their understanding of the physical mechanisms  during their learning experience (Lira, 2020). Therefore, these modest results suggest that students’ prior knowledge mediates the visual and mathematical elements they notice. Longitudinal interventions could support the design of effective learning technologies and interventions  that coordinate biology equations. These technologies and interventions need to draw students ’ attention to the physical meanings of the quantitative variables found across multiple equations  by highlighting how changes to individual entities connect to change patterns represented in equations (Wilkerson-Jerde & Wilenksy, 2015).
