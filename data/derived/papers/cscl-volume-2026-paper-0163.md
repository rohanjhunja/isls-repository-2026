# Meet Bip: Understanding Student–Peer Agent Interaction in an Embodied Exploratory Learning Environment

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Peer agents have been shown to enhance learning in K–12 settings, yet the design of LLM-powered embodied peer agents for exploratory learning remains underexplored. This study examines how middle-school students interact with Bip, an embodied conversationa l agent in a Minecraft-based AI literacy game. Inspired by the Intelligent Novice paradigm, Bip observes players, provides hints, and scaffolds reflection during an AI literacy activity. Findings from nine students highlight the importance of spatial awareness, responsiveness, and bounded understanding in supporting exploratory learning. We discuss design implications for embodied agents that foster scientific inquiry.

Peer learning, where learners collaborate and reason as equals, is a powerful driver of curiosity, reflection, and scientific inquiry (Cassell, 2022; Paranjape, Ge, et al., 2018). Peer agents extend this idea into interactive systems by modeling social and cognitive aspects of collaboration, ranging from chat-based agents to embodied conversational agents (ECAs) in virtual environments. At the same time, exploratory learning environments (ELEs) support inquiry through experimentation, hypothesis testing, and observation (Alawajee & Delafield-Butt, 2021; Y. Zhou & Paquette, 2024). Recent advances in large language models (LLMs) further enable agents to sustain context-rich dialogue and scaffold reflection, making conversational peer agents increasingly effect ive in supporting learning (Earle-Randell et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2024). However, despite progress in both embodied agents and LLM-based conversational systems, little work has integrated the two to create LLM-powered embodied peer agents that reason and interact through both dialogue and spatial action in ELEs. In this work, we build on prior research in embodied AI literacy (Farhadi et al., 2026; Zhou et al., 2024) to study how middle-school students interact with such an agent in a Minecraft-based environment. Inspired by the Intelligent Novice framework (Mathan & Koedinger, 2005), our agent is designed to observe, question, and respond to learners’ actions rather than provide direct instruction. Through qualitative analysis of student –agent interaction s, we examine how these agents can support exploration, reflection, and inquiry. Meet Bip’s system design Bip is an ECA implemented as a bee within a Minecraft-based AI literacy game, where players learn about filter bubbles in recommendation systems. The agent processes player actions, spoken input, and in-game events to provide context-sensitive hints and scaffold reflection. Bip exhibits both verbal and nonverbal cues, including gaze and proximity; while facial expressions are absent, dialogue is displayed above the agent in synchrony with audio (Figure 1). Guided by the Intelligent Novice framework, Bip is designed to demonstrate partial understanding, encouraging peer-like interaction. Bip integrates GPT-4o for dialogue and decision-making, Fabric for Minecraft modding, Whisper for speech recognition, and Typecast for voice synthesis. Figure 1 Screenshots of Bip Introducing Itself, and Reacting to the Learner Pollinating a Flower 809 Participants consisted of nine middle-school students (grades 6–9; seven male, two female), predominantly from African American backgrounds. These learners possessed high Minecraft experience and moderate AI familiarity, with limited prior programming knowledge. During a summer-camp study, students worked in pairs to complete the 30– 40 minute Meet Bip activity. All sessions were recorded (capturing screen, audio, and video) and subsequently transcribed using the Whisper model for qualitative analysis. Conse nt and assent were obtained in accordance with ethical guidelines. We conducted an inductive thematic analysis  (Blandford et al., 2016; Braun & Clarke, 2013; Zhou et al., 2025), iteratively coding salient interactions (e.g., dialogue, movement, engagement with game elements) and clustering them into higher-level categories capturing patterns in Bip’s behavior and students’ responses. Two researchers independently developed the codebook from session recordings and refined codes through cross-session comparison to ensure consistency. Yellow blocks represent verbal behaviors, blue blocks represent nonverbal behaviors, green blocks indicate interactions with the agent, and red blocks denote errors or boundary-violating behaviors. To analyze student –agent interaction, we coded Bip’s and players’ behaviors across verbal and nonverbal dimensions (Figure 2). Figure 2 summarizes the coded behavioral categories for both Bip and players; by examining how these behaviors co-occurred across interactions, we identified recurring interaction patterns that informed the synthesis of three design dimensions: spatial understanding, responsiveness, and bounded knowledge. Bip generally maintained coherent, contextually relevant dialogue, while these  three factors proved most influential in shaping interaction quality.

When Bip grounded its utterances in the spatial context, such as naming nearby flowers or moving toward relevant elements, players displayed higher engagement and moments of shared attention. Conversely, missing contextual details prompted players to use “ repair talk” to re-establish common ground, repositioning Bip as an NPC requiring updates rather than a peer participant. Responsiveness was also critical; while smooth exchanges supported reasoning, latency from transcription or LLM processing led to frus tration and disengagement. Interestingly, Bip’s intentional limitations served a constructive function. Small factual or interpretive errors encouraged students to articulate reasoning and engage in productive friction, such as argumentative exchanges that reinforced understanding. This suggests that bounded imperfection can be pedagogically valuable, as it positions the agent as an "intelligent novice" rather than an infallible guide. However, deep integration in an exploratory learning environment (ELE) requires both spatially coherent referencing and low-latency responsiveness. When these slipped, players defaulted to NPC expectations rather than treating Bip as a partner in exploration.

These findings highlight that agents must be perceptually aware and capable of joint attention to establish presence. Responsiveness sustains interaction flow, while limited knowledge prompts learners to question and reason. By acting as an intelligent novice, Bip redirected attention to overlooked elements and posed interpretive questions, scaffolding reflection while maintaining exploration. Future development should focus on richer multimodal tracking and adaptive response timing to investigate how embodied agents candynamically balance curiosity and autonomy. While the findings are based on a small, exploratory sample (n = 9) and should be interpreted as indicative rather than generalizable, even with a constrained prototype, this study demonstrates that responsiveness, spatial grounding, and bounded knowledge collectively shape whether learners treat an agent as a co-participant or a simple NPC guide. As conversational models evolve, the challenge is to design inquisitive partners that prompt discovery through interaction.
