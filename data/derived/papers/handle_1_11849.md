# Education for Sustainable Development: A Cross-Community Knowledge Building Approach

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study investigates Cross-community Knowledge Building (CKBS) as an approach to Education for Sustainable Development (ESD) that empowers diverse communities of learners to take collective responsibility for advancing sustainable development goals (SDGs) through Knowledge Building. A Grade 1 class in Toronto, Canada and a Grade 9 class in Tarragona, Spain engaged in collaboration on sustainability challenges over four months. Using natural language processing, semantic network analysis, and temporal analysis of student contributions on Knowledge Forum, we examined cross-age and cross-community collaboration patterns that emerged during one phase of their sustainability inquiry. We found that Grade 1 students introduced foundational concepts grounded in their lived experiences while Grade 9 students expanded and deepened these ideas with more sophisticated concepts. However, the collaboration also surfaced challenges related to asymmetric participation and technological constraints for the younger learners. This study demonstrates the potential of CKBS as a model for Sustainability Education.

## Introduction

Climate change and sustainability are among the most pressing challenges of our time. Education for Sustainable Development (ESD) is seen as a critical lever towar ds advancing the Sustainable Development Goals (SDG s), which aims to “empower learners with knowledge, skills, values and  attitudes to take informed decisions and make responsible actions for environmental integrity, economic viability and a just society”(UNESCO, 2020). ESD can help people and societies adapt to the ever yday realities of climate change and nurture mindse ts of environmental stewardship and social equity (Ehlers et al., 2022). However, there are several challenges in ESD. First, there tends to be an instrumental focus on c hanging individual behaviors or transmitting predef ined knowledge, rather than fostering transformative lea rning experiences  (Jickling & Wals, 2008). Secondly, as sustainability issues manifest differently across g eographic locations and co mmunities, there is a difficulty in balancing the specificities of local contexts with larger global perspectives. Third, the enormity of climate crisis can provoke eco-anxiety among students, leading to feelings of helplessness rather than empowerment (Hickman et al., 2021). Thus, there is a need for ESD approaches that go beyond knowledge acquisition and behavior changes towards more transformative, collaborative, emancip atory learning and to give learners agency to engag e with sustainability issues and to make space for plurali stic solutions (Wals, 2011). This pilot study introduces Crosscommunity Knowledge Building for Sustainability (CKBS) and examines its potential through connecting Grade 1 students in Canada and Grade 9 students in Spain in sustained collabor ative knowledge creation across geographic, cultural, and age boundaries to advance knowledge on sustainability.

## Theoretical framework

Cross-community Knowledge Building for Sustainability (CKBS) draws its theoretical foundati on from Scardamalia and Bereiter ’s (2006) work on Knowledge Building and Wals ’ (2007) work on Social Learning. Sustainability is considered a wicked problem as it  is inherently complex, interconnected, value-ridden, and uncertain (Craps, 2020). Sustainability challenges are not merely technical or scientific; they are in tricately intertwined with values, world views, justice, and hu man relationships with each other and the natural world (Misiaszek, 2017). Wals and colleagues (2009) maint ained that transferring expert knowledge to learner s and linear problem-solving is inadequate in tackling the wicked proble m of sustainability challenges. Wals and vander Leij  (2007, p. 18) proposed Social learning as an approach to sustainability education, which they referred to as “learning that takes place when divergent interests,  norms, values, and constructions of reality meet i n an environment that is conducive to learning.” Social learning is rooted in dynamic and complex physical and social reality, and it is a seen as powerful tool for addressing complex sustainability challenges because it allows for the integration of diverse knowledge, perspectives, and values. However, facilitating effective social learning presents challenges-it requires navigating community knowledge, potentially con flicting viewpoints, and the inherent complexity of sustainability issues while embracing the unpredictable and emergent nature of the learning process (Wals & vander Leij, 2007). To address these challenges, Scardamalia and Bereiter’s (2006) work on Knowledge Building (KB) provides the theory, practice, and technology to foster the conditions as well as the socio-technical infrastructure needed to navigate the complexity of  diverse perspectives. In alignment with Wals ’ argument to move beyond knowledge transfer from expert to novic e, KB is a collaborative process of idea improvemen t and knowledge creation, instead of only acquiring exist ing knowledge (Bereiter & Scardamalia, 2014).  KB f osters the production and continual improvement of ideas, positioning individuals and communities to accomplish more than they can on their own (Scardamalia & Bereiter,  2021).  In a KB community, learners take collective responsibility for the advancement of community knowledge and contribute diverse ideas and perspectives, which fosters ‘rise above’ moments, when students synthesize multiple viewpoints into a more powerful idea with higher explanatory power (thus rising above previous ideas), instead of simply choosing between competing ide as or settling for simplistic solutions (Scardamalia, 2002). Such continuous idea improvement and rise-above moments are crucial for addressing complex sustainability challenges. Drawing from principles of Knowledge Building and Social Learning, we propose Cross-community Knowledge Building for Sustainability (CK BS) as a model for Education for Sustainable Development (ESD). CKBS is characterized by the five following features listed below. Figure 1 Framework of Cross-community Knowledge Building for Sustainability Epistemic agency: Learners directly engage in the knowledge creation process, where they set their own goals and agendas, monitor their progress, and dete rmine the next steps to pursue sustainability chall enges that are most relevant and meaningful to them (Scardamalia, 2002). Collaborative knowledge creation: Communities work together not only to exchange insights but also to build upon each other’s contributions to form an ecosystem of local and global knowledge. Sustained collaboration:  Unlike one-off exchanges, CKBS promotes continuous collaboration o ver extended periods. Through sustained collaboration, participants develop a shared language, trust, and mutual understanding, which are crucial for knowledge advancement  (Wals et al., 2009). Local and  Global interdependence: CKBS leverages the unique strengths and expertise d istributed across communities to create a shared space where students learn from each other to accom plish more than they can within one community (Scardamalia, 2002). This mirrors the coordinated global responses required t o mitigate climate change. This process fosters knowledge that is simultaneously localized and globalized, enabling learners to see their local actions as part of a broader, interconnected effort. Transformative skills: CKBS fosters transformative skills aimed at addressing the root causes of climate change and changing the unjust social and economic structures and extractive relationship with nature (LotzSisitka et al., 2015). Transformative skills includ e environmental stewardship, empathy, collaboration, system thinking, solidarity, and intercultural competence (Kwauk & Casey, 2022). In addition to gaining knowl edge, CKBS aims to transform the underlying beliefs, valu es, and behaviors that perpetuate unjust and unsust ainable systems.

The goal of this study is to shed light on the processes of collaboration between two communities across geographic boundaries and age to advance knowledge on sustainability. This paper, in particular, focuses on process of cross-community collaboration by answering the research q uestion -- what patterns of collaboration emerge between the two communities that engage in cross-community knowledge building for sustainability?

## Method

This study used a mixed-methods approach to investigate the process and outcome of CKBS between two communities. This paper focuses on quantitative analysis of Knowledge Forum contributi ons and the process of collaboration between the two communities in one of the phases of their inquiry.

## Participants and context

Participants in this study included 22 Grade 1 students (aged 6-7) and their teacher in Toronto, Canada, and 28 Grade 9 students (aged 14-15) and their teacher  in Tarragona, Spain. The collaboration lasted 4  months from February to May 2024. Students used Knowledge Forum (KF) as the socio-technological infrastructure for CKBS, which makes the knowledge building processes visible and tracible. Unlike traditional linear discussion forums, KF’s flexible canvas design enables complex idea organization through multimodal contributions and cross-linked views (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2021). The Grade 1 class h ad a designated time for Knowledge Building, lasting 45 minutes, either once or twice a week. The Grade 9 students also had a class to engage in Knowledge Building once a week. The two communities interacted both asynchronously on KF and engaged in synchronous exchange twice via video conferences (zoom), each lasting 45-50 minutes. Figure 2 Multimodal Interaction on KF between a Grade 1 Student and a Grade 9 Student The collaboration organically unfolded into four phases:

1) Community building:  Students began with a community-building phase focused on establishing

personal connections. Grade 1 students initiated th e relationship by creating drawings and sharing per sonal introductions, to which the Grade 9 students respon ded with their introductions using multimedia on KF  (see Figure 2).

2) Needs and wants of a community: After the community-building phase, the Grade 1 class started the

semester with one big question: “What is a community?” This was followed by a  sorting and reflection on what a community needs versus what a community wants. As the Grade 1 students could not yet use KF independently, the Grade 1 teacher constructed a physical KF using a cardboard and sticky notes to provide an em bodied and tactile experience of KB for the younger learners.

3) Construction of shared community ‘Tarronto’: Following a school trip to Little Canada (a museum of

miniature figures in Toronto), the Grade 1 students wanted to build a mini model of their community. At the same time, the Grade 9 students decided to build a digital community in Minecraft. The Grade 9 students asked Grade 1 students what they should name the community, and  together they decided on the name “Tarronto,” which is a combination of Toronto and Tarragona.

4) Investigation on waste management:  Upon building the shared community Tarronto, the Grade 1

teacher prompted the students to investigate deeper into one aspect of the community: waste management, which is closely related to Grade 1 students ’ lived experiences. Grade 1 students engaged in an activity of sorting out different types of waste, and the researcher helped them to post their ideas on KF in a view titled “Grade 1’s Trash Detectives”. What was posted included their ideas about which waste should go where, as well as their lingering questions, The Grade 9 students then built upon these ideas and questions. A total of 632 notes were generated between the two communities in a 4-month period. While the Grade 9 students contributed approximately 3 times more notes compared to Grade 1 class, Grade 1 students maintained active participation through a mediated approach that accounted for their emerging literacy skills, with their ideas and visual content being transcribed and uploaded t o KF by their teacher and the primary researcher. This mediated participation strategy ensured that the yo unger students ’ voices and  perspectives were represented, despite their emerging digital and literacy skills. In the last phase of their collaboration on waste m anagement, two communities contributed a total of 181 notes on this view of KF (see Figure 3). This paper focuses on the analysis of student collaboration in this particular inquiry phase. Data sources and analysis We analyzed 181 student notes on the ‘Grade 1’s Trash Detectives’ KF view (19 from Grade 1, 162 from Grade 9), using natural language processing, semantic network analy sis, and temporal analysis to understand their emerging collaborative patterns.

Figure 3 Student Notes in the View on Waste Management in KF Stage 1: Concept extraction, validation, and consolidation We first extracted key science concepts from studen t notes on KF  using a Large Language Model, GPT-4o, through a structured prompt: “As an expert in Knowledge Building pedagogy, analyze the following student note from {grade_text} about waste management:  Provide a detailed analysis in JSON format with the  following structure: “Extract a list of key scientific concepts mentioned  in the note. Each concept should be clear and understandable on its own.” The extraction process returned 641 concepts. For validation purposes, we randomly selected 10% of the dataset and compared AI-extracted concepts with identification performed by an expert who has 15+ years of experience in Knowledge Building. Firstly, we calculated lexical matching metrics including Precision = 0.2656 (the proportion of AI-extracted concepts that also appeared in human coding), Recall=0.2701 (the proportion of human-identified concepts successfully captured by AI), and F1 Score= 0.2658 (harmonic mean of precision and recall). Secondly, we calculated semantic similarity using a Sentence-BERT model (all-MiniLML6-v2) to measure conceptual similarity between AI and human coding beyond exact word matching. The average semantic similarity of 0.7991 showed close conceptual alignment between human and AI concept extraction despite the moderate lexical matching scores. The results suggest that  AI and human experts frequently capture similar concepts but with different wording.

Similarly, to address the natural variation in how students express similar ideas with different wording, we used Sentence-BERT model (all-MiniLM-L6-v2) to create vector representation of each identified concept and to compute similarity scores between concept pa irs using cosine similarity.  Through iterative testing of similarity thresholds from 0.5 to 0.9, we selected 0.7 as the optimal threshold, balancing concept dis tinctiveness with the need to reduce redundant concepts. Concepts that exceeded a similarity threshold of 0. 7 were consolidated, resulting in 328 consolidated concepts.

Stage 2: Semantic network analysis We constructed a semantic network where nodes repre sent science concepts, edges represent co-occurrence of concepts within student notes, and weights reflect the strength of concept relations based on the frequency of cooccurrence. We calculated network m etrics, including density (the ratio of actual conn ections to all possible connections), clustering coefficient (the tendency of nodes to cluster together), and node centrality measures (i.e., degree centrality, betweenness centrality) to ident ify important concepts and their roles in the network. We also detected communities using the Louvain method to identify closely related concepts. Stage 3 temporal analysis For each community, we tracked the cumulative emerg ence of scientific concepts on the view in KF over time. To track concept flow between communities, we ident ified the first appearance of each concept on KF and its subsequent appearances and development i n both communities. We visualized the temporal patt erns using line plot.

## Results

The semantic network analysis of KF notes  between Grade 1 and Grade 9 students revealed their patterns of collaboration around waste management and sustainability. The analysis identified a conceptual space comprising 326 unique concepts  after consolidation  with 1713 connections. While the overall network sh owed moderate connectivity with a density of 0.032, it demonstrat ed a high average clustering coefficient of 0.787. This pattern suggests that students developed their understandin g in coherent thematic clusters, where related conc epts were tightly interconnected. Figure 4 illustrates the semantic  network of the two communities.  The visualization uses a color-coding scheme where blue nodes represent concepts first introduced by Grade 1 students and orange nodes show concepts introduced by Grade 9 students, with node sizes indicating how frequently each concept a ppears in KF. At the center of the network, we see prominent co ncepts like “recycling,” “ environmental impact, ” “resource conservation,” and “pollution reduction” appearing as larger nodes with many connections (shown as gray lines) to other concepts, indicating their role as central themes in the discussions. The network demonstrates a clear dominance of Grade 9 students’ notes who introduced more specialized concepts, while the fewer but wellconnected blue nodes represent basic concepts introduced by Grade 1 students. The community detection uncovered 17 distinct conceptual communities, each representing a cluster of closely related ideas. The three largest communitie s illustrated different patterns of cross-community KB. The first community, containing 75 concepts, centered around recycling and environmental impact, though it showed a relatively low cross-grade integration ratio of 5.33%. The second largest community, with 57 concepts, focused on composting and biodegrad able materials and demonstrated the highest cross-grade integration at 19.30%. A third significant community of 39 concepts emphasiz ed environmental protection and contamination, thou gh it showed limited cross-grade integration at 2.56%. This suggests that conc epts within composting and biodegradable materials transferred more readily between communities. ## Discussion

Coordinating cross-age, cross-culture collaboration among communities across geog raphic boundaries presents significant challenges, such as communication, coordination, and finding common ground for knowledge building (Huang, 2023). This pilot study revealed several pr omises and challenges when implementing Cross-community Knowledge Building for Sustainability (CKBS) betwee n developmentally and geographically distant communities.

Promises of Cross-community knowledge building Despite significant age differences, both communiti es made meaningful contributions to advancing colle ctive knowledge about sustainability through complementar y roles. In the phase of inquiry on waste managemen t, Grade 1 students served as catalysts and introduced foundational concepts grounded in their immediate lived experiences and authentic questions about waste man agement. These concepts, particularly “recycling” and “composting,” became central bridges in the knowledge network as evidenced by their hi gh betweenness centrality scores. Grade 9 students, in turn, built  upon these foundational concepts and introduced more sophisticated science concepts and explanations. Like Yuan (2022), we found that cross-community interaction enriched learning in individual classrooms. Whereas Yuan’s study showed relatively symmetric knowledge exchange between same-grade classrooms; our cross-age collaboration revealed more distinctive roles each community played.

Challenges and opportunities While the collaboration showed promise, several cha llenges emerged. A key challenge was the asymmetric participation between grades, reflected in the disp arity of concept contributions (Grade 9 n=297, Grad e 1 n=29). While existing knowledge building platforms offer v oice-to-text and drawing capabilities, their practical application in busy elementary classrooms was limited. Current voice-to-text tools struggle to accurately capture the dynamic, overlapping conversations typical in Grade 1 knowledge building circles, and the process to enter a note into KF often disrupts the natural flow of cla ssroom discourse. Future implementations of CKBS require technological innovations to better support young l earners’ participation, including  ambient discourse capture systems that can unobtrusively record and distingui sh multiple voices during knowledge building circle s, integrated physical-digital spaces that can automatically digitize and integrate physical artifacts into the knowledge building space in real-time. These enhancements would allow preserving the natural dynamics of early primary classrooms while making students ’ contributions more accessible for cross-community knowledge building. The goal is to capture young learners ’ insights and questions as they emerg e organically. Another opportunity for further development is a knowledge transformation tool that creates simplified visual representations of the knowledge space that make co mplex concept relationships accessible to young lea rners. These could include automated interactive concept maps with adjustable complexity  levels or visual storytelling tools that trace idea development (Zhang et al., 2018).

## Limitations

This work aimed to explore the feasibility and potential of cross-community knowledge building for sustainability. One key limitation of the current study is its focu s on group-level dynamics and patterns of collaboration. While this macro-level analysis yielded valuable insights into how k nowledge building unfolds across communities, it did not fully capture the nuances of individual con tributions and growth. In line with the core princi ples of knowledge building, which emphasize both collective  and individual advancement future  studies should employ a multi-level analysis to investigate how individual studen ts within each community participate in and benefit from the cross-community collaboration. This will provide a more c omprehensive understanding of the mechanisms and impact of CKBS at both the individual and collective levels.  Furthermore, it is important to reemphasize that this paper focused on one of the four phases of cross-community collaboration, and future publications will encompass all phases as well as incorporate the qualitative data collected through interviews and focus groups to shed light both on the process and outcomes of CKBS in this study.

## Conclusion

This study provided a  proof of concept for Cross-Community Knowledge Building for Sustainability, demonstrating how geographically and developmentall y distant communities can engage in collaborative Knowledge Building around sustainability challenges. While asymmetric participation and technological constraints posed challenges, the communities’ complementary contributions suggest the approach’s potential for fostering local-global knowledge advancement. Future work will focu s on developing socio-technical infrastructure to better support symmetric knowledge advancement, analyzing collaboration patterns across multiple KF views and modalities (capturing the oral discussion by th e younger children), using multi-level analysis to understand individual and collective dy namics, and analyzing student focus group s and teacher interviews to assess the impact of CKBS on perceived changes in student skills, attitudes, and mindsets.
