# Rethinking Cognitive Apprenticeship Through Studio Pedagogy

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This poster critically examines cognitive apprenticeship theory through systematic analysis of studio pedagogy in practice. Both pedagogies are rooted in traditional apprenticeship practices. Drawing on a semester-long observational study of a ceramics design studio in leading arts academy in Israel, we analyze interactional episodes to identify fundamental differences between the cognitive apprenticeship theoretical prescriptions and actual studio practices. We found that studio pedagogy manages to achieve many stated goals of cognitive apprenticeship, within educational and academic settings, while successfully maintaining the challenges that many cognitive apprenticeship implementation efforts face. There are several fundamental misalignments between cognitive apprenticeship theory and studio practices. First, studios shifts the traditional master-apprentice power dynamic, with instructors supporting students' visions rather than imposing their own. Second, modeling in studios occurs reactively and fragmentally within students' work process, contrary to the theory's prescribed proactive, comprehensive, performative demonstrations. Third, studio instructional support intensifies with project complexity, challenging the theory's fading support model. While studio pedagogy and cognitive apprenticeship in k12 settings are inherently different, learning from successful studio settings can enrich our understanding of apprenticeship-based learning in educational and academic settings.

## Introduction

When Collins and colleagues developed their influen tial cognitive apprenticeship theory in 1987, they drew primarily from studies of vocational apprenticeship s, particularly Lave's (1982) study of Liberian tai lors. Their goal was to translate organic learning practices from traditional apprenticeship settings into structured pedagogical approaches for K-12 classrooms  (Brown et al., 1995). Meanwhile, a successful form of apprenticeship-based pedagogy had already been operating in academic settings for centuries: the art and design studio. Studio pedagogy—defined as "a model of teaching and learning specific to the education of professional practitioners... characterized by project-based work, one-to-one teaching and the public display of and feedback on student work" (Sawyer, 2018). This approach typically includes collaborative learning through structured peer critique sessions, alongside the instructor guidance, and it has maintained remarkable stability across cultures and centuries (Crowther, 2013; Mewburn, 2012).

Studio pedagogy and cognitive apprenticeship theory  share historical roots and pedagogical goals, yet their implementations and theoretical frameworks ha ve evolved along different paths. Rather than posit ioning these as competing approaches, we use cognitive apprenticeship as an analytical lens to examine studio practices, identifying areas where theoretical prescriptions diverge from successful studio implementation. This comparative analysis offers insights into why cognitive apprent iceship implementation s have faced persistent challenges despite promising theoretical foundations. To clarify the theoretical differences observed, we distinguish between three related but distinct forms of apprenticeship: Traditional Vocational Apprenticeship emerged organically as a method of skill transmission within communities. Apprentices work in service of a master's business, with skill development occurring as a byproduct of  authentic tasks with real economic consequences (Lave & Wenger, 1991; Harper, 1992). Cognitive Apprenticeship Theory  (Collins et al., 1987, 2018) attempted to adapt app renticeship principles for conventional classroom settings, focusing on making  expert thinking visible through:  Modeling: Expert demonstration of processes. Coaching: Guided practice with feedback. Scaffolding: Support that helps students achieve goals, and Fading: Gradual removal of support as competence increase s. This framework maintained a hierarchical power dynamic (teacher as master, stud ent as apprentice) and prescribe d specific instructional sequences.

Studio Pedagogy evolved from European art academies in the 19th cen tury, adapting apprenticeship methods to serve pedagogical rather than production goals (Crowther, 2013). While maintaining hands-on learning and expert guidance, studio pedagogy  shifts traditional power dynamics: instead of apprentices serving the master's vision, instructors dedicate their expertise to realizing students' creative goals (Schön, 1987; Austerlitz et al., 2002).

## Method

Our analysis draws from an in-field observational study conducted over one semester in a ceramics design studio at a leading arts and design academy. The course  was for freshman students, with little experience, and focused on industrial ceramic production methods, requiring  students to create a tea-drinking set with identical cups and plates. While the industrial focus of this particular course may have influenced specific pedagogical patterns, the fundamental dynamics observed remain relevant to un derstanding apprenticeship-based learning more broadly. All participants signed an agreement to participate in the study as was required by the ethical committee of the Hebrew University of Jerusalem. We systematically documented and analyzed distinct instructional episodes across ten studio sessions, each lasting 4-5 hours, video and audio recorded. The physical environment featured individual workstations, shared equipment zones, and flexible areas for breaks, creating a space that supported extended periods of independent work punctuated by instructor interactions.

## Findings

In most episodes, problems originated from students rather than the instructor. The instructor consistently applied her expertise to serve students' creative  vision or goals.  This contradicts the traditional master-apprentice relationship described in cognitive apprenticeship theory. Collins et al. (1987) described classroom modeling as holistic, proactive demonstrations that precede student work, our observations revealed markedly di fferent patterns: most modeling episodes showed only 1-2 steps rather than complete processes  and emerged in response to specific student struggles. In many of the episodes, multiple solutions were explored rather than predetermined demonstrations and the instructor displayed genuine uncertainty.

Rather than gradually fading support as cognitive a pprenticeship theory suggests, instructor support intensified across all measured dimensions (physica l, cognitive, emotional, planning) as the semester progressed and project complexity increased. This suggests tha t student capability and need for support are not s imply inversely related.

## Discussion

In traditional apprenticeship settings, power dynam ics are legitimized by market realities—apprentices serve the master's production needs because these reflect gen uine client demands. Studio pedagogy maintains auth enticity through student ownership of creative vision, even as it shifts the traditional hierarchy. Students engaged deeply with technical challenges precisely because these emerged from their own design goals rather than predetermined exercises. Cognitive apprenticeship theory, by contrast, attem pts to maintain traditional power dynamics within classroom tasks, creating what Lave (1982) might te rm a "legitimacy crisis" —students are asked to subordinate themselves to expert authority without the authentic context that makes such subordination meaningful. When apprenticeship learning succeeds in educationa l contexts, it does so not by simulating traditiona l power dynamics but by creating authentic relationsh ips where expertise flows in response to genuine pr oblems. This insight calls into question not just implement ation strategies but the theoretical foundations of  how we conceptualize expertise transmission in educational settings. This analysis is not intended to suggest that cognitive apprenticeship theory is fundamentally flawed or in appropriate for studio contexts. Rather, by examini ng points of divergence between theory and successful practic e in settings with shared pedagogical  goals, we can identify potential refinements to apprenticeship-based theories that might enhance their efficacy ac ross educational contexts. The persistence and success of studio ped agogy across centuries suggests it has evolved solu tions to challenges that formal apprenticeship theories continue to wrestle with.
