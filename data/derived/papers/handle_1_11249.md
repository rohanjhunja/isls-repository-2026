# Feedback Sources in Argumentative Writing: AI Feedback or Peer Feedback?

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study compared AI feedback with peer feedback, examining both the quantity and qualitative aspects of negative feedback. The results indicated that ChatGPT (the AI tool used in the study) generated a consistent volume of feedback across various dimensions, contrasting with the variable amounts observed in peer feedback. Quantitatively, ChatGPT produced more negative feedback than peers. Qualitatively, it provided more mitigating praises and general suggestions for issues related to both essay content and form, as well as more specific solutions for form-focused issues. However, peer feedback excelled in offering more detailed explanations for content-related issues. The implications of the research findings are discussed.

## Introduction

Feedback is an essential component of effective writing instruction. Immediate and high-quality feedback enables students to assess their own writing skills and ide ntify avenues for improvement. However, providing s uch feedback costs considerable time and efforts, espec ially when dealing with large numbers of students a cross multiple classes. To address these challenges, peer feedback and Automated Writing Evaluation (AWE) feedback have been increasingly used in writing instruction.  In peer feedback, students assume dual roles as as sessors and assessees. This process involves reading and evalua ting peers’ writings, articulating critiques, sugge sting revisions, and refining their understanding of peer review criteria (Wu & Schunn, 2021). Although there is doubt about the ability of students as novice writers to offer high-quality feedback, researchers have identified strategies to enhance feedback quality. These include structur ed criteria, well-organized peer review training, anonymous peer feedback, and multi-peer feedback.

AWE systems offer the advantage of providing immediate feedback. Studies have shown that AWE can improve students’ engagement and writing performance. However, traditional AWE systems predominantly focus on surface-level problems such as  spelling, grammar, vocabulary, often neglecting ess ay content issues like evidence, explanation. With the advent of generative AI tools, there is potential for immediate and individualized feedback that encompasses both the form and content of essays. Students can “communicate” with the tools, and request feedback or responses to specific questions. Althou gh the effectiveness of AI feedback is debate d, with some extolling its virtues and others pointing out its weaknesses, its potential in writing instructio n remains significant. In this study, we examine the efficacy of AI feedback compared to peer feedback by analyzing the quantity and the features of negative feedback provided by each source. Our findings will shed light on how the two kinds of feedback can be used to improve writing instruction outcomes.

## Literature review

Comparing AI feedback and human feedback Recent studies have explored the effectiveness of AI (i.e., ChatGPT) feedback and human feedback. Meyer et al.

(2024) conducted an experimental study investigatin g the effects of AI feedback on secondary students’  text

revisions. Their findings indicated that students r eceiving AI feedback in the experimental group outp erformed their peers in the control group, who didn’t receiv e feedback. This suggests that AI feedback can play  a critical role in enhancing students’ revision skills, particularly when human feedback is not available. Steiss et al. (2024)  compared the quality of human feedback and AI feedb ack provided to secondary school students. Human evaluators included experienced teachers, rese archers, and graduate students who received a 3-hour training on how to give feedback. The study analyzed each feedback comment for its adherence to criteria (i.e., was criterion-based), clarity of suggestions, accuracy, relevance (i.e., was attainable and essential), and tone (i.e., used a supportive tone). The findings revealed that AI feedback was consistently more reliable in being criteria-based, signifying the potential of AI tools to maintain feedback quality. Banihashem et al. (2024) focused on the quality of peer feedback and AI  (i.e., ChatGPT) feedback on undergraduates’ essays. Students provided peer feedback using the peer feedback prompts given by the instructor, which were also used to generate AI feedback. They found that AI feedback tended to be more gener al and descriptive, while peer feedback was more specific.

These studies highlight the different strengths and  weaknesses of AI feedback and human feedback. While AI  feedback shows potential in terms of consistency and adherence to criteria, human feedback, particularly feedback from trained assessors, excels in identifying problems and providing specific feedback. The present study Utilizing forty-six argumentative essays receiving AI feedback and peer feedback, this study aims to compare these two kinds of feedback in terms of the quantit y of negative feedback, and the four features inclu ded in the negative feedback. This study addressed the following two research questions: 1) What are the differences in the quantity of feedback provided by students and AI in argumentative writing?  2) How do AI and peer feedback differ in addressing content-focused and form-focused aspects of argumentative essays?

## Study context

Forty-six essays written by forty-six undergraduate students in China (female: 78%, male: 22%, mean age = 20 years) enrolled in an English writing course were used as the writing corpus for peer feedback and AI feedback in the study. Peer feedback was used as a regular teaching strategy in this writing course. During the 16-week semester, each student was required to write six essays and engage in peer review via an online peer feedback system (Wu & Schunn, 2023).  Students submitted their first drafts to the system, which then randomly assigned the drafts to other students for peer review. Peer reviewers provided written comments on the drafts anonymously. Then authors revised their first drafts based on the feedback and submitted their revised drafts for teacher evaluation. To help students provide helpful peer feedback, the instructor conducted a 90-minute training on peer review strategies at the beginning of the semester, during  which how to conduct online peer review based on the criteria was demonstrated and what made peer feedback helpful was discussed. AI feedback and peer feedback Forty-six students provided feedback on the essays based on the criteria given by the instructor. The criteria from Paulus (1999) were used in this study, including si x dimensions: organization, development, cohesion, structure, vocabulary, and mechanics. The students were requir ed to provide 2-3 comments on each dimension. ChatGPT 3.5 was employed to generate AI feedback for the same essays, aligning with prompt design principles from the AI feedback literature in writing (e.g., Steiss et al., 2024) and the peer review criteria. The iterative process of feedback generation was continued until the quality of AI feedback was satisfied.  ChatGPT 3.5 was prompted with four guidelines to ensure the quality of AI feedback: 1) AI roleplayed as an English writing instructor to provide feedback.  2) AI should identify and explain the problems, and provide cons tructive suggestions in AI feedback. Two to three comments should be given on each dimension.  3) The essay prompt and the peer review criteria were available to AI. 4) All the feedback should be presented in tabular format. Feedback coding Four research assistants were trained to code peer feedback and AI feedback. First,  all peer feedback and AI feedback were broken down into individual comments, each addressing only one issue instead of multiple issues. Comments were then classified as summary, purely po sitive, or negative comments. Purely positive comme nts were used to give praise, while summary comments pr ovided an overall view of the draft. A negative com ment pointed out a specific issue in the draft. The summary comments and the purely positive comments were removed from the further analysis because the present study focused on negative comments. Then, the negative comments were analyzed based on a coding scheme proposed by Wu and Schunn (2020a, 2020b). This coding scheme includes four ke y features of feedback: mitigating praise (i.e., pr aises included in negative feedback to soften criticism),  explanations (i.e., explain what a problem is or w hy it is a problem), general suggestions (i.e., general advice  about how to improve), and specific solutions (i.e., specific advice about how to improve).

Statistical analyses To explore the differences between AI feedback and pee r feedback, we calculated the  amount of negative feedback and analyzed the four features included in AI feedback and peer feedback. Then, SPSS version 29.0 was used to conduct paired sample t-tests to determine if AI feedback was different from peer feedback.

## Results

Paired sample t-tests were performed to compare differences in the quantity and types of feedback provided by ChatGPT and peers. In terms of feedback on both ess ay content and form, AI feedback displayed a much h igher quantity [t (45) = 13.95, p <.001, Cohen’s d = 4.32] (AI feedback: Mean = 17.89, SD = 0.74; peer feedback: Mean = 9.00, SD = 4.15). Because prior research has observed different results varied by feedback on essay content and form, we examined the differences between AI feedba ck and peer feedback separately within the two aspe cts: essay content and form.

For content-related feedback, ChatGPT provided more feedback [t (45) = 6.09, p <.001, Cohen’s d = 3.20] (see Table 1). AI feedback featured a greater quantity of mitigating praise [t (45) = 2.84, p <.01, Cohen’s d = 2.75] and general suggestion [t (45) = 13.67, p <.001, Cohen’s d = 2.50] types. However, peers provided more explanatory feedback [t (45) = -4.59, p <.001, Cohen’s d = 3.15], perhaps due to their contextual knowledge and cognitive skills. There were no significant differences on specific solutions provided by ChatGPT and peers. ## Discussion

The findings revealed that ChatGPT identified more problems than peers, contrasting wi th the results of Banihashem et al. (2024), who reported opposite fin dings. Unlike peer feedback (Mean = 9.00, SD = 4.15) that varied significantly across essays, the quantity of  AI feedback (Mean = 17.89, SD = 0.74) remained consistent regardless of the quality of the essays. This consi stency is likely due to the AI’s programmed respons e to the prompts, which ensures a fixed number of feedback comments per essay. In contrast, the number of peer feedback was influenced by multiple factors, such as the quality of the essay, the reviewer’s writing proficiency, with better essays often receiving more positive feedback.

In terms of content-focused feedback, AI feedback included more mitigat ing praises and general suggestions than peers, aligning with findings of Lu et al. (2024), who found AI feedback included more general suggestions. However, Lu et al. (2024) also found that AI feedback included fewer praises. This is different from our finding that AI feedback contained more praises, which is supported by Wu et al. (2024) who found that ChatGPT generally generated praises and then pointed out problems. AI feedback and peer feedback were not different significantly in term of specific solutions. Both of them did not include many specific solutions. This findi ng is consistent with Su et al. (2023), who reporte d that AI feedback lacked clear and specific suggestions. Regarding explanations included in the feedback, peers provided more explanations than ChatGPT. That may be attributed to  ChatGPT’s limited understanding of  essays and merely guessing of the meanings.

Regarding form-focused feedback, AI  feedback featured a greater quantity of negative feedback, mitigating praise, explanation, general suggestion,  and specific solution, demonstrating its capacity to offer different types of feedback on language form. This finding suggests that AI can play a vital role in a ddressing form-focused aspects of writing, supported by prior research where positive effects of automated writing feedback and AI feedback on language accuracy of writing have been recognized.

## Conclusion

This study compared AI feedback and peer feedback in ter ms of the quantity of negative feedback and four features included in the feedback. Forty-six argumentative essays received both peer feedbac k and AI feedback. We found that ChatGPT provided a similar amount of negative feedback per essay, evenly distributed across six dimensions, while the amount of peer feedback varie d from essay to essay. In terms of the quantity of negative feedback, ChatGPT provided more than peers. Regarding the four feedba ck features, ChatGPT included more mitigating praises and general suggestions in feedb ack concerning both content and form. AI  feedback also contained more specific solutions for form-focused issues. However, peers offered more explana tions on essay content than ChatGPT. AI feedback and peer feedback didn’t differ in terms of specific solutions on essay content. In addition, ChatGPT appears to be better at providing form-focused feedback than peers.

## Implications for practice

The research findings provide implications for inst ructors in disigning and utilizing AI feedback and peer feedback. Firstly, training should be conducted to help students make full use of AI feedback and peer  feedback, and improve students’ feedback literacy (Zhang et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2024). For peer feedback, instrctors can use examples of peer feedback to demonstrate what m akes feedback helpful or unhelpful. For AI feedback, instructors can work with students to develop effective prompts or compare different prompts created by students. Developing AI literacy is crucial for instructors and students to use AI tools approp riately. To cultivate AI literacy, knowing AI’s capabilities and weaknesses, and having realistic expectations of AI tools are imperative. Secondly, AI feedback can complement peer feedback in the context of argumentative writing. Peer feedback included more detailed explanations, sugge sting higher quality of content-focused feedback. AI feedback, on the other hand, can pinpoint more form-related problems and offer specific suggestions for revisions.

Using both kinds of feedback can result in immediate, individualized and diverse feedback. Thirdly, both instructors and students should align the use of AI tools with their teaching goals and learning objectives. AI tools are used to support l earning by provid ing immediate feedback, facilitating interaction, rather than replacing traditional learning activities.

## Limitations

There are several limitations to be considered. First, the present study focused on the amount of feedback without considering feedback accuracy. Prior research has f ound AI tools often generate inaccurate feedback, w hen the problems are related to contextual understanding and logical reasoning. Future research can compare the accuracy of the two kinds of feedback, thereby developing a deeper understanding of the strengths and weaknesse s of AI feedback and peer feedback.

Second, additional information about the essays cou ld be included to explore factors that may influenc e the amount or features of feedback. For example, Wu and Schunn (2022) found that students’ writing proficiency at different grain sizes was associated with their ability to identify problems and provide useful feedback. Future research could investigate how students with differ ent levels of writing proficiency perform when usin g AI feedback (e.g., eliciting feedback) and peer feedback (e.g., identifying problems). Furthermore, the findings can only be generalized t o contexts using ChatGPT 3.5 and online peer feedback systems. Feedback outcomes (e.g., feedback  quantity, feedback features) are likely to vary ac ross different AI tools and peer feedback activities. Fu ture research can compare the feedback generated by  different AI tools.
