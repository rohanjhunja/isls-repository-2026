# A Collaborative Learning Framework for Post-IVR Environments

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper examines the integration of Computer-Supported Collaborative Learning (CSCL) and Knowledge Building Theory (KBT) in post-Immersive Virtual Reality (IVR) contexts to propose a conceptual framework for post-IVR collaborative learning. While IVR offers rich interaction data and collaborative opportunities, current post-IVR activities often replicate traditional teacher-led methods, failing to utilize these affordances for deeper knowledge creation and collaborative learning. We reviewed 18 papers focusing on post-IVR processes and supports. Findings highlight underutilized opportunities to integrate during-IVR data—such as behavioral analytics and interaction patterns—into post-IVR tasks to enhance problem-solving, reflection, and collaborative engagement. The proposed framework emphasizes scaffolds, tools, and feedback to bridge IVR experiences with meaningful post-activity learning and future work in this area.

## Introduction

Computer-Supported Collaborative Learning (CSCL), grounded in foundati onal theories such as social constructivism (Vygotsky, 1978) and distributed cognition (Hutchins, 1995), emphasizes the use of technology to foster interaction, dialogue, and shared cognitive processes (Silseth et al., 2024; Arnseth & Ludvigse n, 2006; Dillenbourg, 1999). Extending CSCL principles, Know ledge Building Theory (KBT) (Scardamalia & Bereiter,

2006) highlights epistemic agency and collective id ea refinement as essential for mean ingful learning. While

CSCL has been applied to online and hybrid learning environments, its integration into Immersive Virtual Reality (IVR) contexts remains underexplored, especially fo r language learning and acquisition. For this paper, IVR is defined as a computer-generated simulation that provides users with a sen se of presence and interactivity within a three-dimensional, multisensory virtual environment  using a head mounted display  (Slater & Sanchez-Vives, 2016). IVR’s affordances, such as contextual immersion and multisensory interaction, offer unique opportunities to deepen engagement and experiential learning. Rec ent innovations to VR technologies have expanded th e potential for students to collaborate across platfo rms in real time, whereas past IVR environments wer e largely uncollaborative (Silseth et al., 2024; Baker et al., 2021). Despite VR advances, research has yet to fully examine how these affordances intersect with CSCL and KBT to enhance post-IVR peer-to-peer collaboration, particularly in supporting scaffolding, knowledge building, and assessment (Dalgarno & Lee, 2010; Lindgren & Johnso nGlenberg, 2013).  This paper addresses these gaps by proposing a conc eptual framework for post-IVR collaborative learning, grounded in systematic literature and prior published IVR frameworks (Hatch & Michaelis, 2024; Silseth et al., 2024). This work provides actionable broader insights for designing collaborative post-IVRbased learning environments as platforms that can be deployed in classrooms in addition to other areas for further study in collaborative learning using IVR.

Post-IVR collaboration process and supports Post-IVR activities can be crucial for consolidating any learning explored or obtained during IVR environment exploration and facilitating knowledge building. Engaging learn ers in reflective discussions and collaborative tasks after IVR experiences enables them to articul ate insights, question assumptions, and co-construct understanding, thereby deepening comprehension and promoting the transfer of knowledge to real-world contexts (Lindgren & Johnson-Glenberg, 2013).  Traditional post-IVR activities often emphasize qualitative feedback through large group discussions, focusing on affective and motivational aspects of the experience. While valuable, this approach may not fully capture the depth of students' learning, or the specific knowledge acquired during IVR sessions. Despite the potential of during-IVR experiences to support collaborative learning, current literature lacks comprehensive reviews on post-IVR collaborative learning and frameworks that guide  effective peer-to-peer collaboration that harnesses the potential of during-IVR data and tools to extend learning and skills acquired. Post-IVR Collaborative Processes can be significantly enhanced by leveraging the ric h data collected during the learner's IVR experience. These data affordances in clude behavioral analytics, interaction patterns, p erformance metrics, and feedback logs, which can inform and de epen post-IVR collaborative processes. For example, playback data, like recorded interactions and decision pathways, supports evidence-based discussions, enhancing critical thinking and collaborative knowledge const ruction (Janssen & Bodemer, 2013). Interaction logs  and scenario outcomes facili tate collabor ative problem-solving by enabling peers to analyze challenges and  co-construct solutions grounded in shared experiences (Stegmann et al., 2012). Activities such as creatin g and exploring are enriched by 3D models and environment al data captured during IVR,  fostering collaborative prototyping (Makransky & Petersen, 2021). Training activities benefit from performance analytics and automated feedback, identifying skill gaps and improving retention (Toth & Kay, 2019). Post-IVR Collaborative Supports  bridge IVR experiences with meaningful knowledge co nstruction, helping learners synthesize, reflect, and deepen th eir understanding. Scaffolds—encompassing cognitive, social, technological, feedback, and emotional supports —use interaction patterns, proble m-solving behaviors, and physiological data to guide reflections and foster self-regulation (Kirschner et al., 2009; Marzano, 2007; Vygotsky, 1978; Bloom, 1956). Tools and artifacts, informed by usage logs and analytics, fac ilitate shared understanding and iterative learning improvements (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2006). Feedback supports le verage task metrics and behavioral data to provide tailore d guidance, extending learning through collaborativ e prompts, discussion boards, and concept mapping (Janssen & B odemer, 2013; Baker et al., 2021; Lindgren et al., 2013). Evaluation and assessment integrate metrics like gr oup interaction quality and role distribution to re fine collaboration (Dillenbourg, 1999). Engagement and motivation, supported by emotional state and immersion data, sustain collaboration through reflective meaning-making (Baker et al., 2021; Makransky & Petersen, 2 021; Marzano, 2007). These supports ensure seamless post-IVR learning.

## Method

Utilizing the established Post-IVR Collaborative Learning Framework, we conducted a systematic literature review of studies on the use of post-IVR collaborative learning and activities to identi fy; (RQ1) what post-IVR activity modalities and types of data are utilized, (RQ2) what are the collaborative processes used in studies on collaborative learning in post-immersive virtual reality, and (RQ3) how are collaborative processes supported in post-IVR activities?

We conducted the review processes using PRISMA (Pre ferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses) guidelines (Page et al., 2020). A literature search was performed using the IEEE Xp lore, ISLS/CSCL, ERIC, ACM Digital Library, SpringerLink,  and Science Direct databases. The search query contained the following inclusion criteria: 1) coll aboration, 2) immersive VR, 3) post-IVR, and lear ning or education, and was limited to studies published bet ween 2010 and 2024 in English to capture recent tre nds and innovations in immersive virtual reality learning research. We identified 3,621 publications using the search terms in September 2024 and then screened them by browsing the title, abstract, and text body as necessary. From here we excluded publications that did not use head-mounted VR, or where post-VR activities and reflections were not utilized with data recorded. We examined the remain ing 85 study publications using the same inclusion criteria by reading the full texts. The remaining 18 publications were then coded using the Post-IVR Collaborative Framework to examine and identify the collaborative processes and supports.

## Discussion

The systematic literature review reveals a signific ant underutilization of post-IVR activities as opportunities for computer-supported collaborative learning (CSCL). Many studies in the review focused on replicating traditional instructional methods, relying heavily on teacher-or instructor-led reflections. This approach often failed to leverage the unique affordances of data collected d uring IVR experiences, which could be instrumental in enhancing knowledge creation, collaborative building, a nd extended learning through post-IVR activities. While the immersive nature of VR provides rich interactio n data, such as decision paths, collaboration dynam ics, and behavioral patterns, these insights were rarely use d to inform or drive data-driven, learner-centered post-IVR activities. This underscores the need for a robust post-IVR framework and evaluation models to guide the design of future IVR systems and post-IVR platforms which then inform best practices for teachers and instructors. This study highlights key implications for the research and design of collaborative learning in post-IVR and immersive technologies, identifying significant  gaps in leveraging digital tools and assets from I VR experiences to enhance knowledge creation and collaboration. Addressing these gaps could guide the development of adaptive platforms that integrate these tools in to post-IVR workflows, fostering deeper learning. Limitatio ns of this research include the lack of analysis on th eoretical foundations, a matrix of post-IVR performance outcomes, and a comparative evaluation of study efficacy. Additionally, space constraints prevented the inclusion of a full literature list, which is available upon request. The insights of this review offer a critical opportunity for advanced research into adaptive post-IVR platforms that harness artificial intelligence,  learning analytics,  and network systems toolsets. From a learning science and CSCL perspective, the se platforms could dynamically personalize support, scaffold collaborative efforts, and extend the learning experience by connecting insights from IVR data to meaningful post-activity engagements. Addressi ng this gap will require interdisciplinary collaboration to reimagine the potential of post-IVR activities as transformative, technology-driven extensions of the learning process.
