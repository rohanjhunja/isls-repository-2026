# Do Gamers Learn Better From Games? – Insights From Hierarchical Linear Modelling

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study examines how learner characteristics, including gaming experience, prior knowledge, and gender, influence learning gains in game-based learning. Using hierarchical linear modeling, the study found that gaming experience was the strongest predictor of learning outcomes, though marginally significant, while prior knowledge showed the greatest per-unit increase. Gender did not significantly affect outcomes, contradicting some previous findings. These results highlight the nuanced relationship between individual factors and game-based learning, emphasizing the importance of game type and instructional context.

## Introduction

Game-based learning (GBL) has been acclaimed as an engaging instructional tool, while not achieving widespread support among students and educators. This hesitati on often arises from several factors, including a l ack of personal interest in gaming, a preference for tradi tional learning methods, and the perceived complexity of game mechanics. Many students and educators may not identify themselves as gamers, leading to resistance rooted in unfamiliarity and discomfort with the gaming proces s. This raises an important question: Is GBL only e ffective for those naturally inclined toward gaming, or does this reluctance reflect a broader stigmatization of game-based learning?

Several researchers attempted to answer the questio n, exploring the relationship between the gaming experience and the effectiveness of game-based learning. Tahir (2021) indicated that previous gamification experience moderated the effects of badges in gamified intelligent tutoring systems in computer science. In Yang and Chen's (2020)  mixed-method study, elementary learners with more gaming experience had better game achievement and socialization than those with less,  whoneeded more in-game guidance. From the interview analysis, they discovered that students with less g aming experience paid more attention to the learnin g content. Others explored the relationship between personal t raits and the learning gains from the GBL environment. Through a quasi-experimental study on international college student s, Lukosch et al. (2017) found that gender and cultural differences can affect GBL performance, with female students excelling in planning tasks. Although existing literature collectively points to  the learning gains from GBL being affected by gami ng experience or other personal traits, a comprehensive effort to explore the relation ship among learner characteristics, including prior knowledge and  gender, with learning gains from GBL has yet to be made. Thus, this paper aims to examine how learner characteristics, specifically g aming experience, prior knowledge, and gender, influence learning gains from the GBL context.

## Method

The study included 243 students (113 female, 124 male, 6 non-binary) from 18 classes in 8th and 9th grades from a K-12 school in Florida. They were not randomly assigned but played Physics Playground in their usual classes. Physics Playground is a 2D digital game for learning Newtonian physics concepts, including the laws of force, motion, linear momentum, energy, and torque (Shute et al., 2019) for five days. At all levels, the objective is to direct a green ball to hit a red balloon. Players achieve th is by drawing various simple machines, such as ramp s or levers, to move the ball toward the targeted balloon. The g ame's problems range from easy to difficult, with difficulty determined by factors like the ball and balloon's positions. Rahimi et al. (2021) have shown that playing this game supports students' understanding of physics principles and concepts. The data demonstrates a nested structure, with level 1 units being individual students and level 2 units being classes. Level 1 variables include age, gender, race, gaming experience, prior knowled ge of physics, and the learning gains from playing Physics Playground, whi ch is measured as a difference between the pre-and postknowledge test on physics concepts, such as the ene rgy transfer and the properties of torque. The thre e variables of the question, namely, gender, gaming experience, and pr ior knowledge in physics, were grand-mean centered and added to the Level 1 model, with estimating fixed effects at Level 2. Gaming experience was measured as an interval variable, with 0 (Never), 1 (Once a month or less), 2 (Once a week), 3 (3-4 times a week), and 4 (Every day). Prior knowledge in physics was a boolean vari able, with 0 (No) and 1 (Yes). ## Results

The fully unconditional model showed a significant difference in learning outcomes in different classes (p <.001). The intra-class correlation was 0.19, which means that 19% of the total variance in learning outcomes was attributable to between-class difference. With gender, gaming experience, and prior knowled ge added as grand-mean centered level-1 predictors, the model showed that the fixed effec t of gaming experience with robust standard errors was on the borderline of significance (γ=0.361, p=.052), while prior knowledge (γ=0.641, p=.282) and gender (γ=0.029, p=.955)  were not significant  predictors on average. The results indicate that gaming experience was a potential predictor of learning outcomes.  When gaming experience increases by a unit, the predicted learning outcome scores increase.361 on average, holding constant prior knowledge and gender.

## Discussion

The findings of this study highlight several key fa ctors that impact  students' learning outcomes in game-based learning environments. With the intra-class correlation of 0.19 identified from the fully  unconditional model, classroom dynamics or instructional differences may  play a meaningful role in how students engage with  gamebased learning. HLM was chosen due to its ability to account for the ne sted data structure, where students were clustered within classrooms. Assumptions of HLM, in cluding normality of residuals, homoscedasticity, a nd independence of errors, were chec ked before analysis. Model diagnostics indicated th e assumptions were reasonably met, justifying its use.

Gaming experience emerged as a potentially meaningf ul predictor, although its effect was marginally significant. Students with more gaming experience m ay be better positioned to navigate and benefit fro m gamebased learning. While prior knowledge was expec ted to play a crucial role, its effect was not stat istically significant. This outcome may be attributed to the binary operationalization of prior knowledge, which simplified variance. The decision to categorize physics prior knowledge as a Boolean variable stemmed from the survey data structure, which only allowed for a yes/no distinct ion. Although this approach sacrificed some variati on, it was necessary due to dataset constraints.

Interaction effects were not explored due to concerns about statistical power and sample size. Given that the study already examined three key individual pre dictors, introducing interaction terms would have i ncreased model complexity and potentially led to  overfitting. Future research with larger sample siz es could further investigate whether interactions between various variables influence learning outcomes. Gender did not significantly predict learning outco mes. This finding contrasts with research suggestin g gender differences affect performance in game-based learning, particularly in planning tasks (e.g., Lukosch et al., 2017). The absence of a significant gender effect suggests that such differences may be context-dependent and influenced by the nature of the game itself.

These findings contribute to the discussion about h ow individual characteristics influence learning in game-based environments. Future research should consider  alternative methods for capturing prior knowledge beyond binary categorizations, explore potential in teraction effects, and examine classroom-level factors in shaping student engagement and learning outcomes.
