Development and Implementation) Intended audience This workshop is designed for scholars and researchers at the forefront of methodological innovation and critical inquiry in technology-enhanced STEM education. It invites Learning Scientists reflecting on how AI redefines the study of cognition and collaboration; STEM Education Researchers integrating data-rich methods with theoretical rigor; Educational Technology Scholars critically examining AI's impact on pedagogy and research; and Methodologists navigating evolving paradigms of validity and ethics in data-intensive environments. We particularly welcome those actively grappling with core tensions between data-driven and theory-driven inquiry, scalable quantification and contextualized insight and who seek to collaboratively forge new pathways for rigorous, integrative, and ethically grounded research in the AI era. Duration Half-day (3.5 hours) Description of the event Theme and goals This workshop directly confronts the methodological dualities and tensions reshaping learning sciences research in the era of AI-enhanced STEM education. Moving beyond mere innovation, our focus is on reconciling and productively advancing competing research paradigms, such as data-driven AI analytics versus interpretative qualitative inquiry, or neurocognitive evidence alongside socio-cultural perspectives. Our primary goals are to:

• Identify and critically examine the core methodological tensions introduced or amplified by AI in

learning research.

• Co-develop integrative frameworks that honor paradigmatic strengths while enhancing overall

methodological rigor.

• Formulate practical guidelines for the complementary evolution of both established and emerging

research methods.

• Foster a sustainable community of scholars dedicated to methodological pluralism, dialogue, and

principled advancement.

Ultimately, this effort is grounded in a deeper understanding of the essential, dynamic relationship between STEM education and learning sciences methodologies. Theoretical background and relevance The rapid integration of AI and data-intensive technologies in STEM education has generated complex methodological tensions, challenging the foundational approaches of the learning sciences. This workshop focuses explicitly on four critical dimensions of this challenge: the epistemological tension between predictive AI models and interpretative qualitative research; the methodological duality of integrating multimodal learning analytics with rich ethnographic classroom studies; the paradigmatic negotiation between cognitive-neuroscientific and socio-cultural frameworks for understanding STEM learning; and the practical challenge of maintaining methodological integrity while adopting emerging AI tools. Additionally, we confront the evolving nature of human–AI interaction in STEM learning environments, which disrupts traditional conceptions of collaboration, agency, and knowledge co-construction, requiring researchers to re-examine long-standing theories of social learning, metacognition, and distributed cognition in technologically mediated settings. This workshop thus responds to an urgent need to refine and expand our methodological toolkit in order to capture, interpret, and ethically engage with the nuanced and rapidly changing realities of learning in the AI era. Themes and goals M any commercial Large Language Model (LLM) based educational tools have been pitched as learning/teaching aids or tutors. Such tools are typically designed to be sources of knowledge—whether a teacher-replacing guide through content or a question and answer machine for students to turn to when stuck. Learning Scientists, however, recognize that learning isn’t simply about the accumulation of information, but rather the social construction, connection, and application of domain-specific knowledge and community practices. This workshop addresses this critical gap: how might we create AI-supported learning environments that complement and enhance the human, social, and cultural dimensions of learning, specifically with regard to complex sociotechnical educational systems such as those found in highly learner-centered and/or constructionist environments? The ISLS community has been actively engaged in the development of AI educational environments for decades and LS and CSCL researchers are currently creating a diverse range of LLM-powered tools that provide support in these environments. While the educational technology sector frequently claims their AI platforms are developed “grounded in learning science” (Google for Education, n.d.), foundational theories of learning and teaching are largely absent. And yet, emerging LLMs have been taken up by LS and CSCL researchers to create a diverse range of learning and teaching environments. By bringing together members of the ISLS connected community (ICLS and CSCL), the goal of this workshop will be to continue to expand the imaginary of AIsupported tools in complex sociotechnical systems. Participants of the workshop will have the opportunity to share and discuss existing and in-process LLM-based educational designs, and will work together to identify shared goals, values, and tensions around the design, use, and study of AI as a support for learning and teaching. The result of this workshop will be a community-facing report documenting these shared goals and tensions to open up new opportunities for expanding theory-backed AI powered social supports and to serve as a reference for ISLS researchers, designers, and partners. Theoretical background and relevance to field and conference The ongoing conversation in corporate spaces around the role of AI in education has largely centered around tool designs and implementations that (tacitly) enact instructionist models of learning—where the AI is the knowledgeable expert that learners should turn to for information, as well as the instructor who dictates the pace and order of instruction (Mishra et al., 2025; Resnick, 2024). Far less explored in these corporate spaces is the design of AI-powered tools that support the social and cultural aspects of learning which are extensively researched in both the Learning Sciences (LS) and Computer-Supported Collaborative Learning (CSCL) communities. Recent work by LS and CSCL scholars has begun to identify best practices for designing learner-centered and collaborative AI tools. For example, Druga and Ko (2025) provide future design considerations for AI Copilots for youth. Kafai et al. (2025) convened CreateAI, a workshop that generated a report with important insights and questions, for how we might build (and evaluate) constructionist experiences for learning about AI. Anderson et al. (2025) brought together a group during last year’s conference to share their explorations in designing AI as support for groups in the classroom as a step in this direction. Constructionist and project-based learning environments center learner voices, interests, and experiences. In these complex sociotechnical systems, knowledge and expertise are distributed across people, materials, technologies, artifacts, and social interaction (Holbert et al., 2020). Constructionist design paradigms recognize that to engage with meaningful and powerful ideas and to feel a sense of self-efficacy, students need to have access to the proper scaffolding for testing their models of how they think the world works, not just be “spoon-fed” information (Papert, 1980). Learning happens in interactions with diverse supports and tools (Greeno,

2011) as learners draw from available resources to address problems they care about and make artifacts that are

meaningful to them and their communities. These sociotechnical systems echo two notions that learning scientists generally agree upon: (1) knowledge is actively constructed by learners (Piaget, 1964) and (2) that the “best scaffolding provides this support in a way that helps children discover the answer on their own” (Sawyer, 2022, p. 11). Moreover, CSCL research provides foundational principles and empirically tested design knowledge for the successful integration and implementation of AI tools that genuinely enhance human collaboration and knowledge building. From a CSCL perspective, the goal is to design technologies “to create artifacts, activities, and environments that enhance the practices of group meaning making” (Stahl et al., 2022, p. 416). Recent systematic reviews reveal that while constructionist AI literacy education shows promise, significant gaps remain in how tools are embedded in practice and how sociotechnical complexities are addressed early in learning experiences (Lin et al., 2025). We see this workshop as in conversation with scholars throughout the ISLS communities that have begun to investigate how foundational LS and CSCL theories inform the use of AI in educational design (Kafai et al., 2025; Mishra et al., 2025; Resnick, 2024). Here we focus the community’s attention on the sociocultural features of AI-supported learning environments and ask: In what ways can we design a nd implement AI to contribute to the broad array of social, cultural, and human elements of complex sociotechnical learning systems? Summary of activities The session will open with an opportunity for each participant to briefly present their AI-integrated tools or implementation designs that focus on support in constructionist and learner-centered learning environments.

Using abstracts written by applicants during the application process, and leveraging Cultural-Historical Activity Theory (CHAT) (Engeström, 2001) as an organizing framework for analyzing sociotechnical learning systems, the workshop team will identify 3-5 areas of interest reflecting different dimensions of AI integration in constructionist learning activity systems related to participants’ expertise. For example, categories might include one focused on relationships, community, and collaboration (e.g., access to expertise) and others attending to the division of labor (e.g., creating groups of learners). A key strength of CHAT is its emphasis on contradictions and tensions within activity systems—these are productive sites for understanding design trade-offs. As we expect new ideas will emerge in participants’ presentations in the opening of the session, we will also create space for additional categories to emerge beyond those pre-planned. Following presentations, participants will engage in small-group analysis sessions organized around these areas of interest. For example, one group might analyze how AI mediates learners’ engagement with domain representations across multiple tools, while another examines tensions between AI-provided support and learner agency. This structure allows participants to develop deep insights into specific dimensions while building shared understanding across diverse implementations. Groups will then reconvene for whole-group synthesis, sharing findings and collectively articulating design principles, values, and ongoing questions. Through facilitated discussion, participants will identify convergent patterns (e.g., common approaches to mediating collaboration while preserving exploration) and productive tensions (e.g., trade-offs between providing structure and maintaining open-endedness, or between AI efficiency and human relationship-building). The session will close with participants identifying priorities for future research and committing to collaborative documentation of insights. The culmination of the day’s activity will result in a community facing interactive report. Expected outcomes and contributions In this specific community of people who are constructionist designers, there is a prevailing view that AI tools should not be the main collaborator, but should be an assistant in finding meaningful human collaborations. After the workshop concludes, outputs will be synthesized by organizers into a digital report documenting the group’s articulated set of values and design directions-questions to start to articulate and solve through design work. This includes values articulated by workshop participants around the design, use and study of AI tools to support learning in learner-centered constructionist environments. The report will include tool examples shared at the workshop to showcase the diversity of the design space as well as central design goals, complex tradeoffs, and obstacles to successfully design and implement such tools. We will explore the potential for submitting as a special issue for the Journal of the Learning Sciences. Relationship to similar events conducted in the past The CreateAI project included a symposium at ICLS 2025 and a workshop held in Philadelphia. As exemplified in the project report (Kafai et al., 2025) CreateAI generated important insights and questions for how we might build (and evaluate) constructionist experiences for learning about AI. Inspired by this work, we extend this conversation and turn our attention to how AI and LLM-based technologies can support constructionist and project-based learning experiences that involve a wide range of topics. Where CreateAI asked “How do we teach about AI?”, we ask “How might AI support collaboration, agency, and meaning-making in learning communities?” This workshop offers an opportunity to collectively discuss and document design considerations and methods leveraged by the ISLS community in LLM-powered learning designs.
