# AI Tools in Arts Learning: A Scoping Review

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
With the proliferation of Artificial Intelligence (AI) tools in art making, AI knowledge and skills have made their way to arts learning. However, an assessment of the current state of AI tools in arts learning is lacking. This study used scoping review methodology to identify current research trends in AI applications in arts learning, and what gaps exist for future research. 58 papers were reviewed revealing four trends and five gaps and opportunities.

AI Tools in Arts Learning: A Scoping Review Jaycee Sansom, Safinah Ali js15197@nyu.edu, safinah.ali@nyu.edu New York University Abstract: With the proliferation of Artificial Intelligence (AI) tools in art making, AI knowledge and skills have made their way to arts learning. However, an assessment of the current state of AI tools in arts learning is lacking. This study used scoping review methodology to identify current research trends in AI applications in arts learning, and what gaps exist for future research. 58 papers were reviewed revealing four trends and five gaps and opportunities. With advancements in generative AI, AI tools and techniques have especially found applications in art making-and have subsequently been introduced in art learning (Ali, 2025). Gaining AI skills and knowledge becomes increasingly relevant to art learners-both as AI finds applications in art making, and as AI tools impact artists’ careers (Jiang, et al., 2023). However, as found by Zhai, et al. (2021), between 2010 and 2020, few papers were focused on AI in arts education and learning. This is concerning because artists can benefit significantly from AI literacy initiatives by expanding their skillsets, confidence, and even sense of belonging in the field, as well as better preparing themselves for a future where AI technologies can adversely impact creative careers. As such, it is important to recognize the trends and opportunities in AI and arts learning that exist so we may better understand where support is needed, as well as to identify where gaps in practice exist. More specifically, we wish to answer the following research questions: what areas of arts learning are being focused on, how is AI being used and looked at in these studies, what are the common themes present in the current state of research, and what gaps and opportunities exist for future research? To address this need, a scoping review is needed to examine the current state of research in AI tools in arts learning. We define arts learning as “a field consisting of creative studies including visual arts, music, creative writing, theater, dance, fabric arts, and other disciplines”. Following this definition and the scoping review methodology we identified 58 articles for review, resulting in four current trends in arts education and AI research, and five gaps in knowledge and applications. In order to systematically evaluate the state of AI tools in arts learning, a scoping review was conducted, following a structured process. A total of 548 papers were screened from AI and education journals and conference Papers that met the following exclusion criteria were then excluded from analysis –: published before 2019 (due recency and relevance), not focused on arts learning or AI, a literature review, not peer reviewed, workshop papers, perspectives papers (no original research), duplicate record, or were not indexed or published in known journals or databases (e.g. Scopus, ERIC, DOAJ, etc.). The 58 papers that met the criteria were analyzed using multiple rounds of coding based on grounded theory (Corbin & Strauss, 2014). Codes were inductively generated through a two-round coding process where themes were identified and refined to answer our research questions. One category of code, methodology, was based on codes developed by Zhai, et al., 2021. Six categories of non-exclusive codes were identified through the thematic analysis process that informed  the research questions of this work: “Area of Arts Learning,” “AI Applications,” “Education Level,” “Study Objectives,” “Learner Objectives,” and “Methodology.” Several studies had multiple codes in one category. The most commonly explored areas of art learning in the reviewed studies were  visual arts (4 7% of papers) and music (36%) (Figure 1.a). Fewer studies (22%) focused on dance, storytelling and writing, fabric arts, and general creativity, and no studies were identified in  the theater domain, even with specific term elicitation. Common AI applications that were focused upon included adaptive systems for personalization to learners (38%), media creation (33%), and feedback (31%) (Figure 1.b). The next group of applications – class management, ideation, theory learning, collaboration, and media augmentation were utilized as well in some studies, but not nearly as often as the three most common applications. The next category of codes that emerged was the education level of the participants (Figure 1.c). A vast majority (59%) of the studies examined focused on university students and professors, with K-12 and vocational schools having much smaller representation (12% and 5%). Three categories for study objectives were identified: perspective studies, experiment studies, and system proposals. 713 Learner objectives evaluated in the reviewed studies most commonly included  domain-specific skills (34%), which was where studies assessed  student acquisition of specific skills, such as, design thinking or technical art skills, as opposed to general performance and achievement (19%).  Other common themes for this category included: motivation, performance, and learner perspectives about AI. Finally, there were some trends found with the analysis methodology used by the studies. Experimental and quasi-experimental methods were used the most frequently (64%), followed by surveys (48%).

Based on the findings, a total of four current trends in research were identified. Current research in AI in art learning primarily focuses on personalized learning and automated feedback through the use of AI. Personalized learning tended to involve creating custom content and resources (e.g. Chen & Qu, 2025) or the personalization of learning pathways and resource recommendations typically through the use of big data and analytics (e.g. Deng & Wang, 2024). Automated feedback provided real-time or quick feedback to students in place of teachers (e.g. Chen, 2020). Another trend we observed is the  use of more formal assessment tools to evaluate students after having used AI technology. This often involved using a pre-established method for assessing creativity and overall performance, such as the Torrance Test of Creative Thinking (e.g. Wang, 2025) or relying on experts and teachers (e.g. Kara, 2025). Current research also revealed that art students found AI tools more useful as an assistant or a collaborator in the early stages of the creative process, rather thanduring the creation of the final artifact (e.g. Ansone, et al., 2025). Studies such as these may imply that art students are aware of the limitations of generative AI as a creator of an end product, but it can serve as a useful tool for exploring creative interpretations of a task. Finally, perspective studies of students and teachers were a notable trend. This often took the form of one of two types of studies. The first type were studies that look exclusively at the perspectives of students and teachers (e.g. Bedir Erişti & Freedman, 2024) while the second type consisted of studies that introduced an AI tool and looked at students’ perspectives regarding the tool  (e.g. Deval, et al., 2025). Five notable gaps in the literature were identified. Studies examined in this review reported having a limited research scope, including small population sizes (e.g. Keune, et al., 2024), lack of a long-term evaluation (e.g. Natheer, 2024), or lack of diversity in either the population (e.g. Zhang, et al., 2025) or the technology used (e.g. Zhang, 2025). Similarly, a vast majority of the papers examined focused on feedback, and personalization in art learning, while applications such as collaboration and ethics remain less explored. Furthermore, there is a scope of exploring more AI technologies that can benefit art learning beyond large language models. Additionally, there were papers that proposed a novel approach to an algorithm or other AI implementation that can benefit art learning (e.g. Jiang, et al., 2025). Few of these studies went beyond benchmarking their algorithms/systems and explored user  studies, which in itself presents an opportunity for future research. There also appears to be a noticeable gap in teacher involvement. Teachers in the examined studies, if involved, were only involved in one of two ways, as perspectives (e.g Bedir Erişti & Freedman, 2024) or as assessors of student performance (e.g. Chen, et al., 2025). The final gap present in this paper is that few works focus on how  students are learning from AI tools in the arts. Learning outcomes were commonly examined through design interventions, but oftentimes, the learning or art creation process itself was not investigated. By exploring gaps and opportunities in the area, this work highlights opportunities for research in the fields of AI literacy, education technology, and the learning sciences.
