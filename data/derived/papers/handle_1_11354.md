# Self-Determined Storytelling: Native Youths’ Digital Restorying Practices in an American Indian Education Summer Camp

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Native communities maintain a rich history of community education, despite historical atrocities that have severely damaged the relationship between Native peoples and the U.S. education system. From this historicity, Native educators remain committed to supporting culturally sustaining/revitalizing learning experiences for Native youth. Within an ongoing collaboration between Native educators, university researchers, designers, and cultural technologists, in this paper, we share our exploration of integrating digital restorying practices in a summer camp for Native youth and their families. Findings illustrate three cases that demonstrate three forms of self-determination in Native youths’ digital restorying practices: how, whose, and what stories are told. Insights from findings present technological and rhetorical self-determination as two enactments of restorying as a critical practice of digital storytelling. This work contributes to the ongoing conversation around the cultural learning experiences of Native youth as they connect with their communities.

Objectives Native communities have a strong history of community education, especially by inviting youth into rich learning experiences around cultural learning (e.g., Ellington, et al., 2024; Mohatt et al., 2006). Native educators and elders take up holistic framewo rks in their implementation of learning, such as th e Medicine Wheel Educational Framework (Ellington et al., 2024) that draws on th e traditional knowledge of the Medicine Wheel to gu ide the design learning experiences for Native youth (Bell et al., 2014). Scholars, though, have found that these culturally sustaining/revitalizing frameworks are often not su pported by the available technologies, particularly  for digital storytelling (e.g., Litts et al., 2021; Winter & Boudreau, 2018). In this pap er, we share our exploration of integrating digital storytelling technologies in a summer camp for Native youth and their families. As a partnership between Native educators, researchers, and designer s, w e present our efforts to support youths’ interest i n exploring technology as a storytelling tool through out a month-long summer camp. Our inquiry is guided by the research question: how do Native youth learn through creating digital (re)stories in the context of a Native Education summer camp?  Findings present the cultural and technological lea rning experiences of youth participants and identify how they utilize technolo gy to share their own stories and connect with comm unity, culture, and history. Insights from this work contr ibute to understandings around the role of t echnological and rhetorical self-determination (Lyons, 2000; Winter & Boudreau, 2018) in youths’ process of digital (re)storying. We posit that supporting Native youths’ self-determination in restorying can support their cultu ral and technological learning through story. We also offer practical consideratio ns for designing for self-determination in digital storytelling workshops.

## Theoretical framework

Our culturally sustaining/revitalizing (McCarty & Lee, 2014) orientation toward and relationship with technology and story is grounded in technological and rhetorical self-determination (Lyons, 2000; Winter & Boudreau, 2018). These stances support Native youths’ self-determination in what and how they use technology and what and how they tell their stories throughout their learning e xperiences, which is especially important given the  complicated histories between Indigenous communities and the U.S. government (Brayboy, 2005). One of the realities that the Native community in this study faces is that many stories written about Native peoples were written and illustrated by non-Native authors, thus, they portray inaccurate depic tions of Native American communi ties, which has negative effects on self-esteem and self-efficacy of Native youth (Fryberg, 2008). This reality of misrepresentation is not unique to the particular community in this paper (e.g, Journell, 2009; Sabzalian et al., 2021; Tippeconnic & Faircloth, 2010) and has resulted in a phenomenon d escribed as soul wound (Duran, 2019). Healing comes  from sharing the whole story as stated by Author 4, a Tribal Knowledge Holder and director of the Native education program in this study, which also resonates with Morris’ (1996) stance that healing comes from connections with stories and nature. Thus, we join scholars who push  for h ealing and culturally sustaining/revitalizing solut ions through expanding Native-created representations of Native peoples by  exploring Native youths’ self-determination in creating digital stories.

Moreover, as part of this move toward supporting yo uths’ sovereignty and self-determination in their own learning, we are further shaped by Indigenous frameworks of storywork (Archibald, 2008). Storywork is the active embrace of storytelling in education, learni ng, and other realms, a process that employs the va lues of “respect, reverence, responsibility, reciprocity, h olism, interrelatedness, and synergy” (Archibald, 2 008, p. 33). Storywork encourages intentional listening to Indig enous voices and perspectives in an effort to count eract colonial narratives that have diminished the lesson s of Indigenous stories, traditions, and worldviews. From this stance, we understand stories as survivance, and st ories as healing (King et al., 2015), particularly when these learning experiences are situated in values of self-determination. With these theoretical frames in min d, we conceptualize learning itself as a cultural process  (Nasir et al., 2006). In particular, we find reson ance with the practice of restorying as a process of learning (Thomas & Stornaiuolo, 2016) th rough which learners produce counternarratives or alternative narratives to the dominant narrative. Restorying invites a learning space where “a culture can be reclaimed as the people from within said culture can tell, possibly, more truthful versions of already occurred events” (Nordin et al., 2023, pp. 91-92). This practice of restorying becomes a particul arly important move toward supporting self-determination as a form of resistance in the cultural and historical context of Native education in the United States (Brayboy, 2005).

Methodological approach As part of a broader ongoing collaboration with Native Education program coordinators, tribal knowledge holders, educators, designers, and technologists, we follow a community-driven design research process (Quayle et al.,

2023) where community partners, not university rese archers, establish the goals of our work. This appr oach is

shaped by TribalCrit Theory’s (Brayboy, 2005) three  key principles for partnering with tribal communit ies: respect and reciprocity in rel ationships, self-determination, and sovereignty. Through our communi ty-driven process, we worked as a team to identify opportunit ies to support youth engagement with cultural learn ing. As part of this work, the program directors (Authors 4  & 5) invited university researchers and designers (Authors 1, 2, 3, & 6) and together planned an all-day archeology field trip to connect youth to the l and and connect them with professional cultural preservation technology (e.g., lidar scanning). With additional conversation and heavy youth interest, the technology integration expanded  to include a digital storytelling component as par t of the Native Education program’s annual summer camp for N ative youth. Our team includes the authors of this paper and a Native cultural technologist who works with I ndigenous communities to preserve and share their c ulture, history, and stories with a range of professional media and technologies.

## Learning setting and participants

Our study took place in the context of an existing summer camp for Native youth during the summer of 2 024. About 87 Native youth and their families participated in the summer camp and 24 (ages 7-16 years old) youth and 3 parents/guardians fully consented t o participate in research. All youth participants h ad direct or indirect tribal affiliations. The summer camp lasted for four weeks, meeting four days per week and four hours per day. Throughout the summer camp, youth participated in s everal Indigenous act ivities, including drum and rattle making, a pageant, a powwow, and several other expe riences that highlighted Native American history and culture. The digital storytelling component of the summer camp included: an initial overview, an archa eological field trip, gathering media throughout the camp, open workdays, and a culminating showcase. Digital storytelling project We introduced youth to iPad Pros, which we utilized because of their portability, robustness of the def ault video editing software iMovie, and Lidar scanning functio nality. At the beginning of the camp, our team shar ed with youth and family participants the practice of story boarding and digital storytelling. We provided yout h with a range of mind-mapping and storyboarding tools to support their thinking. Over the course of the summer camp, youth and their families gathered videos, photos, scans, and other media to include in their digital stories. Toward the end of the summer camp our team guided them thr ough additional digital story development to aid yo uth as they compiled the various media they had collected throughout the camp into the digital stories th ey envisioned creating. Their digital stories shared about a camp experience of their choosing. Youth stories included reflections on the camp’s events, presentations of art youth created, and retellings of personal or Indigenous stories. As a part of the culminati ng experience, many youth contributed their digital  stories to a compilation we screened for students and their families during the final summer camp showcase.

## Data collection and analysis

Across the camp, youth created 23 stories. In this study, we include the 12 stories created from youth participants who both consented and assented. Stories ranged from seven seconds to 5 minutes 34 seconds in length. In addition to the showcased digital story, our analysis included additional data, such as: storyboard and written design work, audio recordings of progress check-ins with youth, and researcher fieldnotes. For anal ysis, we constructed each story-making process as a case (Stake, 2008). Informed  by restorying analysis (Shaw et al., 2023; Thomas & Stornailuolo, 2016), we traced learning through the practice of restorying to understand youths’ decision making and self-determination throughout their design process.

## Findings

Our analysis provides insights to how Native youth restory in a digital storytelling workshop. We share three illustrative cases detailing the range of youths’ story-making process as an enactment of self-determination. “Voices of my Ancestors”: Self-determining how stories are told Across stories, youth restoryed their camp experience through a range of media, such as original video and music, and video editing techniques such as video and audi o transitions. As one example, Madelyn (age 7), app roached our team during our first visit to the camp with an idea to create a story through stop motion animation. She had previously created hand-painted characters and background scenes that she e nvisioned would come together to (re)tell a traditional Navajo story shared by one o f the Native ed ucators (Author 4) in the program. In response, our team added a stop motion app to all the iPads, which expanded the how stories are told beyond iMovie-what we initially planned. With this app, Madelyn create d a roughly three-minute stop motion animation story titled “Voices of my Ancestors.” Throughout the camp, othe r youth participants also utilized the stop motion app to share their stories. In this case, Madelyn, not only restoryed the traditional story she heard, but she also restoryed her experienc e in the summer camp itself by self-determining how the story was told. Specifically, Madelyn arrived at camp with a vision and a goal that did n ot explicitly line up with how we intended to support youth to tell their stories. We had selected the hardware (i Pads) and software (iMovie) to support youths’ init ial digital storytelling yet  aligning with our culturally sustaining/revitalizin g approach we kept space for youth to self-determine how they wanted to create their stories. This meant a quick pivot for  our team to support stop motion as a form of how stories are told, which allowed for more students to express their cultural and personal stories according to their unique visions and build skills based on their own interests. “Importance of Rock Art”: Self-determining whose stories are told Through our analysis, we identified that youth took varied approaches to why they shared their stories. One story, created by Melanie (age 14), was a compilation of documentary-style interviews where several youth participants shared their thoughts on a particular land stewardship issue: protecting Native rock art. In this one minute and 22 second video, Melanie interviewed eight youth who shared their individual  stories, which were clipped together to share a collective story. Not only did this video expand the voices of whose stories are shared, but it also utilized the ‘iMovie’ tools of inserting title cards to ask the questions, which enhanced the interview-based style approach. This editing strategy brought cohesion to the fluidity of the video by allowing each voice to contribute to the unity of the story being shared. Each of the youth contri buted what they felt was important to each question. Together they restoryed the importance of what rock art mean s to Native Americans by sharing what rock art mea ns to them. One of the youth mentioned how rock art helps  them remember their culture and ancestors, while a nother shared that it was a sacred place that tells storie s. They shared what they see happening to the rock art, such as vandalism and damage to the rock art by shootings, and what they feel should be done to stop it, such as making it illegal to shoot the rock art. In the story, the  youth shared information and personal reflection a s individual knowledge holders of their own culture and lived ex perience. In this case, not only did these youth re story the land by expanding whose stories are told about this particular site, but al so Melanie restoryed her experience in the summer camp itself by self-determining whose stories are told as part of her own story. Youth came together to enact Melanie’s vision to restory  the land by sharing their Native perspective, which  is often unknown or ignored by the shooters and other visitors to the land. “Guide to Middle School” Self-determining what stories are told Another thread of our analysis shed light on youths’ push to expand what stories are told to include those that are humorous and playful in nature. As an example, three youth participants, Hannah (age 12), Aaliyah (age 12), and Easton (age 11) came together to create a “Guide to Middle  School,” in which they wanted to share experiences and advice from their lives with the upcoming generations. Hannah and Aaliyah had just finished middle school, and Easton was set to begin middle school in the Fall. The vid eo took the form of a vlog where the three girls recorded themselves on a journey. In an interview s egment early in the video, Easton asked three questions she wondered about middle school, which sent Hannah and Aaliyah on an adventure for the answers for the remainder of the video. Hannah and Aaliyah documented their journey going around the school co mpiling a guide to be successful in middle school. On their journey, they  invited various people to talk about their memorie s and experience in middle school. For example, they interviewed the school janitor they found walking in the hallway and another educator they knew who they found in on e of the classrooms. In this case, Hannah, Aaliyah, and Easton self-determined what stories can be told as cultural representations. They took a playful tone with their digital story and even included some “behind the scenes” bloopers at the end. Their approach restoryed the middle school experience and their camp experience, where initially youth were invited to share stories about their Native culture. This group invited their audience to consi der their restorying that Native culture spans trad itional and heritage knowledge and current and contemporary lived experiences.

## Discussion

Toward the goal of sharing the whole story (Morris, 1996), we present in this paper a collection of youth-created digital stories through which youth restoryed their  relations with stories, the land, and their commun ity. Our discussion highlights two forms of self-determination Native youth enacted through their restorying and provides considerations for designers to design for self-determined storytelling. Technological and rhetorical self-determination through restorying Storytelling remains a central practice in Native A merican culture; the history and teachings of Nativ e communities ha ve been handed down through generational storytelling (Archibald, 2008). The stories youth created through camp were reflexive representations  of what they know and who they see themselves as N ative youth in the world. The youth-created stories served as representations of learni ng not only of th e digital and technical skills they developed but also the broade r practices of learning through restorying. Of particular note, the findings elucidate how Native youth enact restoryin g toward goals of technological and rhetorical selfdetermination. Over the course of the workshop, youth participants  became directors of their own stories and explored several mediums through which to share the ir stories, which illustrates an enactment of technological self-determination (Winter & Boudreau, 2018). In addition to technolog ical self-determination, youth also self-determined what stories they wanted to share, inclu ding the genre of the story itself. This rhetorical self-determination (Lyons, 2000) served as an enactment of youths’ restorying through exploring various topics based on their lived experiences and messages they wished to share with the community. Bringing together technological and rhetorical self-determination makes visible the role digital restorying can play in Native youths’ relation with stories, the land, and their communities.

Designing for self-determination As designers of learning environments, we join the discussion among learning scientists and designers to consider their role as designers in shaping youths’ self-determination to design their own narratives and fu tures. In this paper, we identified three areas of self-determination that youth enacted through their rest orying: how, whose, and what stories are told. We learned that designing for self-determination prioritizes collaboration not only culturally and communally but also strategically in  response to technologies that have failed in digital restory ing (e.g., Litts et al., 2021; Winter & Boudreau, 2018). There is a level of reflexivity and responsivity ne cessary to support youth in sharing the whole story, as Author 4 and the Tribal Knowledge Holder leading our team describes. To share the whole story, we must design learning environments with sp ace for learners to expand the tools we use (how stories are told), broaden who is a storyteller (whose stories are told), and widen what counts as a cultural story (what stories are told). As designers of learning environments, we can – for pragmatic reasons – anchor learning experiences in a particular technology or narrative form, yet t his move can silence particular ways of being and knowing (e.g., Litts et al., 2021). This silencing can mirror the forms of cultural identity silencing Native youth experience in schools (Brayboy, 2005; Leigh-Osroosh & Hutchison, 2019). Thus, much like restorying challenges the dominant narratives, we i nvite designers to cr itically (re)consider design process and technologies vis-à-vis dominant processes and tools (Nordin et al., 2023; Shaw et al., 2023; Thomas & Stornaiuolo, 2016). In this reconsideration, we can engage with a restorying of the design of learning environments as a form of care work (Headrick Taylor et al., 2024) that supports healing futures.
