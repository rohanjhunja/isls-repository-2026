# What AI “Sees” and What Facilitators Know: Supporting Collaboration in Constructionist Making Environments

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
As multimodal AI tools enter educational spaces, questions emerge about whether and how they might replace or complement educator expertise. Experimenting with AI-embedded tools in a middle-school constructionist making program, this study examines what AI and human facilitators attend to when encouraging collaboration between makers. We analyze AI-generated project descriptions and collaboration recommendations, alongside facilitator discussions of collaborations in debrief transcripts. We found that AI identified technical features and suggested direct feature-copying in student projects, while facilitators layered social dynamics, developmental trajectories, and pedagogical goals.. Our findings challenge replacement framings and offer AI design suggestions for leveraging pedagogical expertise. We argue that designing AI for constructionist environments requires strategic offloading: leveraging AI for tasks that extend facilitators’ observational reach while preserving facilitators’ judgment for decisions central to identity development and relationship building.

The proliferation of multimodal generative Artificial Intelligence (AI) has reignited a persistent question in education: Can—and should —AI replace educators? Many commercial initiatives are designed and marketed in ways that diminish learner agency, over‑specify closed‑ended tasks, and undervalue human connection (Resnick, 2024). Yet AI’s role in educational contexts is not predetermined. We have choices about whether and how to design and implement AI technologies in learning environments. Informed decisi on‑making will “shape and determine whether these technologies will reinforce traditional structures or promote greater learner agency and creativity” (Mishra et al. 2025, p. 1). Recent efforts have begun to explore what this looks like in practice across tools, learning, ethics, teaching, and assessment (Kafai et al., 2025). Following Resnick (2024), we treat generative AI as an opportunity to widen the design space for constructionist learning, asking how people can “design and use generative AI technologies to support a more constructionist approach to learning” (p. 7). Constructionist environments are complex socio-technical systems in which knowledge and expertise are distributed across people, materials, technologies, artifacts, and social interaction. Thinking is made visible through public artifacts that invite critique and extension, encouraging learners to see the world as changeable and to iteratively re‑make it with others (Holbert et al., 2020). These environments are designed to support open‑ended projects, scaffold exploration, and focus attention on powerful ideas while embracing productive struggle and debugging (Papert, 1980). Facilitators are active and important members of these socio-technical systems and play crucial roles in surfacing ideas, nurturing community, and scaffolding collaboration.

In this paper, we examine how AI’s analysis of maker documentation from a middle‑school constructionist making program might support facilitators’ existing practices for surfacing collaboration opportunities. We compare which features of students' work AI and facilitators each attend to by analyzing AI-generated technical descriptions and recommendations based on in-progress project media alongside facilitator reflections. We ask: Which features of maker projects or practices do facilitators focus on when d iscussing possible student collaborations? When a multimodal Large Language Model (LLM) is prompted to analyze images and videos of maker artifacts, which features of the project does it reference to suggest collaborations? In this study, we examine data collected as part of a multi-year design-based research project developing a suite of tools and practices to support distributed communities of learners in collaborative making. A key goal of this project was to develop design principles for encouraging and supporting collaboration in makerspaces, even when learners are not physically co-located.

A key goal of the collaboration tools developed for this project was to increase awareness of who is in the makerspace(s) and what each maker is working on. One of these tools to achieve this goal is a dashboard, featuring profile cards for each maker with  their names, biographies, and “affinities” (topics, skills, or interests 1517 they select to indicate interest) (Bawankule et al., 2023). Another tool, linked to the dashboard profile system, is the Make-in-Progress (MiP), where makers upload photos and videos of their ongoing projects. These uploaded artifacts display on screens across spaces, creating a shared record of their making processes (Danzig et al., 2025). While these tools help promote awareness, facilitating collaboration opportunities remains a complex process. As Tissenbaum and Slotta (2019) note, particularly in open-ended projects, just putting students together to work on a project isn’t enough to support collaboration. Successful collaboration depends on mutual awareness of each student’s processes and what Barron (2003) describes as “metacognitive synchronization"—the ability to collaboratively work on problems together (Kumar & Tissenbaum, 2022). Fa cilitators play a central role in scaffolding collaboration, as actors within the socio‑technical fabric of constructionist environments, their engagement with people, materials, and artifacts is constitutive of learning, not merely supportive of it (Lave & Wenger, 1991). This poses challenges for facilitators in distributed learning environments, where engaging with all students and their work becomes difficult as environments scale across physical spaces. To that end, we draw on the concept of co-agency (Katsenou et al., 2025), where human and AI capabilities are mutually shaped through interaction—neither replacing the other, but each extending what the other cando. Co-agency frames AI contributions as meaningful only in dialogue with human expertise and context. We use this lens to examine how AI-generated project analyses might complement facilitators’ contextual knowledge in supporting collaboration. The implementation discussed in this paper took place in 2025 during a two-week summer camp with two groups of middle school learners (15 and 22 makers respectively) at a large midwestern University campus. During the camp, makers created personally meanin gful apps using MIT App Inventor. Eight facilitators, who are also researchers on the project, supported this work: four were physically present across the two rooms, while four participated remotely. Each group also included five student mentors, aged 14-18, that supported camp makers. During the 2025 camp we tested three scenarios where we imagine multimodal LLM tools might support collaboration in constructionist learning environments. In this paper we describe and analyze the third scenario where the LLM was prompted to generate collaboration recommendations based on self-selected student “affinities,” images, and videos uploaded by learners to the MiP. This scenario is especially relevant to the questions of this paper as it was designed to be facilitator facing, rather than automatic ally executing. Here we describe the process used to generate these AI-based recommendations, evaluate the appropriateness of these recommendations, and compare them to those provided by researcher-facilitators present during the camp. Data sources Data analyzed for this study includes: student selected images, video, captions, and affinities; AI-generated descriptions of maker’s projects; AI collaboration recommendations; and facilitator debrief transcripts. The AI-generated data was produced using a private instance of the Gemini for Firebase API that analyzed student project documentation (photos and videos) and student-selected affinities. The prompts were developed iteratively using Firebase Studio, and tested on a sample group of data before the full dataset. The system involved a two-stage prompting process using the following prompts: prompt1 = “You are given a maker’s last project images or video and their current affinities (interests/skills). Analyze each maker’s photo or video. Provide a brief 3-5 sentence description of their project that highlights features of the project and specific mechanics or code used. Your goal should be to provide lots of details that you can later use to compare student projects. If you do not have enough information about their project from the image, return ‘Don’t have enough info’”; prompt2 = “Compare maker projects using only the descriptions you generated (do not use the uploaded images). Are there any features, mechanics, or code from one project that might improve the other project? Provide 2-4 suggestions.” Of note in these prompts is the request for the LLM to generate a description of each project first, an option to not  describe it if the data was deemed insufficient, and a prompt to compare the AI-generated descriptions of student projects to suggest 2-4 possible collaborations. The two prompts were run in sequence with shared context, enabling the second prompt to access the descriptions from the first. These recommendations were only viewed by the project team.

At the end of each day of the two week camp, facilitators met to debrief, discussing successes and challenges encountered and potential opportunities for collaboration and mentorship. Transcripts of these sessions were systematically searched for keywords suggesting collaboration, including “group,” “partner,” “together,” “suggestion,” “pair,” “collaborate,” and “work with.” Transcripts particularly relevant to this analysis include one with in-the-moment feedback from facilitators on the AI-generated recommendations and another from soon after the camp, where facilitators reflected on the workshop and future design directions for the next implementation. 1518 We entered the analysis interested in understanding what role (if any) AI might play in supporting facilitators in constructionist sociotechnical systems. Specifically, we attend to what LLMs are able to identify in images and videos of student work, and how this capability might support the goals of increasing collaboration in a makerspace.

To do this, we iteratively developed a qualitative coding scheme through close reading of the data. The first two authors independently conducted initial open coding (Saldaña, 2013) of AI outputs and facilitator debrief transcripts, taking analytical memos to identify emerging patterns in what each data source foregrounded. Through and contexts present across our data sources. Our final coding scheme included 13 codes: UI element, Project Topic, Technical feature, Feature copying, User experience, Coder experience, Skills development, Social context, Physical context, Conceptual understanding, Computational identity, Community impact, Design phase (Ideation, Prototyping, Testing). While informed by our theoretical understanding of constructionist learning environments and collaboration support, these codes emerged bottom-up from the data. The unit of analysis varied by data source: for the 24 AI-generated project descriptions, each individual description was coded as a complete unit; for the four AI-generated collaboration recommendations, we first coded each recommendation category then coded individual recommendations within them; and for facilitator debriefs, codes were applied turn-by-turn to nine days of transcripts selected based on the search criteria described. What AI identified in maker projects Out of 45 total makers in the program, the AI generated collaboration recommendations for only 13, organizing these under four thematic categories. Each recommendation was presented in a “from/to” format, identifying specific technical features, from one or more makers’ projects that could improve other makers’ projects. All four recommendations involved what we coded as feature copying—direct suggestions to transfer or integrate functionality between projects. Sometimes these suggested ways to improve exis ting features and in other cases they recommended adding features to extend a project. When identifying and describing these potential improvements, our coding revealed that the AI framed suggestions in terms of: improving user experience (“more engaging and accessible,” “ a more dynamic shopping experience”)  and coder experience (“easy updates,” “expansion of content without redeploying the app”).

The AI-generated collaboration recommendations included connections between makers working in different rooms and between peers and mentors, crossing physical spaces and participant roles. Recommendations were based on makers’ documentation of their projects at different points in their making process and in different formats. For example, in one recommendation involving three makers, the AI suggested one maker’s “assistance app concept” (generated from an image of a handwritten sketch), could incorporate a MapScreen component from another students’ app implementation (generated from a screen recording demo of their app) while using the “location services and a mapping API as conceptualized by Alex,” a third maker who had also uploaded an ideation sketch. The AI not only correctly identified technical features in each maker’s app, but also worked across design phases—identifying two projects as “conceptual” or in an ideation phase and making connections between them and a third project that was already a prototype in MIT App Inventor. Despite the “uncertainty outlet” included in prompt 1 (likely a reason only 13 recommendations were provided), some descriptions and subsequent suggestions included assumptions that were not supported by the maker media. For instance, the AI described one project topic as an “informational app” about muscles, when the app screen only contained a clipart image and an unlabeled button. In the same recommendation, it suggested adding a feature for “personalized content based on user profiles or preferences,” w hen no user profile functionality was visible in the uploaded media.

What facilitators identified when discussing collaboration In contrast to AI’s focus on technical features (ex. ‘MapScreen’ component), facilitator discussions centered on the broader social  (40) and physical  (32) context of the workshop, such as students’ working styles and table arrangements across makerspaces, and learners’ skill development (24) such as what computational skills students were building.  Technical features, in contrast, only appeared 4 times across nine days of transcripts. This distribution aligns with facilitators’ roles in the learning environment as members of the community. Even when technical features in student projects prompted the discussion, facilitators layered multiple dimensions of contextual information simultaneously when discussing collaboration decisions, always grounding their thinking in the learning goals of developing computational identity and student agency. For example, when identifying a maker who could potentially be a peer mentor, they discussed her technical skills (“she’s doing cool 1519 stuff with the canvas and being able to touch and draw things”), her conceptual understanding ("she really understands variables"), and her casual confidence in explaining what she had made to a facilitator. Similarly, when discussing how one mentor’s social style led him to guide his table toward prototyping full apps on day one, or discussing a maker that preferred working alone over collaborating with an unfamiliar peer, facilitators attended to how social preferences and physical context, shaped not just who worked together, but how students developed their sense of themselves as computational creators within a broader community. Facilitators negotiated not just whether to facilitate collaboration, but when, how, and with whom support should occur, considering how different approaches aligned with individual students’ development. In one example, facilitators discussed a student working on a shopping cart feature using a list viewer component. While they mentioned the technical challenge—”we’re having some trouble with how to get the list viewer to show” — they also considered her style and independence: “she’s done everything by hers elf…she’s one of those independent workers.” The discussion centered not just on whether to pair her with someone, in this case a remote facilitator, but on whether to encourage her to “try to debug it and figure out how to make that feature work on her own first,” given how they had noticed her working before and the skills she was building in doing so. In the debrief reflecting on the AI-generated recommendations, the facilitators identified some of them aligned with connections they had already considered or tried to make. When reviewing the AI’s mapping recommendation described above, one facilitator n oted: “the connections that are on here are connections we thought to make in person. So they are matching Maya and Jordan. That was one [the] mentors brought up.” However, he noted additional context about the students that was not visible in the AI analysis: “Maya has the maps in her project, but she doesn’t exactly remember how she even got there. Jordan wants to start doing the maps. He doesn’t exactly know where to start, but I don’t think he would understand what Maya had there.” Facilitators also distinguished between cross and within-makerspace suggestions in reflecting on the AI outputs, calling cross-makerspace connections “more valuable” because physical constraints made them less noticeable. When considering how AI might be useful in a learning context, tools often focus on whether AI can perform educator tasks, implicitly framing the question as a 1:1 replacement. However, this study foregrounds the complexity of facilitators’ decision making —a process that layers social context, technical knowledge, and pedagogical goals. AI performed as prompted, finding similarities between projects. Facilitators negotiated multiple dimensions of information —not just what students made, but how they worked,  who they wanted to work with, what they understood conceptually, and what different collaboration opportunities might afford them. While our prompt shaped the AI output and there is potential for prompting different aspects of the making experience, no prompt could provide AI with the full context facilitators draw upon—the ongoing relationships, development, and social dynamics accum ulated from daily interaction as a part of the community. To that end, our findings suggest that designing AI tools to support facilitators’ collaboration decision making process requires careful consideration of what is offloaded to the AI. Our approach maintains the facilitator in the loop–sharing the AI recommendations with facilitators rather thandirectly with students –positioning AI as providing observational support while preserving facilitators’ judgement about when and how to act on collaboration opportunities.

Furthermore, we recommend incorporating “uncertainty outlets” in AI prompting. This gives an overeager LLM explicit permission to indicate when it has insufficient information to make a recommendation, reducing inaccurate outputs. Despite this safeguard, some AI descriptions still included assumptions unsupported by project media, highlighting how technical design choices can shape, but not eliminate the need for human review. These findings suggest facilitator-AI design principles to explore further, including: offloading tasks that extend beyond a facilitators’ reach (such as cross-room collaboration in distributed makerspaces); maintaining human judgement for tasks that are essential to relationship building; and including and refining uncertainty outlets in prompting to help make AI limitations transparent to educators. Facilitators’ nuanced negotiations and layering of contextual information in discussions point to the complexity of their role supporting and scaffolding collaboration in constructionist learning environments. This work would be obscured by direct task-to-task comparison between AI and humandecision making. Such comparisons, historically common in discussions of AI for human replacement (Otaki & Lindwall, 2024), miss the many dimensions of facilitator’s expertise. For example, when both AI and facilitators identified the same pair of makers as a potential match, the facilitators’ additional context that one student, “has the maps in her project, but doesn’t exactly remember how she got there,” demonstrated that the match was only project-level and unlikely to be a fruitful collaboration. This highlights the limitation of replacement framings and points to the need for further research that accounts for the relational complexity and pedagogical intentionality central to collaboration support in constructionist learning.
