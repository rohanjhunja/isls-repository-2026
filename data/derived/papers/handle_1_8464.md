# Multilevel Connected Teacher Learning for STEM Curriculum Innovation

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study investigates how differences in teachers’ learning outcomes may be related to the Architecture for Learning (AfL) at multiple levels of the education ecosystem available to the teachers as well as their own individual characteristics. The MultiLevel MultiScale framework was adopted for the analysis of the AfL available to ten teachers in five schools participating in a University-School Partnership programme targeting pedagogical and curriculum innovation in STEM. The study found that the AfL available to teachers varies across schools and within the same school in terms of exposure intensity and their foci of interaction. Teachers may also have different learning foci depending on their experiences and types of leadership roles. The findings have implications for customization of teacher learning for teachers at different career stages and in different school settings.

## Introduction

Evolving social demands on education calls for changes in educational practice, often in the form of curriculum or pedagogical innovation. This in turn puts pressu re on teacher professional learning. Yet, we notice  there are variations in teachers’ learning outcomes even when they have engaged in the same kind of professional learning communities. Evidence shows that within the same pr oject network, there can be variations in terms of teacher learning across schools (Horn & Kane, 2015) as well as across teachers within the same school (Horn, 2005; Horn & Little, 2010). These observations can be explaine d by an ecological perspective on learning—that tea cher learning depends on their intentional learning orie ntation in relation to their personal characteristi cs, and their interactions with their professional context (Opfer and Pedder, 2011). Affordances that support teacher learning within their professional context are associated with the architectures for learning (AfL) (Law, et al., 2015; Stein & Coburn, 2008; Wenger, 1998) available to them. In this study, we investigate how different AfL could influence teachers’ learning outcomes, as well as whether and  how these outcomes depend on teachers’ personal characteristics through a set of comparative case s tudies of teachers taking part in a design-based in novation network (Fishman, et al., 2013) of schools in Hong Kong focusing on the promotion of self-directed lea rning pedagogy and curriculum integration in STEM related subjects. Two main research questions are explored:

(1)  What AfL at network, school and teacher levels  were available to different teachers within the sa me

innovation network within their respective professional contexts, which may vary across schools, as well as across roles within the same school?

(2)    Whether and how the AfL available to teachers could affect their learning outcomes?

## Conceptual framework

This study adopts the MultiLevel MultiScale (MLMS) model (Law, et al., 2015) as an analytical framework. This model posits that within an education ecosystem, le arning takes place at student, teacher, school, net work, and system levels (Law, et al., 2016). Such learning is influenced by interactions across these levels (thus multilevel), as well as interactions across entities within the same level that may or may not belong to the same organizational unit (e.g., interactions among teachers in different schools, thus the term multiscale to capture the different spheres of influence). This model can guide the examination  of learning conditions and learning outcomes at mu ltiple levels in the context of educational innovations. To identify the conditions for learning at different levels of the ecosystem, we adopt the construct of architectures for learning (AfL) (Stein & Coburn, 2008; Wenger, 1 998). According to Law, et al. (2015), AfL comprises four key components: (a) organizational structures (e.g., teacher design teams), (b) interaction mechanisms (e.g. co-teaching), (c) reification artifacts such as guidel ines and resources, and (d) technology to facilitate actions  and interactions within the ecosystem. Guided by t he AfL construct, the MLMS model examines learning conditi ons at different levels by looking at organizationa l structures of learning environments and differentia ting learning interaction mechanisms within and acr oss organizational units (e.g. school as an organizatio nal unit) (Law, et al., 2015). The MLMS model regar ds reification artifacts and technology as boundary objects (Stein & Coburn, 2008) for embodying ideas and shaping focus in negotiations (Wenger, 1998) travelling across multiple communities of practice.

## Research context

This study adopted multiple case studies (Yin, 2003) as its methodological approach, with an individual teacher as a unit of analysis. The research context was in the second phase of a series of funded university-s chool partnership projects formulated as design-based imp lementation research (Fishman, et al., 2013). The p rojects aimed at promoting self-directed learning in Science education in the first phase (2014-2017) and STEM education in the second (2017-2019), which was in response to the STEM education initiative in Hong Kong (EDB, 2016a). In the second phase, the network consisted of exper ienced and novice teachers from existing and newly joined schools in the network. Schools were expected to design and implement two school-based STEM curriculum units in the first year. The network shared two key design principles for STEM curriculum innovation: (i) self-directed learning (SDL) (Hew, et al., 2016; Knowles, 1975) as the pedagogy of choice, and (ii) interdisciplinary integration as the preferred model of school-based STEM curricu lum design. Interdisciplinary STEM units should con tain intended learning outcomes related to learning elem ents from STEM related subjects (EDB, 2016a). Scien tific investigation and engineering design process were i ntroduced to teachers as forms of disciplinary prac tice that teachers could use to model their design of STEM cu rriculum units around authentic problems/scenarios that provide SDL learning experiences involving different disciplinary elements for learners to engage in. Sampling, Data source and analysis This study focused on teacher learning in the first year of phase 2 in the STEM innovation project series. Of the 32 participating schools in that year, we selected 10 teachers from the five schools for which the fir st author of this paper served as the project consultant. The backgrounds of these teachers are shown in Table 1. The analysis was guided by the four key components  of AfL at different levels. Transcriptions of yearend principal and teacher interviews were coded bas ed on the AfL framework. The investigation of the A fL components for individuals and their practices were  supplemented and triangulated by other data source s, including audio records of co-planning meetings, transcriptions of debriefing meetings after lesson observations, teachers’ project participation data, and individual school reports. These records facilitated the examination of the focus of interactions in different kinds of AfL experienced by individual teachers. Advances in teachers’ practices were analyzed based on the SDL processes (Hew, et al., 2016) and the interdisciplinary elements in STEM (EDB, 2016a). Checklist matrices and contrast tables (Mil es, et al., 2020) were constructed respectively for  exploring and comparing the teachers’ practices as well as the key AfL subcomponents they experienced. ## Findings and discussion

Different sets of architectures for learning (AfL) among teachers To answer the first question about the AfL experienced by individual teachers, we identified and summarized in Table 2 key AfL subcomponents under each of the four AfL components at the network, school and teacher levels experienced by each teacher. The results show that even teacher implementers working within the same s chool experienced different distribution profiles of AfL.  Teachers’ participation at the network level inter action mechanisms such as workshops and open class differed due to school level implementation strategies. There were also differences in AfL sub-components in relation to organizational structures at the school level su ch as the laboratory technical team and e-learning team. Only some teachers (A1, D1, X1 and Y1) had access to interaction mechanisms (discussing or reporting) to inform the school administration or the principal about the pr oject progress because of their leadership roles in their schools. There were other self-initiated interaction mechanisms such as co-teaching arrangement in school A and emerging internal COLO ((COplanning, Lesson Observation & debriefing) cycles for improving STEM lessons among teachers in schools A, X and Z. Wenger’s (1998) duality of the designed and emergent learning architectures was observed. Such duality can exist when learners are given autonomy to co-design their learning environments.

Variations among teachers because of different sets of learning foci ICLS2022 Proceedings 1274 © ISLS For the second research question, the study found t hat the AfL available to teachers can affect their learning by shaping their contextual learning focus. Three major types of foci were classified with reference to working foci in the school environment for evaluating school performance (EDB, 2016b): (i) learning and teaching (L&T); (ii) management and organization (M&O); and (iii) technical issues (Tech). As shown in Table 2, all teacher s were exposed to AfL with an L&T focus. However, only teachers with certain types of leadership roles, such as being a project coordinator or an e-learning team coordinator, would be involved in AfL with contextual learning focus on M&O. Different profiles of AfL with different contextual learning foci explains why even teachers in the same school could demonstrate different intentional lear ning orientations. For example, A1, with an additio nal focus on M&O, could orient his learning also at the leadership level, thinking about how to create conditions for teacher learning such as co-teaching and lesson study as well as scaling up cross-subject collaboration, while A2 was only concerned with his own improvement of pedagogical innovation and new understanding of STEM education in the post interview. Similar patterns were found in the comparison between X1 and X2, and between D1 and D2. As mentioned, experienced teachers, A1, D1 and X1, with leadership roles in a  cross-subject project team had to take on addition al M&O related learning foci. These teachers were able to demonstrate their learning outcomes in facilitating professional development of their colleagues and sustaining cross-subject collaboration. Another experienced teacher Y1 was with M&O related foci, but in a single-subject project team and not given any leadership role for steering crosssubject collaboration in the project. She showed he r learning outcomes mainly in terms of designing in tegrated STEM curriculum units instead. Compared with experienced project teachers, the impact of contextual learning focus of L&T on novice teachers’ learning outcomes is more obvious in terms of SDL pedagogical innovat ion. Particularly, learning trajectories of novice teachers from new network schools were more salient in terms of SDL pedagogical innovation. The two novice teachers, Z1 and Z2, from a new network school demonstrated the biggest changes in their classroom practices. In the first COLO cycle, Z1, Z2 and their other team members learned how ICLS2022 Proceedings 1275 © ISLS not to follow the canned approach in textbooks and let students plan for their investigation as inform ed by the operational framework of the SDL processes. However, in this first cycle, school Z teachers did not gi ve opportunities for students to revise their methods of investigation. Students were given to accepting a pre-set conclusion irrespective of their results. Compared with the other four schools with experienced teachers, the SDL opportunity provided to students and the extent of STEM integration in school Z was the least. In the second cycle, they improved the SDL opportunities and STEM integration for students. They brought in practices of engineering design process and let students plan, create, evaluate, analyze data, and revise their prototypes. Students were able to exercise more self-direction with greater use of e-learning platform. The inexperience of Z1 and Z2 amplified the impact of the L&T focused AfL on their improvement of SDL practices.

## Conclusion and implications

This study shows that AfL available to different te achers within the same innovation network may vary across schools, as well as across roles within the same school. The AfL available to teachers can affect teacher learning outcomes by shaping intentional learning orientations. The influence of AfL on learning outcomes is complicated by individual characteristics like types of leadership roles and innovation experience. This could have implications for personalized designs for teacher learning in in novation networks customized for experienced and no vice teachers in different school contexts to advance the sustainability and scalability of curriculum and pedagogical innovations. The present study is limited in scope not only in terms of the number of teachers and schools involved, but also that it is limited to the study of only one innovation network with a focus on STEM innovation. Further research is needed to develop more nuanced understa nding of how teacher learning is influenced by the interactions between AfL at different levels and the teachers’ individual characteristics.
