# Construal Level Theory of Psychological Distance: Preservice Teacher Learning in Artificial Intelligence-supported Simulations

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study explores how preservice teachers engage with artificial intelligence (AI)- powered virtual students in VR classrooms, focusing on psychological distance and construal-level theory. Using a mixed-methods approach, we analyzed interaction data from 29 participants. Findings show higher levels of abstract thinking and temporal imme diacy during teaching decisions. These results highlight how cognitive distance influences pedagogical reasoning. Insights inform the design of AI agents in immersive environments, supporting adaptive teacher learning through reflective, experience-oriented simulations.

Artificial intelligence (AI) technologies are rapidly transforming educational settings, offering new possibilities for immersive learning and teaching support. Among these, AI-powered virtual agents —computer-generated agents that simulate real student actions and reactions —have gained traction as tools to help preservice teachers practice and reflect on their instructional strategies in simulated  yet dynamic environments (Rickel, 2001; Schroeder et al., 2025). These AI-driven agents in virtual reality (VR) provide consistent and scalable simulations, and introduce alternative forms of interactions that supplement pedagogical approaches (Mikeska et al., 2025). Despite technical advancements, there remains a critical gap in understanding how preservice teachers experience and make sense of their experience and interactions with AI-powered students (e.g., Dai et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025), especially on the cognitive and psychological processes  and distance preservice teachers engage in when working with AI-powered virtual students (cf. Liberman et al., 2007; Vermunt, 2023). As AI systems simulate student behavior and communication in virtual environments, a deeper inquiry into how preservice teachers conceptualize these interactions  and how they exercise pedagogies in this setting, especially in terms of cognitive and psychological distance, decision-making, and sensemaking, is urgently needed. This study addresses th e gap by exploring how preservice teachers engage with AI-powered virtual students in VR  classrooms. Specifically, we examine how these interactions are shaped by varying degrees of psychological distance (Liberman et al., 2007) and how abstract versus concrete thinking emerges during teaching decisions. The study asks: What interaction patterns, influenced by cognitive and psychological distance, do preservice teachers demonstrate when engaging with AI-powered virtual students in VR classroom simulations? Experience design of AI-powered agents in VR learning environments Designing effective VR learning environments with AI-powered agents requires close attention to how users experience interactions with these entities. Experience design emphasizes how users perceive, feel, and act within digital settings (Hassenzahl, 2013;  McLellan, 2000). In teacher learning, it involves crafting immersive, meaningful encounters with virtual students that reflect real-world complexity while fostering reflection and growth (Kavanagh et al., 2020). Winograd (2006) emphasized iterative, design-oriented approaches grounded in assumptions that become especially salient in VR simulations, where the boundary between artificial and authentic experience remains present but increasingly blurred. W hen a teacher interacts with emotive, responsive AI-powered virtual students, perceived psychological distance may shape their reasoning and interaction patterns. Such conjectures underscore the need to study experience-oriented interaction through the lens of psychological distance. That is, how physically, mentally, or emotionally “close” or “far” users feel from AI agents. Construal-level theory (CLT) Construal-level theory (Liberman & Trope, 1998; Trope & Liberman, 2010) provides a lens  for understanding how people mentally represent events, objects, or individuals based on their perceived psychological distance. The theory posits that: 1) greater psychological distance (e.g., imagining a hypothetical or future situation) leads to more abstract thinking—focusing on broader goals, intentions, and meanings.  2) Less/lower distance (e.g., immediate, concrete situations) prompts concrete thinking— emphasizing specific actions, details, and responses. 2812 CLT defines four key dimensions of psychological distance:  1) Temporal (now vs. future),  2) Spatial (here vs. there), 3) Social (self vs. other), and 4) Hypothetical (real vs. imagined). CLT has been adopted as a lens in science education (Sutter et al., 2018) and in the studies of human behaviors and decision-making (Adler & Sarstedt, 2021). By applying CLT, we can investigate how teachers shift between abstract and concrete modes of thinking during VR-based simulations, and how these shifts correlate with perceived psychological distance. For example, does a teacher frame the interaction as a future rehearsal (high temporal distance), or as an immediate pedagogical challenge (low distance)? These patterns offer insight into how teachers make decisions, set goals, and interpret student behaviors when practicing teaching in simulated environments. These conjectures provide the basis  for further investigation regarding how psychological distance and construal levels influence preservice teachers’ reasoning with AI-powered virtual students. Through this lens, we aim to inform the design of AI-powered virtual agents in VR simulations that better support reflective, adaptive teacher learning.

## Method

This study employs a mixed-methods design to explore how preservice teachers interact with AI-powered virtual students. By integrating both computational feature extraction (Nelson, 2020) and a case study (Yin, 2018), we aim to understand how teaching decisions  would be shaped by psychological distance  in VR simulations. Our research involves analyzing teacher discourses and interaction patterns during practices with AI-powered virtual student agents. The study was conducted using EVETeach  (Ke et al., 2021), a VR simulation where preservice teachers engage with AI-powered virtual students. These virtual students are designed to respond dynamically, representing real classroom discourses, discussing scientific phenomena of biology and physics  as well as math. Data were collected from 29 preservice teachers who interacted with AI-powered virtual  students in EVETeach. Participants were  enrolled in educational programs in the US  and participated in at least one  VR teaching session. Data sources included audio-video recordings of interactions, system conversation logs (verbal exchanges between preservice teacher s and virtual students), participatory observation, and teacher reflections. Data were analyzed using a two-pronged approach. First, using c omputational grounded theory (Nelson, 2020), we applied unsupervised machine learning to extract patterns from the data corpus, guided by the lens of construallevel theory (Trope & Liberman, 2010) based on linguistic features. Second, qualitative thematic coding was used to identify instances of construal-level thinking to explore dimensions of psychological distance (e.g., temporal, hypothetical). This mixed-methods analysis allowed for the triangulation of qualitative insights with computational patterns, enriching our understanding of how psychological distance influences teacher decisionmaking and learning in VR with AI-powered virtual students.

## Preliminary findings

Preservice teachers’ construal levels were evident in multiple dimensions. Preliminary analysis of their interactions revealed two key findings from computational feature extraction and case analysis: Abstract Thinking (1.76 times more abstract than concrete) and T emporal Distance (14.8 times more immediate thandistant thinking). Abstract thinking appeared in their efforts to understand student knowledge and teach scientific phenomena. For instance, when discussing a teaching-session on gas laws, Participant 1 hypothesized: “I think he’s not understanding because he can see the container, but steam is not something you can see.” In another case, Participant 4 drew on students ’ concrete experiences to convey abstract scientific properties of temperature: “… maybe I should ask about like snow  because everybody knows snow is cold.” Further, w hile most responses reflected immediate thinking, some participants also engaged in distant, future-oriented reflection. Participant 5 noted: “… you know, having them get  up and move around and do something different. If it were a real classroom would have been the move for sure.” These examples illustrated how participants used construal-level thinking— both abstract/concrete and immediate/distant—to inform pedagogical decisions within the VR environment. These initial findings support the conjecture that psychological distance, including hypothetical and temporal dimensions, plausibly shapes preservice teachers’ thinking modes when interacting with AI-powered virtual students (Trope & Liberman, 2010). Preservice teachers’ teaching strategies to scaffold student thinking were informed by their interaction modes and their construal levels  (Sutter et al., 2018)  of the AI-powered virtual students in VR classroom simulation. Understanding these cognitive and psychological dimensions is essential for designing AI systems that effectively support such interactions and practice.
