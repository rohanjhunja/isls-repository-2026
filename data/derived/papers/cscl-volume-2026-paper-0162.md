# Orchestrating a Web-Based Classroom Game

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Multi group classroom games create a run time orchestration problem: teachers must coordinate pacing, content visibility, and group progress in real time. We examine this problem through a classroom enactment of Fall of Artica, a role-based inquiry game delivered in WISE. Using group centered audio, photographs, authoring screenshots, and planning notes, we analyzed 31 identifiable episodes across 13 step transitions, 14 post reveal intervals, and 4 whole class checkpoints. Findings show tha t lightweight continue gates and in place media enabled the platform to handle within step pacing and visibility, while teacher intervention clustered around logistics recovery, brief procedural alignment, and concept advancing prompts. Whole class convergence remained teacher led, and externally embedded tools increased local recovery work. These findings suggest that, in role-based classroom inquiry, automation is more effective for pre-structured sequencing and visibility than for interpretive, contingent, and convergence work. We argue that platform support redistributed rather than replaced teacher orchestration, and we derive three design implications for the run time enactment of classroom games.

In multi group classroom games, teachers must coordinate activity flow, timing, and shared attention across several groups at once. Prior CSCL work has described orchestration as the coordination of tools, time, and social interaction in technology rich learning environments (Dillenbourg et al., 2011; Prieto et al., 2015; Roschelle et al., 2013). More recent work has moved beyond defining orchestration as a general classroom challenge and has begun to examine more specifically how orchestration load is distributed across forms of teacher support and technology provision (Amarasinghe et al., 2021; Hakami et al., 2024). As platforms increasingly support sequencing and content delivery, an important question is not simply whether automation helps, but which orchestration tasks become manageable by the platform and which remain teacher dependent during class. We examine this question in a classroom enactment of Fall of Artica (FoA), a role-based inquiry game run in the web-based Inquiry Science Environment (WISE) (Linn et al., 2003; Slotta & Linn, 2009). Recent work on critical action games and related role-based inquiry environments has explored how dystopian scenarios, role structures, and agent-based supports can scaffold collective inquiry and participation (Zhou et al., 2023; Zhou, 2024). Students in our enactment adopted roles and pursued region-specific q uests, while WISE delivered instructions, media, and step-based activity flow. We focus on run time orchestration, meaning what happened during class as groups moved through steps, encountered new content, and periodically reconverged. Our questions were: (1) Which orchestration tasks were handled by the platform duri ng class, and which remained teacher work? (2) Across key classroom episodes, when and how did the teacher intervene?

## Context and methods

The study took place in an undergraduate critical literacies course. Students worked in four table groups through four FoA regional units. WISE provided self-paced step navigation, one continue gate that revealed follow up content after a prerequisite task, in place media, and one embedded chatbot activity. Data sources included group centered table audio, photographs, authoring screenshots, and planning notes.

Episodes were the unit of analysis. We analyzed all identifiable episodes meeting our inclusion criteria in the focal enactment, including 13 step transition episodes, 14 post reveal episodes, and 4 whole class checkpoint episodes. A step transition episode was included when movement into a new step could be identified through teacher cueing, student talk, or corroborating visual artifacts such as photographs or authoring screenshots. A post reveal episode was included when a continue action and the subsequent one-to-two-minute interval could be confirmed through synchronized audio, screenshots, or other classroom records. A whole class checkpoint episode was included when the instructor explicitly announced a moment of class-wide convergence. For each episode, we identified which platform supports were active at run time and classified teacher moves as logistics, procedural alignment, or conceptual prompting. Triangulation across audio, screenshots, photographs, and planning notes was used to confirm episode timing, content visibility, and classroom alignment. 806 Because silent self-paced transitions were not always observable in the available data, the dataset should be understood as including all identifiable episodes meeting our criteria rather than all possible transitions that may have occurred during the lesson.

Across episodes, the platform most reliably handled within step pacing and visibility. Continue gates staged the sequence of actions and reduced repeated teacher reminders about when to move forward. In place images and short media also supported smooth flow because students could access relevant content without switching tools or files.

Teacher work did not disappear. Instead, it shifted toward contingent local support. During step transitions, teacher intervention was usually brief and procedural, helping lagging groups identify the next action or rejoin the current step. During post reveal intervals, teacher talk more often became conceptual, pressing students to interpret new material, connect it to prior notes, or clarify what would count as evidence. These patterns suggest that while the platform could structure routine sequencing, it could not absorb the interpretive work needed to advance inquiry.

Whole class convergence remained teacher led. Although WISE supported group level progression, it did not synchronize stopping points across groups. During the tavern checkpoint, the teacher announced the start and end of the exchange, monitored lagging groups, and provided brief support so all students could participate. External embeds also revealed a boundary condition: the chatbot added useful interactional possibilities, but it introduced startup and recovery demands that increased local logistics support. Taken together, these patterns suggest that platform support was most effective for pre-structured sequencing and visibility, while teacher intervention remained necessary in moments requiring local judgment, interpretation, and class-wide realignment.

The main contribution of this case is not simply that teachers still help during classroom games. Rather, it shows a more specific division of labor under partial automation. Tasks that can be pre-structured in advance, such as within step sequencing and content visibility, are more readily absorbed by the platform. Tasks that require contingent judgment, local recovery, conceptual prompting, or whole class realignment remain teacher dependent. In this sense, automation redistributed rather than replaced teacher orchestration. This interpretation aligns with recent work on orchestration load, which has argued that technology can reduce some aspects of classroom coordination while leaving teachers responsible for managing instructional and social complexity in real time (Amarasin ghe et al., 2021; Hakami et al., 2024). More specifically, our findings suggest that within step sequencing and visibility were more readily absorbed by the platform, whereas conceptual prompting, local recovery, and whole class convergence remained teacher dependent. Relative to recent work on critical action games and role-based inquiry settings (Zhou et al., 2023; Zhou, 2024), the contribution here is not simply to argue that such designs can support engagement or inquiry, but to show how they are orches trated moment by moment across multiple groups during classroom enactment. In this sense, the paper contributes a more fine-grained account of how automation redistributes teacher work in role-based classroom inquiry rather than simply making classroom games easier to run.

First, use minimal gates to stage pacing and visibility. Continue gates reduced routine sequencing talk after reveals and helped groups progress with less teacher prompting. Second, keep media in place and embed externally only when clearly necessary. Inli ne media supported smoother enactment, while external tools introduced startup and recovery work. Third, reserve teacher effort for concept support and brief alignment. Teacher intervention was most valuable when focused on helping students interpret new m aterial, reconnect to the task, or rejoin shared classroom moments.

In this enactment of Fall of Artica, the platform reliably handled within step pacing and content visibility, while teacher intervention clustered around logistics recovery, brief procedural alignment, and concept advancing prompts. Whole class convergence remained teacher led. For CSCL, the significance of this case lies in showing that platform support reorganizes rather than removes teacher orchestration. Effective classroom game design may therefore depend less on maximizing automation than on combining  lightweight platform structure with strategically timed teacher intervention.
