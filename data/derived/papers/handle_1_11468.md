# Critically-Oriented Approaches to Mixed Methods

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This symposium critically examines existing educational research methodologies through the intersection of Quantitative Critical Theory (QuantCrit) and Learning Sciences frameworks. Drawing inspiration from Garcia et al.’s (2018) QuantCrit principles—which emphasize the centrality of racism, the non-neutrality of numbers, and the importance of critically examining research categories—the symposium addresses how quantitative data can reinforce deficit-oriented narratives and underscores the necessity of incorporating and centering marginalized voices. Paper 1 explores how young adolescents from historically minoritized backgrounds engage with and resist artificial intelligence technologies within community contexts. Paper 2 presents findings from a Differential Item Functioning (DIF) analysis of an identity survey in a university biology setting, revealing how survey items may exhibit bias despite intentions toward equity. Paper 3 utilizes (post)qualitative inquiry to deconstruct power dynamics, question normative knowledge production, and center diverse voices, highlighting the co-constitutive relationships between researchers, participants, and environments.

Symposium overview This symposium, "Critically-Oriented Approaches to Mixed Methods in Learning Sc iences," aims to engage critically with the existing methodologies employed  in education research, particularly at the interse ction of QuantCrit and (post)qualitative inquiry. In line with Garcia et al.’s (2018) QuantCrit scholarship, we are inspired by principles that emphasize the centrality of raci sm, the non-neutrality of numbers, and the need for critical examination of the categories used in research. Central to this discussion are principles that recognize that numbers often reinforce deficit-oriented narratives, that voice and experiential in sight are essential for understanding marginalized perspectives, and that relying on one methodological approach may forgo contextual ly grounded understandings of educational phenomena. We conside r these principles to be tenets of critical approac hes to methodology, which push on histories of empiricism that treat data as self-evident by requiring research to consider its assumptions and contexts (Dixon-Román, Jackson, & de Royston, 2020). The papers presented offer insights toward new empirical approaches that illustrate how research can study process. All three papers in this symposium align with Garcia et al.’s (2018) assertion that “voice and insight are vital: data cannot ‘speak for itself’ and critical analyses should be informed by the experiential kno wledge of marginalized groups” (p. 151). The first two unders core the importance of centering marginalized voice s with respect to race in data analysis and the third emph asizes undergraduate facilitator and teacher voices  in relation to researcher’s perspectives. Draw ing on the principle that “voice and insight are vi tal: data cannot ‘speak for itself’”, Paper 3 uses (post)qual itative inquiry to move beyond traditional mixed me thods by deconstructing power dynamics, questioning normativ e knowledge production, and centering diverse voice s. It provides a critically-oriented lens that emphasizes the co-constitutive relationships between researchers, participants, and their environments. Through these three papers, the symposium collectively seeks to amplify the voices of marginalized groups and challenge the status quo of quantitative assessment. Paper 1: Mixing QuantCrit and critical conversational narrative analysis to learn about adolescents’ resistant capital across three artificial intelligence camps David W. Jackson, Stacy Scheuneman, Da’Von  L. McCune, Kellyann Ramdath, Finn Goehrig, Ifeoma N wogu, Noemi Waight

## Purpose and theoretical framework

Young adolescents’ voices have often been ignored b y adults in dominant discourses about artificial in telligence (AI), despite youths’ experiences with – and resistance of – AI through commercialized tools. Even with their resistance, young adolescents are  often dehumanized by AI, having their dignity and m ental health harmed through mechanisms such as social media algorithms and copyright predation. Asset-and aspiration-based approaches like community cultural wealth (Yosso, 2005) show promise for addressing and redressing inequities and inequalities related to AI. Though much scholar ship has been published in grades 13-20 education, there remains a need for more research in K-12 settings, especially with students historically minoritized in computing broadly and AI in particular.

Our overall work applies the methodology of social design-based experiments (SDBEs), iteratively working as a collective of community stakeholders towards utopian possibilities (Gutiérrez et al., 2020; Gutiérrez & Jurow, 2016). We call our collective “TEAM-AI”, or Training, Envisioning, Action, and Mentorin g with AI. Interventions include three vacation “training-camps”, with 35 total youth participating across Ap ril 2023, February 2024, and April 2024. In each training-camp, youth studied current AI topics, then developed their own apps through Scratch, code.org, or Microsoft MakeCo de. The present study engages Black and Brown and N ew Americans in the US Rust Belt with community-focused, student-led, action-oriented problem solving. We organize around the research questions, (1) During the TEAM-AI initiative, how do middle-school and high-school youth (re -)apply their knowledges and skills of resistant cap ital?; (2) What impact does the TEAM-AI initiative have on youths' AI concepts, perspective s, and practi ces?; and (3) What connections, if any, exist between resistant capital and AI concepts, practices, and perspectives?

## Methods and data sources

Along with community cultural wealth, our conceptua l framework includes QuantCrit (Garcia et al., 2018) and Impact.AI (Williams, 2024). Our research design is parallel mixed-methods for convergence, generating qualitative and quantitative data in simultane ous strands that support deeper understandings of y ouths’ lived stories with AI. Amongst other benefits, the delibe rate juxtaposition of methods helps us evaluate the  extent to which our quantitative items in fact do not “serve white racial interests” (Garcia et al., 2018, p. 151), regardless of how the items performed in other times and places.

We analyzed qualitative data per critical narrative  analysis (Souto-Manning, 2014), with deductive coding per our conceptual framework and inductive c oding per independent work and group discussions. D ata sources for qualitative analyses were work artifact s, 18 semi-structured youth interviews, daily field notes, analytical memos, and two family focus groups. The interviews were between 18 youth participants and t wo graduate research assistants who were actively engaging with youth throughout the interventions. Eight participant interviews were coded for resistant capital by two coders, and consensus was reached for codes through in-depth discussion. The remaining 10 interviews were solo-coded. Per sampling for intensity and maximum varia tion, four focal youth participants were selected: one Af rican American high-school boy (he/him), one South-Asian American high-school girl (she/her), one East-Asian American middle-school boy (he/him), and one Mexican American middle-school girl (she/her/they/them).

The quantitative analyses are based on pre-and post-surveys, building upon a seven-item subscale on resistant capital extended from Sablan (2019). Based on pilot testing with graduate students, we added three new items to unpack racial and ethnic discrimi nation from discrimination broadly construed. The n ew items were, “I believe I will be able to make a difference in my racial/ethnic/cultural community”; “I believe there are injustices in society”; and “I believe I have faced discrimina tion in society, specifically due to my race and et hnicity”. Our revised subscale had acceptable reliability for a y ounger and more diverse population than the origina l scale was developed with (Cronbach’s α between 0.8 and 0.9 for pre-and post-surveys). To test the assumption of normality for t-tests of means (N = 19), we conducted a Shapiro-Wilk test, for which significance was much greater than.05 for both pre-and post-tests (.63 and.58, respectively) indicating the no rmality assumption was met. Daily exit-ticket items were aligned with AI concepts, practices, and perspectives per Williams (2024).

## Results and conclusions

Paired-sample t-tests of means show a medium effect size for increa sing resistant capital during the five-day training-camps (Cohen’s d=.606, p=.017). Looking at individual items, the greatest increases were in communitylevel matters, with decreases in society-level matters. Exit tickets showed statistically significant increases at the α<.05 level for AI practices, yet no statistically significant changes for AI concepts or perspectives. Those quantitative trends are supported by qualitative findings, as youth and caregivers often identif ied challenges and opportunities at the neighborhood-and city-levels, yet less frequently highlighted country-or world-level issues. One area of consensus was data privac y, with many participants expressing concern over surveillance cameras and cloud-based photos.

In addition to ethical concerns of racial and ethni c bias more directly aligned with community cultura l wealth and QuantCrit, youth spoke of (in)equity rel ated to intersectional markers of gender and social  class. Exploration of these topics was supported by studen t engagement in emotional, behavioral, cognitive, a nd social ways, including access to text-based coding not provided in local public schools, and also increased interactions between middle-and high-school students.

The four focal youths not only demonstrated a nuanced understanding of the potential benefits and harms of the growing presence of AI in society at large, but they also developed and took stances that demonstrate caring towards their communities (e.g., public transportation, plastic use, music choices, and food access). They utilized their experiences prior to their awareness of AI to make sense of these emerging issues, and drew from their stores of resistant capital to envision ways to address in justices at both macro (societal) and micro (communities/neighborhoods) scales.

## Scholarly significance

On a practical level, our results to date provide counter-narratives to dominant discourses around AI and its past, present, and potential implications for social inequity and inequality. They have implications for design of learning environments with young adolescents and AI, which t hus far have been mostly out-of-school-time yet are increasing access by being incorporated into in-school-time activities. In conceptual ways, we contribute to QuantCrit as a  field and community cultural wealth scales in particular, by including younger and/or more ethnic ally and racially diverse populations than many pre vious studies. Most specifically, the addition of three items to the subscale by Sablan (2019) refines our understandings of resistance related to race and ethnicity. These quantitative contributions are supported by qualita tive findings that afford more nuance, in the spirit of what Garc ia and Mayorga (2018) c all critical race transformative convergent mixed methods, reflexively interrogating the equity of scales that we use. Despite our efforts to converge a rich set of quali tative and quantitative data, our study is nonethel ess limited in transferability and generalizability by a relatively small sample size, specific sociocultural dynamics in the local context, and inherent differences between out-of-school-time and in-school-time learning. Future work could use Bayesian statistics to provide more nuance than frequentist statistics, especially for smaller sample sizes often overlooked, or for subcategories subsumed int o larger categories that invite ecological bias. Our resul ts to date could serve as prior probabilities for such Ba yesian approaches. Also, in-school-time interventions could increase equitable access to AI learning experience s, amplifying the voices of young adolescents towar ds the humanization of each and all participants in local and global communities and societies. Paper 2: A critically-oriented approach to exploring assessment bias when measuring student identity in college STEM contexts Fan Huang, Andrew Estrada Phuong, Stanley M. Lo Purpose and a QuantCrit perspective This study examines biases in assessing science ide ntity among college students, focusing on racial and ethnic disparities. Since STEM education seeks to support diverse student populations and broaden participati on, ensuring that the surveys used to measure science identity are valid and equitable across different groups is crucial. This study critically examines these measures throu gh the perspective of QuantCrit, an extension of cr itical race theory into quantitative research, and applies Differential Item Functioning (DIF) analysis to detect bias in survey items. DIF is an analytical approach used in educat ional and psychological testing to identify items t hat function differently for distinct groups of respondents with the same overall level of the underlying trait (e.g., competency level, agreeability) based on other responses in the measure. When an item shows significant DIF, it suggests that respondents from one group may find the item system atically easier or harder compared to respondents f rom another group, potentially introducing bias in the measurement of the construct. We focused on racial and ethnic differences because race remains a significant factor in shaping students’ experiences in STEM education. Historical and syste mic inequities have disproportionately affected Bla ck, Hispanic, and other minoritized students, often influencing their sense of belonging, recognition, and engagement in STEM fields (Phuong et al., 2017; 2022; 2023; We ston, 2019). By examining potential biases in the s urvey items, we aim to uncover whether these tools adequately reflect the diverse ways in which students construct their science identities, or whether they unintentionally  perpetuate cultural biases that disadvantage under represented groups.

In this study, we conduct a DIF analysis on a surve y adapted for a university biology context, drawing on Hosbein and Barbera’s (2020) Survey of Chemistry Identity. They conceptualize science identity through three dimensions: recognition, competence, and performance. The survey in this study examines how students perceive themselves as science learners, focusing on maintai ned interests, relevance, and sense of belonging in  the field. Although prior research showed the adapted survey demonstrated good reliability, it is essential to examine if the instrument functions equitably across diverse student subgroups. We apply a QuantCrit perspective, grounded in critical race theory, to examine whether the survey items can exhibit assessment bias even when they are reli able and designed with equity and inclusivity in mi nd. We focus on the QuantCrit principle that “numbers are not neutral and they should be interrogated for the ir role in promoting deficit analyses that serve white racial interests” (Garcia et al., 2018, p. 151). Evidence of potential bias in specific survey items highlights how number s can perpetuate ine quities, particularly against minoritized students. In service of this goal, we explore the following research question: Based on results from DIF, does the adapted science identity survey exhibit racial bias?

By investigating these biases, this study explores the inherent limitations of quantitative tools and the importance of critically examining statistical anal yses. In a struggle for social justice, we therefor e leverage critical statistical analysis to help  identify and address biases, thereby promoting more  equitable surveys in educational environments.

## Methods and data sources

3,476 students responded to the survey during the 2 021-2022 academic year. Respondents were enrolled in introductory biology or upper-division genetics courses at a large public univers ity. The sample was diverse in terms of race, ethnicity, gender, and fi rst-generation college status. This diversity enabled u s to conduct a DIF analysis of potential biases in the survey items across different student subgroups. The survey was administered via Qualtrics during re gular class sessions in large-enrollment biology courses, using the course management system for dis tribution. The survey items used a six-point Likert scale (1 = Strongly Disagree, 6 = Strongly Agree). The survey items below were included in the analysi s and appear to focus on interests and relevance as part of identity: MIV1: What we are studying in this course is useful for me to know. MIV2: The things we are studying in this course are important to me. MIV3: What we are learning in this course is important for my future goals. MIV4: What we are learning in this course can be applied to real life. MIF1: What we are learning in this course is fascinating to me. MIF2: I really enjoy the biology material we cover in this course. MIF3: I am excited about what we are learning in this course. MIF4R: To be honest, I do not find the biology material we cover in this course interesting. DIF occurs when individuals from different groups (e.g., based on gender, ethnicity, or language) with the same underlying ability or trait level (determi ned by their overall performance on the rest of the  test) have significantly different probabilities of responding  correctly to a particular test item. DIF indicates  potential item bias, where the item may favor one group over another, unrelated to the trait being measured. DIF analysis allows researchers to identify and address these biases, e nsuring that  the assessment tools function equitably across diverse populations (Linacre, 2023).

We employed DIF analysis to identify potential bias es in the survey items. DIF analysis examines whether students from different racial and ethnic groups with the same level of underlying science identity respond differently to specific survey items. We applied a DIF contrast absolute value of 0.426 or hig her, following Paek and Wilson’s (2011) criteria for practical significance, along with a p-value below 0.05 for statistical significance.

This suggests that Hispanic students may perceive a stronger connection between the course content and real-life applications, possibly due to cultural relevan ce or alignment with their everyday experiences. On the other hand, the lower agreement  among Black students may reflect a perception that  the course content is less applicable to their lived experiences or future career contexts, possibly revealing a potential gap in the cultural inclusiveness of the course material. From a QuantCrit perspective, these differences challenge the assumption that standardized survey items are culturally neutral as the numbers and data are not neutral (Garcia et al., 2018). Instead, the obs erved DIF indicates that students from different racial and e thnic backgrounds may interpret the items based on their own cultural contexts, experiences, and expectations. The survey items, designed with a dominant cultural framework, might inadvertently privilege certain groups while marginalizing others. This highlights the need for culturally responsive measurement tools that can better captur e the diverse ways in which students from various backgrounds develop and express their science identity. By noticing, acknowledging, and addressing these biases, educators and re searchers can work towards more equitable assessmen t practices in STEM education, ensuring that all students’ identities and experiences are accurately represented. We greatly respect Hosbein and Barbera’s (2020) wor k and do not claim that their survey is biased. Instead, we found possible evidence of bias in an a dapted version of the survey where items appear to measure interest and relevance as administered in a dif ferent context. These findings may be influenced by  contextual factors such as course pedagogy or university envir onments that shape how different student subgroups engage with the survey. Taking a QuantCrit perspective, we  acknowledge that this DIF analysis may not fully c apture these contextual factors, reminding us that data and certain statistical analyses may not fully accoun t for social and educational contexts and should not always be generalized as universal “truth”.

## Scholarly significance

Recommendations from a QuantCrit and Learning Scien ces Perspective. Based on the findings of the DIF analysis, several recommendations are proposed to i mprove the cultural inclusivity and relevance of th e survey items. First, refining the language of items like M IV4 to clarify what ‘real life’ entails is crucial for making the questions more culturally inclusive. From a QuantCrit perspective, using terms and scenarios that are meaningful across cultural contexts can help reduce bias, whil e incorporating cul turally diverse examples can enhance relevance and engagement, as suggested by Learning Scientists (Galindo et al., 2022; Phuong et al., 20 23). Second, employing mixed-methods validation, such as combining quantitative findings with qualitative feedback from think-alouds, would provide a deeper understanding of how  different racial groups interpret survey items. Finally, we recommended engaging diverse participan ts in survey design to ensure that items reflect mu ltiple perspectives. Involving individuals from various racial and cultural backgrounds can promote inclusivity in survey design and more equitable measurement when examining constructs like Science Identity. Paper 3: A critically-oriented approach to design-based research: Diffractive analysis toward understanding learning Ashley Quiterio, Megan Butler, Marcelo Worsley

## Purpose and context

Inspired by Karen Barad’s (1995, 1996) writings, we apply (post)qualitative inquiry through diffractive analysis. Based on Barad’s thinking on material discursive intra-activity, we explore a design-based implementation of an eight-week curriculum combining sports and computer science. In this way, we connect (post)qualitative inquiry with the context of design-based implementation research to better understand the multiplicity of ways that learning happens within the enactment of a design. We position this diffractive analysis as supporting openness to emergent methodologies that are neither qualitative nor quantitative, but rather beyond the binary. Our diffractive analysis draws on Lenz Taguchi to understand how to read data, such that new realities become possible by going beyond reflective practices of data (2012). This non-representational lens aligns with critically-oriented mixed methods by moving beyond reflexivity (Østern et al., 2023), to a process of escape from taken-for-granted normalized thinking aimed at transformation and change (Deleuze & Guattari, 1987). This approach is especially important for design-based implementation research, because it considers  the role of observation in research design by reframing the significance of interaction s between researchers, participants, and the learning environment (Østern et al., 2023). By doing so, it engages deeply with material, affective, and discursive elements, which expands the scope of mixed methods approaches  beyond mere triangulation to explor e richer, more contextually grounded understandings of educational phenomena. There are ongoing mentions of a new empiricism, which is “the study not of what is but of its becoming” (Dixon-Román et al., 2020, p. 326). This work is an attemp t to extend the conversation of empiricism by highlighting the need for new approaches to answer emerging questions within design implementations. O ur design-based implementation research (DBIR) project follow s the principles of this method, which are “a focus on persistent problems of practice from m ultiple stakeholders’ perspectives”, “a commitment to iterative, collaborative design”, “a concern with developing t heory and knowledge related to both classroom learn ing and implementation through systematic inquiry”, and “a concern with developing capacity for sustaining cha nge in systems” (Fishman et al., 2013). The curriculum was  implemented over 8 weeks in the spring of 2024 acr oss a few 5th grade classrooms within a Midwestern US school district. We analyze the  multiple perspectives of participants as they interact to co-facilitate and learn about the intersection of sports and computer science. The instantiating motivation of the project is to promote culturally sustaining learning opport unities around sports to support learning about computing. We do this  by having learners design their own sports technologies. A core consideration of this design is that mostl y fine arts teachers and librarians implement the curriculum since  there is a lack of computer science teachers within  our neighboring pre-K-8th public school district. In response to this, our design includes support from undergraduate computer science student volunteers to co-facilitate the material with teachers. This design reflects the current educational system as it tries to manage calls for CS4All. We consider through our analysis how we might sustain interdisciplinary learning with computer science within schools. In our collaborative implem entations, multiple layers of power dy namics exist between the students, teachers, administrators, designers, and researchers. Acknowledging the relationships between these groups is important for approaching research as it shapes the narrative of the experience. And, we would like to move beyond acknowledgement; our aim is to explore how we can expand data analysis in this context thr ough (post)qualitative inquiry by addressing how power exists everywhere and in everything through the experience of the design (St. Pierre, 2014). Below we describe our application of (post)qualitative in quiry to diffract the processes of relationships between school, disciplinary content, and inter-and intra-personal structures. Process Our approach to put these theories into practice extends a (post)qualitative inquiry approach through a diffractive reading of our data from an in-school implementation of a sports and computing cur riculum. Our data includes undergraduate volunteers’ debriefs, wherein they describe their experiences in the  classroom each week, and the subsequent interviews with the undergraduate volunt eers and teachers. We printed and assembled each we ek’s debrief in a somewhat linear fashion according to t he teacher and volunteer pairing across the eight w eeks of the program, where one page contained information on one session (reference Figure 1). The combined pages looked like a timeline, where we could read across volunte er perspectives for each teacher. We annotated this  data with a focus on pedagogical decisions made by either tea chers or volunteers, the process or flow of the roo m, and affect. Diffraction comes in as we map the effect of diff erences across process, affect, and pedagogical dec ision making within our dat a (Lenz Taguchi, 2012). After our annotation sessions, we s pent time exploring the documents together and discussed our sense-making and how we might adapt the curriculum given the information we gathered from our reading on world-making, curriculum-becoming, and speculation. Noticings From this process, we find multiple forms of learning within the implementation that are beyond the 5th grader’s learning about computing programing with sports. Learning occurs as people build relationships with each other, such as the volunteers learning about elementary school structures and the 5th graders learning about design and ideation through the process of their projects. We examine the multiplicity of learning processes that  are continuously ongoing through the implementation wit hin the debriefs. For instance, in Figure 1, one vo lunteer answers how they thought a student was feeling within week 5 of the curriculum, and they wrote that the student “…did not feel the need to complete the ideation document, so I thought if they already had an idea in their head and were eager to get star ted—they should do so !”. The volunteer’s comment illustrates a recogniti on of school structures, where children are not always able to m ake their own decisions within the classroom, and t hey are learning about the ways that they can shift these structures through the pedagogical decisions they make when in the room as co-facilitators with teachers. In the following week, this volunteer continued to make decisions that showed their values as they entered the space, and they described more of the ideation pro cess of 5th grade students. They made sure to “be flexible with them and simply allow them to create!... even if some of  them [projects] were not related to sports at all”. From conversations with the volunteers and this description, we gained insight into the design specific learning of the 5th graders, where they each had their own creative process as they made sense of the design process regardless of its connection to sports. Learning about design also showed up as the volunteers learned from the teachers about adapting curriculum as they modified the plan to meet their context and classroom needs. Through our analysis of the process, affect, and pedagogical decisions referenced within the debriefs, we have a better understanding of the many forms of learning that are continuously occurring within the implementation of a specific designed curriculum.

## Scholarly significance

This study presents another form of (post)qualitative inquiry in design-based research. Similar to recent examples, we find that research methods offer insights into specific narratives and forgo others within design-based research (Gouvea & Gravel, 2024). (Post)qualitative methods in design-based implementation research offer new perspectives into the process of folks as they expe rience and adapt the design of the curriculum. We c onsider DBIR as an investigation beyond the production of s tudent learning, and we highlight the process of multiple people’s experiences with the design. The analyses that we offer in this paper are diffra ctive data-creation experiments. As such, they are just one of the infinite ways we might have dealt with this data, and it shows how “voice and insight are vital: data cannot ‘speak for itself’” (Garcia et al., 2018, p. 151). We apply this to highlight volunteer and teacher perspectives as they make the  essential and ongoing learning within design-based implementations, and they offer views into student experiential learning that is embedded and often si delined when researching design implementations.
