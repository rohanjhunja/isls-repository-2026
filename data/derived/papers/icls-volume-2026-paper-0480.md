# Games of Representation: Making the Abstract Tangible through Data Representation with Card- Based Games for Understanding AI Ethics Education

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Middle school students notice that an image search for "physicist" returns predominantly white male faces, but when asked "Who is represented? Who is left out?" they struggle to respond. This reveals a critical gap in AI literacy education: youth lack understanding of representation and the complex interchange between societal patterns and AI systems. Games of Representation (GR) addresses this gap through card-based games that make abstract concepts of dataset representation tangible. Aligned with the Kap or Foundation's Responsible AI and Tech Justice Guide, the design engages players as stakeholders who curate datasets using SET cards. This paper contributes two design principles: (1) scaffolding from individual to system-level understanding of how bias e merges in data, (2) connecting mathematical representation (statistical distributions) to social representation (who gets included or excluded). These principles offer guidance for developing educational responses to emerging technologies where technical and social dimensions are inseparable.

Understanding the socio-technical nature of AI has long been a motivating rationale for AI literacy education. Unlike many other technologies, AI has permeated almost every aspect of daily life and work, therefore, it is essential for individuals to understand how social factors shape technical artifacts and how those artifacts, in turn, produce social impacts and consequences. This need becomes especially critical when young learners grapple with how representation in datasets translates to bias in algorithmic predictions In our work on the Everyday AI project—a study of how to advance teacher and student AI literacy in formal middle school classrooms (Lee et al., 2021, 2022; Moore et al., 2023a, 2023b; Zhang et al., 2023a, 2023b) —we observed that students could often identify disparities in AI outputs —recognizing, for example, that image search results are skewed towards more prevalent images —but struggled to understand how dataset composition produced these biased AI outcomes. Without grasping how datasets  are constructed as social artifacts shaped by stakeholder decisions, institutional priorities, and existing inequities, students remain limited in their capacity to reason about AI systems' implications or imagine alternative approaches to building them. The Games of Representation (GR) project emerged from this pedagogical challenge through a collaboration between curriculum developers and technology justice advocates. The partnership with the Kapor Foundation, whose Responsible AI and Tech Justice Guide articulates core competencies (CCs) for equitable AI education, informed the direction of the design work. This partnership shaped the project's emphasis on three interconnected CCs: 1) examine the AI technology creation ecosystem (CC#1), 2) build a critic al lens in the collection, usage, analysis, interpretation, and reporting of data (CC#5), and 3) minimize, mitigate, and eliminate harm and injustice caused by AI technologies (CC#6). The collaboration exemplifies how curriculum design can benefit from bri nging together complementary forms of expertise particularly when addressing complex sociotechnical issues where no single perspective is sufficient. The Games of Representation Games of Representation (GR) is a curricular innovation that addresses these conceptual gaps. The design employs card-based games that make abstract concepts of dataset representation tangible, allowing students to physically manipulate, curate, and analyze datasets using the familiar and widely accessible SET cards  (1). GR consists of three sequential games that progress from basic to complex concepts. In Game 1, Investigating Samples, players collectively draw 16 cards, place them in a 4x4 matrix as a sample dataset, and discuss what features are represented and underrepresented (e.g., color, shape) in the sample when compared to the full set of cards, the full population. This game focuses on foundational statistical concepts 2868 presented through collaborative, non-competitive exploration. Students develop vocabulary, practice counting and categorizing, and build initial intuitions about samples and populations. Game 2, Competing Interests, maintains the card manipulation from Game 1, but adds strategic thinking and social dynamics through competitive gameplay. Each turn, players draw a card from the deck and can either

a) add it to the dataset (growing the sample size), b) replace a card in the sample, or c) discard it. Players must

reason about their own goals for representation in the same, anticipate others' moves, and navigate emergent collective outcomes to explore how easy it is for stakeholders to manipulat e a dataset. Game 3, Real-World Scenarios, calls for players to apply the rules of Game 2 to the sample dataset that emerged from Game 2 gameplay, now in the context of real-world AI training scenarios. Thus, Game 3 offers transfer learning from card manipulations to real-world contexts. Players engage in analogical reasoning (mapping card features to human traits), ethical reasoning (determining whether representation is beneficial or harmful in specific contexts), and systems thinking (considering consequences of data set composition). This sequence provides a scaffolded introduction to statistical and ethical reasoning aligned with the Kapor Foundation’s Responsible AI and Tech Justice Guide. Statistical reasoning develops from basic counting (Game 1: "How many red cards are there?") to  comparative analysis (Game 2: "How did the distribution change from the starting dataset to the ending dataset?") to proportional reasoning and prediction (Game 3: "If this dataset is used to build an AI, which groups might be recognized accurately and which might not?"). Ethical reasoning develops from individual perspective (Game 1: minimal ethical content, focus on observation) to interpersonal dynamics (Game 2: competing interests, strategic conflicts) to societal implications (Game 3: justice, equity, harm mitigation, data sovereignty).

Thus all three games combined achieve the target core competencies from the Responsible AI and Tech Justice Guide by offering players opportunities to examine a simulation of an AI technology creation ecosystem (CC#1), practice critical thinking about how the data was collected and used for AI model development (CC#5), and how dataset manipulation can lead to harm or the mitigation of harms caused by AI technologies (CC#6). A central design consideration was to increase AI literacy by reducing barriers to access to AI literacy concepts.The unplugged nature of SET cards reduces the need for connectivity, which remains a limited resource in many regions within the United States. By designing games that use physical cards, GR can be played anywhere—classrooms, libraries, community centers, homes —without technology access. This expands the potential reach of AI literacy education to communities often excluded from technology educa tion initiatives.

## Design principles and conclusion

GR addresses a gap identified by our design team during our AI literacy education work in middle school classrooms, wherein adolescents demonstrate the capacity to identify biased algorithmic outcomes, yet struggle to understand how datasets are constructed as social artifacts, how representation shapes system performance, and why bias emerges from socio-technical processes. GR exemplifies the following design principles for making the

## abstract, ethical, and socio-technical elements of AI tangible

Scaffold from Individual to System-Level Understanding: A common misconception about bias is that it results from individual prejudice —a "bad actor" with discriminatory intent. While individual prejudice exists, systemic bias in AI systems often emerges fr om structural factors. Understanding systemic bias requires a conceptual shift from individual-level to systems-level thinking. The games progress from personal experience (Game 1: "What do I see in this sample?") to interpersonal dynamics (Game 2: "How do our different goals affect the dataset?") to systemic analysis (Game 3: "What are the societal implications?"). This scaffolding helps players understand bias as emerging from systems, not just individual prejudices, positioning them to better understand the process of AI technology creation (and the bias that emerges) as an ecosystem (CC#1). Connect Mathematical Representation to Social Representation: The word "representation" carries dual meaning in AI systems. In statistical terms, representation describes how features are distributed in a sample relative to a population —a mathematical concept about frequencies and proportions. In social terms, representation describes who is included or excluded, who is seen or rendered invisible, who has voice or is silenced. The games explicitly bridge statistical concepts (how features are distributed in  a sample) with social concepts (who gets included or excluded). This connection helps players understand representation as both a technical and social phenomenon.
