# “This World is a University”: Farmers’ Epistemic Agency in Climate Change Adaptation

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Climate change poses significant challenges to agriculture, including increased vulnerability to extreme weather, crop disruptions, and reduced productivity. Agricultural extension systems aim to support farmers to adapt and respond to climate change through knowledge transfer and capacity-building. However, research suggests that farmers engage in multiple and distinct ways of knowing and often rely on informal learning to inform their adaptive practices (Berkes 2009; Anh Tran, 2020). Grounded in interviews with farmers in Jamaica this study examines farmers’ epistemic agency and the ways in which they integrate new ideas into their farming practices. Our preliminary analysis indicated that farmers don’t tend to separate learning from living and they seek out new information with the goal of solving problems in their everyday practice. However, we also observed that farmers experience epistemic tensions and attempt to address those tensions in context-specific ways. The study has implications for researching farmers’ ways of knowing and designing relevant learning environments in the context of agricultural extension and climate change discourse

## Introduction

Climate change poses a significant threat to global agriculture  by increasing farmers’ vulnerability to extreme weather and disrupting crop growth cond itions (Mahato, 2014). Smallholder farmers, especially in Small Island Developing States (SIDS), face intensifying natural hazards which directly threaten agricultural productivity and food security (Thomas et al., 2020). Wens et al. (2022) emphasize that training and exte nsion services can boost farmers' uptake of adaptation measures, increasing their resilience  to climate change. As such, governments and intergovernmental organizations are making significant investments to strengthen the adaptive capacity of farmers (Tomlinson & Rhiney, 2018).

Farmers’ agency in their learning and their processes of accepting or resisting new information is critical in understanding their adaptive decisions. Learning is often conceptualized as an intentional effort and process that happens in a formal context with the goal of understanding new informat ion presented by a  teacher. This concept is mirrored in models of agricultural extension that focus on technology transfer, with one-way, top-down flows of knowledge from ‘experts’ to farmers in which, farmers lived experience, existing knowledge and ways of knowing are rarely considered (Cook et al, 2021). Although researchers have identified a range of knowledge sources that farmers draw upon, there is a paucity of resea rch on how farmers seek out information and navigate tensions between distinct ways of knowing to inform their adoption of new practices. Understanding farmers’ epistemic agency has implications for learning design in the agricultural sector-for the practices and technologies that are introduced, but  also for the way they are positioned i n reference to  farmers’ existing knowledge, lived experiences and ways of knowing. This study examines how farmers in Jamaica exercise epistemic agency in regard to their adaptive practices in the context of climate change. We consider the agentic role of farmers in their own learning and the various – sometimes conflicting – ways of knowing that farmers draw upon to inform th eir practice Contextual and conceptual background Agricultural production in Jamaica is susceptible to a range of climate-related impacts, including soil erosion, droughts, floods, hurricanes, and pest and disease outbreaks (Tomlinson & Rhiney, 2018). Farmers in this study often discussed the challenges of climate change in relation to their learning. For example, several farmers shared the need to adapt to  new pests and disease because of  changes in weather patterns. Ms. D. commented on th e importance of learning about  pest management: “Cause most of the time when you plant – because bugs and things eat it up, but right now, you have to  know how to plant it and how to care for the, the p lants thing”. Experiences with extreme weather were also raised b y participants. The data collection  for this study  spanned approximately three months, including a period before and after Hurricane Beryl-a category 4 hurricane – hit the island on July 3, 2024. Mr. B shared that he lost a large piece of his land in a landslide while sustaining significant damage to his crops because of the hurricane: “Hurricane came, in fact, up to three pieces of land that slipped out, one piece about probably 500 feet or more…it took away a whole lot of my yam that I was to plant”. The impacts of climate change were positioned by farmers as sig nificant challenges that often motivated them to  learn. To address the severe and increasing impacts of climate change on smallholder farmers, extension services in Jamaica encourage farmers to adopt a range of climate change-focused practice s (RADA, 2023). Several large-scale climate change adaptation projects in Jamaica have been funded by international agencies, signaling it as a global priority (RADA, 2023). Given the considerable investment by national and  global actors in building smallholder farmers’ adaptive capacity in the context of climate change, understanding farmers’ learning processes and agency in learning is crucial.

Epistemic agency refers to the capacity for individuals to take ownership of their learning (Damsa et al,

2010) and their management of learning resources to create and advance knowledge (Smith, 2017). It is an active

and productive process in which the learner generates and/or uses new knowledge (Damsa et al, 2010; Miller et al., 2018). Agency often emerges in response to complex or problema tic contexts through adoption of new practices or adaptation of existing practices  (Emirbayer & Mische,1998). E pistemic agency is an appropriate framework for understanding how farmers adapt to climate change given their constantly changing environment. In this study, we understand epistemic agency to inclu de farmers’ choices to use (or not use) new practices and technologies to address climate-related challenges. This framework offers a useful lens for understanding farmers’ learning and adaptive practices given the  various resources and knowledge brokers within the ir sector, and the fact that traditional knowledge may-at times-conflict with knowledge  shared by experts or other knowledge brokers (Kaup, 2008; Munyua & Stilwell, 2013). We present the concept of ‘epistemic tension’ to descr ibe a learner’s perceived conflict between disparate information, advice or knowledge ascertained through distinct ways of knowing, and/or the values associated with them. We examine the ways in which farmers in Jamaica navigate epistemic tensions, to address climate-related challenge and adopt appropriate interventions. We consider the agentic role of farmers in their learning and the various – sometimes conflicting – ways of knowing that they draw upon to inform their agricultural practices.

## Methods

The interviews – conducted as part of a doctoral thesis research project-focused on a cohort of 18 farmers within the Mount Salus community in St. Andrew’s, Jamaica. Support for identifying and connecting with the far mers’ group was provided by the Rural Agricultural Develo pment Authority (RADA) who also provided access to key documents. In-depth semi-structured interviews were conducted with the participants, includ ing farmers from Mount Salus and the neighboring communities referred by other farmers. The interviews aimed to understand how farmers learn about farming, address practical challenges, and improve their practices. On average, interviews lasted 40 minutes, and were transcribed. We used open-coding with an iterative process (Strauss & Corbin, 1990). The principal researcher also participated in and o bserved multiple events and meetings. M emos from these observations were used to explicate farmers’ perceptions/experiences of cl imate change-related challenges and their learning.

## Findings

While learning in the agricultural sector is often conceptualized as a vertical, expert-driven process, th is study reveals that farmers develop expertise informally, through daily experiences and interactions. Their learning originates from problems of practice, which they se ek to solve through various strategies-evidence of their adaptive expertise. They navigate epistemic tensions that occur when new inf ormation or practices conflict with their existing values or understandings, and exhibit epistemic agency-often relying on experiential and traditional knowledge-when making decisions for climate adaptation. Learning as living For many farmers in this study, learning is understood as their direct, lived experiences and is conceptualized as innate. Mr. T’s response to the question of how he learns a bout farming beautifully illustrates this: “How do I learn about it? It's like asking, … how do I know that I'm thirsty? It's like, how do I  know that I'm hungry? …It comes informally, naturally… You live in the environment, you have to take care o f the environment”. Learning is described as unstructured and an ever-present feature of his life, so intertwined with his day-to-day ‘living’ that it is indiscernible as a distinct or separate proce ss. He also points to a sense of stewardship for the env ironment that has been inculcated in him, relating that learning is not ju st the acquisition of skills but also  a process that cultivates his core values and identity as a farmer.

Learning as living continues beyond childhood as farmers construct kno wledge through their daily interactions with community  members and their natural environment. Mr. D. describes his conceptua lization of learning in which the environment and everyone in it is seen as holding knowledge: “I tell you what, this world is a university. I believe in you telling me what you know, and I telling you what I know, and we can mov e ahead like that …. So, that's the way it should be. Everybody learns from everybody. No one person knows everything”. This conceptualization challenges the notion of lea rning as a top-down process of knowledge transfer, wherein the teacher or the instructor is the only expert. It values mutuality in learning and knowledge sharing. Problems of practice and adaptive expertise While farmers gain and construct knowledge through learning as living, they also intentionally draw upon various ways of knowing and sources of information in response to emerging and novel challenges. Adaptive expertise is defined by an individual’s ability to  tap into interconnected knowledge networks to solve  problems through innovative means (Bransford et al, 2000). The knowl edge representations that adaptive experts hold are flexible and fluid, allowing them to respond to unfamiliar situations (Schwartz,  Bransford & Sears, 2005). Adaptive expertise does not refer to the static possession of knowledge or a know-it-all mentality by experts; rather, it is an active process through which farmers identify challenges, determine their blind spots, and seek information from various sources.

Farmers’ adaptive expertise often originates from problems of practice, wherein they are motivated to develop or identify solutions to concrete challenges.  Ms. D discussed how her identification of new/unknown pests would lead her to reach out for advice: “You go to inform  [Chemical Company Representative], or you’re calling RADA and you suggest the problem to them, a nd they will tell you say, alright insects, the ins ect spray, you get it, you can buy.”  Ms. D first had to identify the presence of a pest  and recognize her lack of knowledge. She then decides to reach out to the ‘formal experts ’ in her network for advice. Mr. M shared a similar approach of contacting ‘formal experts’ when establishing a new crop: “I go to the Jamaica Agricultural Society, you have a lot of team members. Those guys are advanced. So, I could just call them up and say ‘Please man, how you think about this?’ And they would all give me a different perspective on how to approach that problem.” Like Ms. D, he has already identified the problem and  is seeking advice. Interestingly, he is looking for  a perspective different from his own, suggesting not only  that he is open to information that may push the boundaries of  his current knowledge and practice, but  that he actively seeks it. This active,  self-initiated knowledge-seeking behaviour, involving the identification of a specific problem, is evidence of adaptive expertise in action. Farmers as scientists Though scientists are often conceptualized as degre e-holding, formally-trained experts in a scientific field, there is an emerging body of literature that explores informal scientific processes, including farmers as ‘citizen scientists’ who collaborate with formal institutions to conduct research (vande Gevel et,al, 2020).  This study reveals that even in the absence of the formal structures of ‘citizen science’, farmers frequently engage in scientific processes. Independent research using secondary sources, consul tation with experts, and experimentation feature prominently in farmers’ learning. Mr. J described his process of learning about goat farming: “I did research on it, that technical support from the various agencie s, and over time, just by doing it, take time and d evelop the principles behind it, and still introducing it to t he system to see how it works ”. His process exhibits features of adaptive expertise, in that he seeks information from various sources, but it also reflects a scientific process – the generation of a hypothesis based on secondary resea rch and consultation with other experts, and the su bsequent testing of that hypothesis. He relates how he gener ates new knowledge representations “develop[ing] th e principles”, based on his interpretation of the ‘experiment’. Mr. M draws upon various knowledge sources, in a similar order: I see what the book says, and I see what the real p eople in the field say, the people who does the research like me-intellects-they have a different intellect. And then I'll asse ss it. And then I'll analyze. And I said, “All right, you know, all  of these things, seem okay”, and trim it down to X amount. And then I said, you know, “I'm gonna try these two, these three approach to solve those problems”. And sometimes it acts right away. Sometimes it takes more than one try…We have meetings, you get a feedback. Or sometimes my ideas might come into the real life –“Guys, that's not gonna work”.

He begins with independent research  to determine which practices to test, relying on pre-existing knowledge, experience, and consultation with experts. Experimentation is iterative and repeated several times to confirm findings. Finally, there is collaborative generation of feedback – a process which is reflexive and relational. Navigating epistemic tensions Through learning as living, adaptive expertise, and informal scientific research, farmers draw upon multiple sources of knowledge and ways of knowing. However, at times the information they receive from these va rious sources may conflict, creating an epistemic tension. A common source of epistemic tension discussed in the interviews is the use of synthetic fertilizers and pesticides. Because of the impact of climate change on crop growth and pests/disease, synthetic fertilizers and pesticides have been promoted by ex tension agencies and chemical companies as a means of climate adaptation  (Tomlinson & Rhiney, 2018). Farmers’ rationale when navigating epistemic tensions  related to chemical use is often informed by an ethos of environmental stew ardship that emerges from their lived experiences and traditional values. Mr. D described why he rejected synthetic fertilizers: “I will not use synthetic fertilizer, because I am a firm believer in synthetic fertilizers damaging the soil … if you go into the deep forest, you will notice that we don't use fertilizer in the dee p forest. And the plants, they are thriving”. He draws on his observations of the natural world  to support his traditional practice of using natural fertilizers. Moreover, at the heart of his choice is a sense of obligation to protect the natural environment. Whether this reasoning is based on ‘scientific’ evidence or not,  the rationale is an important consideration for learning design, which must start from a learner’s current understanding, addressing their concerns and beliefs. Similarly, Mr. R described how training from chemical companie s to promote synthetic fertilizers or pesticides is ofte n rejected by farmers who favor traditional methods: They [chemical companies] will come and do the training and tell you-because we grow up used to our manure and things like that...Well, for the chemical, now you will need some of that now to do the spraying, you understand … which is, of course, at time necessary, but for the food purposes, the natural organic is much better, because sometimes when you use a fertilizer, the food… it come quick…So, then you will find now it won't serve long. It easily spoils. Mr.T. also described epistemic tensions between traditional knowledge and technocratic knowledge and the ways that farmers evaluate information and make decisions:  “You're going to have non-traditional practices in your way, if you're thinking about focusing on traditional practices. So, as you know, we have to sift through and select from the non-tradition, you know, the non-traditional to select the traditional.”  He specifically refers to the use of fertilizers and pesticides noting: “I think it's important for farmers to really know a bout the chemicals that has been used in agriculture. I know how poison they are, how toxic they are…  it actually poison s the earth…”. He also noted that this view aligned with his culture, which promotes natural foods, as well the ‘learning as living’ that he experienced as a child watching his grandfather on the farm: “I never see this man use any form of chemical to spray anything is what I'm saying. He makes his own”.

## Discussion and Conclusion

Kaup (2008) asserts that farmers are “reflexive actors w ho actively negotiate between ‘expert’ and local knowledge” (p.11). This study revealed that farmers frequently turn to the knowledge generated through informal, self-directed, experiential processes which reflect and build upon the ways in which they  initially learned about farming in childhood. These informal “shadow systems” (Tran, 2019) of learning exist outside of, but interact relationally with, formal institutions  and experts. Informal learning processes, adaptive expertise and epistemic agency should be considered in the design of learning, as highly structured, expert-centered models characterized by passive knowledge acquisition do not reflect the actual learning processes of many farmers. Where new agricultural practices or information challenge traditional knowledge it is important to create dialogue between the two. As Berkes (2009) claims, we should not see the two as dichotomous and opposi ng; rather, we must foster “science and traditional knowledge dialogue and partnership” (p. 151). It is also important to address the fact-value distinction in framing science knowledge and agricultural training. In some cases, tensions exist because of farmers' misconceptions. Addressing misconceptions requires identifying the values and beliefs that underpin their rationale and form the foundation of their preferred practices. Understanding epistemic and decision-making processes is crucial for the design and implementation of agricultural extension programs in the context of climate change, particularly when introducing novel practices. Extension systems must take farmers’ existing values and beliefs as a starting point for  learning design, including addressing misconceptio ns through ways of knowing that are familiar (experiential, learning, observation e tc.) but also by integrating t raditional knowledge into formal systems, to inform the content, practices and technologies that are promoted.
