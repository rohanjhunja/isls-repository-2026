# Relationships Among Impulsivity, Keystroke Patterns, and Learning Outcomes in Middle School Students' Online Problem-Solving Tasks: A Latent Profile Analysis

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study investigated associations among students’ impulsivity, engagement patterns, and learning outcomes during online problem-solving. Using latent profile analysis, we analyzed keystroke data from 788 middle school students during Korean language problem-solving tasks, with impulsivity scores and learning outcomes including correct answer, comprehension, and attitude scores. Results revealed two distinct patterns: Profile 1 showed lower total keystroke count and longer release-press duration than Profile 2. Impulsivity did not correlate with keystroke patterns; however, lower impulsivity and Profile 2 keystroke patterns were associated with better learning outcomes. Findings suggest keystroke patterns provide meaningful insights about student engagement and performance during online problem-solving.

## Introduction

Online learning environments capture data about student learning processes. Keystroke patterns in online learning can reveal cognitive and emotional regulation (Jang et al., 2024; Tate & Warschauer, 2019). Online learning with limited supervision demands greater self-regulation than traditional classrooms.  Impulsivity, characterized by quick and unplanned actions (Patton et al., 1995), may create additional challenges in online learning. Impulsivity hinders academic performance in both traditional and online settings (Leshem & A ltman, 2021; Jenkins & Ogg, 2019; Richardson, 2015). However, its relationship to actual learning processes remains underexplored. Keystroke patterns are associated with academic performance (Conijn et al., 2022; Dixon et al., 2024; Khan et al., 2023) and engagement (Allen et al., 2016; Bixler & D'Mello, 2 013). Recent studies use keystroke data to measure engagement (Talebinamvar & Zarrabi, 2022; Zhang et al., 2019).  This study examines how middle school students' impulsivity and keystroke patterns relate to learning outcomes during online problem-solving.

## Methods

We recruited 788 first-year middle school students (369 males, 401 females, 16 unspecified) from Seoul, South Korea, after excluding participants with health issues that could affect problem-solving. Impulsivity was measured using the Korean-validated 15-item Barratt Impulsiveness Scale-11 (BIS-11; Spinella, 2007; Heo et al., 2012) assessing cognitive, motor, and non-planning dimensions on a 4-point scale. The problem-solving task consisted of 20 Korean language problems aligned with the nat ional curriculum, each with comprehension and attit ude assessment questions. All participants used identical tablets with keyboards to ensure consistent data collection. Participants completed the mental health questionna ire before engaging in a 45-minute online problemsolving session  in a controlled laboratory.  Software recorded all keystroke inputs, timing, and  responses. Keystroke indicators included total keystroke count, space key ratio, delete key ratio, press duration, and releaseto-press duration. Outcome variables comprised correct answer scores, comprehension scores, and attitude scores. Latent Profile Analysis (LPA; Bauer, 2022; Sinha et al., 2021) identified distinct patterns in students' keystroke data using standardized keystroke indicators with varying variance and covariance models. The optimal model was selected based on Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC)  and entropy  (Akogul & Erisoglu, 2017). Logistic regression examined the relationship betwe en impulsivity and profile membership. M ultivariate regression tested associations among impulsivity, profile membership, and learning outcomes using R 4.3.

## Results

The LPA revealed two distinct keystroke patterns du ring online problem-solving, with the two-profile model demonstrating optimal fit based on BIC (10,367) and  entropy (0.631). Profile 1 (42.3% of participants, n = 329) was characterized by significantly lower total keystroke count (M = -0.48, SE = 0.09, p <.001), lower delete key ratio (M = -0.47, SE = 0.09, p <.001), and longer release-press duration (M = 0.94, SE = 0.22, p <.001). Profile 2 (57.7% of participants, n = 448) demonstrated higher total keystroke count (M = 0.20, SE = 0.06, p <.001), higher delete key ratio (M = 0.14, SE = 0.06, p <.05), and shorter release-press duration (M = -0.29, SE = 0.05, p <.001). Space key ratio and release-to-press duration did not significantly differ between profiles (see figure 1). Multivariate regression revealed significant relati onships between impulsivity and all outcome variables. Higher impulsivity was associated with lower correct answer scores (β = -0.21, p <.001), lower comprehension (β = -0.22, p <.001), and more negative attitudes (β = -0.18, p <.001). Additionally, keystroke patterns were significantly correlated with learning outcomes: students in Profile 2 obtained higher correct answer scores (β = 0.35, p <.001) and better comprehension (β = 0.45, p <.001), although the  attitude scores were not significantly different (β = 0.12, p =.201). The F-statistics for the models were significant for correct answer (F(2,

768) = 26.6, p <.001), comprehension (F(2, 767) = 32.9, p <.001), and attitude scores (F(2, 774) = 13.2, p <.001).

## Discussion

Impulsivity significantly correlated with poorer le arning outcomes, including lower correct answer, comprehension, and attitude scores. This extends Ri chardson’s (2015) finding that students with greate r impulsivity face challenges in digital environments  without immediate teacher support. The non-significant impulsivity-keystroke relationship contradicts Leshem and Altma n's (2021) findings that impulsivity links to faster response. Our results indicate impulsivity manifests differ ently in adolescents durin g structured problem-solving, hindering focus rather than accelerating r esponses. This aligns with Vigil -Coleṭ and Morales-Vives'

(2005) view that impulsivity undermines academic performance through reduced attention and strategic thinking.

Our analysis identified distinct keystroke patterns  with clear associations to learning outcomes, with Profile 2 strongly correlating with better performa nce. This profile, with higher keystroke count and shorter pauses, resembles the "rapid writers" identified in Talebinamvar and Zarrabi's (2022) study of college students. The association between active typing and better performance (β = 0.3 5-0.45) supports Tate and Warschauer's

(2019) findings on keystroke activity and writing s cores. Conversely, Profile 1 students with fewer ke ystrokes

and longer pauses showed poorer performance, contra sting with Zhang et al.'s (2021) finding that longe r pauses indicated productive cognition in college essay wri ting. This difference reflects task requirements —pauses may benefit open-ended writing but signal difficulties in structured problem-solving that demands focused attention.

## Conclusion

This study identified two distinct keystroke patterns among middle school students during online problem-solving tasks: one with fewer keystrokes and longer pauses, and another with more keystrokes and shorter pauses. Impulsivity did not predict keystroke patterns, yet lower impulsivity and greater keystrokes correlated with better learning outcomes. Findings suggest keystroke data provides insights in to the online learning process and outcome.

Our results offer practical implications for online  education. Teachers should provide extra support for highly impulsive students, who performed worse acro ss all measures.  Monitoring keystroke patterns could identify students needing extra guidance. Limitations include the cross-sectional design and sample specificity. Future research should examine diverse demographics and analyze keystroke pattern changes over time.
