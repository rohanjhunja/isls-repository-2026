# Becoming Facilitators of Change: Recognition and Agency in STEM Professional Development for Minoritized Outreach Instructors

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study explores how a grassroots STEM Gap-Year Program for outstanding precollege students from the Arab minority in Israel disrupt historically, culturally, and politically grounded inequalities experienced by this minoritized community by fostering participants’ capacity to act as agents of change in their communities. The program engages youth Arab instructors (YAIs) who serve as STEM outreach facilitators in Arab-speaking schools while participating in a year-long professional development program combining STEM-focused and empowerment and activism (E&A) sessions. The analysis of 11 videotaped sessions showed that STEM sessions strengthened epistemic and relational agency through disciplinary and pedagogical practice, whereas empowerment sessions cultivated critical agency through reflection, dialogue, and identity work. Recognition from mentors and peers validated the YAIs’ capacities and reinforced their authority. The findings highlight how community-engaged STEM programs can bridge academic preparation with social transformation.

Despite growing efforts to promote diversity and inclusion in STEM, students from underrepresented backgrounds continue to face inequities in preparation, access, and persistence (National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine, 2019). To address  these challenges, academic and social institutions have developed initiatives such as summer bridge programs, mentoring networks, and professional development opportunities to support students’ transition to undergraduate STEM studies (Strayhorn et al., 2019). Research shows that such programs can strengthen students’ academic preparedness, confidence, and sense of belonging, all of which are factors that foster persistence and success in STEM (Carlone & Johnson, 2007; Chang et al., 2014; Hernandez et al., 2018). This study examined the Gap-Year Program led by Alrowad for Science and Technology (henceforth, Alrowad). Alrowad is a grassroots organization founded by academics and educators in STEM, who are members of the minoritized Arab community in Israel. The Arab population of Palestine who remained within Israel’s borders after the 1949 armistice and their descendants are considered by Israeli law as Arab citizens of Israel. This minority comprises 21% of the Israeli population. Due to historical, political, social, and economic factors, ACIs have faced many inequalities (Ali & Da’as, 2018). Alrowad  addresses these inequities by enhancing STEM education as means to promote socioeconomic mobility.

Each year, Alrowad recruits 10-20 outstanding Arab STEM high school graduates to join its Gap-Year Program. These youth Arab instructors (YAIs) work as STEM outreach facilitators, reaching 50,000  to70,000 K6–K12 students across the Arab community  in Israel. Shaped by economic disparities, collectivist cultural values, and the historical and political context, Arab-speaking classrooms typically implement hierarchical, teacher-centered instruction (Dkeidek & Issa, 2021). T he program seeks to transform this reality through innovative pedagogy and social engagement. Parallel to their field work, the YAIs participate in a professional development course (180 hours) focused on STEM content and pedagogy, empowerment, and social activism. Pre-college STEM programs for underrepresented students often focus on helping participants adapt to existing institutional structures rather than empowering them to critically navigate or transform these structures (Ong et al., 2018). The Gap-Year Program takes a di fferent approach. In a recent study (Ganaiem et al., 2025) based on interviews with program alumni, we found that their guided experiences as community science outreach facilitators became a powerful pathway for developing and expanding their sc ience capital (Archer et al., 2015). The alumni described forms of epistemic, relational, and critical agency (Miller et al., 2018; Edwards & 1332 Mackenzie, 2005; Basu et al., 2009) that were closely tied to their participation in the program. This multifaceted agency emerged as a lasting source of science capital, shaping their learning, professional trajectories, and their ability to claim and redefine their place in STEM while connecting it to their social, ethnic, and communal identities. Without explicit attention to agency, pre-college STEM programs for underrepresented students risk reproducing inequities by preparing marginalized students to “fit into” existing structures rather than equipping them to challenge or reshape the barriers they face. The present study longitudinally examined the discourse in the Gap-Year PD sessions over a full year to address two questions: (1) How did the PD design foster the development of the YAIs’ multifaceted epistemic, relational, and critical agency? (2) How did interactions with mentors and peers facilitate their enactment of agency?

## Theoretical framework

Agency as a multifaceted construct Agency refers to the capacity of individuals or groups to act purposefully to create, influence, or transform themselves and the conditions in which they live (Holland et al., 1998), as well as to disrupt structures and mobilize resources to achieve their goals and reshape the systems in which they operate (Varelas et al., 2015). Agency can be enacted in terms of knowledge, people, and social institutions. Epistemic agency denotes the ability to construct, evaluate, and shape knowledge and practices within one’s environment (Miller et al., 2018; Stroupe, 2014). Relational agency involves drawing on and contributing to relationships  by seeking and offering support, aligning actions with others, and leveraging social connections as resources for collective action (Edwards & Mackenzie, 2005; Edwards, 2005). Critical agency involves developing critical awareness and engaging in social transformation; here, science education that cultivates students’ critical science agency not only deepen ed their understanding of science but also empowered them to apply it to the individual. The role of recognition in the emergence of agency Agency is realized through decision-making and action (Edmonds, 2019) and depends on recognition by others (Esteban et al., 2022). It cannot be exercised in isolation, but rather relies on networks of relationships and resources that legitimate  and enable it. When children’s and adolescents’ agency is acknowledged, they are positioned as capable social actors who can act and interact independently (Mühlbacher & Sutterlüty, 2019). Such recognition not only validates their capacities but also requires providi ng the material conditions that allow them to enact these capacities effectively (Liebel, 2018, p. 621).

## Procedure

Eighteen YAIs (9 males, 9 females) from the 2023-2024 cohort of the GapYear Program and 5 mentors took part in the study. Four mentors, all of whom are experts in STEM content and pedagogy, facilitated the STEM-focused sessions, and 1 mentor, Alrowad ’s educational counselor (the first author), led the empowerment and activism (E&A) sessions. In this ethnographic study, the first author served as a participant observer in the STEM sessions, led the E&A  sessions, and accompanied the YAIs during portions of their fieldwork. Each recording was segmented into 2 to 3-minute intervals and each segment was accompanied by a brief narrative description. Thematic codes were developed  through an iterative process that combined deductive and inductive approaches that moved continuously between the theoretical framework and the empirical data (Thornberg & Charmaz, 2014). The codes were iteratively refined and organized into thematic categories clustered by content, activity type, enactments of agency, forms of recognition, and the mentors’ positioning. The final coding output was exported to Excel. The code frequencies per session were calculated and normalized by session duration, and the relative distribution of codes within each cluster was examined over time to compare the STEM and E&A sessions. The figure illustrates how these sessions complemented one another across five concentric spheres, moving gradually from self to society. Personal expressions of affect and values (Circle 1) appeared frequently in both types of sessions, indicating their equal significance for the YAIs at the personal level. In contrast, discourse in the STEM sessions focused mainly 1333 on teaching the subject matter and teaching in school (Circles 2-3), whereas the E&A  sessions centered on the YAIs’ interactions with students, staff, and the broader community; i.e., their engagement with society and culture (Circles 4–5).

Figure 2 presents the normalized distribution of the codes in the activity cluster, which captures the types of activities participants engaged in, comparing the STEM and E&A sessions. The figure highlights how the two session types complemented each other in fostering the YAIs’ agency across five spheres reflecting increasing learner agency. Both sessions included structured instruction (Circle 1), but diverged as the spheres expanded: the STEM sessions focused mainly on instructional design and microteaching practices (Circle 3), whereas E&A sessions emphasized collaborative interaction and reflective feedback through dialogue, co-construction, and reflection (Circles 2 and 4). Activities promoting informal community building (Circle 5) were prominent in both, underscoring the program’s emphasis on social networks and peer support as the core elements of the Gap-Year PD.

Whi le such outcomes are vital for access and persistence, programs that focus primarily on adaptation to existing structures risk reproducing inequities (Ong et al., 2018).
