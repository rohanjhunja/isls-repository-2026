Responsive teaching, which centers and pursues students’ thinking (Robertson et al., 2015), is essential for equitable mathematics teaching (Horn & Garner, 2022; NCTM, 2014). Responsive teaching places additional demands on teachers, however, as they must notice and make sense of student thinking in the midst of socially and interactionally rich classroom environments (van Es & Sherin, 2008). Preparing teachers to teach responsively therefore necessitates a practice-based approach which grounds teacher preparation in the actual work of teaching, learning in and from practice through actual classroom practice, simulated and representational teaching experiences, and classroom artifacts (Ball & Cohen, 1999). At the same time, there is an increasing use of online teacher preparation to serve areas experiencing heightened shortages of highly qualified teachers, such as rural contexts (Corbett & d’Entremont, 2024). In this conceptual design poster, we consider how large language models (LLMs) can be trained to approximate the social and interpretive complexity of classroom interaction while maintaining coherence with theories of teacher learning and pedagogical judgment, presenting an AI-based student simulator, MAIa, used in an online, asynchronous mathematics methods course for elementary pre-service teachers.

## Theoretical perspectives

Teacher educators  have long used technology to support teacher learning and professional development (Self & Stengel, 2020). Teaching simulations—involving face-to-face and virtual formats (e.g. Amador, 2018; Dieker et al., 2017), storyboarding (e.g. Herbst & Milewski, 2018), and chat-based dialogue simulations (e.g. Bywater et al., 2019)—allow for professional experimentation  in a structured yet participant-driven way that is centered on instructional practice (Milewski et al., 2018) and offers pre-service teachers interactive representations of practice that mirror authentic classroom instruction (Castro Superfine et al., 2025). Simulations can help pre-service teachers refine instructional practice, attend to student thinking (Bywater et al., 2019; Castro Superfine et al.,

2025) and promote mathematical discourse (Amador, 2018; Mikeska et al., 2023). Such work aligns with situative

views of teacher learning in which teachers’ knowledge-in-practice is socially, culturally, and historically negotiated through interactions with colleagues, institutional demands, workplace experience, and mediating tools (Horn, 2019). We focus on pedagogical judgment (Horn & Garner, 2022) in which teachers’ pedagogical actions are supported by pedagogical reasoning and rooted in pedagogical responsibility. The rise of generative AI invites further innovation in developing constrictive and interactive technologies to support teacher learning (Chiu et al., 2022) and to further iterate teaching simulations that leverage AI-based technologies to create approximations of practice in which to ground teacher training experiences. Yet, few efforts explicitly ground AI-based designs in theories of teacher learning or pedagogical judgment (PandayShukla, 2025). Centering teachers’ pedagogical judgment therefore necess itates designing for an interactive process as opposed to a desired end product.

MAIa Guided by the question, how can generative AI be leveraged to simulate classroom activity in ways that support situated, practice-based teacher learning?, MAIa was designed as a socioculturally grounded, AI-mediated approximation of practice that supports pre-service teachers in rehearsing the interpretive work of responsive teaching and reflecting on their practice. Drawing on situative theories of teacher learning (Horn, 2019) and practice-based teacher education (Ball & Cohen, 1999; Grossman et al., 2009), we conceptualize MAIa as both a 2898 simulation and a context for teacher sensemaking—one that preserves uncertainty, multiplicity, and the emergent character of classroom interaction. We designed MAI a for an online, asynchronous mathematics methods course for elementary pre-service teachers at a midsized public university in the Mountain West that serves rural populations and offers flexible online learning options within the teacher preparation progr am. MAIa was constructed using OpenAI’s Custom GPT platform to simulate a K–2 student persona. with a K–2 student persona informed by research on children’s number sense (Johnson et al., 2019; Wager & Parks, 2016) and sociocultural participation (Cobb, 1995), the  system embeds realistic developmental patterns—skipping, double‑counting, hesitations —and enforces “setup‑response” and “no‑feedback” rules to preserve interactional uncertainty. We also developed a specific prompt structure that further specifies how the GPT should interact with the user by defining specific mathematical content, potential strategies, and common errors. MAI a functions as a practice space where teachers engage in short text‑based dialogues, annotate transcripts, and reflect on pedagogical reasoning, thereby fostering development of pedagogical judgment across action, reasoning, and responsibility.

In the assignment, pre-service teachers completed a simulation exchange with M AIa, annotated their transcripts, and completed a brief reflection afterwards. We see this brief example as a proof-of-concept for an interactive, AI-based simulation tool to support pre-service teachers’ reflection on and development of pedagogical judgment. Importantly, our design and enactment centered the interaction between the user and MAIa as opposed to an end product (such as a summary report or evaluation). In creating a simulated student that could approximate emergent mathematical reasoning, we sought to preserve the interpretive uncertainty of teaching as a generative feature of the design. MAIa positions AI as a medium through which pre-service teachers can engage in the relational and sensemaking work of teaching. Rather than using AI as an instructional assistant or to evaluate teaching, AI can instead be leveraged as a mediating tool through which teachers can engage the interpretive, uncertain, and relational dimensions of practice.
