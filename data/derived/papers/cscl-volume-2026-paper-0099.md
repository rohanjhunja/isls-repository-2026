# Boundary Spanning Narratives in a Constructionist Museum Game

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Museum learning is highly social (Allen, 2004). To support and enable socioculturally mediated learning, previous studies have demonstrated how designing exhibits for collaboration can yield productive learning outcomes and engagement. However, few studies have explored the learning value of naturalistic collaborative play in museums (in which there are no explicit prompts/scaffolds for collaboration). We present a museum game and both computational and qualitative observations of interaction modes. Visito rs showed diverse collaborative and cooperative behaviors, including clarification, seeking help, negotiation, goal-adaptation, and modeling. Our analysis also revealed that boundary spanning narration (or BSN, Tissenbaum et al., 2017) enabled “talk with evidence of learning.” Additionally, we found that social play (in this case, cooperative and competitive play) varied with the types and amount of BSN. Through the mediation of BSN, two-person visitor groups (“dyads”) co-constructed knowledge, coordinated on tasks, and set shared goals for themselves in non-competitive play, while in competitive play, dyads tended to use BSN towards surveillance and dominating play.

Museums are loci of public connections that allow diverse audiences to join and thus have great potential in building diverse communities of learning (Falk, 2002; Horrigan, 2016; Berland, 2020; Wardrip et al., 2017). Learning in museums is socially situated (Allen, 2002; Falk & Dierking, 2016), so, in order to understand how people learn with others and build a community of creating, we need to understand how visitors learn through interacting with each other in naturalistic settings. Prior research has primarily examined collaborative learning through explicitly designed structures, such as assigned roles (Klopfer & Squire, 2005), shared tasks (Apostolellis & Bowman, 2015), or challenges requiring joint action (Rowe et al., 2014). However, we know far less about the naturalistic social interactions that emerge between youths when playing constructionist museum games. Therefore, our research question is:  how do youth visitors play differently when a constructionist museum game is played competitively versus when playing collaboratively? We investigate the question through Rainbow Agents, a 2-player constructionist museum game which is designed to support both individual and group learning of computational literacy. The focus of the game is to invite players to build their programs to tend  a shared garden with animal characters that are agents that execute the coding logics they created. The game does not explicitly prompt for tight-coupled collaboration, instead, it just invites players to tend the garden, which enables naturalistic social interaction to emerge. Through this work, we explore the learning engagement and the collaborative learning mechanism that emerges from naturalistic social interactions in museums.  In the game, players program animal characters to tend a shared garden using computational logic cards. Each player operates a touchscreen console for character selection and programming, while a large TV displays the communal garden space. The game features three programmable creatures, each embodying distinct computational modeling concepts (e.g., loops, conditionals, sequences). Players select territories (3×3 tiles), choose cards, and sequence actions such as planting and watering. While the game-directed goal involves filling three orbs to trigger a storm or open a treasure chest, the constructionist design encourages emergent, player-defined objectives. The game does not mandate tight-coupled collaboration, allowing naturalistic social interactions to unfold organically.

Even less has been studied in which ways people communicate and what learning looks like in competitive play, and what it looks like in museums.  Multiplayer games offer a productive lens for examining the relationship between informal collaborative and competitive play (Gee, 2008; Plass et al., 2015; Steinkuehler, 2012). Visitors come to museums with diverse backgrounds and divergent personal goals, raising questions about how collaborative learning emerges when participants are not working toward a shared objective. Tissenbaum et al. (2017) demonstrated that museum visitors can engage in productive collaboration even with divergent goals, introducing the Divergent Collaborative Learning Mechanisms (DCLM) framework to capture these interactions. Building on this work, we examine how collaborative learning mechanisms emerge across different interaction modes including cooperative, collaborative, and competitive play in a two-player constructionist game designed to develop computational literacy.

## Method

Data was collected at a science and technology museum in North America. At the exhibit, we used a “lollipop” consent sign to indicate participation and permission for video/audio recording.  Over 10 hours of video were recorded using a fixed camera positioned behind the exhibit to capture gestures and gaze without disrupting visitors. Audio was captured via table microphones, and system interactions were logged automatically for all consented participants. Our study employed interaction analysis (Jordan & Henderson, 1995; Ash, 2003) within a multiple case study design (Yin, 2009) to investigate how learning processes differ across interaction modes in museum constructionist gaming.

## Findings & Analysis

We found that competitive play and non-competitive play varied significantly in learning process and learning engagement. In competitive play, the narration is dominated by dominance claiming and resources bragging. The effects of competition in game-based learning are mixed. Results from our study showed that players’ competition tends to limit individual as well as collective mean-making. We observed players engaged overwhelmingly in the idea of “winning/conquering” over the actual game content (Vandercruysse et al., 2013), thus engage heavily in score talk (in our case, territory dominance claiming), echoing the results of Meece et al. (2005). We found that their talk is qualitatively different in competitive play vs. cooperative  play. In competitive play, the talk was unidirectional rather than reciprocal, showing a misaligned engagement on topics and interests, which may have inhibited the collaborative interactions. Similar to Kohn (1992)’s finding, we saw player anxiety and frustration in this environment; players more likely turned to a competitive play mode. Tissenbaum et al. (2017) showed that learners who are simultaneously engaged in divergent (or non-convergent) goals can still effectively scaffold each other and engage in productive collaboration. In this study, we provide further empirical evidence for  these findings. Additionally, we found players engage in fluid and dynamic interaction modes, switching from individual/parallel play to cooperative or collaborative play, and generate productive collaborative learning interactions even when no explicit tight collaboration is prompted. In Rainbow Agents,  it is designed to be easy to observe the other player’s activities along with themselves because of the shared display and side-by-side seating design. The shared display shows the shared communal garden space that belongs to both players, which allows them to watch things abo ut themselves, about the other player, as well as the overview of the shared group status. Previous study about multiuser tabletop territoriality has demonstrated that people categorize the space into personal and group uses in using shared space with others (Scott et al., 2004). In this game, there is neither a single dedicated personal space, nor any “boundaries” of group and personal  space. The ambiguity of ownership of the territory may have led to the behavior of boundary spanning perception and other forms of narration, thus incubating multiple BSN-facilitated collaborative interactions (e.g., scaffolding, meaning-making, assess and review).

In this paper, we investigated the learning of competitive and cooperative play in a 2-player constructionist museum game. Our results showed limited individual and collective meaning-making in the competitive play, while visitors engaged in a variety of collaborative learning activities through boundary spanning narration (BSN) mechanism. This study is an exploratory one by nature as we used only a small part of a large data set. In the future, quantitative analysis on BSN can reveal scalable insights abou t the effect of BSN in terms of game-directed learning outcomes.
