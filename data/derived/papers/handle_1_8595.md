# Undergraduate and Graduate Student Perspective on Teaching for Critical Pedagogy

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In critical pedagogy there is an intrinsically interrelated relationship between education and society. The fundamental purpose for education is to improve society and fight for justice for all. These core ideas translate into a set of principles for the design of transformational learning. These principles involve rejecting positivism and embracing constructivism, developing critical consciousness, questioning assumptions, learner autonomy and authority, and transforming realities. To explore these principles, the first and second author presents an autoethnographic analysis of these principles in their own educational experience.

## Background

There is not a set of practices for critical pedagogy, but it provides a set of principles that guide the design of courses, learning activities, and learning environments. The first involves rejecting positivism and embracing constructivist and situative perspectives (Kincheloe & Steinberg, 1998) because positivism reproduces and enforces systems of power that cause marginalization and oppression resulting in social injustice. The second principle is developing critical consciousness—a frame of mind that creates and rebuilds the world by selftransformation of one's context which cannot occur from the traditional “from the top down but from the inside out” (Freire, 1974), and is developed by building knowledge through human and world relations and interactions (hooks, 1994). The third principle is transforming realities towards alleviating the suffering of those around you who are marginalized and/or oppressed. This is only possible when working with learners to gradually understand and embrace their own authority (Donaldson 2019). In critical pedagogy, we have students and teachers in positions of autonomy and authority—the fourth principle (Freire, 1974; Kincheloe, Steinberg, & Tippins, 1999). Developing learner authority and autonomy is necessary to enact all principles of critical pedagogy. The fifth principle is questioning assumptions (Kincheloe, 2008) and recognizing that not everything has one answer, and that answers are constructed by people who have intersecting identities and power.

## Methods

We used theory-focused autoethnographic study (Jones 2016) to analyze our own educational experience through the lens of critical pedagogy. We approached our analysis by first delineating three aspects of autoethno-graphy. The “auto” (self) aspect includes the experiences of the two authors of this paper. The “ethno” (socio-cultural context) aspect includes three communities: 1) the family and community of the first author, 2) the family and community of the second author, and 3) the community of higher education in a large land-grant university in the United States. The “graphy” (formal research investigation) aspect included analysis of researcher-generated field notes. The first author is a graduate student, first in her family to graduate university. She grew up in a marginalized community in San Antonio, TX, where the community lacked access to healthcare, higher education, and other resources. The second author is an undergraduate student, and will be the fourth in her family to graduate from college. She grew up in a predominantly Hispanic community where many families live below the poverty line. Both authors grew up in low-income communities and attended Title I schools.

## Findings

Principle 1 findings: Reflecting on our grade school education, specifically high school, we both remember students at our schools being generalized as troublemakers because of the school’s Title I status. Both schools consistently reported low test scores, high pregnancy, and dropout rates, and violations. On the surface, from a positivist perspective based on the statistics and facts, it was assumed that these students didn’t care about school—that school was something students had to do, or they would go to truancy court. By rejecting positivism and looking deeper past just numbers, we can understand why the statistics are what they are. Analysis of our own experiences helped us realize that factors like race, citizenship status, mental illness, sex, and class are socially constructed concepts that have real-life consequences, especially in terms of producing and reproducing societal hierarchy. These hierarchies invade institutions like education, creating differences in its quality depending on socioeconomic status. As we continued with our education, we realized that these hierarchies that have been put in place—are not a natural nor inevitable feature of reality. The realization that ICLS2022 Proceedings 1830 © ISLS social constructs are not “real” in terms of natural sciences but do have real-life consequences transformed our worldview.

Principle 2 findings: During my undergraduate career, I (the first author) was faced with obstacles that only presented themselves to students who are different from the traditional college student. Being a woman of color from a low socioeconomic background who is also the first in her family to go to college, I quickly realized that the environment of higher education I was experiencing was not created for people like me. And yet, I also realize that even though the system was not created for me, I belong in higher education. I have the right to be here, and a right to succeed.

Principle 3 findings: Do I belong or not? Looking at sociohistorical assumptions in society, I (the first author) was able to recognize that the structures and institutions of higher education were designed by and for white upper middle class men. It became important to me that I challenge these barriers that impact disenfranchised communities. Education is not a privilege but a human right, which contradicts the initial assumption that education is reserved for the higher class. Growing up, college was seen as unattainable in my community and family due to lack of resources and awareness of how to access and navigate higher education. Within higher education, the lack of representation of minority students deepened the notion that students from marginalized communities are unwelcomed. There is a constant battle between knowing I belong, yet being aware of the preexisting systemic issues within the institution of higher education. Principle 4 findings: During the spring semester of my sophomore year, I (the second author) was enrolled in a course where most class periods consisted of open discussions surrounding the ways in which education has failed marginalized communities, as well as discussion of present and future solutions. This course was one of the first I had that focused on discussion-based learning where students had the autonomy to express their opinions and lead discussions. The professor also allowed students to decide what to research and how they want to demonstrate their understanding. Through the professor’s use of open discussion and choice in assignments, they were able to engage students in developing identity. It proved that I have the authority and autonomy to explore what I find interesting, know that I’m intellectually valued, and use my experiences to demonstrate knowledge.

Principle 5 findings: We are currently working in a learning sciences research laboratory that assists faculty from all disciplines conduct research about learning in their own courses. We are able to create a positive impact in higher education. We take different principles from critical pedagogy in order to help faculty revise their courses. In our lab we approach our roles and responsibilities through exercising agency and autonomy. We have the authority to craft our own research projects, collaborate with members in the lab, and work with faculty as co-equals. We support the lab in other ways such as data cleaning and writing overview papers for faculty to better understand learning theories. We are working in collaboration with top professors in their fields to develop more powerful and empowering learning experiences in their courses. This allows us to have an impact on transforming student’s learning, as well as their worldviews and critical consciousness.

## Discussion

As both learners and scholars, we are driven by a need to work toward creating an equitable education system that benefits everyone. Through learning and collaborating with others we create knowledge that has the potential to change the society we live in. By transforming how we approach teaching and learning by using critical pedagogy we have the ability to create an institution that changes lives for the better. Our autoethnographic analysis found that theoretical constructs from the critical pedagogy literature reflect the truth of our own lived experiences.
