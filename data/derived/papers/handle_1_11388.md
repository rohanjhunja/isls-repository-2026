# Pre-coding Scaffolds for Computational Thinking in an Open-Ended Middle School Social Robotics Curriculum

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Research suggests that scaffolding students’ Computational Thinking before coding can support their learning experience. We examine how a CT App supports students’ learning process and how the MyTurn social robotics curriculum impacts students’ perceptions of Computer Science and sense of belonging in an in-classroom deployment. We find evidence that pre-coding scaffolds benefited students’ CT and coding process and that the open-ended, design-based robotics curriculum influenced many students’ perceptions of CS.

## Introduction

Current computing curricul a often focus on knowledge-based scaffolds during coding (e.g. block-based coding; Resnick, 2012) that may limit opportunities to address persistent stereotypes about Computer Science (CS). To address these challenges, we developed a social rob otics curriculum, MyTurn, with pre-coding scaffolds to support computational thinking (CT) that uses a  student driven problem-based approach that  focuses on social utility, such as helping family members. In this paper, we  report on the impact of the MyTurn in-classroom program on the process of learning CT and developing interest in computing for 6th grade students.

## Background

Both material and social scaffolds (Puntambekar, 2022) can complement CS education to foster CT and challenge negative stereotypes. Material scaffolds, such as software tools,  help structure learning tasks, guid e learners through key components and support planning (Reiser, 2004). Block-based environments were developed to offer this type of scaffolding for developing coding skil ls in CS as they minimize the cognitive load of complicated syntax and debugging errors (Xu et. al, 2019). However, researc h suggests students still encounter conceptual difficulties related to coding (Grover, 2017). Thus, it may be that such tools do not sufficiently sup port student CT processes and may overlook crucial aspects of CS beyond mere coding skills (Batni & Junaini, 2024). Pre-coding scaffolding tools may address this issue by  supporting students’ computational problem solving in early stages, particularly for inexperienced learners. However, simply building knowledge is likely not enough to promote interest and participation in com puting – CS education must also address stereotypes that obscure the value of CS and exclude marginalized gr oups (Michaelis & Weintrop, 2022). Researchers have identified negative CS stereotypes, including that it is boring, asocial, individualistic and disconnected from student interests (Goode et al., 2018). One way to address these stereotypes is through curricula emph asizing computing for good (Kaczmarczyk, 2016), such as imp roving healthcare and education (Khan & Luxton-Reilly, 2016). As such, programs that focus on computing as  having social utility, that helps others, can support value and belonging in computing to attract and retain more diverse groups to the field (Goode et al., 2018). MyTurn App and social robotics curriculum MyTurn utilizes problem-based learning that emphasizes collaboration and social good (Ibtasar et al., 2023). We developed a MyTurn App to support student learning of CT concepts and scaffolds students in three pre-coding activities: (1) problem formulation  by creating storyboards to depict social robot interactions; (2) problem decomposition by annotating each storyboard frame to describe robot actions; (3) algorithmic design by writing pseudocode to represent the annotations; and (4) code authoring by pre-planning robot block-codes based on the their pseudocode for social robots. Our study of the impact of the MyTurn curriculum and App, is guided by two research questions:  (RQ1) Do pre-coding scaffolds impact CT concepts and skills? And (RQ2) How does the MyTurn curriculum impact students' perception of computer science and sense of belonging in computer science?

## Method

We conducted a three-week in-classroom study with 6th-grade students in two classes at a small private school in the Midwest with 29 participants (male = 12 and fem ale =17). We provided the teacher with lesson plans and powerpoint slides, as well as handbook for implemen ting MyTurn. Each class was assigned to either work with the full MyTurn App including pre-coding scaffolds for storyboarding, annotations, ps eudocode and block code (treatment condition; N = 8 groups) or a version of the MyTurn App that o nly included scaffolds for uploading and annotating storyboards (control group, N = 7 groups). Due to unforeseen circumstances of  scheduling with the school, the treatment group received one fewer session during the coding phase in week 3, than the  control group. For RQ1, we conducted an artifact analysis of student work using an adapted rubric based on Grover (2017) to assess 7 dimensions: simple loops, advanced loop s, conditionals, nested statements, motion, sensor and user interaction. We calculated artifact scores by counting the total number of unique blocks used across these coding dimensions. Two authors independently scored all student projects, and we conducted a test of interrater reliability (IRR) and found a sufficiently high reliability (κ = 0.988). We tested for differences in mean rubric scores between conditions at the end of each week using a nonparam etric Wilcoxon Rank Sum test due to the robustness of nonparametric testing in small sample sizes (Okoye & Hosseini, 2024). We present the normalized freque ncy of using each block type, where we  divided total block counts for each group by the nu mber of groups in the condition. For RQ2, we examined two questions on ea ch of the three weekly surveys. We coded responses with yes or no for the perception change question and lo w, neutral, and high for the sense of belonging question.  We had a sufficiently high IRR for sense of belonging (κ = 0.87) and perception change (κ = 0.92) and resolved all codes found to have disagreement through discussion. We used a Sankey diagram to illustrate changes in responses across the three weeks (Lee & Tan, 2017). Finally, we conducted a Thematic Analysis (Braun & Clarke,

2022) of the content of students’ responses over the weekly reflections. We began with a process of open semantic

coding to generate a set of latent  axial codes. Three authors used these codes to iter atively propose and refine emergent themes,  and finally reached consensus on themes that reflec t students’ sense of belonging and perceptions of CS as they engaged in the MyTurn in-classroom curriculum. Consistent with Reflexive Thematic Analysis, we did not conduct interrater reliability on themes (Braun & Clarke, 2022).

## Discussion and conclusion

We found some evidence to support that the pre-coding scaffolding tool benefited students’ CT and coding skills (RQ1) and that the open-ended, design-based robotics curriculum influenced many students’  perceptions of CS and sense of belonging in CS (RQ2). Students using  pre-coding scaffolds (treatment condition) demonstrated a more efficient coding process where they began coding earlier in Week 2 that persisted to include larger and more complex code by week 3, compared to the control gro up. Specifically, treatment groups appeared to use more sensors, user interaction blocks, and advanced loops such as While or Loop Until. This finding may indicate that the pre-coding scaffolds for simpler coding concepts and skills may be faded (Puntambekar, 2022) relatively early in a coding environment as students get more experi ence, but that more advanced concepts and skills may need scaffolding for longer.

In terms of belonging, we found that throughout the  program students’ sense of belonging was tied to their perceived ability to do activities that they considered as part of CS. If students felt they had  been able to build and code their robots, they reported themselv es as being a fit in CS. Thus, it is important for designers of open-ended and problem-based learning environments to help tie student views of these activities to CS and to consider the successful execution of activities by students as it can impact their feelings of belonging within CS. Finally, our findings show how the MyTurn curriculum, including social robotics and open-ended problem-based activities can broaden perceptions of computi ng. We found some students expanded their views of CS to include building, designing and creativity. This outcome was not always the case, where we fo und that many students’ views did not change. In particular, not many stude nts seemed to have pre-existing ideas about building and design in CS, and our program was not able to change those perceptions. The biggest area of persistent perceptions seemed to be around positive and negati ve feelings about CS. We believe that the high frequency of students reporting their feelings about CS when asked if their views of CS had changed demonstrates how tightly connected these feelings are to percept ions about what CS is. This finding is consistent w ith prior work in CS education that ties views of CS to motiv ation and stereotypes about  CS (Master et al., 2024). Therefore, we feel there is a need to continue to make more explicit connections for students about computing as a problem-solving and creative discipline that allows them to address societal and communal problems.
