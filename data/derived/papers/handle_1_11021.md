# Different Sub-Domains of Biology May Be Influencing Practices in Simulated Authentic Science Inquiry

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Epistemological beliefs, defined as cognition about knowledge, is important for science education. Despite this importance, few studies explored epistemological beliefs about science among undergraduate students in the context of biology and none examine potential differences between subdisciplines of biology. This paper investigates how epistemological beliefs about science could differ between two biology subdomains, ecology, and molecular biology, among first year life science students. Initial findings indicate that different sub-domains of biology may influence epistemological beliefs.

## Introduction

In recent years, demand for STEM jobs increased. However, retention rates of students pursuing a STEM degree are not keeping up with job market demand (Lucas and Spina, 2022). Previous work suggests that Epistemological Beliefs About Science (EBAS), which are beliefs about the nature of science knowledge, and how that knowledge is generated during inquiry (Peffer et al., 2020), could be related to lack of retention in STEM (Reim er et al., 2016). Evaluating the educational experiences that facilitate the dev elopment of sophisticated EBAS could be used to improve science education and ultimately improve STEM retention rates. Since beliefs cannot be directly observed, indirect metrics must be used  to assess EBAS. A possible method is to examine student practices in the context of s imulated authentic science inquiry which could refl ect underlying epistemological beliefs  (Peffer et al., 2020). Although the value of EBAS for science education is irrefutable, there are few studies exploring EBAS knowledge a mong undergraduates. Of these studies, there are few in the context of biology, and none between subdisciplines of biology. Given the lack of studies, we examined EBAS as seen through SCI simulation practices between simulations from two subdomains of biology.

## Methods

Participants for this study (n = 38) were undergrad uate life science students enrolled in introductory  biology courses. Most participants were female (63%). The predominant ethnic group was Caucasian (71%). Participants were mainly freshmen (81.5%). Between the two test groups the d emographic distribution was statistically equivalent.

Since non-epistemological factors like self-efficacy can influence EBAS (Peffer et al., 2020), participants first completed the Motivated Strategies for Learning Que stionnaire (MSLQ) (Erturan Ilker & Demirhan, 2014). The MSLQ includes 81 items divided into 15 subscales about student’s motivation and use of learning strategies. The MSLQ was coded as described in the scoring manu al and a mean score for each subset was calculated.  The average values of each subset were compared for the two datasets using a t-test. After the pre-test, students then completed either a field-based ecology SCI simulation, Invasion of the Grackles (Peffer et al., 2020) or a new lab-based molecular biology simulation called Cracking the Next Pandemic. In each simulation, participants were given an auth entic science problem and asked to investigate pote ntial explanations for a phenomenon.   19 participants completed Invasion of the Grackles  while 19 participants completed Cracking the Next Pandemic. Participants were assigned to each simulat ion randomly. The SCI simulation application captured written responses to in simulation prompts and clickstream data. We counted the number of investigative actions, information seekin g actions, total actions each participant completed  and coded if the subject completed a simple or complex investigation following the guidelines in Peffer et al, 2020.  To avoid potential stereotype threat, participants completed a demographic survey upon completion of the simulation.

## Results

We observed no difference in the majority of motivation subscales between the two populations. We observed statistically significant differences a mong the average number of information seeking actions, average number of tests, and average amoun t of total action, between individuals who took Invasion of the Grackles and individuals who took Cracking the Next Pandemic. ## Discussion

The demand for STEM employees has already outpaced the number of college graduates with a STEM degree (Lucas and Spina, 2022). However, a possible and pr omising route to combatting this problem is to impr ove science education overall (Reimer et al., 2016).  Since EBAS influences how students learn in a science classroom (Peffer and Ramezani, 2019), it is important to understand how EBAS are  conceptualized within biology and between biology subdisciplines.

We noted that there was a difference in inquiry pra ctices between the two simulations. Those who took the field-based Invasion of the Grackles simulation were more likely to make a conclusion af ter finding one correlation between a test result and the question at hand. Those who took the lab-based Cracking the Next Pandemic simulation were more likely to complete more inform ation seeking actions, perform more tests, and overall complete a more complex investigation. This means that individuals were more likely to try to understand the problem by connecting information together in a way that tells an interconnected story. These differences were not the result of baseline differences in motivation or learning strategies as assessed using the MSLQ. Therefore, it may be that the subdomain of the simulation could influence the types of practices that we are seeing. Our data suggests that the subjects’ number of testing actions or investig ative actions did not seem to have a significant bearing on determining the sophistication of that subject’s investigation. Rather, the way they created hypotheses, came to conclusions, and rationalized their approac h to the simulation seemed to be a better indicatio n. Participants taking Cracking the Next Pandemic showed more nuanced and thoughtful approaches to th e simulation. Therefore, it could be that different contexts causes students to engage in a more sophisticated manner, which influences our interpretation on EBAS via practices. One of the limitations of our study is the sample size. With 3 8 subjects, we cannot say for certain that our data is indicative of the undergraduate population. Additionally, there may be other factors in play that explain the subjects’ differences in practices during the simulation, such as their understanding about the nature of science. In future work, we will look at other factors that could explain the differences in practices between the two simulations. We will use interviews that will  be directly after a subject completes the simulation t o qualitatively assess students’ EBAS and interrogate how these beliefs may influence inquiry practices.
