# Extending CSCL Affordances for Multilingual Classrooms: Teacher Perspectives on AI -Supported Collaboration

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Multilingual classrooms add layers of complexity to collaboration: teachers rarely share every student’s home language, peers struggle to build common ground, and AI translation can miss linguistics nuance. We report findings from focus groups conducted with teachers of multilingual students to gauge their view on AI-supported collaboration across three collaboration pathways: student-student, teacher-student, and student-AI. Teachers welcomed real-time subtitling/translation to support comprehension and questioning, and lightweight tools that surface peers’ ideas via transcripts and highlights for clarifications. They were skeptical of in-class 1:1 student-AI partnering, citing risks to social connection, teacher workload, and scope creep. Using Jeong & Hmelo-Silver’s seven affordances as our lens, we identify where affordances blend within pathways, and where they need rethinking for multilingual equity. We translate teacher concerns into CSCL-aligned design adaptations and contribute extended versions of affordances to guide AI-integrated CSCL that is equitable for linguistically diverse classrooms.

Collaborative learning positions knowledge building as a social process in which learners co-construct understanding through discourse, shared artifacts, and interdependence (Smith & MacGregor, 1992). Decades of Computer Supported Collaborative Learning (C SCL) research demonstrate that well-orchestrated collaboration supported with appropriate tools, scaffolds, and assessment improve achievement and engagement across domains (Chen et al., 2018).

In our work, we conceptualize collaboration as manifesting along three complementary interactional pathways: (1) student-student collaboration, in the form of small group discussions, problem solving, and knowledge co-construction, (2) teacher-student collaboration, in the form of teachers engaging with and encouraging student ideas, teachers providing conceptual guidance and resources, and students asking clarifying questions, and (3) emerging student-AI collaborations, in the form of students engaging with agents, robots or chatbots for self-regulated learning. Student-student collaboration leverages peer explanation and shared regulation to deepen reasoning (Järvelä et al, 2018). Teacher-student exchanges remain foundational for framing tasks, diagnosing understanding, and apprenticing disciplinary talk (Erkan, 2019). Finally, as conversational agents enter classrooms, there is growing interest in the role of generative AI as a participant that may facilitate brainstorming, feedback, orchestration and knowledge co-construction with human learners (Benson, 2023; Wei et al., 2025; Choi et al., 2025).

Nevertheless, these collaborations are hard to enact equitably in multilingual classrooms. Teachers often do not share students’ home languages; peers may not understand one another; and tools that translate words can still miss cultural nuance, dialectal variation, and the multimodal ways in which students “language” ideas (Barron et. al., 2025). Teachers worry about over-reliance on translation, amplification of safety risks in low-resource languages, and the practical burden of monitoring many groups simultaneously (Guha et al., 2025a). At the same time, they see promise in valuing students’ full linguistic repertoires through strategic use of their home and classroom languages, visuals, and multimodal resources, if supported by technology and human media tion to preserve pedagogical intent (Guha et al., 2025b).

CSCL literature provides several useful frameworks for addressing these tensions but the intersection of AI-integrated CSCL and multilingual settings remains underexplored. Early studies suggest AI can influence team processes and creativity, but they also underscore risks to equity, agency, and discourse quality if designs are not carefully constrained (Wei et al., 2025). We therefore adopt Jeong & Hmelo-Silver’s (2016) seven affordances of CSCL as our analytic lens, which articulates how technology can enable and shape productive collaboration. The seven interdependent affordances defined by Jeong & Hmelo-Silver that together capture the social, cognitive, and technological mechanisms of collaborative learning are: (1) Establishing and understanding joint tasks  (i.e., ensuring learners share goals and awareness of the activity), (2) Communication (i.e., supporting the exchange of ideas and questions), (3) Resource sharing (i.e., enabling access to information, representations, and artifacts), (4) 274 Engaging in productive processes (i.e., facilitating collective reasoning, elaboration, and reflection), (5) Engaging in co-construction (i.e., coordinating meaning-making and knowledge building), (6) Monitoring/regulation  (i.e., sustaining shared awareness and self-regulation across participants), and (7) Finding and building community (i.e. fostering sustained relationships and collaboration networks). We adopted this framework because it is technology-agnostic, spans specific mechanisms of collaborative learning (e.g., discourse moves, shared artifacts, regulation), and gives us a principled way to trace how technology can support collaboration across our three collaboration pathways: teacher-student, student-student, and student-AI interactions. Building  on prior CSCL literature, this framework provides a structured lens to examine and extend CSCL affordances for multilingual contexts. We also drew on Wise and Schwarz’s (2017) provocations for the future of CSCL as a conceptual backdrop when framing our s tudy and interpreting its implications. Their provocations challenge the field to reimagine how collaborative learning research evolves amid shifting technologies and sociocultural conditions. In particular, their calls to re-center learner agency (Provocation #2), emphasize contextual adaptation and alignment with real-world practices (Provocation #4), and reaffirm teacher-mediated orchestration as the anchor of technological innovation (Provocation #8) guided our conceptual orientation. Partnering with teachers who navigate local curricula and students’ linguistic/cultural histories in multilingual classrooms, in this paper, we examine AI’s role across teacher-student, student-student, and studentAI collaborations. We synthesize teachers ’ views on the appropriate use of AI, evaluate how Jeong & HmeloSilver’s seven affordances manifest in teachers’ concerns and extend them where reinterpretation is required. Finally, we surface concrete design adaptations that make CSCL practices linguist ically inclusive by allowing students access to their home language in collaborative learning, while supporting teacher oversight on safe use of AI and students’ development of classroom language. In summary, we show how the original CSCL affordances from Jeong & Hmelo-Silver need to be extended for linguistically diverse classrooms. Building on a survey study conducted by Guha et. al., 2025a, Guha et. al. 2025b with teachers of multilingual learners, we identified real-world representative scenarios of multilingual classrooms that may struggle with the three mentioned collaboration pathways. Taking inspiration from teacher responses to open-ended questions, we also developed potential AI solutions as a point of deliberation for further discussion on AI affordances and concerns regarding AI support for collaboration in multilingual classrooms. For student group collaboration, we presented a tool that can provide translations during small group discussions supported by AI. Students can use the tool to highlight comments from peers for clarification and teachers can view the students’ conversation transcripts to ask the tool questions about what was discussed (Tool #1). For student-teacher collaboration supported by AI, we presented a tool that can provide all students real-time translations of teacher’s speech during a whole group lecture and the reverse translation of student speech to teachers for when the student would like to ask a clarifying question (Tool #2).  For student-AI-teacher collaboration, we presented a 1:1 AI conversational partner that can support students in understanding a concept and communicating in the language of instruction; the teacher can later view the transcript and understand where the student may be struggling (Tool #3).

The study was IRB approved and recruitment was conducted by emailing teachers who participated in the initial survey study. We conducted 4 focus groups with 15 teachers all of whom taught multilingual students. Each focus group was 60 minutes and consisted of 3-4 teachers. Each scenario and solution was presented through google slides with some guiding questions and discussed by at least 2 groups of teachers. 13 of these teachers were teaching in the United States, the other 2 were in Brazil and Indonesia respectively. 6 teachers were English support teachers (e.g. English as Second Language (ESL), while the others taught content areas such as Science, Social Studies, Math, English, Kichwa language studies, Life Sciences, and Information Technology. There wa s a balance across grade levels such that we had 4 high school teachers, 3 middle school teachers, 3 kindergarten/elementary school teachers and 2 teaching assistants at the post-secondary level. To analyze the focus group data, we followed the affinity diagramming method as described by Lawrence and colleagues (2023). We analyzed 119 data points and inductively clustered feedback on presented scenarios into concerns and design adaptations. To achieve reliability, two researchers analyzed the focus group transcripts from which data unique quotes were extracted as data points. Two additional researchers analyzed data points negotiating themes and reviewing the data until consensus was reached. Throug h an iterative process of clustering, two researchers leveraged those themes to identify both unique and common concerns and design ideas for each collaboration type.

We frame our findings using the seven CSCL affordances proposed by Jeong & Hmelo-Silver (2016) after extending it to use of AI to support collaborative learning in  multilingual and multicultural classroom contexts 275 as: (1) establishing understanding of collaborative tasks, (2) communicating multilingually, (3) sharing resources in students’ home and classroom language, (4) structuring language-scaffolded productive processes, (5) facilitating co-construction in students’ home and classroom language, (6) monitoring/regulation, and (7) finding and building inclusive groups and communities. While Jeong & Hmelo-Silver (2016)’s CSCL affordances describe what collaboration technologies should enable, our focus groups revealed how those affordances must be re-interpreted in multilingual, AI-mediated classrooms from teachers’ standpoint. We further describe how these newly defined affordances show-up along three collaboration pathways: student-student, teacher-student, and student-AI-teacher, showing how teachers envision AI not as a replacement for human roles but as a supporting tool that scaffolds comprehension, connection, and linguistic growth. Student group collaboration supported by AI Jeong & Hmelo-Silver’s framework begins with the affordance of communication, which students build on to engage in productive processes and co-construction of knowledge. This is followed by students’ ability to monitor/regulate their own collaborative process. Teachers described small-group dialogue as an essential practice in multilingual classrooms to support peer learning and language growth yet challenging to put into practice. One teacher noted this by saying, “we don't all have a common language, we can't really converse [by] speaking, and so I think that's our biggest barrier”. Therefore, they welcomed the idea of a transcripts-and-highlighting tool (Tool #1; see Methods section) to facilitate discussions and understanding across languages. building inclusive groups and communities + Communicating multilingually Student-AI-teacher collaboration Jeong & Hmelo-Silver’s framework goes beyond supporting productive processes, sharing resources, and facilitating co-construction to also assist students in independently finding and building groups and communities for future collaboration. This framework originally assumed collaboration among students or students and agents/robots, however, based on teacher interactions our final collaboration pathway extends this idea, considering the potential of generative AI as becoming an intermediary participant betw een teacher and student collaboration. Teachers in our focus groups were skeptical of 1:1 conversational AI that assists their students asynchronously and report to teachers post hoc (Tool #3; see Methods section). The tool was meant to introduce 278 the possibility of personalized support in multilingual classrooms where it may be difficult for teachers to give students individual attention during class time.

Teachers highlighted new demands when AI becomes an intermediary participant between student-teacher collaboration. They endorse targeted 1:1 support by AI only if it (a) maintains in-class engagement and student-student collaboration, (b) constrains content scope (clarifies misconceptions without teaching new content), (c) reports back to the teacher succinctly with evidence quotes, and (d) protects social r elationships (avoids isolation from teacher/peers). The student-AI-teacher scenario thus reframes AI as a teacher-bounded participant in student learning. While AI directly engages with students through 1:1 interactions, its role is shaped and constrained by teacher-defined expectations. Design adaptations such as summaries, “ask-teacher” flags, controlled content scope, and restricting AI use to flipped classroom settings (Table 3) bound how AI participates—limiting what it can introduce, when it should gu ide students to the teacher, and how it maintains visibility for teacher oversight. In this way, AI collaborates with students, without displacing teacher authority or peer interaction, instead supporting and channeling student engagement within structured  limits. Finally, managing developmental fit called attention to differences in how younger versus older learners interact with technology. Teachers stated that younger students may benefit from simplified interfaces, limited features, and focus guards that reduce distraction, while older students might engage more confidently through text-based rehearsal spaces before speaking. Together, these themes call for rethinking how AI supports collaboration through culturally responsive, teacher-mediated, and developmentally attuned design. In summary, across the three collaboration pathways i.e., teacher-student, student-student, and studentAI-teacher interactions, we found that teachers believe AI can best support multilingual collaboration when it is responsive to the cultural and linguistic nuances of multilingual classrooms, keeps teachers in control of how it is used, and adapts to learners’ developmental needs. Together, these refinements position our results as a practical re-specification of Jeong & Hmelo-Silver’s framework for the m ultilingual, AI-enabled era of collaborative learning.

Our findings on teacher perceptions of AI support for collaborative learning in multilingual classrooms resonate with core CSCL affordances as proposed by Jeong & Hmelo-Silver (2016), while extending them to the realities of multilingual classrooms. Research in collaborative learning shows that outcomes improve when roles, scripts, shared artifacts, and assessment are intentionally designed (Chen et al., 2018; Miller & Hadwin, 2015; Smith & MacGregor, 1992). Our proposed design adaptations like teacher-facing controls, role rotations, and context-aware knowledge bases echo these results. Additionally, features recommendations such as curriculum-locked corpora and teacher-curated glossaries extend existing affordances as defined by Jeong & Hmelo-Silver (2016) into multilingual contexts where both communication and comprehension must be mediated across languages. In our study, we consistently treated collaboration as having three facets: student-student, teacher-student and student-AI, but the teachers stressed that the student-AI case cannot stand alone. The need for teacher oversight is consistent with prior work that emphasizes that orchestration, monitoring, and adaptive guidance by teachers remain central to productive collaborative learning, particularly in technology-mediated environments (Holstein et al., 2019; van Leeuwen, 2015). Therefore, even though the initial scenario imagined an emerging student-AI partnership, teachers asked for human oversight, converting it into student-AI-teacher collaboration, modifying AI’s role to become a teacher-bounded participant (instead of just a tool or an autonomous participant). Their reasons were practical and relational: keeping classroom relationships intact, preventing scope drift intonew content, and ensuring any help returned to the teacher with short evidence quotes and an invitation for followup. This preference does not reject AI support but situates it. Teachers also favored targeted, time-boxed use of AI that prepares learners to engage with peers without AI presence but with clearer questions and fewer misconceptions.

In line with the Extended CSCL Affordances in Table 1, 2, and 3, teachers consistently emphasized and repeatedly brought up topics that mapped to certain affordances like: communicating multilingually  (in-themoment translation and clarify moves), structuring language-scaffolded productive processes (role rotation, turntaking queues, sentence starters, and light on-task nudges), facilitating co-construction (transcripts that show who said what so peers can build shared understanding), sharing resources in students’ home and classroom language (simpler definitions, visuals, cognate cues). However, affordances such as establishing understanding of collaborative tasks, and finding and building inclusive groups and communities  appeared less often in teachers’ reasoning around the different scenarios. This reflects immediate priorities around language access, comprehension, and orchestration control. Evaluating whether the narrowed focus on aforementioned immediate priorities strengthens or limits equitable collaboration requires further empirical work. Including students’ perspectives and classroom enactments in empirical studies may surface needs for affordances teachers did not explicitly identify. Including students alongside  teachers in the co-design process can also be helpful for understanding whether and how underrepresented affordances like finding and building communities or establishing collaborative tasks can be cultivated as classrooms mature in their use of AI. 280 Additionally, this work concretely responds to several provocations articulated by Wise and Schwarz

(2017) in “Visions of CSCL: eight provocations for the future of the field”. In addressing, Provocation #2

(prioritizing learner agency over collaborative scripting), teachers consistently rejected heavy scripting (i.e. associating groups of learners with specific tasks, roles, or resources, and/or constraining the mode of interaction among peers) or fully automated scaffolding, preferring “scaffold-then-fade” structures that respect both learner initiative and teacher judgment. Across collaboration types, the principle of design for guided autonomy was the same. In student-student collaboration, this took the form of light peer facilitation features such as t urn-taking queues, role rotation, and “clarify” buttons, that preserve conversational choice while ensuring mutual understanding across languages. In teacher-student interactions, 1:1 translation support and language scaffold recommendations were thought to help comprehension without replacing humandialogue. Lastly, in student-AI personalized support, use of AI in flipped  classroom settings  and “ask-teacher” flags protect classroom relationships from over-automation. Together, these adaptations reconcile agency and structure through co-regulation rather than control, advancing Wise and Schwarz’s vision of learner-centered yet coordinated collaboration.

Our results also respond to Provocation #6 (learning analytics and adaptive support) by grounding adaptation in teacher mediation. Teachers in our study wanted analytics that guide, not replace their professional judgment. Mechanisms like evidence-bearing summaries with quote snippets from student-student or student-AI alignment, ensuring that what adapts, when, and how remains under human oversight. The shift from generic personalization to instructionally aligned adaptation turns Wise and Schwarz’s abstract call for “adaptive support” into a concrete, teacher-controlled reality. The convergence of these responses underscores that CSCL frameworks can be constructed bottom-up, grounded in knowledge of what teachers actually do to manage learning rather than from broad, top-down frameworks.

Finally, our results connect directly to Provocation #8 (CSCL should give up on educational change), not by abandoning improvement, but by relocating change to orchestration-in-practice. Rather than promising sweeping reform, teachers in our study mention small, durable moves that travel across teacher-student, studentstudent, and student-AI-teacher contexts like curriculum-locked corpora, teacher-curated glossaries, evidencebearing summaries, 1:1 constrained use of in-class translation tool, timeboxing, and show/hide/approve controls. In this view, equity is produced by defaults and routines, not declarations, through linguistically-aware workflows and progressive fading of AI support that value home languages for sense-making while safeguarding long-term growth in the language of instruction. This is changing at the scale of daily practice: credible, sustainable, and owned by teachers.

## Discussion & Implications

In conclusion, the implications of this work extend to two communities. Learning designers should work with teachers and students to embed design adaptations mentioned here into different collaboration contexts. Researchers in natural language processing and speech technology must simultaneously advance models along the dimensions teachers identified as essential: stronger support for low-resource languages and dialects; speech systems that work well with many accents and with students who have speech disabilities; explanations that focus on ideas rather than word-for-word translation; and clear indicators when the system is unsure. Progress on both fronts is required to make CSCL’s promise credible and equitable in linguistically diverse classrooms. In doing so, we not only extend Jeong & Hmelo-Silver’s framework into multilingual, AI-mediated contexts but also materialize Wise & Schwarz’s provocations, showing how unity, agency, and adaptive support can be achieved through situated, teacher-driven design.
