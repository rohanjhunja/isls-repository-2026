# The Practitioner's Voice in Co-Design: Agentic Boundary Crossing

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Practitioner knowledge is essential to the success of co-design teams. This case study reports on a teacher’s role within a design-based research team, over four projects spanning a decade. Using a boundary crossing lens, the analysis describes the change from passive to active co-designer involving the use of boundary objects and agentic actions. Findings contribute to the growing literature on teachers as boundary spanners and their roles in making educational change sustainable.

## Introduction

Co-design is defined as a "highly facilitated, team-based process” involving researchers, teachers, developers and other educational stakeholders in the exploration a nd creation of curricular and technological innovat ions (Roschelle, et al., 2006, p.606). It is inherently iterative and context sensitive, w ith its participatory nature positioning it as a core mechanism in praxis-driven approaches, including researcher-practitioner partnerships (RPP; Coburn, et al., 2013; Sjölund et al., 2023), teacher-researcher collaborations (Gomez et al., 2018), and design-based research (DBR; Askela, 2019). Viewed through the framework of RPP, co-design strives to create usable and relevant solutions to problems of practi ce, while from the DBR perspective it aims to gener ate theoretical insights and knowledge about learning, teaching, and enactment. Further, co-design is characterized as a mutualistic process, often involving mutual appropriation and mutual learning, where a likely outcome of the interactions is the transformation of the partners (Farrell et al., 2020). As a socio-cultural process, it relies on submechanisms that establish shared meaning and values through intentional strategies––such as defining rules, roles, routines, and protocols––to support effective collaboration (Coburn & Penuel, 2016). Traditionally, teachers in co-design play roles centered on identifying, framing, prototyping, refining and implementing innovations (Coburn et al., 2013). Inc reasingly, however, these roles are seen as limitin g the sustainability and scalability of change, as they u nderutilize the context-sensitive expertise of practitioners (Jarl et al., 2024). Shifting from a theory-to-practice model that privileges researchers to one o f equity partnership presents challenges (Gomez et al., 2018). As Penuel  et al. (2013)  state, success requires building bridges “more than halfway across the cultural divide” (p. 252). While recent studies have begun to describe the evo lution of practitioners’ roles (Sjölund et al., 2023), few have documented h ow symmetrical partnerships are formed ––and even fewer are told through the voice of the teacher (Jarl et al., 2024). This study seeks to fill that gap by examining a case study of one teacher’s 10-year trajectory within a DBR team. Told in his own voice,  this study explores: How did a teacher’s role evolve within a design team, and what impact did this have on his practice?

## Background

This study uses boundary crossing as a lens to analyze the data and highlight the agentic actions that mediated the teacher’s transformation. Boundary crossing is a me chanism that supports co-design (Akkerman & Baker, 2011) by involving four sub-processes: identification (appropriating values), coordination (sharing routines), reflection (confronting assumptions), and transformation (impa cting interventions). Individuals who move across boundaries are known as boundary spanners, and thei r roles may shift over the course of a project (Cob urn & Penuel, 2016). Boundary objects––such as curriculum materials––can mediate collaboration by fostering common ground and enabling coordination (Akkerman & Baker, 2011).

## Methods

We use a case study methodology to examine the teac her’s 10-year (2014 to 2024) experience within a DBR team––a group composed of 2-3 learning scientists and 3-5 college-level practitioners, primarily physics teachers. The data corpus includes the teacher’s personal mee ting notes, classroom artifacts, interviews and rec ent reflections prepared for this study. These were collected during four DBR projects ––each a three-year investigation focused on a distinct design challeng e––and retrospectively during the writing of this stud y. Data analysis began with the chronological organization of the teacher’s journal notes, followed by the ide ntification of key pivotal points as recalled by the teacher (first author) and researcher (second author). These moments were analyzed using a boundary crossing framework to identify episodes of identification, coordination, reflection, and transformation, and to examine how his role evolved  over time. Actions interpreted as boundary spannin g were also explored. This narrative analysis approach, grounded in thick description (Geertz, 2008), allowed us to trace the development of practitioner agency and role redefinition across multiple design cycles.

## Case study analysis and discussion

Project 1, which investigated the impact of a peer-instruction platform on physics learning, marks the  beginning of the teacher’s trajectory and illustrates the process of identification. Although not yet an official member of the DBR team, the teacher was  invited by a colleague ––his officemate and one of the practitioners ––to deploy an instance of the intervention. This initial involvem ent led to regular meetings, which became part of his routine. Reflecting on this period, the teacher described his role as ensuring the faithful reproduction of the “physics-based activity script”. However, a turning point occurred  when his colleague introduced visualizations of students’ conceptual understanding––artifacts that served as boundary objects. These mediated their conversations, shifting the teacher’s focus from enacting the intervention with fidelity to a deeper interest in the research intention: students’ conceptual change.

Project 2, which explored the implications of colla boration within the joint problem spaces of shared artifacts, marked the next stage in the teacher’s t rajectory and illustrates the added process of coor dination. Initially, the teacher resumed his origina l role of implementing the intervention. However, d uring ethnographic classroom observations, he began offering the resea rchers practical insights into his classroom cultur e––insights that improved students’ uptake of the intervention. Consequently, he was formally invited to join the weekly DBR meetings as one of the practitioners. Reflecting on this change, he described his role as that of an advisor, helping the team adapt and customize the activity script to  better fit the classroom context, while maintainin g fidelity of the designed enactment. We interpret this as an exp ansion to his role rather than a shift in the perceived  role, which occurred in the subsequent project.

Project 3, which investigated a peer feedback instructional approach, marks a clear shift in the teacher’s role and illustrates the process of transformation ––one of the boundary crossing mechanisms. By this st age, he was a regular attendee in the weekly DBR meetings, and the data––meeting minutes and classroom notes––reveals his growing appropriation of the team’s cultural co nstructs and vocabulary. These documents also show his increasing understanding of the team’s expectations, as he contributed insight s that were discussed and occasionally integrated into the intervention activity scripts. A pivotal moment occurred during a research meeting when the teacher recalls asking: “How do you think this script will be implemented in our classes?” to which the researcher responded: “Tell me how you think this should be implemented in your class?” We interpret this as an agentic move, one that was accepted by the team and reflected their recognition of the importance of his contextbased insights. The teacher iden tifies this exchange as the first time he truly fel t part of the DBR team. We also view the researcher’s response as an act of boundar y spanning, demonstrating what Farrell  et al., (2022) call absorptive capacity ––the ability to recognize and support the integratio n of practitioner knowledge into collaborative design work. In short, this marked th e teacher’s transformation from a role focused on t he goal of achieving fidelity or appropriation to one of shared responsibility, characterized by mutual learning and co-design. Project 4, which focused on designing scaffolds to support inquiry-based labs, illustrates the process of reflection, as the teacher expanded his involvement  to include additional aspects of DBR work ––examining and comparing implementations, refining methods, etc. He recalls, “I realized I had a dual role: at times reflecting on the scripts more as researcher, and less as practitioner––putting on opposing lenses.” The data also indicate a shift in his self-perception, particularly in his academic communications, where he demonstrated a growing awareness of legitimacy––not just as a knowledge sharer, but as a knowledge-builder.

## Conclusion

This case study offers key insights into the design and analysis of co-design work. The teacher’s evolving role reflects the four functions identified in Jarl et al. (2024): teacher as active agents in data collection, collaborative partners in reflection and interpretation, contribu tors to knowledge dissemination, and potential prac titionerresearchers. The mechanisms of change —identification, coordination, transformation, and r eflection—were not linear but recursive, enabled by the team’s recepti vity and trust. Notably, boundary crossing was faci litated by dialogical spaces ––weekly meetings and classroo m––where the teacher moved beyond implementation to question, interpret, and co-construct the meaning of new practices. The researchers’ boundary-spanning role also highlights the importance of sharing authority and positioning practitioners as equal partn ers. The teacher’s reflexive stance––straddling roles as both self-observer and the observed ––enabled his shift from enactor to co-designer. These findings suggest that embedding early opportunities for practitioner reflection and role expansion may be critical to fostering symmetrical participation and sustaining scalable innovation in educational design.
