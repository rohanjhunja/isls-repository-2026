# Exploring Preschoolers’ Engagement with Intelligent Agents (IA) Mediated by IA Features and Teacher Scaffolding

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
As intelligent agents (IAs) become increasingly integrated into young children's lives, it is crucial to understand how children perceive and interact with these technologies. We designed and implemented an IA-centered curriculum in a PreK classroom featuring three robots (GO1, Spot1, and Astro). We apply interaction analysis to unpack how IAs and teachers’ scaffolding facilitate children's understanding of technology. Our findings reveal that IA features (e.g., appearance, functionality, and intelligence levels), combined with teacher facilitation strategies (e.g., revoicing, explicit explanation, and thoughtful questioning), act as mediators shaping children's conceptual understanding and interactions with IAs. These mediations foster young children's sensory exploration, reasoning, and adaptive behaviors with technology. We recommend that future designers incorporate diverse IA features to enhance young children's learning opportunities about technology and its limitations. We also encourage educators to thoughtfully integrate IAs into teaching practices to support young children’s developmentally appropriate growth in this increasingly digital society.

## Introduction

Intelligent agents (IAs) have become ubiquitous in daily life, ranging from voice assistants such as Alexa and Siri to household robots like Roomba (Andries & Robertso n, 2023; Pew Research, 2020). These technologies sh ape young children’s cognitive development by supporting AI and computational thinking skills (Williams et al., 2019) and foster non-cognitive skills like creativity, collaboration, and adaptability (Kewalramani et al., 2021), playing a pivotal role in their early experiences (Su & Yan g, 2022). Early and frequent exposure to such techn ological environments further increases the likelihood that children will engage with IAs throughout their live s, shaping how they learn, communicate, and interact with the world (Li et al., 2023). Therefore, it is essential  to support children in understanding and interacting criticall y with IAs to help them adapt to an increasingly di gital society (Andries & Robertson, 2023).

Children’s conceptual understanding emerges through  interactions with their social, cultural, and physical environments (Barsalou, 2010). Previous studies have found that the features of IAs distinctly shape how children perceive and interact with them (F lanagan et al., 2023). For instance, Wright et al. (2022) found that children’s engagement with IAs varies significantly  based on the IAs’ interaction capabilities, while Chin et al.

(2024) demonstrated that children adapt their inter action behaviors depending on the agent’s features.  Robot

appearance, such as animal-like robots, is often perceived as existing between living beings and objects (Reinecke et al., 2021). Children's understanding of robots b lends companionship with control, which will shape how they interact with technology and build social relationships in the future (Kahn et al., 2013). Additionally, some studies found that caregivers' support influences children' s understanding of IAs (Tolksdorf et al., 2021). St rategies through rephrasing and asking questions can scaffold young children to engage in higher-level cognitive processes while learning from IA (Wang et al., 2023). These f indings underscore the mediating role of IA feature s and teachers’ guidance in shaping children’s attachments, social responses, conceptual understanding, and interactions with IAs Despite these insights, most existing studies have been conducted in controlled laboratory settings, which may not fully capture the complexity and dynamics o f classrooms. While some research has designed IA-based curricula for young children using various features (Williams et al., 2019), there is limite d discussion on how IA characteristics combined with teachers’ scaffolding  influence children’s understanding of the concepts  and technologies behind IAs (Li et al., 2023). Additionally, recent advancements in IAs have introduced more complex features that have received limited attention, incl uding semi-or fully automatic responses, adaptable behaviors, social functionalities, and proactive engagement (D ruga et al., 2018). To address these gaps, this stu dy explores the following research question: How do different types of IAs and teacher scaffoldi ng mediate young children’s interactions with and understanding of IAs in a pre-K classroom?

## Theoretical framework

Our theoretical framework draws on Vygotsky’s Media tion Theory (1978), which posits that young childre n’s learning and cognitive development occur within soc ially and culturally mediated processes. Cultural a rtifacts such as tools, language, and symbols act as mediators in sh aping children’s understanding and interactions wit h the learning environment, effectively bridging thei r internal learning processes with external experie nces (Vygotsky, 1978). In technology-enhanced learning environments, these mediating influences are e nriched by feedback from technology, teachers, and peers (Keifert et al., 2020). Specifically, technological artifacts serve as mediators in shaping learning and understanding by providing new opportunitie s for young children’s activities (Tu et al., 2020). In addition, diverse individuals  in learning communities, such as children and teac hers in the classroom, collaboratively share their understandin g, which helps in the co-construction of knowledge (Resni ck et al., 2018). Teachers play a key role in scaffold ing young children’s learning in meaningful ways, e mploying strategies such as dialogue, age-appropriate feedback, and responsiveness in decision-making (Wang et al., 2023). These psychological tools significantly influence the flow and structure of the learning process (Vygotsky, 1981). Building on this sociocultural perspective, we reco gnize the vital role of cultural artifacts in media ting human understanding within the technological environment. In this study, we explore how technological tools, such as IAs with distinct materials, functions, and levels of perceived intelligence, act as mediators to reshape and facilitate young children’s social practices. Simul taneously, we explore how teacher scaffolding throu gh their language serves as a vital mediator in guiding chil dren’s engagement with IAs in developmentally appro priate and meaningful ways. These mediating factors collec tively create a dynamic learning environment, enabl ing learners to co-construct knowledge alongside peers and teachers (K ewalramani et al., 2021). Through social interactions, young children adapt and refine their understanding of IAs by integrating cognitive and s ocial processes within their classroom technological activities. ## Research design

This study is part of the larger project “Exploring Young Children’s Perceptions and Interac tions of Robots”, conducted at a university-affiliated early childhood center in the Northeast U.S. The project explores how to support young children learning about IAs through a  customized curriculum designed to resemble regular  early childhood routines for relevance and accessibility.  We implemented the curriculum every Wednesday for eight weeks, with each session lasting about 20 minutes. In collaboration with a robotics lab in the Northea st U.S., we arranged three IAs (two dog-like robots and a chatbot) selected for their diverse designs, materials, functions, and intelligence levels to enrich children’s understanding of IAs. To align with young children’s developmental stages, we designed the curriculums by following the Five B ig Ideas from AI4K-12.org and the New Yor k State Prekindergarten Learning Standards (New York State Education Department, 2021). ## Participants

In this study, we invited twelve young children (fi ve girls and seven boys) from a pre-kindergarten (Pre-K) classroom. Two classroom teachers were involved in the study. We use Teacher A and Teacher B to refer to the participating teachers: Teacher A has two years of teaching experience; Teacher B has three years of t eaching experience. There were also two to three intern teachers in the activities. Two to three graduate research assistants were present in the classroom to manage video recor ding and assist with distributing materials. Additionally, the study employed the Wizard of Oz approach to ensure transparency in the operation of the IAs. This appr oach involved an operator (a graduate assistant) who controlled the robot, guided the children during malfunctions, and provided feedback as a co-teacher to supplement the teacher’s limited familiarity with the IAs’ capabilities.

## Data collection and analysis

All class activities were video recorded, with fiel d notes, memos, and supplementary materials include d.  We applied interaction analysis (Jordan & Henderson, 1995) to explore children's engagement with different IAs. We start the analysis by generating a content log, and  then the lead author reviews recordings to identif y moments relevant to our research questions and organizes th ese moments in the shared workbook. After identifyi ng foci clips, we conducted collaborative video review sess ions with all authors to perform a detailed analysi s of how children’s interactions varied based on the IAs' ap pearance, function, and levels of intelligence. Thi s led us to identify key cases across sessions to analyze behav ioral differences. Finally, we select three clips f rom focal sessions to present in this paper: Case 1 captured children’s initial interactions with Go1, Case 2 highlighted their understanding of Spot's sensors in sessions 3 and 6, and Case 3 covered their engagement with Astro in  sessions 7 and 8. The following analysis presents these cases, exp loring observed differences and patterns related to  our research questions.

## Findings

Across eight sessions of interacting with different IAs, we found that children’s understanding varied and shifted based on each IA’s unique features: appearance, mat erial properties, functionality, and intelligence l evel. In addition, teachers’ scaffoldin g played a significant role in mediating young chil dren’s understanding and interaction with IAs. These findings align with Vygotsky’s Mediation Theory (1978), which suggests that learning is shaped by cultural artifacts that children inter act with. To illustrate this alignment, we selected three cases for detailed analysis. In each case, we first present t he interactions within the activities, then connect  them to the Mediation Theory (Vygotsky, 1978) to analyze how th e features of the IAs, blended with te achers’ scaffolding, mediate children’s learning.

Case 1: “What’s the difference between a robot dog (GO1) and a real dog?” The first segment took place after a group discussi on about the differences between the robot dog (GO1) and a real dog (shown in a video clip), during which the children did not touch the robot. Most children bel ieved the robot's appearance (e.g., size and  components) was the primary reason for the differen ces. Case 1 showed how the operator controlled the GO1, moved it onto rugs, and encouraged the children to move forward and “ pet” the robot (Figure 2, line 1). Then, the children kept p etting and observing the robot (line 2). While they  were interacting, teachers asked whether the robot felt the same as a real dog (line 5). Most children resp onded that they were different (line 9). As the children interacted with the robot more, the teacher asked about the differences between a robot and a real dog, and one child answered, “It’s cold,” while another added, “It’s warm.” In addition, one child then pointed out that the hot part was on the robot dog’s back (line 15). The operator explained that the warmth was due to the robot's material properties  (line 18), specifically the battery area (lines 26-30). We found that most children in this case noticed significant temperature differences between the robot dog and the real dog. Their detailed observations through the sensory int eraction led to a deeper understanding of the mecha nical attributes of the robot dog.

Figure 2 Interaction with GO1 in Session 1 In this case, the interaction between young children and the robot dog GO1 illustrates how young children learn about the features of robots through the dynamic mediation between the subject (young children), the object (learning the distinction between animate and inani mate things), and blended mediators, such as the ro bot's mechanical attribut es (dog-like appearance and material properties) and the te achers’ facilitation (e.g.prompts, revoicing, and explicit explanation). The robot’s d og-like appearance initially serves as a mediator that  sparks children’s curiosity and reflects their manner of  treating real dogs in their interactions. This inte raction enables the children to begin exploring the robot’s material properties (e.g., temperature differences on the robot's surface caused by its batteries), providing a foundation fo r understanding its  mechanical rather than biological nature. The teacher’s prompts (line 5) and revoicing (line 16) about the sensory differences between a robot d og and a real dog, as the additional mediators, further enco urage the children to articulate their understandi ng by guiding them to explore the causes of these sensations thro ugh their sensory feedback (e.g., "cold" or "warm"). This process enhances the children’s use of analogical reasoning to compare their immediate sensory experiences with the robot to their pr ior experiences of touching animate beings. As the children further touched the back of the robot, the operator (acting as co-teacher) explained the robot’s battery system, whic h enhanced the children’s understanding of why there was an uneven temperature on the robot’s surface. This case reflects how blended mediators, including  the robot’s features and the teacher’s scaffolding, effectively and dynamically deepen the subject’s (c hildren’s) conceptual understanding of the object (distinguishing living beings from mechanical syste ms). In the context of the interaction described, t he learning process facilitates a shift in the children’s evolv ing focus from external, superficial features (such  as shape and size) to the robot’s internal mechanical structures. This cognitive progress involves a deeper conceptualization of IAs, such as dog-like robots as mechanical, inanimate objects rather  than living entities, which is important for young children’s moral and cognitive development. A lthough the activity was planned to use human-controlled, non-living IAs to explain the differences between the d og-like robot and a real dog, the children’s nuanced interactions revealed how material properties can e voke life-like associations, sparking analogical reasoning and finally supporting young children’s conceptual understanding of IAs. Case 2: Sensors and Eyes of Spot–“Because he has a bunch of eyes” The second case from Session 6 involved two robots (GO1 and Spot) competing in two races to test their capability. This session aimed to strengthen children’s understanding of IA perception systems as an important con cept in IA learning (Touretzky et al., 2019). The first race focused on speed, while the second assessed obstacle-avoidance capabilities. The children were asked to predict and test the results for these two features. In the s econd round of voting, most of the children predicted that GO1 mig ht be able to dodge obstacles while Spot could not,  as they already knew that GO1 had the running mode based on the first race. This information initially led them to connect speed with the capability of the obstacle-avoidance feature to explain the race resu lt. Case 2 recorded the testing process after their voting. Watching from behind a fence, the children felt supervised and disappointed when GO1 failed to dodge the obstacle, and their tone notice ably dropped (Figure 3, line 4). However, after obs erving Spot successfully dodge the blue couch, and when the teacher asked why they thought Spot won (line 10), the children changed their minds. Two children clearly explained that Spot could avoid obstacles because it had "eyes," using gestures to analogize the robot's cameras to their own eyes (lines 11-14). After this activity, the children gently touched and softly greeted the robots.

The two robots with distinct functio ns and capabilities served as mediators in the subjects’ (children's) learning experience, allowing them to explore the objects (strengths and limitations of t he different robots). The teacher’s thoughtful questioning further enabled the children to articulate their reasoning, as observed in other early technological learning experiences (Wang et al., 2023). Specifically, positioned as mediators within the activity, the robots provide opportunities for the children to explore different capabilities,   such as running modes and obstacle-avoidance features (Table 1). As active participants, the children engaged in p redictions, testing, and reflections based on their understanding of the robots' capabilities, which initially led them to assume that speed implied agility, including the ability t o dodge obstacles. Reasoning and critical thinking were demonstrated when the children identified the senso rs that allowed the robot to dodge barriers, adjust ed their assumptions, and proposed new ideas based on observ ations and discussions about the robot’s functions. The teacher’s thoughtful questioning (Figure 3, line 10) further supported this learning by encouraging the children to reflect and articulate the robots' specific capacit ies, helping them recognize the differences between  robotic functions.

This segment demonstrates how the robots’ functiona l properties and the teacher’s thoughtful questioning mediate young children's understanding of IAs’ functionality during robot races. Influence d by both mediators, the children critically reflected on their reasoning and prior assumptions, adjusted their reasoning about the causation between the robots' sensors and obsta cles, and developed an emerging understanding of se nsors as a key component of the robots' functionality and pe rception systems. Thus, this case highlights how th e robots’ properties engage young children in learning about IA perception and support their understanding of IA s’ capabilities and limitations.

Case 3: The response to Astro’s malfunction Our last segment focused on young children’s interaction with Astro. In sessions 7 and 8, the children interacted with Astro, the most intelligent robot in this stud y. The segment captured the process of a target chi ld adapting her commands and successfully prompting the malfunctioning Astro to respond after several rounds of interaction. Initially, when the child attempted to ask Astro a question, the robot did not respond (Figure 4, lines 1-3), leaving her feeling overwhelmed (line 4). This prompted the operator to explain that Astroneeded to be woken up before asking the questions (lines 5-6). The child adjusted her approach by trying to wake Astro with the command, “Hi, Astro,” and waiting for a response. However, Astro still didn’t respond, causing the child tonervousl y rub her clothes and step back (line 9). With this child’s c onsent, the operator then suggested  she try asking a “weather question” that Astro could answer based on its uplo aded information (line 10). Despite this, Astro sti ll fails to recognize her questions (line 13). With her consent, the operator modeled how to reactivate the robot and encouraged her to try again (lines 14-17). Undeterred, the child adapted by breaking her questions into shorter, three-word phrases, which finally elicited a response fro m Astro (line 18). However, Astro’s reply was incor rect and said, “The weather today is …in… (line 19),” without accurately identifying their location, which caused the group to laugh together (line 21). In Session 8, during the second interaction between the same group of children and Astro, the children actively approached and tested Astro’s commands to stop its actions when needed. Several children (including the target girl from session 7)  repeatedly tried to communicate with Astro but rec eived no response. Compared to Session 7, children’s feelings of being overwhelmed and laughing at Astro’s errors shifted. The children continued trying and even touched the screen to give instructions, demonstrating their adaptation to the IA and changes in their emotional responses.

In this segment, there is a clear transition in the child's initiative: from uncertainty about how to prompt Astro to actively adapting her questions and receiving responses. Notably, Astro’s misunderstood answer (line 19) led to shared laughter, reinforcing the child’s awarene ss of the IAs’ technological limitations. This case implies that robots with reactive behaviors and intelligent malfunctions, paired with the guided support of an operator, can mediate subjects’ (youn g children’s) adaptive behavior and understanding o f the objects (language routines and technological constraints of Astro). These blended mediators encouraged children to experiment with commands, gradually adjusting th eir expectations as they learned to navigate Astro’ s limitations. Moreover, observing the shift between sessions 7 and 8, the children’s emotions evolved f rom initial hesitation to confident problem-solving, exploring alternative commands and control s. These findings suggest that the value of imperfect technology in learning, when paired with proper guidance, can promote both confidence and adaptability. Such experiences have the potenti al to help children develop a practical and positiv e approach to IAs’ capabilities and limitations.

## Conclusion and discussion

To understand how young children interact with and understand IA, our study implements an eight-session curriculum in a pre-K classroom that allows young children to interact with 3 types of IAs (Table 1). Across cases shown in this paper, we found that IA s’ features intertwined with teachers’ scaffolding acted as mediators in children’s learning about emerging technology, extending the model of Vygotsky’s Mediation Theory (1978)  as illustrated in Figure 1. First, the various feature s of IAs (e.g., appearance, functionality, and intelligence level) mediate young children's conceptual understanding o f IAs in different ways. These concepts include perceptio n systems, natural interaction, and intelligence, which are also important powerful learning ideas in  Five Big Ideas of IA learning (Touretzky et al., 2019) to help young children build a foundational understanding of technology’s capabilities and boundaries. The process of their learning is reflected through their interactions, including sensory exploration, expressions of critical reasoning, and adaptive engagement. Three IAs progressively mediated children's understanding and interaction: (1) GO1, a dog-like robot, prompted young children’s interaction l ike “petting a dog”. It encouraged children to explore sensory information, such as temperature, which also led them to understand the battery configuration of the IA. Ultimately, young children come to understand that Go1 is a mechanical, non-living entity—a key conceptual milestone aligned with the five bi g ideas proposed by AI4K-12 (Touretzky et al., 2019; Goldman et al., 2023).  Thus, it indicates that by interacting with Go1, yo ung children started to explore the mechanism of intelligent agency instead of only based on their appearance. (2) Spot, a robot with a “vision” sensor, representing the type of IA that h as a perception and detection system. It inspired y oung children to make connections with sensors and human eyes. Such interaction also showed us there’s  a potential for young children to explore and build connections to real human behavior to understand an intelligent agency's function. (3) Astro, in this research, is the most advanced robot with functions such as information searching and conversational capabilities. This function mediated dialogues between young children and malfunctioning Astro, changing their expectations and emotional responses. Children displayed  adaptive engagement with structured inputs and simple commands, developing an awareness  of technological constraints. These processe s align with Vygotsky’s Mediation Theory (1978), underscoring th e educational potential of IAs as mediators to fost er understanding and practical engagement with technology among young learners. Moreover, our findings also find scaffolding serves  a critical role in young children’s interaction (Kewalramani et al., 2021). In teachers’ prompts, they used multiple strategi es, such as revoicing, explicit, encouraging responses, and thoughtful questioning to guide the child’s co-construct understanding of the IA technique, which echoes some research findings (Wan g et al., 2023). Specifically, when the teacher revoices the child’s words, it draws the child’s attention to co ntradictions in a peer’s observation, sparking furt her discussion (Case 1). Additionally, questions about cause and e ffect fostered the child’s reasoning and expression  about the perception system (Case 2). In another instance, th e co-teacher’s supportive explanation during a malfunction (Case 3) facilitated the child’s exploration, blend ing the IA’s unique features with guided learning. While we focus on the learning opportunities created by IA c haracteristics, the teacher’s role remains vital and cannot be overlooked.

In sum, with these three cases, our paper showed th e process of how young children understand IA in the classroom. The different features of IAs mediat e young children’s way of interacting and understanding mechanisms. Additionally, facilitators such as teac hers and operators played a crucial role in guiding  children to reflect on their interactions and deepen their unde rstanding of IAs. These findings can enrich our und erstanding of how to help young children learn about and build relationships with intelligent agents in classroom settings.

## Implication

This study highlights key implications for future research and educational practice. These insights emphasize the need for educators to enhance their learning, under standing, and engagement with technologies to facil itate teaching, learning, and adaptation to a changing wo rld. Future practice could expand Vygotsky’s framew ork to include non-human mediators, such as IAs, emphasizing the collaboration between human and material mediators to enhance young children’s understanding of techno logy (Keune, 2024). Moreover, future designers coul d enhance diversity (e.g., incorporating varying responsiveness, movement capa bilities, and sensor feedback) to create opportunities that deepen children’s understanding of technology and its limitations.  In the face of rapid technological advancements, these efforts are pivot al in shaping technologies and educational framewor ks that help young children understand and engage with advanced technology.
