# Computational Modeling of the Process of Scientific Inquiries

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Scientific inquiry is a process to understand the complex real world. To automatically assess and foster learners ’ scientific inquiries by a computer-supported collaborative learning (CSCL) system, thinking processes required for scientific inquiries should be modeled in a machine-readable and traceable form. By using a computational modeling approach, the present study extracted patterns of such thinking processes from the data repository of our educational practices and investigated how the processes can be computationally modeled.

Scientific inquiry is a process to understand the complex real world. In this process, understanding of the world (e.g., internal representations of the world) should be formatively constructed (National Research Council, 2012). Based on the theory of grounded cognition (Barsalou, 2008), which explains how a person understands the world through real-world experiences, the present  study considers that the formative construction process of internal representations involves abstracting information from the world and grounding the representations on real-world experiences. However, even with the latest computer-supported collaborative learning (CSCL) systems, automatically tracing and assessing  such process is considered a challeng ing issue. One of the difficulties  is considered to be constructing machine-readable and traceable learner models. By using a computational modeling approach that represents a person ’s internal information processing as “ computation” (Huys et al., 2021), the present study investigates scientific thinking processes that learners should cultivate for formatively constructing the internal representations of the world, and discusses how the processes can be computationally represented. Progressive refinement of a computational model Since modeling unknown computation processes is generally difficult, this study (i) initially assumed a qualitative model of the thinking processes for formatively constructing internal representations, and (ii) progressively refined this model to be expressed in a computable form, by using the data repository of our educational practices (e.g., the data of real-world learning in the natural environment, the data of lectures in classroom learning). In the step (i), our previous study (Watanabe et al., 2023) assume d that a learner performs hierarchical information conversions to update his/her knowledge space which is a hierarchical structure of several information layers with different abstraction levels. Based on this assumption, the previous study made a model that the internal representation is a hierarchical structure where the top layer is semantics (e.g., systematic functions, metrics, model representations) and the bottom layer is observational information, and  is constructed through intra-layer information manipulations ((a) and (b) in Fig. 1) and inter-layer information manipulations ((c) and

Following these ideas,  for computationally representing the model in Fig. 1, t his study analyzed scientific thinking processes included in lecture data in a classroom setting and extracte d patterns of practical computations for formatively constructing internal representations of the world. Based on the idea that a person’s thinking process is externalized as his /her verbalization (Ericsson & Simon, 1980), the present study analyzed scientific thinking processes included in the data of utterances and slides of two lectures at universities, by using the  data repository. The transcribed time-series utterance data were classified based on our initial assumptions of the information  manipulations (a) –(d) in Fig. 1, from which practical computations were extracted. As systematic operations, this analysis extracted the following thinking processes, corresponding to (a)–(d) in Fig. 1, performed to encourage learners to scientifically acquire knowledge at the level of semantics in Fig. 1 (e.g., cross-disciplinary model representations):

• Establishing rational associations based on semantic similarities in real-world phenomena ((a) in Fig. 1).

• Filling in missing logic to provide possible explanations for the real-world phenomena ((b) in Fig. 1).

• Deductively interpreting observational information by using semantic knowledge ((c) in Fig. 1).

• Deriving semantic knowledge by induction of, analogy to, and generalization of observational

information ((d) in Fig. 1).
