# Learning about Artificial Intelligence in (an Imagined) Museum Setting: Investigating the Effects of Critical and Everyday Context

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
To investigate how different ways of presenting information about AI affect learning, we conducted an experiment with 200 adults who were asked to imagine visiting a museum and received information about AI in the mining industry. We tested whether adding an everyday AI example or a critical message had an effect on recall, attitudes towards AI, and situational interest. Everyday context negatively affected participants’ information recall. Neither intervention had significant effects on attitudes or interest.

## Introduction

Informal learning institutions such as museums and science centers have been proposed as promising settings for fostering AI literacy  (Long et al., 2021), which includes not only the ability to successfully  use artificial intelligence in everyday life, but also the ability to critically evaluate this technology (Long & Magerko, 2020). It may indeed be important to challenge current pub lic perceptions of AI and emphasise the importance of critical reflection. Social scientists criticize the one-sided way in which AI is presented in the media and show that the same biased views are reproduced in policy documents such as AI strategies (Bareis & Katzenbach, 2022; Campolo & Crawford, 2020; Chubb et al., 2024). The belief that AI is a necessary solution to many soci etal problems and the optimistic view of the future of A I are part of the mainstream discourse, often drive n by technology entrepreneurs. Alternatively, some autho rs paint a dystopian picture and warn of the danger s of AI, leading people to be intimidated by the technology and to misunderstand the true capabilities of AI. It is typical for contemporary museums to aim to ch ange people's preconceptions and attitudes and to demonstrate controversy (Novak et al., 2024). In de signing exhibitions on AI, museum professionals aim  to address and challenge mainstream images of AI by demonstrating both the risks and benefits of AI, and by grounding the idea of AI in real-world applications (Maksimova, 2024). Whether this approach is productive in changing people's attitudes and views of AI has not yet been the subject of empirical research. Thus, the present study focuses on investigating th e effects of both everyday and critical contextualization of information on learning in ima gined museum settings. Everyday context was added to information about specific AI applications in order  to make AI more relevant to readers and to increas e the perceived authenticity of learning about AI. In the case of the addition of a critical context, we wanted to test whether it would lead to more balanced, i.e. less n egative and less positive, attitudes.  Our ce ntral research questions concerned  the effects of the way AI is presented on 1) participants' attitudes towards AI, 2) their situational interest, and 3) their ability to recal l the information. To investigate these questions i n a controlled laboratory study, we mimicked the context of a muse um visit by developing the input in a specific way and instructing participants to imagine that they were viewing an exhibition on new technology in the mining industry. This controlled online study was conceived as a preliminary stage before conducting a field study in a museum.

## Method

The data were collected using the Prolific platform  for recruitment and an online questionnaire design ed in UniPark. 200 US-based participants took part in the study (57% female, Age: M = 38, SD = 12.13). We implemented a 2x2 experimental design to examine the effects of the way of presenting information about AI on participants’ AI attitudes, situational interest, and recall. We varied two factors: everyday context and critical context. Each had two levels  (present or absent), resulting in four experimental  conditions. In the first case, an example of everyday AI use was added, for instance, AI in the camera app in smartphones. In c ritical condition, a message was added that emphasized the concerns and risks: potential abuse of AI technolog y, risks of incidents, etc. Participants were randomly assig ned into one of the four conditions: the first only got the basic information about the uses of AI in mining, two other conditions read texts with either everyday or critical context, and the fourth both.  The four applications of AI in mining described in the texts were: AI for exploration of potential mining sites, AI for health and safety monitoring, autonomous trucks, and monitoring of conveyor belts. Participants were first asked questions about their  socio-demographic characteristics, trust in different media reporting on new technologies, interest in ne w technologies, prior knowledge of AI, and attitudes towards AI. After a filler task, they were presented with t exts about AI applications in mining. Participants'  attitudes towards AI were then measured again, along with their situational interest and ability to recall the information. To assess participants' attitudes towards AI  before and after the input, we used the General Attitudes towards Artificial Intelligence Scale (GAAIS) (Sche pman & Rodway, 2020, 2023). To assess participants' situational interest, the scale from Lewalter and Willems (2009) was used. A measure with 20 items was created for assessing how well the participants could recall the content of the texts. Participants’ level of interest in new technology and their trust in different media sources with relation to information on technology (e.g., news media, museums, technological companies) were included as control variables. We used a 5-point Likert scale throughout the questionnaire.

## Results

Attitudes towards AI.  To investigate RQ1 (Does the way in which AI is presented have an effe ct on attitudes towards AI?), we conducted a repeated measures ANCOVA with everyday context and critical context as two between subjects factors, time of measurement as  a within subjects factor, and positive and negative attitudes towards AI as dependent variables. Due to significa nt correlations with our dependent variables, we in cluded interest in new technology and trust in technologic al companies as a source of information about techn ology as two covar iates. The results show no statistically significan t interaction effects and no main effects of time o f measurement or of critical context on participants’ attitudes, but a significant main effect of everyd ay context on their negative attitudes (F(1,194) = 5.78, p =.02, ηp² =.03). Situational interest.  To investigate RQ2 (Does the way in which AI is presented have an effec t on situational interest?), we conducted a MANCOVA with everyday context and critical context as two between subjects factors, and situational interest catch and hold as two dependent variables. We again included interest in new technology and trust in technological companies  as a source of information about technology as cov ariates due to significant correlations with our dependent variables. The analysis showed no statistically significant main or interaction effects of the two factors on either participants’ triggered (catch) nor maintained (hold) situational interest. On average, people reported high interest  in reading the texts (Catch: M = 4.04, SD = 0.78; Hold in AI: M = 3.55, SD = 1.09).

Information recall. To investigate RQ3 (Does the way in which AI is presented have an effe ct on the ability to recall information?), we again conducted  an ANCOVA with our two experimental factors, recall as dependent variable, and (due to significant correla tions) trust in museums as a source of information about technology and interest in new technology as covari ates. The ANCOVA revealed no main effect of c ritical context, no interaction effect of critical and ever yday context, but a significant main effect of ever yday context (F(1,194) = 4.83, p =.03, ηp² =.02). The participants who received everyday context prompts recalled information worse (M = 15.82 out of maximum of 20, SD = 2.66) than people who did not receive those (M = 16.55, SD = 2.87). On average, participants could recall the contents of the texts well (M = 16.19, SD = 2.78).

## Discussion

Our results indicate that the way AI information wa s contextualized had an impact on recall, but not o n participants’ attitudes or situational interest.

The absence of an effect on attitudes towards AI may have different explanations. We hypothesize that in our case the treatment was too short and subtle,  and a deeper engagement might be needed  for the attitudes to change. A similar result in a study by Novak et al.  (2024) leads them to suggest that different means of presentation, other than just text, are needed. The fact that situational interest was not affected by our experimental manipulation suggests that all texts, disregarding the added contexts, w ere perceived as equally interesting, and the sustained interest in the topic was similar in different groups. The reason for this might be that  AI in mining was a new topic for the majority of the participants: they all learned something new and th erefore were equally interested. With regard to the effect of the intervention on recall, our results show that participants who read the texts that included everyday examples later recalle d information worse than those who did not. Emphasi s on everyday life is o ften a part of museums’ approach to provide authentic and easy-to-understand messages. However, we see that in our case this strategy was counterproductive. A possible explanation for the e ffect on information recall is that everyday examples designed by us had a seductive detail effect on participants. Seductive details are interesting bu t irrelevant and unhelpful pieces of information th at are added to increase interest, but distract learners (Mayer et al., 2020; Rey, 2012).

The limitations of this study include the short duration and limited depth of the intervention, which may not provide enough engagement for lasting impacts o n AI attitudes.  Conducted online, the study required participants to imagine that they are visiting a museum, thus limiting ecological validity and potentially affecting how participants engaged with the content compared to a real-world museum setting.
