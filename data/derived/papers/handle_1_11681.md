# What “Ice” do Ice-Breakers Break or Ice?

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper reimagines ice-breakers beyond warm-ups, reframing them as integral to learning. While traditionally they are seen as tools to reduce tension and build community, they can reveal group dynamics, roles, and identities. Through figured worlds and participation structures, we argue ice-breakers may serve as interactional spaces where students negotiate identities, use humor, and bring cultural artifacts into learning. Well-designed ice-breakers can strategically support educational goals, fostering critical thinking, engagement, and collaboration across diverse contexts.

## Background

In educational research, researchers mainly used psychological ana lysis to examine the influence of ice-breakers in terms of ‘how much’ engagement and motivation take place in a learning environment. However, we argue that we can understand ice-breaker activities as serving purposes beyond being  just a traditional ‘warm-up’ activity that makes people engage with each other if we exam ine them from a sociocultural perspective. These activities can offer deeper understanding of the social dynami cs and participation structures that contribute to creating effective and meaningful learning environments.

We adopt a lens grounded in figured worlds theory (FW; Holland et al., 1998), particularly by analyzing the participation structure (Erickson, 1982) of ice-breaker activities to understand how the members relate to each other or how/the ways members are engaged. We argue that shifting our attention towards the roles, interactions, meanings, and structures being enacted by individua ls in these ice-breaker activities provides a window into the developing relationships and social dynamics within the learning environment more generally and can be a design lever for creating more inclusive and productive learning. Theoretical underpinnings We define learning as a sociocultural process whereby constantly changing relations happen through community participation and participation in practices (Lave & Wagner, 1991). Since ice-breakers often play a crucial role in community-building, they are in herently part of the learning experience. P rocesses of learning and identity are simultaneous, reciprocal, and situated within cultu ral practices (Nasir,  2015). Identities develop in and through social practice (Holland et al., 1998) and people’s participation in community activities (Lave & Wagner, 1991). We suggest utilizing ‘figured worlds’ (Holland et a l., 1998) as an analytical framework to explore ice-breakers. A figured world (FW) is “a socially and culturally constructed realm of interpretation in which specific characters and actors are acknowledged, particular acts are assigned significance, and certain outcome s are prioritized” (Holland et al., 1998, p. 52). A FW is built on individuals’ capacities to imagine and participate in shared ‘as if’ realities, where people’s identities  and agency are dialectically shaped through dialog ue and interaction (Holland et al., 1998). There is also a dialectical relationship between the development of FWs and the construction of roles, activities, discourses, perf ormances, and artifacts. Using the lens of FW to characterize an ice-breaker activity, we argue that the actors (e.g., s tudents and teacher in a classroom) make sense of t he roles they take on, their identities, the shared meanings, artifacts, and practices within the world that sh apes their figuring or participation and being shaped by their figuring or participation over time. Actors can use ice-breaker activities to practice agency and identity and crea te an interactional space that becomes part of the FW of their learning environment.

We also take up the concept of participation structu re (PS) from Erickson’s (1982) work, highlighting the immediate learning task environment, to analyze and design ice-breaker activities. According to Erickson, “the social participation structure places gatekeeping constraints on the individual’s access to information sources and help information available in speech, writing, and physical objects, and the help available in the  actions of other people.” (p. 168) Looking at PSs in ice-breaker activities allows us to see the operating principles and helps us understand the ways and how individuals relate to each other. Each ice-breaker activity can lead to different or modified PSs shaped by the actors involved. Each ice-breaker becomes an opportunity for actors to dynamically (re)shape PS influencing how roles, statuses, and interactional rights are distributed,  and help actors to figure out the worlds of their learning environment. In other words, when we look at the FW  and PS together, t he actors are editing (see Lave’s 1988 work on “editing” setting) the PS, and the PS simultaneously edits the ice-breaker activity. Practical applications We created the following vignette to argue how FW and PS can be used critically in practice in designing and analyzing an ice-breaker activity. This vignette is  inspired by our noticing in an informal observation  of the students’ interactions in weekly after school science program sessions focusing on composting over 10 weeks. Vignette on Introductions: In the first class of an after-school high school science program, the teacher began with introductions. They asked students to in troduce themselves, by telling their  names, pronouns, and grade level, and why they love or do not love scien ce. The teacher started off by sharing that they like science because they have many questions, and sometimes the y do not like science because it takes a very long time and it is tough to find the answers. After they finishe d, they gestured toward the students and invited th e next person to share. One by one, the students took turns shari ng what they loved about science and what they did not love about it. As each student finished, a brief silence  hung in the air. The class proceeded in this manner. There was no conversation between the students themselves, no  follow-up questions, or reactions to others’ sharing excep t on the part of the teacher—just a series of individual responses, sometimes followed by the teacher’s comment. We have this example to show how thinking through a FW lens and looking at PS can provide us opportunities to learn about the group dynamic (even though they do not know each other or it is their first ti me being in this group, each have their own understanding of the FW of after-school science, and they brought those meanings, ro les, and identities to be edited in this group–and at the same time editing this group). In this example, the interaction is marked by unidirectional engagement from students to the teacher, with no further reactions or comments on others’ responses. The teacher can n otice this unidirectional way of engagement and design ice-breakers to disturb the hierarchy and power structu res (e.g. teacher student, knowledge source  unknowledgeable one, evaluator  evaluated, etc), where the teacher’s authority remains central, and students are simply passive responders. The teacher can benefit from th is kind of traditional ice-breaker by understanding the PS and designing eithe r learning activities that work for the  current ways of engaging or the ones that challenge the PS to work for the community and the learning.

## Conclusion

In traditional educational settings, the role of ice-breakers has often been underestimated. When examined through a sociocultural lens, these activities reveal how l earners navigate power relations, cultural norms, c ollective identities and their agency, which in turn influence how they participate and learn within the community. We candesign ice-breakers as tools for engagement and as critical sites where participants begin tonegot iate and reconfigure the social structures of the learning e nvironment. In doing so, ice-breakers contribute to the creation of more inclusive, decentralized, and collaborative  learning spaces that are at tentive to the cultural and social contexts in which learning takes place. Teachers candesign ice-breakers that deliberately disturb and shift the PS in their classrooms, or implement alternative participation structures, or craft ice-breakers that align with the goals of their teaching activities, thereby allowing ice-breakers to function as spaces where the core learn ing practices and dynamics are modeled in advance.
