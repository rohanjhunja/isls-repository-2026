# Do Students Recognize Personalization? Comparing Personalized vs. Common- Solution Parsons Code Puzzles as Problem-Solving Scaffolding

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Parsons code puzzles, which require students to arrange mixed-up code blocks into a correct program, can scaffold students who struggle while programming. Large language models can generate personalized Parsons puzzles based on students' existing incorrect code. However, the effects of such personalization remain underexplored, especially compared to the standard use of common-solution puzzles. We examined it with 85 students through a programming classroom study: students had access to personalized (PP) or only common-solution (CP) Parsons puzzles as scaffolding. We found that students who received a personalized puzzle rated the solution’s alignment with their own approach as 16% higher than with a common-solution puzzle. We also found preliminary evidence of a mediated pathway through which personalized puzzles improved scaffolding puzzle solution understanding via higher perceived solution alignment. This work offers insights for approachable AI-powered personalization during problem-solving.

In computer science education, short write-code problems are a common active learning approach (Córdova - Esparza et al., 2024) to practice programming skills (Kalyuga et al., 2001; Renkl, 2014). Scaffolding supports students when they struggle to reach a correct solution (Wood et al., 1976). One such scaffolding form is the Parsons code puzzle (Parsons & Haden, 2006; Hou et al., 2022), where students arrange mixed-up code blocks into a correct solution for a given task. It can serve as a bridge supporting the transition from fully worked examples to independent problem-solving (Renkl, 2014; Sweller et al., 2019). Traditionally, Parsons puzzles used as scaffolding were created in advance of students’ problem-solving and typically based on a common student solution (Hou et al., 2022, 2023). Nevertheless, students struggled with the scaffolding when the puzzle’s solution did not align with their own approach (Hou et al., 2022, 2023). Over the past few years, researchers have advanced generative AI to provide pers onalized learning materials and activities (Garcia, 2025). Large language models (LLMs) enable the real-time generation of personalized Parsons puzzles from students’ code to provide personalized scaffolding during problem-solving (Hou et al., 2024). Prior work comparing personalized Parsons puzzles and directly-shown personalized solutions found that students were more engaged and invested more time in the practice when receiving puzzles (Hou et al., 2024, 2025). However, the specific impact of this form of personalization in puzzles, compared to standard commonsolution versions, is not well understood. C ompared to the traditional use of common-solution puzzles (Hou et al., 2022, 2023), do students recognize this personalization? H ow does personalization influence students' perceived alignment between their own approach and the puzzle solution, and their understanding of the scaffolded solution? These fine-grained insights are crucial to designing future AI-powered programming tools that better support personalized learning. Therefore, this work aims to address this gap in a study with two conditions: one had access to LLM-powered personalized solution-based Parsons puzzles (PP) vs. the other always received common solution-based Parsons puzzles (CP) as write-code scaffolding. The RQs were: RQ1: How do personalized vs. common-solution puzzles impact students' perceptions of the alignment of the puzzle solution with their own approach?

RQ2: How do personalized vs. common-solution puzzles impact students' perceived understanding of the puzzle solution?

This IRB (Institutional Review Board)-approved study was conducted as an in-class practice in an undergraduate Python course on CSV Readers and Writers. After a 15-minute introduction, students completed a knowledge review, pre-survey, and 12-minute pretest. They were then randomly assigned to PP or CP conditions and completed a condition-specific tutorial, which included a short write-code example problem. Students then 1187 worked on four write-code practice problems with Parsons puzzle scaffolding available on request; PP students received puzzles based on their current code, while CP students always received common-solution-based puzzles. Students were reminded to request help after 5 minutes on a problem. After each practice problem, students completed a perception survey, from which we analyzed two items related to our RQs. The first question (Q1-Perceived Solution Alignment) asked students to rate “ How closely does the provided solution in ‘Get Help’ align with your initial idea for a solution?” on a scale from 1 (very low) to 5 (very high alignment), with option 6 for those without an initial solution idea  before getting help. The second question (Q2-Scaffolding Puzzle Solution Understanding) asked students to rate “ How well did you understand the provided solution in ‘Get Help’? ”, rated from 1 (not at all  understood) to 5 (fully understood), with option 6 for those who did not arrive at a solution for the puzzle. The study ended with an isomorphic posttest. AI tools were used to assist with the classroom deployment. The final sample included 85 students (37 PP, 48 CP) who completed materials without violating the intended task order. Practice consisted of four problems of varying complexity with unittest feedback. Both tests consisted of three problems at varying difficulty levels (18 points total), graded by two raters.

An LLM-driven puzzle generation pipeline was designed and deployed with integrated safeguards to ensure appropriate classroom use. Potential latency in this LLM use case could come from two main aspect s: (1) response regeneration due to LLM errors or low-quality outputs, and (2) fully prepare all puzzle blocks before display, since puzzles cannot be progressively streamed like text. This work dealt with these latency concerns in three ways: (1) loading screen design: displaying rotating encouraging messages with explicit time-remaining indicators to improve students' waiting experience (see Figure 1-Step 2); (2) generation route: to improve generation efficiency, an updated safeguard mechanism limited regeneration attempts and defaulted to a common solution if the generation exceeded a time threshold or retry limit. This is designed to maintain responsiveness and quality; (3) model: OpenAI GPT-4-mini was used to meet the fast response-time needs. We examined the extent to which students could perceive the “personalization” in the scaffolding they received. Following each practice problem, the student rated the alignment between their own approach and the solution in the puzzle. Since each student r ated multiple times, i.e., completing the survey questions for all the cases in which they requested scaffolding, the alignment rating becomes a “repeated measure”. To understand how students rated the alignment between their approach and the solution provided in the puzzle as scaffolding (Q1), we built a linear mixed-effects model with students' perceived solution alignment as the dependent variable. We used their assigned condition and the type of scaffolding puzzle as independent variables. A random intercept was used to account for student-level differences. We excluded cases where students reported seeking scaffolding without an initial solution idea (Option 6, Q1), resulting in 230 help-seeking cases from 72 student respondents.

The results (Table 1) indicated that the type of scaffolding puzzle provided (personalized or not) was a significant predictor of students’ perceived alignment between the solution used in the puzzle and their initial problem-solving idea (p <.001). In particular, the perceived solution alignment ratings increased by 0.62 units on average (a 16% increase relative to the scale range)  when they received a personalized solution-based puzzle as scaffolding. Students perceived the solutions as more closely aligned with their initial problem-solving approaches when they received a personalized scaffolding puzzle, compared to a common-solution puzzle. A similar linear mixed-effects model was conducted with understanding of the solution in the scaffolding puzzle as the dependent variable. We excluded cases where students did not reach a scaffolded solution (Option 6, Q2), resulting in 255 help-seeking cases from 75 student respondents. However, puzzle personalization (Coef. = 0.07, p  = 0.692) and assigned condition (Coef. = 0.27, p = 0.191) did not predict students' understanding of the scaffolded solution. We then explored whether students’ perceived alignment between their own problem-solving ideas and the puzzles they received (Perceived Solution Alignment) influenced their understanding of the scaffolding puzzle solution (Scaffolding Puzzle Solution Understanding).  We built a linear mixed-effects model predicting scaffolding puzzle solution understanding from perceived solution alignment, puzzle type, and assigned condition, with random intercepts per student. Results indicated that the perceived solution alignment significantly predicted scaffolding puzzle solution understanding, whereas puzzle type was not a significant predictor (Table 2). Together, we found that receiving personalized puzzles significantly predicted higher perceived solution alignment (RQ2), which in turn associated with greater solution understanding (exploratory analysis result), while personalization did not directly predict solution understanding. This indicates a potential underlying mechanism (Pearl, 2022) that the personalization of the scaffolding puzzle influenced students’ understanding of the puzzle solution through the perceived alignment between the provided solution and their own problem-solving ideas. First, both conditions showed significant learning gains, consistent with the goal of cognitive scaffolding (Belland et al., 2013), which is enhancing students' knowledge and skill on a topic. Learning gains did not significantly differ across conditions, possibly because not all PP students were fully exposed to personalization. Personalization was based on students' incorrect code, capturing their problem-solving approaches. However, students could request scaffolding before writing any code, limiting personalization opportunities. Prior work suggests students may need Parsons puzzles as scaffolding early when unsure how to begin (Hou et al., 2022). This design choice was informed by this need. Future work should balance the amount of student input require d with the precision of personalization.

In addition, this work highlighted the contribution of student-code-based personalization in Parsons puzzles as scaffolding versus the common-solution approach. These findings align with prior research showing the benefits of personalizing tutoring systems to match student strategies (Aleven et al., 2016). The personalization in this work was based on students' existing incorrect code. Students could recognize such personalization, which was reflected in the greater alignment between the solution in the puzzle and their own initial problem-solving approach. With such higher perceived alignment, they could continue their own thinking process, and did not have to interpret a new approach. This reduced the required comprehension effort. Also, high solution alignment could provide a correctness confirmation and increase their perceived competence, which might also improve intrinsic motivation to put more effort into fully understanding the puzzle solution. To make such personalization approachable, future systems could ask students to express their ideas in pseudocode, plans, or sub-goal labels, enabling personalization even before any code is written. This work has several limitations. First, this study was conducted in a single session in one undergraduate programming classroom, limiting its generalizability across contexts, demographics, and programming languages. Additionally, self-reported solution understanding and alignment may contain subjective biases. Future work should explore more behavioral measures. Moreover, while the exploratory analysis showed statistically significant effects, the limited sample size (Lee et al., 2015 suggested over 500 samples) affected the stability of these exploratory results. Future work should explore whether this effect can be replicated with larger samples and in other environments.
