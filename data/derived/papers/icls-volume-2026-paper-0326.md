# Reflecting on the Process of School Change through Leadership and Teacher Narratives: A study of a first-generation school in Southern India

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper examines the role of school leadership in enacting innovative pedagogies within a first-generation, low to middle-income public school in India. In partnership with the Critical Action Learning Exchange (CALE), teachers have been supported to de sign and enact inquiry-oriented lessons that engage students in personally and culturally meaningful learning. Leadership and teacher interviews were analyzed as narratives, and overarching themes illuminated the critical dispositions that enabled curricular innovations for the past few years. These findings provide a depth of insight into our experience with culturally responsive school reform in a South Asian context.

Education must shift from producing students like industrial outputs to fostering young people as agents of change (Freire, 1970; Sachdeva, 2022). With one of the world's largest youth populations, India will play a critical role in any global crisis or re sponse and must prepare its youth to be critical thinkers and active problem solvers (Bedadur & Butani, 2024; Kareepadath, 2018).  Moreover, current governmental policy supports the transformation of Indian education towards more diverse learning methods. The National Education Policy (NEP) specifically outlines school curriculum to “make space for critical thinking and more holistic,  inquiry-based, discovery-based, discussion-based, and analysis-based learning” (p. 12, Government of India, 2020). In response, CALE has developed a research partnership with the leadership of a public school in southern India, which has over 1500 students, comprising low and middle-income backgrounds and first-generation learners. For the past few years, over 40 teach ers have participated in CALE workshops, with strong support from school leaders (Raman et al., 2024). This paper presents a first report from a larger ongoing study aiming to understand the role of leadership and teachers within these curricular enactments. Thus, we examine the following research question: what leadership and teacher narratives emerge around the long-term implementation of critical curriculum reform in an Indian school context? Critical Pedagogy (CP) originated in the works of scholars like Freire (1970) and Giroux (1985), who sought to transform education into a process of empowerment for marginalized and disadvantaged populations. This theoretical perspective interweaves reflection and transformative action and is relevant to the broader context of educational change (Jemal & Bussey, 2018). CP advocates for practices that empower teachers and students as transformative agents who recognize that they can shape their futures, utilizing education as a tool for empowerment. However, despite a substantial body of research that has explored CP and its efficacy for education and empowerment, like many inquiry and dialogic pedagogies, it has failed to gain wide adoption or autonomous practice by teachers.

The Critical Action Learning Exchange (CALE) was launched in 2019 to help make Critical Pedagogy more practicable for K-12 teachers, allowing them to design lessons that empower their students and add meaning and purpose to their curriculum. CALE outlines approaches and strategies for teachers, links to examples, and design guides (Carvalho et al., 2023-see https://www.cale.org/). CALE has also created a teacher-professional development model which allows teachers to join a broader network of “critical action educators.” For CALE to help schools move beyond what Freire (1970) described as the “banking model of learning” to one of sustained critical inquiry, we must deepen our support of teachers to include a school-centred approach, in which teachers’ efforts are connected with their wider school community, including leaders at the department, school and board level.

Deeply interwoven within school reform, school leadership plays a vital role in the sustenance and outcomes of curricular enactments, particularly within culturally sensitive contexts (Khalifa et al., 2016; Leithwood et al., 2004). Different frameworks of leadership, such as transformative, provide further 2121 conceptualizations of the layered dimensions within this field. Transformative leadership builds upon CP, emphasizing critical consciousness and arguing for equitable change within schools (Shields, 2010; Freire, 1970). Transformative leadership calls for leaders to question, dismantle and reconstruct education by acknowledging impositions of power and oppression. It requires leaders to provide spaces for the voices of all involved parties in education, including students, parents and teachers. CALE enables  school leaders to enact transformative leadership through the practical application of CP.

Leadership narratives have transformed from tales of heroic leaders who have inspired top-down revolutions to stories of co-constructed change through terms like distributed leadership (Spillane, 2005). Thus, merging leadership narratives with teacher narratives deepens our understanding of the process of school reform by acknowledging and understanding the multi-layered dimensions in enacting change (Hutt & Lewis, 2021). While leadership narratives provide context and storied accounts of the visions for sc hool reform, teacher narratives showcase the practical realities for reform implementation. Such narratives also provide critical intersecting accounts of teacher identity and professional development by surfacing implicit and explicit teacher beliefs and practices (Beauchamp & Thomas, 2009).

Present literature on school reform is biased towards high-resourced, Western populations (Fullan, 2016; Fishman et al., 2013). However, this research does not necessarily translate into diverse South Asian contexts, despite the need for school reform (Patel, 2015). For instance, the Indian government outlines the necessity of systemic school reform in the NEP, emphasizing that it “will require the entire education system to be reconfigured” by 2030 (p. 4, Government of India, 2020).

## School Context

This research took place in a Kindergarten to Grade 12 school in Southern India, specifically within the city of Bengaluru. Over 60% of students come from low and middle-income backgrounds, with over 40% identifying as first-generation learners. Several international collaborations on curricular and non-curricular projects have allowed for innovative peda gogical projects involving integrating criticality within rangoli (an Indian art form), and developing fractions and statistics curricular games imitating financial budgets of local households (Raman et al., 2024; Carvalho et al., 2023). experience. School leaders interviewed included the academic head for all teachers and an administrative stakeholder. Teachers interviewed taught a variety of subjects and grades, ranging from grade 5 to 10, with subjects from math to social studies.  Leadership and teacher interviews lasted  approximately 30 to 40 minutes and took place on the school premises. Semi-structured interviews comprised introductions, discussions of school culture, initial interest in CALE, and experiences of curriculum design and enactment. Supplemental data includes classroom observations, researcher field notes, group and meeting discussions. Data analysis was conducted using thematic narrative analysis (Clandinin & Connelly, 2000). This method ensured that leadership and teacher voice remained intact while facilitating a deeper understanding of underlying issues. Qualitative data analysis began with multiple viewings of interview videos. Transcripts were generated, and open-coding was conducted using NVIVO 15 software. Generated deductive codes from leadership and teacher interviews consisted of larger segments which maintained participant narratives. These were pooled together and synthesized into distinct themes that represented coherent categories of narrative. Findings are presented according to these categories of narrative.

Curricularizing holistic and cultural aspects Leaders and teachers at the school emphasize a holistic environment by providing students with different opportunities to prioritize these relationships. The academic head highlighted, “So I feel that at the school, nothing is mechanical, and the students, they see teachers as a second mother rather than just a teacher. And yeah, that's how it is, like how a mother nurtures the child.”. Similarly, one teacher commented how “Not only we concentrate on the academics, we concentrate on the holistic development  of the children. So be it cultural sports. So everywhere children get involved, it is more of a family.” Moreover, both leaders explicitly expressed their commitment to caring for students' needs alongside academic achievement. One leader described a rece nt school 2122 incident regarding a student who had been unable to complete their homework, “If it is not done today, I would not send him out of the class. I would not punish him or I, I would talk to him, and I would first ask him, Why haven't you completed it?” While leadership outlined how overall school culture encompasses holistic aspects, there was no explicit requirement to extend this into the lessons. School pedagogy and curriculum retained a more traditional and didactic form of teaching and learning, with occasional hands-on, experiential and project-based learning integrated throughout the year. A teacher describes the typical process as, “with the exams, patterns, syllabus, we just concentrate on giving a question and answer. So there it is only a memory power. What is that we are going to test it on?” However, with the introduction of CALE, teachers began brainstorming beyond textbook content and considering alternative curricula. The holistic and cultural aspects of school culture began to organically transfer into their lessons. For instance, in a CALE project about water pollution the teacher outlined how the relationship between the individual and the community was nurtured, “So they were able to relate it, what their acts, how their acts are affecti ng the environment. So it created an awareness among them.” This relationship is further emphasized by another teacher who states, “So a lot of discussion is taking place, and they are sharing their ideas, and they are getting more collecting more informat ion through other things earlier. It was not like that.” Space for teacher pedagogical growth School leaders naturally encouraged different modes of learning within classrooms. One leader stated, “I feel like I have, you know, been instrumental in bringing up new, innovative ways of learning, especially, I think the SMART Board was my suggestion, and we had a demonstration.” However, they acknowledged the lack of any critical pedagogy or similar approaches within their school, prior to the introduction of CALE. The academic head explained, “But we loved the idea, because the critical action was never a part of our curriculum, and we have never thought that way out of the box. It was always about finishing the portions, even though we were innovating in finishing the portions, but we never went out of it.” CALE represented a unique opportunity to innovate within the curriculum for the school leaders and teachers. Leadership also highlighted how the collaboration enabled new forms of curricular collaboration with teachers, “So that was extremely interesting for us, and also the fact that we-we would be able to collaborate with, you know, educators like you and others who are part of Cale in helping build the curriculum. We thought, more hands the better.” With the expansion and diversification of their curricula, leadership created an open space for teachers to engage independently with CALE. Since teachers had varying levels of experience and understanding of critical action learning, this space was crucial for fostering comfort and facilitating growth.

In turn, this allowed teachers to begin challenging the narrative of academic intelligence superiority — that the better grades a student received, the more intelligent they were. Teachers noted that CALE allowed students who were academically “weaker” to participate and learn in different ways. One teacher highlights this, “But this way we'll identify which student is-What are the strengths of each student? It gives a-they cando an intelligence candictate the career path of an individual, the organic suggestion of differential strengths and their equally strong capabilities is a critical insight. The teacher continues dissecting this below, “During the regular class, that might not happen, but with this, they will be able to identify what their strengths are. So when they are working in a group also, we could identify who is able to guide, who is able to do the work. So that way it would be future leaders. ” The emerging narratives highlight the potential for change when leaders and teachers are aligned and willing to work together to enact reform. For instance, the circularization of holistic school culture, as described by leaders and teachers, suggests that when school reform aligns with local and community values, it may be more readily absorbed. Space for teacher pedagogical growth is also an important, practical consideration for South Asian schools interested in reform, since teachers from diverse backgrounds may have varying theoretical and conceptual understandings. Moreover, w hen reform projects provide teachers with timely opportunitie s for creative expression in curriculum design, traditional teaching and learning styles may naturally be challenged, enabling greater critical insight and deeper student engagement. Ultimately, our results demonstrate how the process of school reform is co-narrated by leaders and teachers within a South Asian context.

Our findings suggest that continued study and understanding of leadership and teacher narratives may enable more cohesive integration of innovative pedagogies such as CALE. While this research begins to fill the gap on school reform within South Asian cont exts, it is important to note that the present paper  discusses an 2123 ongoing school reform project within South India. While leaders, teachers and researchers can potentially claim the introduction of new pedagogies, we do not claim that school-wide reform has already occurred in its entirety. Moreover, as our analysis was limited to the aforementioned data sources, the interpretation and generalization of these findings are similarly constrained, even within South Asian contexts. While outside the scope of this paper, future research on school reform may  consider the intersection of other voices within the school environment, such as student and parent narratives.
