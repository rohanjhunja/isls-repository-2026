# Teachers’ Co-Evolving Insights on E-Learning Design and Design-Aware Learning Analytics

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Learning analytics (LA) can provide teachers with timely insights into student learning, helping them adjust learning design (LD) and optimizing educational outcomes. However, the concept of LA is relatively new, and the use of LA integrated with e-learning platforms remains uncommon, particularly among K –12 teachers. This paper reports on a Research Practice Partnership (RPP) project involving primary and secondary schools to develop a system and workflow that supports the integration of LA into teachers’ everyday professional practice, beginning at the LD stage. A qualitative case study of a teacher team over two years reveals how their professional learning evolved, from identifying meaningful learning analytics questions (LAQs) relevant to their LD context to recognizing the importance of selecting appropriate e-learning tools as a prerequisite for meaningful LA, and finally to incorporating targeted LAQs into their LD decision making process, thereby consciously closing the LD-LA loop in their professional practice.

Learning analytics (LA) is defined as “the measurement, collection, analysis, and reporting of data about learners and their contexts, for the purposes of understanding and optimizing learning and the environments in which it occurs” (Siemens & Gasevic, 2012). The benefits of LA have been widely recognized in numerous studies. For example, LA enables educators and researchers to examine student engagement and its relationship with performance (Willans et al., 2019). To enhance the potential of LA, scholars have advocated for aligning learning design (LD) with LA, emphasizing that LA needs to be interpreted within the pedagogical intent of the LD (Lockyer et al., 2013). Several frameworks have been proposed to facilitate LD-LA alignment. For instance, Bakharia et al. (2016) identified different types of LA, such as comparative analytics and tem poral analytics, which provide instructors with information to evaluate and refine their LD. Similarly, Ahmad et al. (2024) developed a mapping between LD and LA by identifying four key aspects: learning activities, learning objectives, LA indicators, and metrics.

However, LA indicators and metrics are technical terms that are relatively unfamiliar to teachers. This limitation can pose a significant challenge for teachers to incorporate LA within their professional practice. Corrin et al. (2016) identified a tension between researchers ’ objectives and teachers ’ practical interests in LA. The research team aimed to use learning analytics to delve deeply into students’ online learning processes and interactions. In contrast, teachers primarily focused on basic concerns, such as student attendance, access  to resources, and overall engagement trends. Additionally, the study found that teachers were often unaware of the use of e-learning tools to capture and process data for analyzing interactive learning. This suggests that professional support is needed to help teachers connect LA with their pedagogical goals, beginning from the LD stage.

The current study is conducted within the context of a project to develop an intelligent learning support system that integrate s LD with  design-aware LA. Incorporating LA into a learning management system can facilitate proper alignment between intended learning outcomes and assessments, ultimately enhancing learning and LD (Divjak et al., 2023). The system used in this study features an intelligent platform with an LD component connected to a Moodle-based e-learning platform, capable of generating LA visualizations. To address the challenge of making such a system accessible and professionally meaningful to teachers, a n RPP (Coburn & Penuel, 2016) was established to provide structured support, helping teachers to engage in LD-LA integration as an integral part of teachers’ professional practice as learning designers (Law et al., 2024). This requires teachers to (1) determin e which LAQs, and associated data, metrics, and analytic results are needed  as part of  the LD process (Law & Liang, 2020), and (2) us e the LA results to inform and refine LD practices (Mangaroska et al., 2018). To understand how to support teachers in LD-LA integration, it is necessary to investigate  how teachers make LA decisions when in the LD process (Macfadyen et al., 2020). T his paper is a preliminary report on how a team of ST EM teachers from a secondary school progressively learned to achieve LD-LA integration over a span of two years. The following research questions are addressed: RQ1: What kinds of challenges did teachers encounter related to the process of LD-LA integration? 2016 RQ2: What changes in teacher design practice can be identified?

## Conceptual framework

Macfadyen et al. (2020) highlighted that a key challenge of LD-LA integration is the lack of a common language regarding its purpose and definition. Law and Liang (2020) drew on design pattern languages and the nature of layered pedagogical decisions (Goodyear, 2005) to develop a multilevel framework for understanding the role of LA in the LD process. Their framework is based on a seven-step LD process. The first four steps involve decisions such as intended learning outcomes, the sequence of learning tasks, and their detailed configurations, such as time and social settings  for learning tasks. The last three steps focus on determining LA questions, selecting LA solutions, and checking the alignment between LA solutions and LA questions. In the Law and Liang (2020) framework, asking Learning Analytics Questions (LAQs) serves as a bridge between LD and LA. This emphasizes teacher inquiry in LD and reinforces the idea that LA is not something given to teachers but autonomously determined by them. Echoing Corrin et al. (2016), who highlighted that if LA is the answer, questions about the pedagogical intent of LD for students must come first. Asking questions is a crucial step in teacher-led inquiry, underpinning the integration of LA and LD (Mor et al., 2015; Persico & Pozzi, 2015). Saar et al. (2022) developed the Analytics Model for Teacher Inquiry (AMTI), which presents an eight-step teacher inquiry process. It begins with clarifying the purpose and motivation for inquiry, then progresses to formulating specific questions and determining the necessary data to answer them. The process continues with selecting data collection tools, gathering data, and engaging in sensemaking and interpretation to derive insights. While technology can automate  some of these steps to generate LA solutions, teachers’ design decisions —such as which technological or e-learning tools to use and how students use these tools in what kind of learning tasks— affect the selection of analytics to adopt and their interpretations. LD-LA integration (Law & Liang, 2020) begins in the design phase to ensure appropriate student data will be collected for generating meaningful LA. Understanding both the affordances of technology-based tools and learning design is essential for interpreting LA (Kennedy et al., 2014). When e-learning tools are involved, selecting suitable tools to curate student data determines which LA can be generated to address teachers’ inquiries, as outlined in the AMTI framework (Saar et al., 2022). The LD-LA loop starts with LAQs and closes when generated LA answers these questions, producing actionable insights. This supports evidence-based decisions that inform LD and improve student outcomes. Throughout this loop, teacher learning is enhanced by data-driven reflection and pedagogical choices (Sergis & Sampson, 2017). Raising LAQs is crucial as a bridge between LD and LA, yet many teachers are unaccustomed to explicitly posing such questions during design or articulating LAQs that translate LA outputs into actions (Wang & Law, 2025). How teachers can effectively connect LD and LA in practice, and what support is needed to overcome challenges, remain open questions.

## Research design

There are 13 primary and secondary schools in an RPP project that investigates LD-LA integration. Each school has its own school-based teacher co-design team and was supported by a university-based consultant to scaffold the LD process based on Law and Liang’s (2020) 7-step design process framework. Teachers were also supported by an integrated LD-LA system, which  comprised two connected platforms. One was an LD platform for teachers to design their curriculum units, and another was a Moodle-based platform for e-learning implementation and LA  generation. The LD platform has already embedded a technological scaffold for the first four steps in the Law and Liang (2020) framework, but it does not include a scaffold for the last three steps regarding  LA decisions. Thus, the guidance of the last three steps was scaffolded by university consultants. These consultants would discuss with teachers after they have completed the first four LD steps to determine (a) the LAQs they were interested in getting answers for, (b) the e-learning tools that could provide data to  address their LA questions. A fter implementation  of the LDs, teachers  would share their interpretation of the LA results and possible follow-up actions to take. This study adopted a case study approach. The research context was a secondary school with which the first author has a long-term collaboration, ensuring access to rich contextual  data. The school had a relatively positive experience in the LD-LA integration process. This  allowed us to uncover a potentially productive  path for professional learning for LA integration in everyday professional practice. Data sources consisted of audiorecorded teacher team interviews conducted annually at the year-beginning and year-end over the two years, and video recordings of teacher sharing sessions during two public dissemination events. Additional data sources were used for triangulation, including teachers’ presentation materials, learning designs, and courses on the intelligent system. All recorded audio tracks were transcribed for content analysis.  We analyzed the data by identifying LAQs articulated by teachers, the pedagogical rationale for selecting e-learning tools, actionable insights, and 2017 challenges encountered in different time periods: before and  after the first and second cycles of implementation in the first and second year, respectively.

The learning trajectory of the co-design team teachers  at School X spanned two years. It began with a lack of awareness of LA in LD decisions, continued with efforts to overcome technical difficulties associated with a new system, progressed to recognizing the benefits of LA, and culminated in justifying LD decisions based on LA. Their learning journey and the challenges they encountered are discussed below. Lack of awareness of LA in LD decisions: School X had a team of 9 teachers at the beginning of the RPP. These teachers had some experience in using different e-l earning tools to support student learning, but without any LA considerations. They explained their pedagogical decisions on e-learning mainly in terms of projecting students’ work or answers, as illustrated in Teacher A’s and B’s saying: For e-learning, I use Nearpod. Chemistry often requires drawing. With Nearpod, each student has an iPad, and after they finish, we can see all the students’ work on the big screen (Teacher A, first year pre-interview).

We will use Kahoot! and set different questions for students to answer, then project all the answers and discuss them (Teacher B, first year pre-interview). Resonating with Corrin et al.’s (2016) observation, our study also found that teachers lacked awareness of LA and did not connect LA to their learning design choices when selecting e-learning tools. When asked about LA, teachers only acknowledged that the school had established a new team the same year for using data to enhance teaching and learning. The learning data collected was mainly based on traditional tests and examinations. Teachers were aware of being “stuck” on how to use data for improving the learning effectiveness: We've fed several years of data, and our goal is to use this data to inform our learning and teaching…I wonder if other schools are stuck here too: even though they have data, they are unable to actually improve learning effectiveness (Teacher D, first year pre-interview). While teachers themselves did not have any experience or idea in using LA to support learning, the school’s goal of using data to improve learning and teaching aligned with the goal of RPP. This goal was attributed to the driving force for teachers to explore new practices in the RPP. Efforts to overcome technical difficulties: There was some procedural knowledge about the use of a new system, which may require teachers to have some self-learning time, as Teacher C said: Of course, there are challenges... Now with this system, I must learn something new. There is a system with different functions. Maybe I usually just listen to the workshops and have not actually used them. When I really need to use them, I really need to watch different videos to learn (Teacher C, 1st year post-interview).

The understanding of technology-based affordances is key to  the LD-LA integration (Kennedy et al., 2014). Building this understanding can pose a challenge for teachers. In this case, when teachers were able to exercise the autonomy of selecting different e-learning tools in the system, they needed to first understand how to use the different tools in the system. The challenge of familiarizing with the system persisted even in the second year. The team overcame these challenges via peer learning among the co-design team. Recognizing the benefits of LA: The first cycle of practical implementation experiences enabled teachers to articulate (a) what LAQs can be answered by (b) what LA measures through (c) what kind of e-learning tools. Their understanding of (b) LA measures went together with their (a) LAQs and (c) their selection of e-learning tools. For example, Teacher C indicated his use of LA solutions such as keyword analysis in Forum  as well as student performance analysis through their responses in Quiz  to answer his inquiries as LAQs about student learning such as “what their (students’) learning need is”, “where the majority of students got the wrong answer”, for his construction of follow-up actions based on the learning needs identified through LA. We teachers can also quickly get some data. For example, there can be a Forum with a keyword needs are. There may also be some online Quiz, and we can give corresponding answers based on their abilities. Or finally, statistics after the entire course can tell us where the majority of 2018 students got the wrong answer. We can focus on their learning needs in class and make them understand some key points of the subject better (Teacher C, first year post-interview). Apart from performance-related analysis, teachers also articulated  LAQs about supervising student learning behaviors via the system, with the provision of engagement related to LA. They considered this to be an “advantage”, which implied they valued the use of the LA. Another advantage is that the system  can help teachers easily supervise students’ learning situations, that is, how many people watch the video, how many people talk in the Forum, these are all ways we can quantify students’ learning situation (Teacher D, first year post-interview). Justifying LD decisions based on LA:  In the second year, the co-design team comprised 10 teachers. Apart from continuing the first year’s curriculum unit for ninth graders, they designed a new curriculum unit for eighth graders with the system. They continued to employ a range of e-learning tools. When sharing their learning design, teachers were able to justify  their choice of e-learning tools with what they would like to know from the subsequent LA generated, and what follow-up actions they could take. For example, Teacher A justified the use of the e-learning tool, Forum, which generated the word cloud as an LA measure to answer LAQs about “what students wrote” and “whether they know” about the topic. The teacher also articulated the follow-up actions, such as asking students, “why they used certain words”.

I used a Forum because the system has a keyword function (word cloud). After they answered some questions in the forum, I could use the keyword function to find keywords, and their comments would be useful. This word cloud is mainly useful for me when I am teaching, because I can understand what they wrote and whether they know what vertical farming is.

Using this visualization (word cloud), we can ask them what they think word by word in class, or ask them why they used certain words, so that their writing can be used in class. (Teacher A, sharing session 1).

In contrast to the start of the first year, where teachers justified the use of e-learning tools with “projection” function, teachers ’ design practice became complicated by  LD-LA integration with conceptual understanding about (a) what LAQs can be answered by (b) what LA measures through (c) what kind of e-learning tools. The generated LA also prompted teachers to think about their timely follow-up actions. The above findings reveal a learning  path of LA-LD integration among a group of teachers in a co-design team. Developing the capability to justify the selection of LA in the LD process requires teachers to first understand the related technology (Kennedy et al., 2014), which can present challenges to teachers. Teachers’ implementation experience in the RPP context helped them consolidate the benefits of LA through making conceptual connections between LAQs, LA measures, and e-learning tools. The justification of e-learning decisions among this team of teachers was no longer only based on projection but also on their LAQs. This presents a co-evolution of their understanding of LD-LA integration and their e-learning design practice. While this study is limited by its focus on the learning trajectory of a relatively successful school team, it allows us to identify challenges among K-12 teachers when developing the practice of design-aware LA, even when they succeed. First, LA is a relatively novel concept for teachers, who may have had no prior practical experience with it. Second, teachers may find the digital technologies associated with LA unfamiliar and must invest effort to familiarize themselves, their teammates, and their students with them. Teachers’ learning trajectory and challenges provide practical implications for promoting the use of LA. While conceptual mapping of LD-LA is important (Ahmad et al., 2024), to realize LD-LA integration in practice, this article contributes to uncovering the need for supporting teachers in making connections between (a) their LAQs, (b) LA measures, and (c) e-learning tools. We suggest t he development of technological scaffold s for enhancing teachers in making connections with the above three elements for scaling up the LD-LA integration. Future research can be conducted to compare the learning trajectories of teacher teams in different contexts and the range of challenges they encounter, to inform the development of various scaffolds for teachers to benefit from LA-LD integration. Additionally, future studies could explore strategies to address the identified challenges to inform the design and improvement of teacher professional development or technological tools, with the goal of promoting LA-LD integration.
