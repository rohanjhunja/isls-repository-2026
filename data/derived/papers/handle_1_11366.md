# Immersion and Motivation in Educational AR Games:
Investigating Narrative Design and Feedback Type

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Game-based learning (GBL) in Augmented Reality (AR) has great potential to make learning experiences interactive, immersive, and motivating. We investigated the impact of narrative and feedback on immersion and motivation while learning about soil science through an AR game using HoloLens 2. An experimental study with N = 123 participants employed a 2×2 between-subjects design with narrative (absence vs. presence) and feedback type (pure outcome information vs. additional process information) as factors. Results indicate that feedback on the process significantly increases immersion, flow, and focus of attention. The effect of the narrative and feedback type on motivation is interdependent: only when the feedback lacks process information the integration of a narrative boosts motivation by increasing their confidence and satisfaction, and emotional attachment. Considering feedback and narrative in research and practice can help fully realize the potential of AR learning games.

## Introduction and background

Technological innovation and its widespread availability are shifting learning toward fully interactive and playful game-based learning (GBL) in augmented reality (AR). By leveraging their immersive nature, GBL and AR enhance engagement and motivation, ultimately optimizing learning (e.g., Garzón & Acevedo, 2019; Plass et al., 2015). AR blends physical and virtual information, offering real-time interaction in 3D (Azuma, 1997), while GBL offers rule-based quantifiable challenges and results meet both play and learning goals (Plass et al., 2020). At their core, games follow a loop of challenge, response, and feedback, supported by design features such as incentives, aesthetics, and narrative (Plass et al., 2015). Feedback is a key aspect of game interactivity responding to the learners’ actions during gameplay (Plass et al., 2015). In educational games, well-designed feedback helps monitor progress and close knowledge gaps, with outcome-oriented feedback providing correctness information and process-oriented feedback offering prompts to guide learners toward correct input  (Johnson et al., 2017). Games often naturally link feedback to goals, rules, and incentives through a narrative  (Plass et al, 2015). Narrative in this context refers to the underlying story that situates the game within a specific context, connecting rules, tasks, and events (Plass et al, 2015) and frames learning goals as a conflict to be resolved through interaction (Dickey, 2020). Combined with AR that enables a coherent perception of virtual and physical elements (perceived contextuality; Krüger et al., 2019,) a coherent narrative can improve the ability to place oneself into the learning setting (Palzer et al., 2024). Narratives can positively influence  engagement, motivation, and learning by representing relatable and familiar events within a contextualized learning environment  in which to immerse oneself (Naul & Liu, 2020). Immersion plays a key role in games, narratives, and AR, understood as a psychological state in which individuals feel enveloped, integrated, and capable of acting within an environment rich in stimuli, interaction possibilities, and experiences (Witmer & Singer, 1998). In educational AR games, this refers to the feeling of being there. Brown and Cairns (2004)  conceptualize immersion as a construct with three levels: engagement, engrossment, and total immersion. Engagement as the lowest level depends on accessibility and user investment. Engrossment involves emotional attachment and attention to the application. Total immersion describes a state describes a state of full absorption, transportation, and presence in the virtual world, often accompanied by a flow experience  (Csíkszentmihályi, 1992). These states can be induced by advanced technologies, but also  by narratives—even in offline activities, such as reading a thrilling novel (Weibel et al., 2010). Using feedback that guides game progress can help focus on the task and understand next steps, promoting sustained engagement and flow (Ambati et al., 2022)  and thus immersion. As shown in virtual reality research, immersion also affects cognitive and affective learning variables such as interest, self-efficacy, and motivation (Makransky and Petersen, 2021), which may similarly mediate  learning in AR games. Motivation can be influenced by instructional design elements such as narrative and feedback. According to Keller’s (2010) ARCS framework, motivation progressively builds on attention, relevance, confidence, and satisfaction. Attention can be increased by narratives through sustaining curiosity, and fostering active particip ation and knowledge-seeking. Process-oriented feedback can help maintain this curiosity, directing attention to in-game tasks. Relevance reflects how instruction aligns with personal goals and motives.  N arratives can  support this by providing  personally relevant contexts (Naul & Liu, 2020). Confidence is shaped by the expectancy of success,  but also  by fears, abilities, and situations that can hinder learning (Keller, 2010). Both too much or too little confidence can lead negatively affect performance. Narratives that engage learners actively promote a sense of agency and control, fostering confidence. Process-oriented feedback provides insight into the learning progress and support next steps, reinforcing learners’ control and boosting confiden ce (Ambati et al., 2022). When attention, relevance, and confidence are met, learners can experience satisfaction—strengthening motivation and encouraging returning to the learning task (Keller, 2010). Narratives support this by providing meaningful opportunities to apply what has been learned, fostering a sense of achievement. Informative feedback can reinforce challenge and success, leading to satisfaction and long-term motivation (Li et al., 2024). This study aims to investigate how narrative and process-oriented feedback in an educational AR game affects learners’ perceived immersion and motivation, and to their role in learning. All hypotheses were pre-registered on AsPredicted. Our first  investigation focusses on  immersion, considering total immersion  (1) including perceived presence (2) and flow (3). We hypothesize that adding a narrative enhances immersion (H1a) and that process-oriented in addition to pure outcome-oriented feedback enhances immersion (H1b). Additionally, we suggest that the effect of a narrative is strengthened when combined with process-oriented feedback (H1c). To get a complete understanding, we examine immersion indicators: engagement (interest  and usability) and engrossment (emotional attachment and focus of attention). Our second investigation focuses on motivation that arises from the learning material considering total motivation composed of attention, perceived relevance, confidence, and satisfaction. We hypothesize that adding a narrative enhances motivation (H2a). In addition, we assume that process-oriented compared to pure outcome-oriented feedback enhances motivation  (H2b). Moreover, we assume that the positive effect of using process-oriented instead of outcome-oriented feedback is enhanced by using a narrative (H2c). Lastly, we explore whether immersion (E1) and motivation (E2) improve learning and whether motivation mediates the effect of immersion on learning (E3).

## Method

We applied a 2×2 factorial between-subjects design laboratory study with a total of N = 123 participants. The narrative factor determined its absence (N -) or presence (N+), while the feedback factor, determined its type, being either outcome- (PF−) or process-oriented (PF+). This resulted in four conditions (nN−/PF− = 30, nN−/PF+ = 30, nN+/PF− = 32, nN+/PF+ = 31). This paper focuses on the affective-motivational dependent variables, motivation and immersion, while Flottmann et al. (2025)  sheds further light on cognitive variables  such as cognitive load and learning performance. Age ranged from 17 to 36 years (M = 22.3, SD = 3.4). 74 identified as female, 46 as male, and one person as non-binary. The majority were students (96.7%) with a general higher education qualification (89.3%). To learn more about the participants’ familiarity wi th using games or AR, we asked them about their frequency of previously using and learning with games and AR on a five-point scale from never (0) to regularly (4). Participants reported using games often (M = 2.6, SD = 1.2) but rarely for learning (M = 1.0, SD = 0.9). They almost never  used AR either in general (M = 0.3, SD = 0.6) or for learning (M < 0.1, SD = 0.2). Accordingly, participants had some experience with games. In contrast, they had little to no experience with AR. The study has been examined and approved by the ethics committee of the institution.  The following outlines the procedure, instruments and questionnaires used, along with their internal consistency indicated by McDonald’s Omega (ω). Participants were informed about the study, provided consent, and completed the eye calibration on Microsoft HoloLens 2 before b eing randomly assigned to one of the four conditions. After a pre-knowledge test on  the learning topic soil science, they received instructions and interacted with the HoloLens 2. During the learning phase, participants played a two-level educational AR game on soil science, developed in Unity with the Mixed Reality Toolkit. In the first level, players were challenged to exam ine virtual soil samples for various properties by using different virtual tools, such as a microscope and a feature analyz er. The goal was to identify the sample with the most fertile soil and place it in a box to  complete the level. In the second level, players w ere given a virtual rock that they had to turn into soil by app lying different types of weathering to it. For this, they had the possibility to add different liquids and temperatur es to the rock, including combinations. The goal wa s to apply all types of weathering to the granite to produce c lay minerals. Game time wa s limited to a maximum of 60 minutes. Depending on the assigned conditions, participants received either a narrative video  or no story,  and either outcome-oriented or process-oriented feedback. In the narrative condition s (N+), participants watched an animated video about an alien invasion targeting Earth’s soil  and were tasked wi th acquiring soil science knowledge to save the planet. In the non-narrative condition (N−), no story was presented. All participants received auditory outcome-oriented feedback (PF−), while  two conditions also included additional processoriented feedback (PF+) that provided guidance on further game interactions. In the narrative conditions,  both feedback types referenced the s toryline. After the game, participants completed questionnaires  on perceived immersion and motivation. Immersion was measured using a German version of the Augmented Reality Immersion questionnaire (Georgiou & Kyza, 2017), which evaluates immersion as a multilevel construct using a seven-point scale. Engagement (ω =.82) includes Interest (4 items, ω =.79) and Usability (4 items, ω =.71), assessing task attractiveness, willingness to invest time, and ease of use. Engrossment (ω =.68) includes Emotional Attachment (3 items, ω =.59) and Focus of Attention (3 items, ω =.61), measuring emotional involvement and focus on the task rather than external distractions. Total Immersion (ω =.79) includes Presence (4 items, ω =.69) and Flow (3 items, ω =.70) assessing the feeling of being in virtual-real blended space and full engagement with the task. Means for each immersion level were calculated by averaging the respective subscale scores.  Motivation was measured using the reduced version of the Instructional Material Motivation Scale  validated by Loorbach et al. (2015), using a five-point scale to measure total motivation (ω  =.90) including Attention (ω =.80), Relevance (ω =.60), Confidence (ω =.82), and Satisfaction (ω =.80). Each subscale consists of three it ems with scores calculated as the average of the items. The mean value of these scores is used to calculate the total motivation score. Learning was measured using a 20-question knowledge test based on the AR game content, covering recall and transfer tasks with different question types (e.g. multiple choice, and labeling tasks). After the questionnaires, interviews were conducted with a sub-sample of n = 40 to provide qualitative insights supporting the quantitative results. Finally, participants were debriefed on the manipulation and offered course credits for their participation.

Surprisingly, the fictional narrative did not enhance immersion. Interviews revealed that while participants favored narratives in games, but were not aware of the story or the goal of saving the world from an alien invasion during gameplay. Although feedback referenced the narrative but lacked a strong link to the game task. Additionally, play ing only two levels  may prevented participants form experiencing a resolution of the narrative conflict, potentially c ausing retrospective biases in self-reported immersion due to a sudden exit from the game. Parti cipants also mentioned perceived inconsistencies between the narrative content and the task in AR, which may have disrupted immersion, as mismatched elements can pull learners out of the experience (Palzer et al., 2024). Significant main effects of feedback type were found on total immersion, flow, and focus of attention (Table 1). Participants with process-oriented feedback experienced higher total immersion, t(119) = 3.05, p =.003, d = 0.55, flow, t(119) = 3.77, p <.001, d = 0.68, and improved focus their attention, t(119) = 2.09, p =.038, d = 0.38, partly supporting  H1b. Process-oriented feedback, which provided guidance rather than just outcome s, enhanced immersion, particularly flow. It also improved focus of attention, suggesting that step-by-step prompts helped learners concentrate on the  task rather than external distractions. Sustained focus is crucial for flow, where gameplay actively becomes all-encompassing, and time perception fades. The guiding function of process-oriented feedback likely helped direct attention, r einforcing immersion by keeping learners on track to explore the learning e nvironment, aligning with Ambati et al. (2022), who also found that guidance fosters flow. Further, a significant interaction between narrative and feedback type was found for engrossment and its subscale emotional attachment  (Table 2). Participants without a narrative felt more engross ed with process-oriented feedback than outcome-oriented feedback, t(119) = 2.82, p =.028, d = 0.73. However, pairwise comparisons for emotional attachment were not significant (p ≥.05). The highest emotional attachment value occurred in the N−/PF+ and N+/PF− conditions, while the lowest were in the N−/PF− and N+/PF+ conditions, not supporting H1c, due to the direction of the effects. Process-oriented feedback was only effective in promoting engrossment when no narrative was present. Conversely, when only outcome-oriented feedback was provided, a narrative was beneficial f or fostering engrossment. This pattern also applied to emotional attachment, reflecting players’ curiosity, excitement, and suspense.  Which decreases with a narrative and feedback rich learning environment. Regarding learning, only increased engagement significantly predicted improved learning, β =.35, p <.001, explaining 9.4% of the variance  (p =.008), while other immersion level provided no additional explanatory power. A detailed analysis of all immersion sublevels significantly explained 22.7% of the variance (p <.001). Learning increased with higher interest, β =.57, p <.001, and flow, β  =.26, p =.044, but decreased with greater emotional attachment, β = −.30, p =.006, and presence, β = −.30, p =.011. No other sublevel contributed explanatory power. E ngagement enhanced learning, aligning with  Plass et al. (2015). Interest and flow fostered learning, while  emotional attachment  (c uriosity, surprise, excitement) and presence (authenticity and agency) may have distracted from the task, likely due to the novel AR experience. Regarding motivation, no significant main effects of narrative or feedback were found (p ≥.05), meaning H2a and H2b were not supported. However, a significant interaction effect emerged for total motivation and its subscales, confidence and satisfaction (Table 2), though pairwise comparisons were not significant (p ≥.05). The highest total motivation  score occurred in the N −/PF+ and N+/PF− conditions, while the lowest were in the N−/PF− and N+/PF+  conditions, mirroring confidence and satisfaction patterns, not supporting H2c. Processoriented feedback may boosted confidence and satisfaction by providing clarity on expectations and offering new perspectives, according to the interviews. This inc reased sense of control, which is associated with c onfidence and, after success with satisfaction (Keller, 2010). However, when combined with a narrative, this effect was reversed – while the narrative seemed to boost confidence by assigning learners an active role in resolving the conflict, excessive process-oriented feedback may have made them feel less capable and patronized by the added information, therefore diminishing confidence and s atisfaction. The narrative role in providing a central, social contextualized feedback may have intensified this e ffect (Plass et al., 2020).  Emotional overload from too much supportive feedback, as indicated by emotional attachment patterns, likely reduced  curiosity, surprise, and excitement leading to reduced confidence and satisfaction, thus motivation. Further results on cognitive aspects are investigated in Flottmann et al. (2025). Regarding learning, increased total motivation significantly predicted higher learning, β =.27, p =.003, explaining 7.2% of the variance, (p =.003). A detailed analysis of subscales explained 10.1% of the variance  (p =.013) with o nly confidence significantly predicting learning, β  =.30, p =.016, while other subscales provide no additional explanatory power. Our finding highlight  that motivation influences learning, with confidence playing the most critical role, meaning learners with strong expectations of success and personal control (Keller, 2010) benefited most from the educational AR game. For t he assumed mediated effect of immersion on learning by motivation (E3), we found that total motivation mediates the effect of engagement on learning, β =.19, p =.029. Increased engagement leads to higher motivation, β =.62, p <.001, which in turn enhances learning, β =.30, p =.024. The direct effect of engagement on learning was not significant (p ≥.05), indicating indirect-only mediation. To gain deeper insight, we examined motivation subscales as mediators between immersion sublevels and learning. We found that confidence mediates the effect of interest on learning, β =.11, p =.032. The direct effect of interest on learning was also significant, β =.45, p <.001, indicating complementary mediation. Additionally, confidence mediates the effect of usability on learning, β =.09, p =.026 with no significant  direct effect, indicating indirect-only mediation. Increased confidence results from higher interest, β =.33, p =.002, and usability, β =.29, p <.001, which in turn enhances learning, β =.32, p =.004. Building confidence is key in educational games and factors like interest and usability can enhance it by creating a familiar learning envi ronment. Adapting games to match learners’ interest s holds potential for improvin g engagement. While narrative s could support this, they were  ineffective in our study  – possibly because the science fiction theme did not align with all learners’ interests.

## Discussion & Implications

In conclusion, this study offers initial insights into how narrative and feedback design impact immersion and motivation in an educational AR game focused on soil science. Disentangling the richness, presentation, and content of narrative in GBL is crucial  to examining their mechanisms and to ensuring they are neither too subtle nor too overwhelming. Further research on narrative  and feedback in various domains, adaptable to lear ners’ needs, will provide a clear understanding of their influence on immersion  and motivation. These insights canderive design recommendations for effective AR lear ning environments in both classroom and  leisure settings. As learners gain more AR experience, the ease of interacting with the system increases, allowing the immersive experience to be used to focus on learning itself.
