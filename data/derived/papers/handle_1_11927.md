# Theorizing on the Intersection of Futures Thinking and Sociomaterial Approaches when Designing Learning Environments

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
ISLS 2025 calls for Learning Sciences researchers to embrace utopias, agency, imagination, speculation, and futures thinking, as central aspects of education and educational research. This poster investigates how designers of new learning environments can incorporate these central aspects by focusing on five design principles: broadening voices in defining and embracing utopias, analyzing the sociomaterial actors, implementing practices to imagine futures such as improv, using new methods to reflect with others on the utility of environments, and acknowledging and considering marginalized voices in framing “that-which-ought-to-be” (Nørgård & Holflod, 2024, p. 2, emphasis in original).

## Introduction

This year’s conference invites researchers to embra ce five central aspects of education and educationa l research:

(1) embracing utopias, (2) agency, (3) imagination, (4) speculation, and (5) futures thinking. Nørgård and Holflod

(2024) position these five aspects in relation to e ducation and technology. Utopias point to an ideal learning

environment, agency focuses on how humans and non-human actors design such an ideal environment, imagination is defined as how humans widen thinking  around what may be poss ible, speculation allows for the numerous possibilities in considering how things ca n be different, and futures thinking points to “ that-whichought-to-be” (Nørgård & Holflod, 2024, p. 2, emphasis in original). This poster investigates how the five central aspects intersect with Learning Sciences and learning environments research to promote five design principles:

(1) including voices in defining and embracing utopias in the development of learning environments;

(2) analyzing the sociomaterial: human and non-human agency within our learning environments;

(3) affording imagination in our learning environments;

(4) adding speculation in the reflection on our learning environments;

(5) considering that-which-ought-to-be and futures thinking in our learning environments.

This poster theorizes the five central aspects in relation to the design of learning environments and aims to engage researchers whose work overlaps with sociomaterial and learning environments frameworks. Design principles Design Principle 1: Including polyvocality in defining  and embracing utopias in the development of new learning environments From controlling interaction to conditioning moveme nt (Nespor, 1997), learning spaces are infused with  power (Orr, 1993). As such, when embracing utopias, it is critical to ask, whose utopias are included?  To consider a broadening voice, Rook et al. (2015) advocate for  having a learning theorist and someone embedded in the Learning Sciences in the designs of spaces and envi ronments, but that is not enough. Approaches such a s Afrofuturism, have shown promise for providing lear ners a critical voice in designi ng spaces towards reframing them more inclusively and representatively (Holbert et al., 2020). As such, researchers should consider including additional voices such as community members and stu dents, but also attend to the possibility that incl usion can lead to exclusionary practices. For instance, bringing in traditionally minoritized populations (e.g., Black girls in STEM), may ignore the multiple intersectionalities that they inhabit as individual s, resulting in artificial generalizations, that exclude their more diverse voices (Constanza-Chock, 2020). Design principle 2: Analyzing the sociomaterial: human and non-human agency within our learning environments Sociomateriality involves analyzing learning as an entanglement of learners and non-human actors in a network, providing emerging theories and new conceptualizati ons of learning and how they drive methodological developments (Damşa et al., 2023; Fenwick, 2 015; Latour, 2005; Barad, 2003). Learning is an eme rgent activity that involves interactions among human and non-human actors such as tools, resources, and norms of  behavior (Tietjen et al., 2023). To consider the sociomaterial, researchers  can apply analy tical frames to understand how non-human actors afford and contribute to emergent acti vity, public sensemaking, collaborative benchmarkin g, and learning residue (Kumar & Tissenbaum, 2022; Rook et al., 2024; Tietjen et al., 2023). Design principle 3: Affording imagination in our learning environments Imagination can be framed as a process of relating to people, institutions, and other forms of power i n order to project into the present and future (Khasnabish & H aiven, 2012). From this perspective, imagination is  essential to learning and creating new futures for people and communities. Constraining this process are systems of power of oppression (e.g. white supremacy) that limit the  kinds of emotions, ideas, and practices that indiv iduals can participate in (McCausland, in press). This constraining of participation hinders the imaginative proleptic thinking needed to produce new kinds of environments, learning, and the futures they make possible (Gutierrez, 2019). To design more imaginative learning environments, we s uggest cultivating an improvisational  ethos, or “a shared commitment to affirming and validating the existence and experience of others” (Tanner & McCloskey, 2023, p. 6). For preservice teachers, cultivating an improvi sational ethos produced emotions and sensemaking th at were productive for h elping them take risks, question, and play around w ith new ways of being and teaching (McCausland & McDonald, 2023; 2024). For students, allowing them to creatively explore making for themselves and their communities has resulted in them creating  more personally meaningful designs (Bawankule & Tissenbaum, 2023). Affording imagination by finding new ways to have people relate to one another, the material, and ideological systems around them, holds possibilities for making visions of the future a reality. Design principle 4: Adding speculation in the reflection on our learning environments Speculating on the design of learning environments encourages reflection on the perspectives embodied in the design itself (Cleveland, 2009; Monahan, 2002). Methodological approaches, such as interaction geogr aphy (Shapiro et al., 2017), provide opportunities for visualizing teaching and learning experiences across space-time. In addition to reflecting on space and interaction, interaction geography can cr aft opportunities to reflect with learners, teachers, and designers. Such representations of teacher-learner movement, proximity, and pedagogical encounters offer potential to recognize the utility of learnin g environments, and, significantly, speculate on possible learning futures. In turn, these speculati ons foster opportunities for educators and learners  to  design future-oriented learning spaces.

Design principle 5: Considering that-which-ought-to-be and futures thinking in our learning environments Future learning environments such as those identifi ed in Rook et al. (2020) have the potential to fost er desired, that-which-ought-to-be learning communities by bringing together individua ls and enabling shared affective responses, holding community together, and moving c ommunity together. Designers also can apply that-whichought-to-be as a framework. Zuboff’s (2023) concept of Who Knows, Who Decides, and Who Decides Who Decides helps designers engage all when considering that-which-ought-to-be. Even with good intentions, persons that are considering that-which-ought-to-be may exclude marginalized individuals. If marginalized voices are not included as partners throughout the design process, designers only further this exclusion (Costanza-Chock, 2020).

## Conclusion

When paired with designs for new learning spaces, t he five central aspects of education promoted by th e ISLS theme enable productive, new design principles that  allow for designers and developers of environments  to embrace utopias, agency, imagination, speculation, and futures thinking. These five design principles move the conversation beyond including diverse voices to those voices that have been excluded traditionally from the design of spaces—community members and marginalized individuals. In other words, in the conversation of imagined futures, we can look to a future that invites those marginalized to play a central role in the design of spaces. Instead of designing for people or for specific pedagogical  practices, we candesign with new voices that prom ote new ways to learn to address powers of oppression, such  as improv. We can also explore new methods to visu alize activity that enable us as designers to reflect with learners, teachers, and designers and apply analytical frames to understand the role of non-human actors. Combined, these design principles prove to be useful tools to move our field forward towards a future that-which-ought-to-be.
