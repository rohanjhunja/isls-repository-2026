# Interdisciplinary Curricular Co-Design: An Interaction Analysis of Teacher-Researcher Collaboration

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Drawing on a prominent theorization of collaboration, this paper analyzes interaction between a teacher and a researcher engaged in curricular co-design. The episode occurred during a process to develop a new interdisciplinary teaching sequence for secondary students on the sustainable use of electric vehicle batteries. The analysis illustrates the dynamics of collaboration in curricular co-design, highlighting joint work on the problem of what topics to teach. The participants’ work on this problem proceeded by co-elaborating candidate topics; evaluating the suitability of topics for the teaching at hand; and reformulating one topic in light of participants’ knowledge and expertise. This activity involved the teacher and researcher orienting to different knowledge resources, only some of which had been introduced in the task. This empirical illustration demonstrates how collaboration in the domain of curricular co-design may proceed, and contributes to a more nuanced understanding of how teachers and researchers learn in co-design processes.

## Motivation and background

Recent economic, political, social, and cultural fa ctors—such as student disengagement and the gap between education and societal needs —highlight the importance of interdisciplinary educa tion and the development of 21st-century skills for future work, active citizenship, and addressing societal challenges. Interdisciplinarity refers to collaboration across disciplines and with non-academic fields like community, industry, politics,  and private life (Markauskaite et al., 2024).  Interdisciplinary work requires resourcefulness to i nteract productively and co-create knowledg e with people who have different expertise and who do  not share disciplinary knowledge, vocabularies, and backgrounds. Interdisciplinary education, in turn, requires resourcefulness from both students, to engage across domains  in schools and at universities, and from educators to develop designs for interdisciplinary teaching and learning. Understanding the dynamics of such interactions  and how to facilitate these interactions in various learning contexts is key to successful knowledge co-creation in multi-disciplinary settings.

Over the past fifteen years, collaborative design has featured as a generative approach  for developing educational, technology and curricula. Collaborative design, or co-design involves “a highly-facilitated, teambased process” in which participants “work together in defined rol es to design an educational innovation, realize the design in one or more prototypes, and evaluate each protype’s significance for addressing a concre te educational need” (Penuel, Roschelle & Shech tman, 2007, p. 51). Co-design of curricula specifically may also support teachers’ agency in reform efforts by creat ing an arena where teachers can influence what is t aught and how it is taught (Severance et al., 2016). Goldman and colleagues (2022) further argue that co-design is a context for both researchers’ and teachers’ professional learning, and observe that “to support this learning, we first need to understand it–for all who participate” (p. 1).

In an effort to understand learning through co-design, we analyze interaction between a secondary school teacher and a university-based researcher engaged in the process of curricular co-design. Our analysis is informed by the notion of a joint problem spac e (Roschelle & Teasley, 1995; Sarmiento-Klapper & Stahl, 2008), and the co-elaboration of knowledge via inter-discursive operations (Baker, 2015). Baker (2015) argues that collaboration is characterized by synchronous interaction, joint attention to representations of a shared problem or  task, and “the process of co-elaboration of knowledge relating to the task domai n” (p. 455). Baker’s understanding of collaboration at the micro-genetic level draws on the notion of a joint problem space as “the structure that enables meaningful conversation about problem solving to oc cur” (p.,76-77). In addition to its original characterization as “emergent and socially negotiated” (Roschelle & Teasley, 1995), more recent conceptual work on joint problem spaces has emphasized the temporal and sequential a spects of interaction in collaboration (Sarmiento-Klapper & Stahl, 2008). Drawing on these notions, we  explore how a teacher and a researcher collaborate  in a curricular co-design meeting, and consider the implications of this empirical illustration for understanding interdisciplinary collaboration.

## Context for curricular co-design

The researcher-initiated curriculum co-design process took place as a part of an interdisciplinary research project on sustainability, electric vehicle batteries, and the Norwegian ene rgy system at the University of Oslo. Three upper secondary school teachers from a local high school were recruited to participate in the co-design process. From the perspective of the university-based researchers, the objective of the co-design process was to develop a in-school workshop to engage secondary students in interdisciplinary content from the  research project, and to pilot the workshop with participating teachers and their students.  The co-design process involved four in-person design meetings (Table 1). The first author, in consultation with the second author, planned the activities for each meeting and served as the main facilitator; both authors participated in all four co-design meetings. The output of the curricular co-design process was a sequence of teaching sessions on sustainability, electric vehicle batteries and the Norwegian energy system for use in required courses. Table 1

In spring 2025, all three teachers who participated in the co-design process implemented the teaching sequence with two cohorts of students (approximately 60 students in total). Implementation of the co-designed curriculum is not the empirical focus of this paper,  however the teachers’ commitment to implementation was an impor tant framing condition for the co-design process with researchers. The interaction analysis (Jordan & Henderson, 1995; Hall & Stevens, 2016) in this study focuses on the work of Roger,  the social studies teacher, and Thomas, a researcher with domain expertise in energy systems modeling, on one task during the first design meeting. Both are referred to by pseudonyms. The pair’s work on the task lasted approximately 45 minutes, and was followed by a plenum discussion with the other teachers and researchers. For the task, the first author, in her role as facilitator, provided each group with slides or a report authored by one of the researchers; groups were instructed to explore the document together as a way of learning about the sustainable use of batteries, and be prepared to report back in plenum on what they had learned. Thomas and Rogers were provided with slides from a present ation Thomas had given earlier in the year.  In her role as a researcher in the project, the first author then joined Roger and Thomas’ group, participating in their discussion.

## Data collection and analysis

All participating university researchers and teachers consented to the collection of video data during the design meetings. Each meeting was recorded using three video cameras and external microphones, allowing for the capture of participants’ talk, gaze, gesture and use of material artefacts during individual tasks, group work, and plenum discussions. Initial transcripts of the video recordings were generated using a university-managed AI video-to-text transcription tool and corrected by a research assistant. The account of Roger and Thomas’ interaction that follows was developed by first identifying talk related to the task in the transcripts of the first design meeting. The first author then reviewed this section of the transcript sequentially to develop an initial sense of how Roger and Thomas  had approached the overall task of learning about the sustainable use of batteri es, and reviewed video of Roger and Thomas’ work on t he task to identify whether and when the pair referred to their respective copies of the slides. The focal episode below was selected for analysis because it is the first time  in the co-design process that a specific graph (Table 2, Video still C) was discussed. This graph  was referred to in the second design meeting, and eventually incorporated into teaching materials used in implementation.

Collaboratively constructing a topic for teaching We argue that the teacher and researcher co-elaborated both the content and rationale for a teaching topic, working towards a shared understanding of what to teach. Th eir interaction suggests a collaborative approach t o defining the topic. Roger and Thomas ’ evaluation of the topics can be seen as co-elaborated, unfolding through their dialogue in response to the first author's question. The reformulation of the second topic similarly e merged from both participants' contributions, referencing the graph. Two key aspects stand out in their evaluation and r eformulation of the topic of increased electricity consumption. First, their collaboration bridges (Sarmiento-Klapper & Stahl, 2008) Thomas' initial introduction of the topic and Roger's re-introduction, with the graph serving as a crucial p hysical artefact that supports this bridging. Roger’s reformulation incorporates domain-specific concepts, which he names after the graph is located by Thomas.

Second, their evaluation also draws on personal knowledge beyond the task at hand. Roger connects his childhood experience of power outages to the abstra ct topic, contrasting it with his students' likely assumptions about constant electricity. By linking this to students’ everyday experiences, Roger argues for the topic's relevance and suitability for teaching. His reformulation thu s integrates personal experience with professional insight into students' prior knowledge.

Their joint evaluation further suggests a joint problem space  (Roschelle & Teasley, 1995), where deciding what to teach becomes a collaborative process, not simply a teacher learning from the researcher. Initially framed by the researcher’s knowledge, the problem s pace evolves as the teacher and researcher co-elaborate to focus on the suitability of topics for teaching, wi th the teacher’s knowledge playing a central role i n the reformulation process.

## Discussion and implications

This analysis of teacher-researcher interaction enhances our understanding of learning in co-design by illustrating how interdisciplinary collaboration in curricular design unfolds. The participants first identified potential teaching topics using resources provided in the co-design process, then evaluated and reformulated the se topics based on their respective expertise. The shift from identify ing to evaluating and reformulating topics can be f ramed as an evolving joint problem space, shaped through their interactions with various epistemic resources and e ach other. While much  of the empirical  research on collaboration  focuses on student interactions in formal  educational settings, this analysis highlights the relevance of  key concepts like joint problem space and co-elaboration of knowledge for interdisciplinary learning in other c ontexts, such as curricular design.  In addition, by making an analytical distinction between knowledge resources introduced in the co-design process by researchers on the one hand, and participants’ references to everyday and professional knowledge on the other, our approach is consistent with the work of researchers such as Severance and colleagues (2016) who conceptualize and study curricular codesign. Our approach to understanding learning in co-design is well-aligned with recent calls to study teacher collaborative learning at the micro-genetic level (Walkoe & Luna, 2020). Such analysis of interaction in curricular co-design additionally allows for further specification of the “interlocking agentic processes of participants taking up and building on the ideas of others” (Severance et al., 2016, p. 558) through which choices about what to teach are made. It is, by extension, relevant for exploring the agency afforded teachers in specific co-design processes. For educational practice, these insights provide an  emerging understanding of the collaborative design dynamics involving both teachers and academics. This can contribute to developing a model for facilitating such interactions across domains and professions, as well as a framework for scaled implementation.
