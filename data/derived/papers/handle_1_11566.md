# Collaborative Inquiry into Climate Change Information Literacy: A Participatory Approach with Young Learners

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study explores how participatory research and mentorship enhance critical information literacy (CIL) regarding climate change information. Grounded in socio-constructivist theory, the research involved a Grade 11 student mentor working with Grade 6 students. Findings indicate that through collaborative activities, students developed their knowledge about climate change-related topics and strategies for critically evaluating digital information, demonstrating increased empowerment and engagement.

Major Issues addressed Developing critical information literacy among the younger generation —empowering them to seek, evaluate, utilize, and create digital information for informe d personal and collective decision-making—is paramount in contemporary education (Osborne et al., 2022; UNESC O, 2023). Young people increasingly use social medi a as a source of science-related information, including topics on climate change (Greenhow et al., 2015; Kresin et al., 2023). While these platforms can inform students, they also expose them to entertaining, commercial, and pseudoscientific content (Allchin, 2023; Dolan et al., 20 19; Höttecke & Allchin, 2020; Mavrodieva et al., 20 19). Given this mixed landscape of information, science and climate change education needs to adapt to equip students with effective strategies to evaluate the credibility of  climate-related information on social media for informed decision-making (Breakstone et al., 2018; Kresin et al., 202 3; Osborne & Pimentel, 2023). This research aims to address the gap in understanding how youth perceive and interact with climate change information and seeks to enhance their critical information literacy (CIL) skills.

## Potential significance

By involving students as co-researchers, this study adopts a participatory approach that empowers young learners to become critical consumers of information. The co llaboration between a science education professor, a Grade 11 student mentor /researcher, and Grade 6 student researchers offers a unique mentorship mode l that promotes peer learning and engagement. The potential significance of this research includes: (1) Educational Advancement: Providing insights into effective methods for integ rating CIL into elementary science education.  (2) Skill Development: Enhancing critical thinking, media literacy, and research skills among participating students. (3) Leadership Development: Facilitating the growth of leadership skills in t he student mentor through active involvement in mentoring younger students and co-leading research activities.  (4) Model for Collaboration: Demonstrating the benefits of cross-age mentoring and participatory research in educati onal settings.  (5) Contribution to Climate Education: Addressing misconceptions and improving understanding of climate change among young learners.

Theoretical and conceptual frameworks Socio-constructivist theory This study is grounded in socio-constructivist theory, which posits that learning occurs through social interaction and collaboration. According to Vygotsky’s (1978) concept of the Zone of Proximal Development (ZPD), learners can achieve higher levels of u nderstanding with the support of more knowledgeable  others. In this context, the Grade 11 mentor and the researcher serve as facilitators for the Grade 6 students, guiding them through tasks they might not accomplish independently. The mentor provides scaffolding—supportive structures and guidance—that helps the younger students navigate media analysis, understand biases, and evaluate the credibility of information sources. The social interaction within the ZPD allo ws them to internalize new skills and concepts, thereby advancing their cognitive development. For the Grade 11 Student Mentor, serving as a more knowledgeable other also facilitates significant learning and personal growth. The process of teaching and mentoring reinforces their own knowledge of critical information literacy and climate change. Additionally, the mentor develops skills such as leadership, communication, empathy, and pedagogi cal strategies. This reciprocal learning  dynamic embodies the essence of socio-constructivist theory:  knowledge is co-constructed through social interaction. Critical information literacy (CIL) Critical information literacy (CIL) extends beyond basic information literacy by includ ing the critical evaluation of the socio-political context of information, recognizing biase s, and understanding power structures in information dissemination. It emphasizes not only t he skills needed to locate, evaluate, and use infor mation but also encourages a deep understanding of the social,  political, and economic contexts that shape how in formation is created and shared (Kapitzke, 2003; Tewell, 2015). This approach prompts individuals to question and challenge the power structures, biases, and inequalities embedded within information systems. Participatory research Engaging students as active co-researchers promotes empowerment, ownership, and re levance in their learning. By directly involving them in research activities, students shift from passive recipients to active co ntributors, fostering deeper engagement with the subject matter (Cook-Sather, 2006; Fielding, 2001). This participatory approach acknowledges their unique perspectives and  empowers them to influence the research direction and outcomes, enhancing their critical thinking and ref lective skills as they collaboratively construct kn owledge alongside educator s (Bovill et al., 2011). Participatory research val ues students ’ input and facilitates mutual learning processes (Cornwall & Jewkes, 1995), leadi ng to more meaningful and contextually relevant fin dings through collaborative inquiry and dialogue (Wenger,  1998). This approach not only enriches the data co llected but also contributes to the personal and intellectual growth of the student researchers (Bradbury & Reason, 2003). Methodological approaches This study was informed by participatory research m ethodology. The series of activities began with a d rawing exercise where younger students shared their existi ng knowledge about climate change. This was followe d by a media analysis activity in which they collected and evaluated various media sources relat ed to climate change, including social media posts, news articles, and Yo uTube videos. Through collaborative decision-making, the student mentor then led hands-on inquiry activities, discussed the nature of climate science (e.g., how evidence is collected and what counts as reliable evidence), and co-developed and practiced strategies for evaluating climate change information.

## Preliminary findings and Implications

This research is still in its early stages. This se ction reports preliminary findings; more details wi ll be provided during the conference presentation.

Students’ awareness and perceptions around climate change Analysis of Grade 6 students’ conversations and discussions during the activities (e.g., evaluation of social media posts) revealed that these younger students are awa re of climate change. Alongside knowledge about cli mate science such as the greenhouse effect, water cycle,  and the im pact of human activities like burning fossil fuels, some misconceptions and developing ideas were also identified. For instance, students knew that defore station contributes to global warming but had difficulty ar ticulating the mechanism of how this occurs. Additionally, younger students expressed strong emotional reactions to this global crisis. Enhancement of critical information literacy Engagement in critical literacy practices enhanced students’ skills in critically evaluating digital information. The researcher, student mentor, and elementary students  collectively developed and practiced strategies. T hese strategies included questioning  who the information provider is, cross-checking the content (what) with trusted sources, and evaluating how the information is presented, aligning with the str ategies suggested by Kresin et al. (2023). It was evident that elementary students adapted these strategies to diverse contexts. Impact of the participatory research and mentorship Engaging students as active co-researchers promoted empowerment, ownership, and re levance in their learning. It was evident that students in this study exercise d agency as they actively participated in co-designing the activities and formulating questions to support each other’s thinking and reasoning. Numerous examples showed students reminding each other to critically evaluate information, demonstrating the development of collaborative critical thinking skills. Student researchers also reflected on their roles and gains from participating in the research.
