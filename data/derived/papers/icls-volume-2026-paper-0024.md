# Pursuits of Social Change: A Comparative Case Study of Youth Learning to Reduce Food Waste in School

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In the face of global polycrisis, we call for greater support for youth to learn about complex problems and make social change. In this comparative case analysis, we explore how high school students engaged in a socioeducational ecology designed for the study of food waste in a climate education class. Conceptually, we recontextualize an established framework in the Learning Sciences (Productive Disciplinary Engagement) to reframe engagement as pursuit-a collective, interest-driven traversal of settings and people over time (Jornet et al., 2025). Through analyses of students' work, video presentations, and semi-structured teacher interviews, we find student groups emerging with different pursuits; encountering and responding to challenges differently; and reasoning through different articulations of the problem and solution. And yet, they uniformly expressed authority and a sense of accountability for creating just and sustainable futures. This study contributes new tools to design and study ecological learning and youth-led social change. Key words: ecological design, social justice, climate education, social design experiment

Key words: ecological design, social justice, climate education, social design experiment Youth today are growing up amid global polycrisis (Rakowski et al., 2025): pandemic, climate catastrophe, wealth inequality, mass migration, genocide, and the rise of authoritarianism. In the face of immense challenge, youth are turning toward protest and grassroots movements of  social change and, unlike the previous generation, are less engaged in formal processes  like joining political organizations or voting (Teixeira, 2024). Between the “informality” of protest and the “formality” of political parties, however, lies the potential of public education. In this study, we consider how, by partnering with purpose, researchers can work with educators and youth to reimagine schools as a liminal space for learning to make social change: learning opportunities designed in school but that resist being bound by the school. Informed by decades  of research on learning (NASEM, 2018; Nasir et al., 2021), we see multi-setting, multi-stakeholder designs, or what Jornet and colleagues (2025) describe as socioeducational ecologies, as unique in supporting youth to meet the global polycrisis that previous generations have bequeathed them (NASEM, 2018; Jornet et al., 2025).

Designing socioeducational ecologies is challenging: Educators must connect the classroom to other sites of learning and connect their students to a diversity of experts. Moreover, youth must learn about complex real-world problems and the micropolitics of deliberating the viability of their solution  with other stakeholders (e.g., peers, administrators, staff). Just as designing socioeducational ecologies  is not trivial, the study of socioeducational ecologies is not trivial. If youth are learning across se ttings and with various stakeholders over time, how is such learning to be analyzed or represented, especially  when the “outcomes” are as diverse as the paths they take to identify a viable solution? Indeed, despite a preponderance of evidence that youth engage in deeper and more critical thinking when they can explore complex problems that matter to themselves and their communities (e.g., Kang, Talafian & Tschida, 2023; Calabrese Barton & Tan, 2018), few studies elucidate how socioeducational ecologies advance youth learning to create more just and sustainable futures. In this social design experiment (Gutierrez & Jurow, 2018), we explore how high school students engaged in a socioeducational ecology designed for the study of food waste in  a climate education class. Our comparative case analysis  focuses on how the socioeducational  ecology supported groups to learn about food waste and to take action in reducing it. Drawing from Jornet and colleagues (2025), w e trace learning as pursuit-a trajectory across settings, people, and over time. The research questions guiding our analysis were: (RQ1)

How did each group formulate their pursuit as they grappled with complex problems?; and (RQ2) What does a comparison of pursuits surface about students learning to make social change? This study is significant for two reasons. First, it advances theoretical and methodological approaches to the study of learning. We use and propose an analytic framework that builds from Engle and Conant’s (2012) Productive Disciplinary Engagement, and recontextualize  it to capture engagement as pursuit, and to reframe “disciplinary ideas” as learning about complex real-world problems (defying single-discipline description). 159 Second, our cross-case representation of youths’ pursuits show how socioeducational ecological approaches resist justice-oriented learning as solution-convergence or as preordained-there is always more than one way through. Conceptual frame Socioeducational ecologies and pursuit Socioeducational ecologies are “collaborative arrangements consisting of social, educational and community agents that expand the traditional school’s boundaries and which have the potential to organize for joint [local or global] work toward more just and sustainable futures” (Jornet et al., 2025, p. 3). Consistent with sociocultural theories of learning, ecological approaches are organized for expansive forms of learning where youth learn to take cooperative action and reflect critically in the development of evermore powerful forms of learning and action (Engeström, 1987; Gutiérrez, 2023). It follows that the design of socioeducational ecologies hold promise in supporting students to learn the complexities of environmental injustice, a multiplicity of ways to address injustice, and whom we must negotiate solutions. This temporal view across a designed topography of settings and people invites new analytic perspectives in studying learning-here, that of pursuit. Pursuit refers to “a trajectory of an individual or group that is in some way driven by interest and that takes place within and across ecosystems of learning” (Jornet et al., 2025, p. 4). In this analysis, the socioeducational ecology was designed through a partnership between university science education researchers and a high school Climate Education teacher. Despite navigating the “same ecology,” as we will discuss, student groups find themselves in very different pursuits-pursuits that shapeshift over time and in relation to the people they meet, the places they go, and their own deepening understanding of the problem. In short, their pursuits reflect “multiple purposeful re-engagements over time” that “may change by specific social and material conditions” (Jornet et al., 2025, p. 4). It is this process of learning that we find compelling in the analysis of socioeducational learning ecologies where, as in this case, youth are also learning what it means to a ffect social change. In taking up pursuit a s the analytic view of how  such powerful learning happens within the socioeducational ecology, we are also analytically in need of a way to capture what is being learned over time. Productive disciplinary engagement and learning to make social change Since its earliest articulations, the Productive Disciplinary Engagement (PDE) framework has inspired  scholars to engage and expand on its groundbreaking contributions to the study of children’s disciplinary learning (Engle, 2012; Engle & Conant, 2002). PDE defines three principles for teachers and researchers, with the necessary resources, to support students to: problematize disciplinary ideas; have the authority to find solutions to disciplinary problems; and hold themselves and others accountable  to disciplinary norms (Table 1, left). Indeed, even a cursory review of prior research shows how the framework has been used to great effect in mathematics and science education, where the primary unit of analysis was discourse among students and teachers, and where disciplinary ideas were well defined in classroom settings (e.g., Sengupta-Irving & Enyedy, 2015; Koretsky et al., 2021). Given how socioeducational ecological approaches push past the classroom and, when inclusive of social change, push past clearly defined disciplinary ideas, we see reason to recontextualize the framework. Our recontextualization of PDE aligns with others who have extended it toward new reaches (e.g., Agarwal & Sengupta-Irving, 2020; Freedman et. al., 2025). In the context of the Food Waste Unit (FWU), we expand the framework in two ways (Table 1, right). First, while engagement  in PDE is often characterized by discourse, engagement is framed as pursuit, which reflects collective reasoning and action across settings, people, and time. Second, in place of progressing clearly defined disciplinary ideas (e.g., photosynthesis, probability), we cast complex real-world problems and social change as ideas to be progressed. In this rearticulation, and perhaps unique to projects of social change, we must also attend to youth learn about making social change. In the rearticulation, we candirectly infer a  theory of change (or theory of action) from how students articulate a problem of food waste (problem articulation), take ownership of it (authority) and the corresponding solution (accountability).

(Multiple) Settings These data draw from a yearlong study of an elective interdisciplinary climate education course for 11th and 12th graders at a public high school in the Western United States (n= 26). The course was designed to support students to: (a) deepen their critical thinking about the complexity of climate change and how it impacts the human and natural worlds; (b) work with peers and learn from community members in developing a plan of action to address some aspect of climate change; and (c) implement their co-produced solution and collect data that helps to assess its strengths and weaknesses. Multiple units were taught (e.g., extreme weather, land restoration); the foc al unit was on food waste (FWU). Relevant to this analysis, the classroom was an anchor setting within the socioeducational ecology. It was where groups deliberated and worked; where their teacher provided formal guidance and assessments; where groups shared ideas informally and in a final formal presentation; and, where some of the external experts (e.g., Nutrition Director) engaged with students. During the FWU, students worked in groups of no more than five students, and were charged with the question: How can we mitigate the climate crisis by reducing food waste in our school cafeteria? S tudents were expected to learn across multiple settings. Within the school, in addition to their climate education class, settings included: (a) the cafeteria, food storage facilities, and the supervisor’s office; (b) the school dumpsters; (c) Advanced Placement Culinary Art class; (d) the principal and custodians’ office; and (e) the quad where groups engaged peers during Earth Week or for  data collection, and where they conducted a public final presentation. Beyond the school, settings included: (a) a local farm; (b) a food bank; and (c) a city landfill.

## Pedagogical context

The FWU took place over eight weeks (Figure 1), during which students: (a) learned to articulate a problem of food waste in the school; (b) learned across various settings  and from a diversity of experts (e.g., custodial and cafeteria staff, the district Nutrition Director, Waste Management professionals); and (c) co-produced a solution that they could study and implement in school. Guided by the Teaching for JuST framework  (Kang, Tsai, & Voragen, in press), instruction first  focused on supporting students to make connections to the problem of food waste. Next, students explored the food waste issues through various activities and were guided to attend to the scientific and social dimensions of the problem, while sharing their questions and feelings. Students, working in groups, then identified a problem that mattered to them and engaged in an action project to redress it. Borrowing from quantitative characterizations of cross-case analysis through diverse case selection (e.g., Seawright & Gerring, 2008), we chose two groups –‘Vegetarian Spring Roll’ and ‘Smaller Juice Box’ –which were different on a number of measures (e.g., how they responded to roadblocks, academic achievement, racial/ethnic makeup). The Vegetarian Spring Roll group included five m ale students, all of whom identified as Asian or Asian American with ties to Vietnam, China, or  Korea. The Smaller Juice Box  group included four male students of different  ethnic/racial backgrounds: Korean American, Indian American, white, and Mexican-American. Both groups had one member enrolled in the Advanced Culinary course. Data We traced the groups’ pursuits through video recordings of in-class and public presentations (with transcripts) and student-generated videos (e.g., touring the food storage area, making spring rolls). These data provided information about their pursuits and how they saw them as related to more just and sustainable futures. We also collected student work throughout the unit. One example was a planning document (Bending the Curve), which groups updated throughout the unit with reflections on what they learned from various experts (e.g., the Nutrition Director, Custodian, Principal, Cafeteria Supervisor); and the solution those exchanges inspired. Another example was the Food Action Story, a summative document where students reflected on their personal connections to food waste; the environmental and human impacts of food waste; how their actions relate to a more just or sustainable future; and, thoughts on group dynamics. Student work provided information about their experiences and interactions with people in multiple settings over time. Finally, we conducted four semi-structured interviews with the teacher that allowed her to reflect on the pursuits of each group.

## Data analysis

In operationalizing pursuit as a group’s trajectory over time, our analytic approach needed to chronologically render students’ ideas in relation to various learning experiences and people. Thus, our first phase of analysis involved reading and interpreting each group’s student work as generated over time. We then wrote an analytic memo (Hatch, 2023) that coordinated our interpretations of student work and videos with the specific activities, settings, and people they referenced or were introduced to at a given time. Analytic memoing is a data reduction method that allows the analyst to reason across multiple data sources and in relation to the broader inquiry. In this case, these analytic efforts were in service of the first research question: How did each group formulate their pursuit as they grappled with complex problems?

Continuing the analytic work of the first research question, we then reviewed the teacher interview transcripts, paying close attention to her recollections of how a group’s ideas evolved over time, her impressions of how they engaged with various activities, settings, or people, and what stood out as seemingly consequential for their inquiry. Through crystallization across sources and methods (Ellingson, 2009), we emerged with an analytic trace of each group’s sensemaking narratively (final analytic memo) and visually (Figures 2, 3). The second phase of analysis centered on the second research question (What does a comparison of pursuits surface about students learning to make social change?). To prepare for the cross-case analysis, we applied the rearticulated PDE framework to the maps and memos. This meant asking, for example, how did the group’s problem articulation change over time if at all? From the group analysis we moved into a parallel analytic structure of comparative questioning: “How does this group’s articulation of the problem compare to the group?” We used a table to track the comparison of similarities and differences in the cross-case analysis.  This phase was highly dialogic with the team interpreting and deliberating together, and returning to data sources as needed. Figure 2 illustrates the group’s pursuit. At the start, the group focused on food waste as a problem of individual behavior. The group articulated the problem in response to a dumpster dive activity where they sorted trash into organic waste, recyclable paper, plastic, and so on. As one member remarked, “it disappoints me that there is so much food going to waste and not a lot of steps toward recycling.” Drawing on their personal connections to food waste, the group knew that individuals can reduce it (e.g., compost bins at home, mothers repurposing leftovers). Thus, changing peer behavior might reduce waste in their cafeteria and local landfills. In the second module, the group’s visit to community settings expanded their articulation of the problem from one of individual food waste to a systems view. Seeing trash at the city landfill and how organic waste was diverted to compost; and, visiting a zero-waste facility food bank that worked with business partners to redistribute food in the community, advanced the systems view. As one member wrote, “I learned from this field trip that there is more to the story and complex systems that go into operating a food system,” while another wrote of the “win-win effect” when food waste from farms is repurposed to feed families in need. While the system's view deepened their articulation of the problem, the group remained committed to co-producing a solution that would change individual behaviors contributing to excessive food waste in their cafeteria. Between Earth Day (M3) and the culminating modules (M5-6), their pursuit was redirected. During Earth Week, the team prepared a recycling awareness campaign designed as an interactive game station to educate peers on how to sort properly. In interacting with peers, however, they soon learned that the problem of individual food waste was, more specifically, a problem of peers not knowing how to sort food waste. For the group, this new articulation meant a more specific solution: introduce a three-bin system (landfill, compost, recycle) and increase the number of bins across campus.

Expressing their authority, and in light of their redirection, the group shared their solution with the custodian. Through conversation, the group came to see how more bins meant more work and maintenance for custodial staff, and that more bins alone could not guarantee accurate sorting. This was reaffirmed when they spoke with the administration, who supported their solution of more bins but wanted to first  see “more data and numbers” to be sure “students are committed to properly utilize them.” These exchanges with experts constrained the pursuit (hexagons in Figure 2); but, this  did not lead the group to abandon it. Rather, in light of a twice-affirmed articulation of the problem (peers are not guaranteed to sort well), their solution was to install an Oscar Sort smart waste system that could provide real-time guidance to student s as they sorted waste. Once again exercising their authority, the team contacted Oscar Sort but did not receive a response. What the students learned at this point in their pursuit is significant: matters of feasibility, labor, and implementation within a set timeframe necessitates redirection (see “X” in Figure 2).

Two experiences help account for the group’s subsequent trajectory. First, during Earth Week, the culinary class took commonly wasted food items from the cafeteria (carrots, raisins) and baked carrot cake for their peers; additionally, the culinary students explained how vegetarian meals reduce carbon emissions in landfills (an idea raised in the Climate Education course, also). Additionally, the group met with their district’s Nutrition Director and Dietitian in their Climate Education class. From these experts, they learned that the district had adopted a new “Offer versus Serve” policy, by which students could choose meal components that meet the National School Lunch program requirements (i.e., a “complete” meal). This surprised the group because the policy was incorrectly administered at their school-students were only “served” additional items, which the group saw as contributing to food waste. As one member reasoned: “We can let students choose a smaller first portion 163 and then return for seconds if they’re still hungry (second meal is still free), and combine this with a clearly labeled share bin, where unopened food and juice can be dropped off and picked up by others.” Taken together, the idea of vegetarian ingredient s and the need for a  complete meal, directly informed their next iteration in articulating the problem: a lack of vegetarian (complete meals) contributes to food waste in the school, and thus higher levels of carbon emissions in landfills.

At this juncture, with the authority of a local expert, the group conducted a food waste audit on their own, focusing on the cafeteria share bin. Their qualitative and quantitative data indicated that fruits and vegetables were frequently discarded, especially in the morning. They also noted that American-style breakfasts rarely include vegetables (they found 71.2% of school meals were not vegetarian). Moreover, drawing on their family histories and practices, they were all familiar with breakfast foods tha t were primarily composed of vegetables: Vietnamese spring roll, Korean kimbap, and congee (often associated with China). The group then surveyed their peers with those options and of the 85 respondents, nearly half voted for Vietnamese spring rolls. Supported by their culinary teacher, the group created a recipe and prepared several prototypes of the spring roll for peer feedback. The spring roll was also presented to the Nutrition Director, whose enthusiasm impacted them. As one member noted, “I’m proud to know that our voices were actually heard and will make a difference.” Another reflected, “What speaks to my heart the most is discovering how many students have healthy or fair breakfast options at school,” and still another, “cooking spring rolls inspir ed [me] to take more actions on combating the climate crisis.” Indeed, in their final explanation of the solution, the group amplified the importance of a vegetarian option beyond reducing carbon emissions-specifically, how the lack of vegetarian breakfast options was unfair to their vegetarian peers (many of whom are also a religious minority), and thus their solution was accountable not only to food waste in the school and in landfills, but to their peers who deserved a menu that reflects them. In fact, in the subsequent year, the school increased its offerings of vegetarian meals as the district continue d to explore USDA approval of the spring roll.

They learn that their peers didn’t like the flavors served, and that their peers also recognized the problem. While tracing juice boxes in the food system, they  saw how juice box waste had significance beyond the school: “This activity…deepened my understanding by showing truly how many hours go into the production and transportation aspect of getting food to our cafeteria…all those emissions and packaging costs really play into how bad the problem of food waste is.” The group then moved beyond flavor to more centrally deliberate how juice is packed, as part of their solution. Leading up to and including the culminating modules (M5-6), there were several key insights the group gained: (a) by developing a survey, they identified which flavors appealed most to their peers; (b) they learned about the offer/serve policy from the Nutrition Director and the role juice plays in making a “complete meal”; (c) in sharing their solution with their teacher, she reminded them that new juice boxes would need to fit the existing cool storage space; and, (d) that the cafeteria supervisor was open to their recommendations as long as it was from a district approved vendor. To these ends, the group conducted online research to identify approved vendors who could supply the right flavors in smaller (4oz) cartons with no straws. Additionally, they met with the cafeteria supervisor to tour and measure the cool storage area in order to assess the viability of their plan. The group, reflecting on their final public presentation to peers proposing new juice boxes and flavors, took pride not only in the multidimensional way their solution mitigated the problem of food waste, but also how their solution engaged their peers. One member wrote: “There is less juice being produced, packaged, shipped, and presented to our cafeteria, so it would in turn produce less energy usage than the system of larger proportions that we have now,” while another said: “[when] we went around to gai n feedback on the implementation of our new juice boxes...[it] felt like a new perspective to my work, in which I truly saw just how connected I am to the people around me, and how our ideals don’t set us apart, they pull us together…towards a greater pur pose.” Although everyone in the class was struck by the vast number of juice boxes to emerge from the dumpster dive, this was the only group whose pursuit remained focused on the juice boxes throughout. As important, within one year, the school switched to the juice boxes the group proposed. Learning to make social change (RQ2) Like other complex real-world problems, mitigating food waste goes far beyond what a group can learn and enact in a semester and yet, both groups engaged in pursuits that led to different kinds of change. As our analysis shows, the groups engaged in the same activities and spoke with the same people but those interactions set each group on a different trajectory. The dumpster dive activity (M1), for example, drew the Vegetarian Spring Roll group’s attention to individual behaviors. In contrast, the Juice Box group focused on an individual product. Further still, the fieldtrips (M2) gave the Spring Roll group a strong sense of the systems-level challenge of food waste while the food tracker activity (M4) seemed to support the Juice Box group drawing similar conclusions. In short, the “same” socioeducational ecology allowed a diversity of pursuits to emerge that reflected what interes ted and compelled them about the problem. Both groups encountered challenging conditions (hexagons in Figure 2, 3) that could have derailed them. Interestingly, however, their subsequent actions either reflected a new direction (Spring Roll group in Figure 2) or new parameters (Juice Box group in Figure 3) for their pursuit. A cross-case comparison of their theories of change surfaced what groups perceived as the impact of their actions. For the Spring Roll group, the theory of change seemed straightforward: if individual behavior, due to lack of knowledge, contributes to food waste, then create a system of knowledge that fixes the behavior (i.e., technology-assisted sorting). Th e reality of the approach-its impact on custodians, its cost, whether such technologies are available, time-conspired to show them the unpredictability in affecting social change. As they redirected their efforts, so too did their theory of change: if a lack of vegetarian options at breakfast contributes to food waste, then offering a desirable “complete” vegetarian meal will reduce it. Now their theory of change was less about individual behavior and more about a systems-level reform where students are offered choices that better assure less food waste and carbon emissions in landfills. In comparison, the theory of change for the Juice Box group remained largely the same but their solution became increasingly  sophisticated. For them, the theory of change was that if you offer more environmentally sound packaging (no straws) and in a size that can satiate a student while leaving room for seconds (smaller cartons, share bin usage), then you will reduce the impact of juice boxes in cafeteria food waste and in turn, local landfills. For both groups, being affirmed around their actions by peers and adults in the socioeducational ecology was vital to how they felt in their pursuit– their learning to collectively engage in social change making. For the Spring Roll group, the redirection from technology-supported sorting to a vegetarian complete meal was prompted, in part, by understanding how their original solution would impact custodial labor and choosing to intervene differently. Moreover, the redirection clarified a sense that their so lution was not only mitigating food waste (in school, in landfills) but also, a tangible recognition of the food needs or preferences of vegetarian 165 students, which was otherwise overlooked in the daily menu. This perspective deepened their sense of a just solution: one that advances a more sustainable future and also a more responsive approach to meeting the needs of students they saw as minoritized  in the school, and without increasing the labor of the custodial or cafeteria staff. For the Juice Box group, their written reflections show  their solution felt most accountable to peers who were being “pressured” to take unwanted large juice boxes, and which was also “inefficient and harmful [to the land] in the way we use resources.” In that sense, their solution advance d a more just and sustainable future that addressed a concern shared with their peers; that alleviated harmful pressures to waste; that took into account the needs of cafeteria staff and limited storage; and, that reduced the potential transportation impacts (smaller boxes, more stored, fewer truck trips) and labor  in bringing the boxes to their school. Ultimately, the analysis surfaces how students conceptualized what it meant to act in just ways, and how their acts expressed accountability to people and places they encountered and learned from over time as they traversed the designed ecology. At a time of global polycrisis, the Learning Sciences must meet its challenges with robust ideas of design and theories (Cole et al., 1978; Lave & Wenger, 1991, Greeno & Engeström, 2006), cultural historical activity theory (Engeström, 1987; Engeström & Sannino, 2010), and taking up the notion of pursuit in socioeducational ecology (Jornet et al., 2025), this study examined how youth cultivated diverse pursuits to reduce food waste in schools and their learning to engage in social change. Based on the findings, here, we discuss both affordances and considerations of this approach to designing and studying new forms of learning in schools. Affordances The design of learning environments must support and advance a multiplicity of ways into and through challenges, if youth are to be prepared for the polycrisis that surrounds  them. The findings of this study demonstrates the design of a socioeducational ecology and how the generative pursuits of youth through them. Each group in the overall class were unique in their pursuits of change. And, while we offered a cross-case comparison, we rightly refrained from assessing one pursuit as better than another-after all, it will take all the solutions to truly move the needle of climate catastrophe, including (but never limited to) more vegetarian options in school menus and less (and smaller) packaging of food or drinks. As illustrated across cases, engaging in the seemingly “ same” ecology yielded important different pursuits: a key insight to any justice or social change effort. Moreover, the socioeducational ecological approach heightened groups’ sense of accountability in creating a more just and sustainable fu ture for their peers and those in the broader school and local community. All this, while also leveraging their personal histories and practices in the face of complicated external policies and micropolitics: federal nutrition rules, district vendor approvals, labor rights, and space use. This analysis also extended conceptual and methodological tools for studying learning in socioeducational ecological contexts. As described in  Table 1, we expanded an established framework on disciplinary learning to consider engagement as collective pursuit and disciplinary learning as complex real-world problems in need of change. Using a pursuit as a unit of analysis, shifts toward better ways of studying how youth learn to  mitigate complex real-world problems as more than the progression of ideas or pra ctices of a single discipline. As important, this analysis invites new lines of inquiry into ways of studying how and why youth evolve their theory of change when engaged in socioeducational ecological learning. Considerations This study leads us to two important considerations in the design and study of learning in new ways.  First, a reconceptualizing of teaching and the role of teachers. Whereas disciplinary  teaching relies on purposeful action that supports students progressing knowledge and practices in known and predictable ways, teaching in socioeducational ecologies  requires purposeful partnering to support a diversity of processes and unknown outcomes. The teacher must coordinate and broker learning opportunities for students across settings and people, taking her far beyond the classroom. The teacher must also navigate micropolitics in and beyond the school with stakeholders who may or may not see value in engaging youth. Further still, the teacher must prepare for students’ frustration as they grapple  with consequential problems like  food waste in their midst. Without the teacher ’s socioemotional and relational support, groups may abandon the pursuits or, equally bad, come to see problems as intractable. The second consideration is  that we need more precise tools to capture the dynamism  of learning in socioeducational ecologies. We rearticulated PDE and still, we seek a place for theory of change within it. As important, we need tools to study the emotional labor of students (and teachers) in enacting social change.
