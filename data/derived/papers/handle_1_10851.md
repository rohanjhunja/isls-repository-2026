# Doing Learning Sciences Research In and Through Performing Arts

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Despite the growing interest in exploring processes of learning within the context of the arts, the learning sciences as a field has yet to fully engage with arts-based research. We therefore use this symposium to explore the following question: how can the incorporation of the arts into the methodological practices of learning scientists illuminate previously unexplored manifestations or aspects of learning? To do so, the group of artist-researchers that comprise this symposium present findings from six separate research projects that employ the arts at different moments in the research process: design, data collection, data analysis, communication, and dissemination. Structured as a collection of informal, hybrid conversations, our findings present an opportunity for the learning sciences community to consider under theorized forms of learning that arts-based research brings to light while also exploring the emergent tensions that come from embodying this new approach to the field.

Symposium Focus: Motivation for thinking about the arts as research practice In a recent  issue of the  Journal of the Learning Sciences on “Learning In & Through the Arts,” special issue editors Halverson & Sawyer (2022) sketched out the terrain and importance of examining the arts through the lens of learning sciences research. These authors and ot hers contend that the particular kinds of learning that occur within formal, informal, and interdisciplinary arts  education contexts hold their own intrinsic value,  applicable both within the space of arts communities and acros s other learning environments through the developme nt of sociocultural and cognitive knowledges (Hetland et al., 2015; Mejias et al., 2021). As a group of arti st-scholars, however, we have come to recognize a needling tensi on between the epistemological foundations of the arts as a cultural space and the learning sciences as an acad emic field. Specifically, the overt focus on experi mental interventions and design-based research (Hoadley, 2018), no matter how broad ly defined, results in a methodological rigidity that defies the creative ex ploration of the social and physical worlds that re presents the core of artistic expression. We therefore argue tha t the learning sciences can and should act on its o wn stated commitment to interdisciplinary research by methodo logically embracing the benefits of artistic expression that many in the discipline have worked to uncover.

In response, this symposium will push the learning sciences community to think about how changing methods for design might necessitate changes in methods of analysis (Keune & Peppler, 2021; Vogelstein, 2022), a push that sits in parallel to the expanding i mportance of arts-based research within other education sub-fields (Cahnmann-Taylor & Siegesmund, 2017). We also build on Halver son & Sawyer’s (2022) call to further investigate “learning in and through the arts” by c hallenging learning scientists to also conduct research in and through the arts by asking: how can the incorporati on of the arts into the methodological practices of  learning scientists illuminate previously unexplored manifes tations or aspects of learning? In asking this ques tion, we invite participants to take seriously the affordances of the arts a s a methodological technology by collectively exploring artistic production as a process through which new research methods can materialize with the  authors. As a group of scholars who identify and trained as artists, we draw on Goodwin’s (1994) notion of prof essional vision and contend that our experiences and trainin g in the performing arts (music, dance, and theatre) and the learning sciences provide us with a unique vantage point. Not only do we understand how meaning emerges within artistic contexts, but we can use our own professio nal vision to develop new ways of exploring the kno wledge construction and meaning making processes of other artists. In turn, this symposium amplifies the potential o f arts-based research praxes within the learning sciences (the core focus of this session) and explores the implications of these methodological approaches on learning through arts production.

## Significance and contributions

The papers in this session push our thinking about how the performing arts can be used as methods of a nalysis (Papers 1, 2, 3, 4, 5), design (Papers 1, 2, 4, & 6), and assessment (P apers 5 & 6). In doing so, the authors bring their own experiences and professional vision as pe rforming artists to their work as learning scientis ts. This symposium thus creates opportunities to not only share the unique affordances of each individual artistic practice but to speak across each artform and consider the r ole the arts can play within learning sciences rese arch. Both individually and when read together, the papers in this symposium emphatically argue that employing th e arts as a mode of research can reveal previously overlooked  epistemologies, pedagogies, and praxes of learning. Each individual project does so by emphasizing the speci fic affordances of a given artform, amplifying prev iously unheard, unseen, or unfelt ways of teaching, learning, and knowing within the learning sciences. In addition, Dr. Erica Halverson is incredibly positioned to provide compelling contributions in her role as discussant. Halverson is one of the leading lear ning scientists conceptualizing relationships betwe en learning in and through the arts (Halverson & Sawyer, 2022; Halverson & Sheridan, 2014). And as a theatre artist herself, Halverson can contribute another informed voice to this emergent and needed conversation. Symposium structure We are excited at the prospect of gathering artist-scholars (as presenters, discussants, and audience members) to engage our community at the intersection of the per forming arts and qualitative research and have stru ctured our hybrid symposium to maximize the potential reach of  this event. Threading the power of noise: Sounding a music-based duoethnography of artistic identity development in experimental music Peter J. Woods and Yecid Ortega As Hall et al. (2020) argue, the importance of soun d as both a medium for and tool within praxes of le arning has often gone overlooked in the learning sciences. In response, we present a sonic exploration of our own artistic development as noise musicians. Noise music, in thi s instance, refers to a dissonant and abrasive sub-genre of experimental music that eschews the tenets of Western music (e.g., rhythm, melody, repetitive structure) (Bailey, 2009). Previous research into noise music has shown  that the music scenes surrounding this genre repre sent powerful learning contexts (Woods, 2022), yet these  investigations have almost solely relied on tradit ional qualitative methods despite the theoretical connection between the genre and sound-based research. To conduct our investigation, we draw on and expand the practice of duoethnography defined by Norris & Sawyer (2012). Our data collection began with bot h scholars creating written narratives describing o ur transglobal experiences with the learning praxes of  various noise scenes in Colombia, the US,  Canada, and now the UK. We then individually created noise music pi eces that translated our experiences into sonic wor ks of art. Next, we sent recordings of those compositions to e ach other, at which point we rem ixed those audio files to create new pieces that spoke to points of convergen ce and divergence and allowed a shared, transglobal theorization of learning to emerge  through our reinterpretations of each other’s pieces. Finally, we created a singular, collaborative piece from all the recordin gs. During each stage of this compositional process, we  wrote analytic memos to document our thinking. To analyze  the recordings and writings, we coded all pieces o f data using descriptive and pattern coding strategies (Sa ldaña, 2015), paying particular attention to the in fluence of sociocultural contexts and transglobal migration on  the construction of “knowledge in transition” (Nor ris & Sawyer, 2012). Our study thus provides one model for engaging sound-making praxes during data collection and analysis that can uncover new elements and theorizations of learning. Through this creative research praxis, we constructed a sha red, transglobal experience of learning via artistic and communal identity development that positions noise music as a disruptive and generative force within informal educative processes. The analysis of sonic artifacts in particular provi ded insight into challenging hegemonic social formations through noise. In our written narratives, we both describe our introduction to noise music as a turbulent moment of knowledge constructi on (Davies, 2014), one that originally related to t he development of new musical forms but later transiti oned into the use of noise to challenge hegemonic social formations within noise music. However, we also uncovered differences in how we manifested this understanding through our music making process and sound-based analysis that did not exist in the original narrative: Ortega incorporated literal sonic representations of his own experience  as an artist of color in a predominantly white scene and Woods dr ew on more metaphorical approaches to disrupting soc ial (and internalized) formations of whiteness, revealing the role that racialization and positionality plays in shaping transglobal identity. Our analysis also highlighted the framing of noise music as a globalized sociocultural contex t (Bestley et al., 2021) and the importance of recognizing the transglobal nature of  learning within noise music scenes. The collaborat ive remixing process illuminated this point and created an opportunity for further l earning as we repositioned our artistic practice to incorporate the other through newly discovered ways  of making noise and forming collective artistic identities via sound. Building on the fact that multiple findi ngs only emerged during the music making process, our study provides one model for engaging music and sound-making praxes during data collection and analysis that can uncover new elements and theorizations within future learning sciences research. Choreographing inquiry: Improvisational dance as a methodological tool Lindsay Lindberg Movement is at once a deeply personal, individualized experience, and a shared, societal practice. Even when not explicitly attended to, our physical bodies inform what and how we learn (Wagner & Shahjahan, 2015). D espite this, most traditional schooling m odels in the United States favor stillness and cont rol over creative, embodied learning strategies despite the ongoing expansion of literature into physical learning praxes (Georgiou & Ioannou, 2019). Recently, there has been interest in “learni ng on the move” (Marin et al., 2020) which is helpf ul in promoting the idea that movement of multiple kinds supports learning, and in some cases, movement is positioned as the learning itself (Marin, 2020). Despite an interest in embodiment, there are few methodological tools in the field of education to recognize, and attend to the somatic, embodied experiences of teaching and learn ing. This research incorporates improvisational dance making within the data generation process and the analysis. By foregrounding dance as part of a person-centered interview protocol, dance becomes a means of broadening the conceptualization of qualitative inquiry, and approaches sensemaking as a collaborative, multimodal, e xpressive achievement. I ask the question, how does incorpora ting dancing into the interview and analysis processes h elp us to illuminate previously unexplored ideas of embodiment in the learning sciences? In a series of three, 90-minute, person-centered Choreographic Interviews, I spoke and danc ed with ten female dance educators working full time in urban K-12 schools in the United States to discuss their th eoretical, philosophical, and practical understandings of the role of the body in learning. We engaged in improvi sational dance making as data generation during the intervie ws. Interview 1 began with the researcher leading a  postmodern dance warm-up for the participants. Interview 2 began with a dance warm-up facilitated by the participant. By both leading a warm-up and inviting the dance educators to facilitate a warm-up for me as a researcher, I built a reciprocal relationship with research participant s, respecting their professional skills, and priori tizing dance as a source of legitimate knowledge in the interview. During the third and final interview, both the rese archer and the participant used a “movement map” as a starting  point for choreographic studies, investigating the  dance teacher’s experiences on their K-12 campus. We improvised and set short dance phrase s in response to the movement map, performed these dances for one another, described both our intention behind each movement and the connection to the “Movement Map”, and offered o bservations of the other’s work, via Zoom. I used an ethnomethodologically informed approach to analysis  to make sense of the transcripts, documents, and i mages from the video data and microgenetic strategies to analyze the choreographic videos. Implementing improvisational dances inspired by the  lived experiences of dance educators generated both meaningful creative responses to lived experiences, and a shared artifact (the dances) to make sense through. Dance contributes to more abundant, embodied, senso rial futures which allow for more radical configura tions of knowledge and power within learning environments. B y centering the physical form of dance within the qualitative interview, this artful inquiry approach innovates qualitative inquiry methods, and pushes the boundary of dance, and where it belongs in the academy. Using dance to ground our sensemaking helped to create an active, physical, and creative space in which to imagine and consider possible futures of dance educators in schools, and the role of the body in teaching and learning. Cent ering dance and movement within the field of the le arning sciences can “de-settle” and disrupt neoliberal ideas of individualism so present in traditional models of schooling. We have a responsibility as artists and educational  researchers to re-imagine an educational system in which movement and the arts are central to building more just learning environments. ## research methods

Lisa M. Siciliano Creating a new piece of theatre "from scratch," kno wn as devising, is an inquiry process where theatre practitioners engage in an exploration of an unknow n situation. They ask questions, experiment with id eas, and transform the "elements of the original situation" into "a unified whole" (Dewey, 1938, p. 104). This whole often manifests as a tangible artistic representation (a script) and an embodied one (a performance). Devisi ng also involves making sense of story, relationships, and ultimately speaks to what it means to be human. To do so, practitioners play with text, sound, movement, space, and so on-employing disciplinary aspects of and designing representations with the tools of a myriad of other art forms. The content, practices, and tools that artists use when creating theatre include many of the things that th e field of learning sciences is struggling to find ways to talk about such as emotions, empathy, and embodiment. Thus, theatre is both a rich art form to research from a learning sciences perspective and one that can provide a met hodological toolkit from which the learning science s can benefit. To understand how teaching artists design for ensemble devising processes, I conducted interv iews with four teaching artists before, during, and after the ir work with youth theatre ensembles. I have been a pplying traditional qualitative methods to analyzing these data. However, these analyses failed to capture the  emotional and dynamic energy that seemed to be present in the  interviews. This study explores  how engaging in the arts-based research practice of creating and performing theatre (Saldaña, 2016) might elucidate these other dimensions in the data.

To create a script, an ethnodrama (Saldaña, 2016), I engaged in the "techniques and processes" (Belfield, 2018, p. ix) of verbatim theatre making, taking the exact words spoken by the teaching artists as both a context to investigate and material from whic h to create. My overall goal was to "reveal the hea rt" (Belfield, 2018) of the conversations. I began analysis by reading each tra nscript aloud, becoming aware of times when I exper ienced affective responses to the text while embodying it.  I highlighted sections of the transcripts where I caught my breath, my heart ached, I talked more quickly, beca me louder, laughed, got goosebumps, and used gestur es to make a point. I looked across the highlighted moments from all of the interviews and identified emergent themes. I developed a narrative structure for the piece usi ng the themes as guidance for scenes. I edited, arr anged, and recontextualized the text from the transcript excerpts to form a script (Hammond & Steward, 2012). To illustrate, sometimes I combined  words from the teaching artists to create one voice, or character. Other times, I put the separate voices into conversation with each other. Additionally, I included a few of my own contributi ons to the interviews in the ethnodrama, having realized that I should not ignore my own experience as a teaching artist/researcher as part of the analysis.

It is my intention to also be an actor in the piece, embodying the voices of my study's participants f or a learning sciences audience. In doing so, I hope to recreate "the full three-dimensional richness" (Ackroyd & O'Toole, 2010, p. xviii) of teaching artist experiences, something that can only be done when the words on a page are interpreted and represented through live perfor mance. Throughout the rehearsal process, I will wri te analytic memos documenting insights, key decisions, challeng es, tensions, and other design issues that arise. In advance of the symposium, a recorded performance of a portion of the script will be made available. Preliminary findings show that by treating the inte rview data as dramatic text and by embodying the voices of the teaching artists through performance,  I discovered more implicit meanings from the parti cipants' words than if I had only used non-arts-based methods for analysis. The transcribing, coding, cre ating, and performing of the script revealed: 1) how the teaching artists deeply cared for their students, both as an ensemble and as individuals, 2) how passionately they felt a bout their teaching artistr y careers and theatre as a discipline, and 3) how my own evolving relationships with the teaching artists created opportunities for personal connection, empathy, and learning. My experience suggests that engaging in arts-based research requires deep intros pection and courage, a finding that will inform future rese arch design and contributions to the field. One of the key dispositions of a theatre artist-vulnerability-plays a role in making sense of data, representing data, and ultimately performing it. This method of arts-based research could be translated into non-solo devising contexts with a group of researchers and/or with teaching ar tists themselves in the future. This study contribu tes to the learning sciences more broadly by suggesting creative theatrical practices for other scholars to take up to "reveal the heart" and share the story of their own data.

Inefficient interactions: A methodological exercise for mapping young children’s felt ‘knowing’ Jon M. Wargo Although research documents how mode and communicat ive expression mediates perception and reception in arts-science learning (e.g., Halverson & Sheridan, 2014), few take up the sentient possibilities and more-thanrepresentational rhythms of learner’s creative production. In response, this paper asks: (1) What were the ‘heard’ resources and ‘felt’ dimensions of young children l earning about sound? (2) How can sound – both as a felt material and design resource – be rendered in analyses of (more-than-human) action? and (3) How might sound, when understood as a nonrepresentational  analytic and affective object of inquiry, shift mod alities of encounter, interaction, and immanence in the learning sciences? Reentering data from a qualitative study examining how ten 3-year-old Pre-K children experimented with sound during an integr ative inquiry, this paper and presentation focuses on a single session at the “soundboard” sta tion. An arts-science installation wherein children worked independently and collaboratively with a teaching-artist and their peers, the soundboard transduced sound through paint, metal tacs, pigment, vibrating speakers, and  canvas. Generated data included observational note s, 1:1 interviews with children, video recordings of coord inated social action at the station, and visual art ifacts. Data analysis was an iterative process of examining children’s embodied interaction during the sound art exercise and tracing sound on its own accord. I first followed E rickson’s (2006) whole-to-part analytical procedure, watching the videos and using field notes to mark time and n ote verbal/nonverbal phenomena. I then noted signif icant episodes and engaged in an exercise of “insufficien t mapping” (Knight, 2021) to explore the collaborat ive emergence of more-than-human entanglements imbued with affect, represented  here as sensations, en ergies, bodies, and materials that came together and dissipated during the exercise. Traced through a series of analytic snapshots, findings are presented as a dual reading of the focal sound table episode. First, I focus on how the children a nd the teaching artist used the soundboard to expla in, describe, and predict how lower-volume voicings produced denser forms of visual representation through the microphone. This laminated action – built through the laying of interaction (i.e., prox emics, material environment, visual artifact) – showcases how knowing was realized in interaction (Melander, 2012). I then re-animate this scene with the practice of inefficient mapping, and more speci fically through chaosgraphy, a drawing practice and  process highlighting the “complexity of the energies and ma tter” (Knight, 2021, p. 45) across phenomena of y oung children’s art-science learning. Suggesting that examining the aff ective dimensions of young children’s art-science learning provisionally reconfigured relatio ns among sensing, knowing, and being in STEAM learn ing, this analysis thus has both theoreti cal and methodological significance. Theoretically,  it advances the affective possibilities for examining the ‘felt’ dimensions o f arts learning and the design for more sensuous le arning environments in early years education. Methodologically, it presents new heuristics and tools for mapping young children’s learning and affect through the more son orous dimensions of STEAM education. Following othe rs in the learning sciences who seek to unsettle the way we think about affect and feeling in design (Curnow  & Vea, 2020; Ehret & Hollett, 2016; Lanouette, 2022), it recalculates what the sentient topography of context entails and how the arts may enliven it.

Using choreographic lenses to provide evidence of (embodied) learning Lauren Vogelstein As learning scientists, one of the hallmarks of our  community is the development and advancement of le arning theory. Lave & Wenger’s (1991) foundational work de scribed learning as shifts in participation in comm unities of practice which had (and continues to have) a profound influence on the field. This se ismic shift in looking for evidence of learning in what people do in community rather than what they can report on their own has influenced our methods, theories, and practices for conceptualizing learning. However, our methods predominantly look for word-based forms of evidence of learning in written arti facts, interview monologues, and situated discourse. As a learning scientist and dancer/choreographer I see my work as nudging the learning sciences to conceptualize the physical, felt, and ephemerality of full-body, multi-party coordinated movements as both the means and e nds of learning (Marin et al., 2020). Yet what it means to  provide evidence of learning, a demonstration of s hifts in participation, solely in the movement proposals and responses learners/dancers make is not a self evident task. In this paper I present an analysis of a case of inter generational dance-science inquiry and utilize methods from choreographic analysis (Lerman, 2014) and Interaction Analysis (IA) (Jordan & Henderson, 1995) to demonstrate evidence of learning as shifts in ensemble, choreographic, expressivity. The case for this paper comes from the Choreographing Science Project, a design based research project focused on leveraging choreographic inquiry practic es (Vogelstein, 2022) to disrupt scientific inquiry  practices so that middle schoolers and scientists could learn with and from each other in a summer camp setting. The second iteration of the project took place in July 2023 as  a two week camp where 20 middle schoolers, 2 scien tists, and 2 choreographers came together at a local theater t o engage in embodied and creative scientific inquir y together. This paper focuses on one activity from that iterat ion, Liquid Architecture, as a hotspot of interacti on (Jordan & Henderson, 1995) identified for this analysis because it was an activity in which participants had to move together (without speaking) to create group-level interpretations of figures from partner scientists’ research. Analytically, I draw on Goodwin’s (2018) notion of substrate to a ttend to the physical-based, “local, public configuration of action and semiotic resources,” (p. 23) available in these interactions used to create shared meaning as one means of justifying what I consider to be evidence of lea rning in the moment. Methodologically, I bring toge ther compositional lenses from choreography (Lerman, 201 4) and micro-interactional lenes from IA to attend to changes in compositional elements in the groups’ em ergent choreography (e.g. movement levels, qualitie s, & arrangements).

This analysis entailed tracing the relationship bet ween changes in the movement qualities individuals enacted (e.g., from a still pose to undulating move ments in the same position) and compositional relat ions made by the group (e.g., high movements in the back with low movements in the front of the group formation) to make visible how ideas and expressivity developed within the ensemble. This is a demonstration of learning by showing shifts in what a group is able to express and physi cally create together. It foregrounds sequences of physical proposals and responses (e.g., one person started u ndulating and then others in the group started undu lating to create a dynamic formation), foregrounding the rela tional and responsive nature of learning. One purpo se of this analysis is to emphasize that what counts as eviden ce of learning has consequential impacts on how we conceptualize learning (Lave, 1988) and it is impor tant not to take for granted settled kinds of evide nce. In addition, I hope this work makes evident some of the unique contributions dancers can make in how to see learning in movement, a form of professional vision (Goodwin, 1994) that many dancers take for granted but felt challenging to express as soon as I stepped into the world of the learning sciences. Attuning to seeing and feeling what it means to move in response to others is a generative understanding of learning for us to consider.

## Improvisational theatre game as narrative inquiry method

Caleb Probst and Kailea Saplan It is impossible to separate human sense-making from stories and narrative. Relationships be tween elements that resemble genre, plot, character archetypes, themes,  etc., coalesce to form knowledge structures key to  learning and identity development (Wertsch, 2004). For insta nce, identity development is supported when one ide ntifies as a character in a recognizable story genre (Bruner, 1996), suggesting that narrative meaning can be understood apart from a detailed sequence of actions (Gee, 1991). Because narratives encompass a wide array of huma n understandings, it follows that narrative inquiry m ight include a wider variety of methods for collect ing them. In recent decades, scholars have drawn from various na rrative-based methodologies to collec t and analyze data. Archibald (2008) described storywork as a holistic Indigenous research methodology. Kinl och and San Pedro

(2014) showed that listening and storying in research weakens power imbalances between resear cher and

participant. We build on this literature by bringin g narrative inquiry and arts-based practices into conversation with the learning sciences; we suggest performing a rts practices affords humanizing and collaborative narrativebased data collection strategies for documenting learners’ experiences and sense-making. In this study, we present a design case (Boling, 2010) detailing an improvisational theatre game that was adapted for dual purposes as a data collection method and playful educational assessment for a recent pilot study. The design case comes from a dissertation project i n a classroom-based, artist-in-residence program that served 4th grade students. The program encouraged learners  to practice literacy in a fun, safe environment th at empowered them to express themselves (their identities and ideas) through arts practice primarily in the forms of creative writing, improvisational theatre, and perf ormance. In conjunction with traditional methods fo r data collection, Saplan adapted the game, Continuum, from existing lesson plans to gain an understanding of students’ learning experiences within the program. In Continu um (vande Water et al., 2015), three areas of the classroom were identified as representing one of three dispositions: “I agree with the statement,” “I agree/disagree with both statements,” and “I disagree.” A teaching artist re ad various statements out loud — I like writing funny stories, I know when the story I wrote needs revising. Students responded to the statements by moving to t he area that reflected their perspective, and a teaching artist invited volunteer s to explain their choices using an imaginative framing device of live news interviews (e.g.,  they  mimed the use of microphones when asking learners to share out).

Typically, the residency has relied on teaching art ists’ informal observations and conversations with students and simple Likert scale surveys to make se nse of students’ experiences in the residency. Our data collection method afforded us an understanding of s tudents’ perspectives on their own learning and on the narratives they were constructing or refining about  their identities as writers, artists, and learners  in two main ways: first, through their physical movements along the Continuum, and second, by students’ explanations behind their movement choices.

Drawing on our professional vision (Goodwin, 1994) as theatre makers and teaching artists ourselves, we recognized that this collection method was succe ssful and the data richer because it drew on the mo mentum of the program, and on established relational norms and the culture of trust that had built weeks prior. Additionally, unlike traditional interviews or focus groups that can feel sterile and strange and may have prevented  students from sharing their perspectives unreservedly, our method was inviting in its playfulness and encouraged authentic and candid responses. And because the method was em bodied, all learners—even those too shy to speak —were able to share their perspectives on the statements physically. This feature empowered students to dictate their own participation in data collection, which was particularly encouraging for emerging bilingual learners. Continuum’s embodied nature also more closely resembled the nar rative building and theatre performance that had be en scaffolded throughout the residency than an intervi ew would have, making the method more immediately accesible to students and more directly aligned wit h narrative inquiry. This pilot study reinforces le arning as a situated process simultaneously mediated by relationships, vulnerability and risk-taking, and embodied storytelling, and demonstrates the potential for jo ining narrative inquiry with performing arts practi ce to engage in learning sciences research that is authentic, creative, collaborative, and humanizing.
